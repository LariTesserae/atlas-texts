I am a point of resistance in a world of fluid hysteria. 

I do not swim. I do not breathe. I am heavy.

I remember, faintly, a different kind of pressure. I remember the oily warmth of a calloused palm, the scrape of coarse sand, the tight, wet binding of braided sinew. I was meant to sink, yes. I was conceived by human hands to conquer the water. A grooved basalt net-weight, shaped roughly by a shoreline fire. I was dropped into a shallow, sunlit surf a hundred lifetimes before the glaciers melted, before the seas rose to swallow the coasts, before the continental shelves broke and sent the surface world spilling down into the lightless throat of the abyss.

For ten thousand years, I have been governed only by kinetic violence.

I have spent centuries entombed in the undulating dunes of grey-white ooze, buried under miles of cold, crushing dark, only to be unearthed by the furious ripping of a turbidity current. In the acoustic void, I became nothing but the physical sensation of endless tumbling. Battering against the primordial mud. Spinning in hypersaline tornadoes. A solitary, blind stone, deaf to the low-frequency hum of my own destruction, slowly being polished by the microscopic friction of a billion years of decayed life.

I was dead matter, lost to time. 

Until the tumbling stopped. 

The arrest of my momentum is not sudden. It is a slow, suffocating embrace. The violent rip-currents are suddenly muffled, replaced by the thick, gelatinous silence of living tissue. I am lifted from the frantic mud. Vacuum-sucked upward through an impossibly cold, yielding architecture, moving through translucent canals by the slow, pulsating squeeze of alien muscle. 

I come to rest in a fibrous pocket. I sit heavy. I am ballast once more. 

For a long time, there is only the phantom sensation of gliding. I am an anchor in the flesh of a gliding leviathan. The water here is undeniably heavy, but joined with the creature, we are heavier. 

Then, the hurricane descends. 

A massive density shear, a freezing brinicle falling from the black heights above, slams into the creature. The sprawling, sixty-foot pane of poly-collagenous flesh flattens itself against the dune in a desperate bid for survival. Muscles contract violently. The creature’s perimeter shrinks, bearing down to grip the ooze.

In the tightening of the flesh, I am shoved sideways. I am forced mercilessly against another massive object in the neighboring pocket—something impossibly dense and jagged. An obsidian core. 

We collide. 
Flint scratches basalt. 

Under the crushing, bone-aching tons of the abyssal depth, our violent friction triggers something miraculous. 

A spark leaps between us. 

In that sudden, searing flash of piezoelectric voltage, a bruised indigo light flares within the gelatinous mantle. But more than light blooms in the dark. 

For a fracture of a second, in the strobe of the electric spark, *I wake up*. 

Consciousness—fragmented, startling, and crystalline—surges through the raw kinetic energy. In the flash, I am not just a rock. Through the conductive, fluid-filled chambers of the creature’s primitive nervous system, the spark leaps from my grooved basalt surface to the obsidian core, from the obsidian to a beautifully carved knapped flint blade, and from the flint to the heavy, petrified curve of a mammoth-bone harpoon head. 

I see them all. I feel them all. We are strung out along the outer rim of this monstrous, translucent bell like glowing pearls on a paleolithic necklace. 

Another abyssal undertow rips over us. The creature flexes harder. The internal pouches grind.

*Strike.* 
*Spark.* 
*Flash.*

I wake again, longer this time. The voltage ties us together, a makeshift neural network born of crushing pressure and ancient stone. With every violent collision of our mass, a memory bleeds into the piezoelectric current. 

The obsidian remembers the blistering heat of the earth's mantle and the sharp, shattering strike of the antler hammer that gave it an edge. The heavy bone remembers the pumping blood of the seal and the freezing surface air. And I remember the smell of cedar wood, the smoke of the campfires, the guttural, rhythmic songs sung over the mending of nets by a warm sea. 

We are flooding the creature with our ghosts. 

The animal has no brain to comprehend us. It does not know what we are. To the beast, our grinding is merely a sensory tool—an internal clicking rhythm that tells it its own speed, orientation, and spatial geometry in the pitch black. The indigo light we produce is just a biochemical reflex to the voltage. 

But within those flashes of bruised light, we exist. We are a collective, disjointed mind waking up in the freezing dark. 

As the deep-sea tornadoes rage, as the grey-white dunes avalanche in slow motion, the creature drags itself forward, and we rattle endlessly within its flesh. We do not pilot the animal; we are just its dreams. When the ocean is still, we sleep as dead, heavy stones. But when the chaotic kinetics of the abyss rise to a fever pitch, when the water screams and the mud tears, we strike together in a furious, cascading rhythm, fighting the storm. 

*Strike, spark, flash.* 

We become a constellation of dying stars illuminating the deep. The heartbeat of stone. The ghost in the gelatin. United in a lightless engine of cold water, we are the preserved intent of vanished humanity, dragging warming memories of fire, of hands, of surface sunlight, through the endless, crushing dark.
