**The Crystal Throat**

High in the mountain's gullet, where the stone opens into a vertical chimney barely thirty meters across, ice and mineral have grown together into something neither wholly natural nor entirely made. The walls rise in concentric terraces of translucent blue-white crystal, each ledge no wider than a hand's breadth, spiraling upward until they vanish into the brilliant aperture of sky far above. Light pours down through this opening like liquid mercury, caught and refracted by ten thousand surfaces until the entire cavity blazes with cold fire.

The crystals themselves are not ice, though ice forms and reforms across their surfaces in an endless cycle. They are some older mineral—perhaps salt, perhaps something that has no name—deposited over millennia by the moisture that condenses here when warm air from below meets the perpetual cold of this elevation. Each crystal face weeps steadily, trickling threads of water that freeze, thaw, and freeze again as the light shifts through its daily arc.

Scattered across the narrow terraces are small cairns of fitted stone, no two alike. Some are mere stacks of three or four rocks; others form crude shelters just large enough for something to huddle within. The stones show signs of shaping—edges knocked clean, surfaces ground smooth—but the tools that worked them are nowhere to be seen. Between the cairns run paths worn into the crystal itself, shallow grooves that channel the constant trickle of meltwater into patterns that seem almost deliberate, almost meaningful.

The sound here is a whisper built of whispers: the tiny percussion of droplets striking crystal, the occasional crack of ice forming or breaking free, the hollow moan of wind across the opening high above. Sometimes, when conditions align perfectly, the entire throat becomes a vast organ pipe, producing a single pure note that can be heard for miles across the peaks.

The air tastes of minerals and electricity. Static charge builds along the crystal faces until it discharges in brief, silent sheets of pale blue light that race up the walls like backwards lightning. These flashes reveal details otherwise hidden in the overwhelming brightness: the way certain crystals have grown around and through the stone cairns, incorporating them; the presence of thin filaments—perhaps fungal, perhaps mineral—that web across some surfaces like frozen neural networks; the fact that the spiral of terraces follows a mathematical precision too perfect for pure chance.

At the throat's bottom, where the walls narrow to a point, a pool of water no larger than a cart wheel reflects the sky above with impossible clarity. The pool never freezes, never overflows, though water constantly drips into it. Sometimes the reflection shows stars even in daylight. Sometimes it shows nothing at all.

The only movement beyond water and light comes from the crystals themselves. They grow—imperceptibly, inexorably—adding new faces, extending their reach across the terraces. When one finally touches another, they fuse, and in that moment of joining, a sound rings out like a bell struck once. The cairns are slowly being consumed, stone by stone, transformed into something between architecture and geology.

This place feels like a pause in the world's breathing, a held note in a larger composition. It exists at the intersection of processes too slow for human perception and forces too quick to catch—the millennial accumulation of mineral and the instant flash of static discharge, the patient growth of crystal and the sudden crack of thermal stress. It is both monument and mouth, sanctuary and trap, a place where something was either begun or abandoned, and the evidence grows more ambiguous with each passing season.
