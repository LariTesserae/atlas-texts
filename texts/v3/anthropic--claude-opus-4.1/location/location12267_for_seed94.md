The plateau breathes moisture through ten million pores.

At this elevation, the clouds don't pass overhead—they move through the landscape itself, threading between the towering cushion-plants that have claimed every square meter of available ground. These are not individual plants but colonies, ancient beyond counting, their surfaces packed so densely that from a distance the entire plateau appears upholstered in deep green velvet. Each colony rises in a perfect dome, some reaching twenty meters high, their billions of needle-leaves beaded perpetually with condensation that never quite falls as rain but transfers directly from fog to leaf to root in an endless, silent circulation.

The scale defies comprehension. This highland stretches beyond any visible horizon, a continent-sized altar raised into the permanent cloud layer. The cushion-plant colonies have grown together so tightly that their edges fuse, creating ravines and valleys not of exposed earth but of compressed plant matter—corridors where the green walls press in from both sides, sweating darkness. In these depths, the temperature drops even further, and strange bioluminescent threads pulse through the plant tissue like slow neural activity, the only sign of the industrial-scale chemistry occurring within.

For these plants are factories. Their root systems, plunging kilometers deep, tap into mineral veins and thermal gradients, running a vast biological engine that processes rock into complex organic compounds. The quiet here is not peaceful but concentrated—the silence of immense work being done just below the threshold of hearing. Occasionally, a deep thrumming passes through the ground as some far-off colony adjusts its internal pressure, sending ripples through neighboring plants like the movement of a sleeping giant's breath.

The fauna have evolved to live entirely within this vegetal matrix. Creatures tunnel through the plant colonies rather than walking on them—the surface is too soft, too wet, too treacherous. Networks of natural passages wind through the green mass, worn smooth by generations of passage. The animals themselves are elongated, compressed, their fur or scales adapted to shed the constant moisture. They navigate by vibration and by reading the faint electrical fields the plants generate. Predation happens in slow motion within the green corridors, muffled struggles that barely disturb the surrounding stillness.

Water moves everywhere but rarely flows. It seeps horizontally through the plant mass, emerges as springs that immediately sink back into green, pools in natural cisterns within the colonies where specialized roots form dense filters. During the warmest hours, when sunlight briefly penetrates the cloud layer, steam rises from the entire plateau in columns that create their own weather systems—micro-storms that play out in spaces barely larger than a city block, complete with tiny lightning strikes that feed the plants' chemical processes.

The aerosol atmosphere carries spores and chemical signals across vast distances. The colonies communicate through these airborne molecules, coordinating their growth, warning of damage, sharing resources through root networks that span the continent. Sometimes a wave of color change passes across the landscape—subtle shifts from deep green to bronze to jade—as millions of plants respond to some signal, some need, some opportunity detected far away.

At the plateau's edges, where it meets lower elevations, waterfalls pour continuously but impossibly from the plant mass itself—not from rivers or streams but from the sheer accumulation of moisture wrung from the clouds. These falls dissolve into mist before reaching the lands below, creating a permanent halo of rainbow-shot fog that marks the boundary between this world and the more familiar landscapes beneath.

The age of this place shows in its completeness. Every niche is occupied, every resource cycling through the system. The cushion colonies have had geological time to optimize their processes, to evolve their chemical factories, to achieve a balance so perfect it appears static despite the constant motion within. Thunder without sound rolls through the green valleys as pressure systems adjust. Nutrients pulse through root networks in tides that take months to cross the plateau. 

This is industry without artifacts, technology without tools—a biological machine the size of a continent, running on fog and minerals, producing oxygen and complex compounds that drift downward to feed the world below, its ten million factories disguised as a carpet of green velvet, breathing quietly in the clouds.
