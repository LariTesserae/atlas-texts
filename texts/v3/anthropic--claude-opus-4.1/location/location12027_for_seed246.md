## The Singing Caldera

High on the mountain's shoulder, where the stone has worn down to its bones over countless ages, sits a bowl-shaped depression no larger than a market square. The rock here is smooth as river stones, polished by millennia of wind and ice into flowing curves that catch moisture from the clouds that perpetually graze this altitude. The ancient volcanic caldera has been ground down until only its heart remains—a shallow cup of dark basalt, cracked into hexagonal columns that lean against each other like tired sentinels.

Water seeps constantly through these cracks, not enough to pool but sufficient to feed an impossible abundance of vegetation. Cushion plants grow in violent profusion, their tiny leaves packed so densely they form a continuous carpet that rises and falls over the worn rock like green fur. Between the columns sprout ferns that shouldn't exist at this elevation—massive tree ferns with trunks like bottles, their fronds unfurling in the thin air with desperate vitality. The cold should kill them, but the thermal mass of the black stone and some quirk of the mountain's breath keeps them barely, impossibly alive. Their fronds are edged with frost even at midday.

The emptiness here is profound. Though the space is small, it feels hollow, as if the mountain has been scooped out beneath. The vegetation grows in isolated eruptions with bare stone between, and the air itself seems thin, stretched. You could fit the entire grove within a cathedral nave, yet it feels vaster for its elevation, for the way clouds move through it rather than over.

Built into this ancient garden are the remains of something deliberate—not structures exactly, but arrangements. The hexagonal columns have been shifted, centuries or millennia ago, into patterns that channel the wind. Stone has been stacked and balanced to create acoustic chambers. Moss-covered steps carved from single blocks spiral down between the ferns. These modifications are so old they've become part of the landscape's geology, their edges softened until you can't tell where intention ended and erosion began. A city's worth of careful construction compressed into this single grove, layered and reused until the original purposes are lost.

The sound is what makes this place alien. The wind, channeled through the arranged stones and hollow spaces beneath, creates a constant chord—not quite musical, not quite voice. It rises and falls with the gusts but never stops. The ferns add their own percussion as their fronds clash and shiver. Water trickling through the cracks provides an irregular rhythm. Together they create a acoustic environment that seems too organized to be natural, too chaotic to be designed. The sound reflects off the stone and amplifies in the hollow spaces until it becomes almost tangible, a presence that makes the sparse fauna—the occasional high-altitude butterfly, a single species of translucent beetle—seem even more absent.

The light here is clean and harsh, unfiltered by the thin atmosphere, casting sharp shadows even through the mist that periodically rolls through. When the sun hits the wet stone, it gleams like oil. The frost on the ferns catches it and throws it back in prismatic glints. 

Everything here is in motion despite the geological age of the place. The ferns sway without cease. Moisture condenses and evaporates in visible cycles. The moss grows so slowly you can't see it, but so persistently that it must be scraped from the carved steps monthly by some process—perhaps the wind itself, channeled just so. The clouds flow through like a river, depositing their moisture and moving on. Even the ancient stone seems to breathe with the temperature changes, the cracks opening and closing imperceptibly.

This is a place at the end of its story but still somehow vital, a garden growing in the skeleton of something far older, arranged by intelligence but abandoned to its own strange logic. The cold and altitude should make it barren, but instead it thrums with a focused, almost desperate life, as if the mountain is dreaming and this is where the dream leaks out.
