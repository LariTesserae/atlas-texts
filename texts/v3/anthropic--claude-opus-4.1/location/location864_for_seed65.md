The plateau stretches impossibly high, a vast shelf of pale limestone thrust up into the thin atmosphere where clouds crawl far below like slow rivers. At this elevation—perhaps four thousand meters, perhaps five—the air bites cold and sharp, carrying barely enough oxygen to sustain the peculiar life that has claimed this place.

The rock here has been sculpted by ages of patient erosion into a labyrinth of shallow basins and worn channels, each depression collecting what little moisture the altitude permits. Morning condensation pools in these hollows, fed by the frigid night air meeting stone that still holds yesterday's sun. By noon, most have evaporated, leaving only mineral rings like the ghosts of ancient lakes. The deeper basins—no more than knee-deep even after the occasional snow-melt—harbor water year-round, their surfaces skinned with ice most mornings.

What vegetation exists clings low and desperate: cushion plants that form gray-green domes no higher than an ankle, their roots diving deep into cracks where moisture hides. Patches of wire-grass spread between the stones, sharp enough to draw blood, growing in spirals that follow some inscrutable mathematics of survival. Here and there, a solitary specimen of what might generously be called a tree—though it rises no higher than a person's waist—spreads twisted branches parallel to the ground, sculpted by winds that never cease.

The fauna is what makes this place remarkable, and terrible.

They are everywhere: colonies of whistling moths that roost in the millions along the cliff faces, their wings catching and amplifying the constant wind into a sound like ten thousand tea kettles. The noise never stops. It rises and falls with the gusts but never drops below a penetrating keen that works its way into the bones. The moths themselves are translucent, their bodies barely there, but in such vast numbers they create shifting veils of near-invisible movement across the plateau.

Between the moth colonies move the larger inhabitants—flat-bodied mammals with oversized lungs and dish-shaped ears that can fold completely shut against the sound. They graze on the cushion plants with peculiar efficiency, their peglike teeth adapted to grind the tough vegetation. Their young are born with their ear canals sealed; they don't open for the first month of life.

Stone cairns dot the plateau—not built things but natural formations where the limestone has weathered into tall, balanced stacks. The wind plays these too, adding hollow tones to the moths' shrieking symphony. Some of the animals have learned to shelter in the acoustic shadows of these formations, small pockets where the sound drops to merely overwhelming rather than unbearable.

The light here is relentless. Above the cloud layer, with the thin atmosphere barely filtering the sun's radiation, everything appears overexposed. Shadows are sharp as knife edges. The limestone reflects and amplifies the brightness until even the animals squint constantly, their eyes evolved into slits. When storms do come—rare but violent—they bring snow that falls upward as often as down, caught in the updrafts that surge up the cliff faces.

At the plateau's edge, the world simply ends. The drops are thousands of meters straight down into cloud-shrouded valleys that might as well be different planets. Sometimes, on the clearest days, you can glimpse green far below—forests that look like moss from this height. But mostly there are only clouds, and the sensation of standing at the edge of existence itself.

The only evidence of any human presence consists of scattered fragments—a worked piece of flint here, a carved bone there, half-buried in the gravelly soil. Tools abandoned or lost by whoever once tried to exist in this place, or perhaps dropped by travelers who passed through quickly, hands pressed against their ears, fleeing toward lower, quieter elevations. The tools are primitive—scrapers, simple points, a fragment of what might have been a whistle carved from moth-wing, though why anyone would add to the cacophony is unclear.

Time seems suspended here. The erosion patterns suggest millions of years of patient work, yet nothing appears to change from day to day, year to year. The moth colonies occupy the same cliff faces their ancestors did countless generations ago. The grazing mammals follow the same paths their kind has always followed. Even the wind patterns are locked in place, carved by the topography into eternal loops and spirals that play the same deafening song they always have.

It is a place that exists at the very edge of what can support life—too high, too cold, too bright, too loud—yet life has not only found a way but flourished in its strange fashion. A plateau that feels like it belongs to another world, or to no world at all, but simply is: bright and screaming and eternal under its thin shell of sky.
