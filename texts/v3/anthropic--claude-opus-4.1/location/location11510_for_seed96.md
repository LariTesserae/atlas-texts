The terraces rise in perfect mineral steps, each level a shallow basin of collected water that reflects the bone-bright sky. Someone—or many someones—carved this entire mountainside into these geometric pools, transforming raw stone into a vast amphitheater of water-catching shelves. The work is crude but monumental: chisel marks still fresh in the white limestone, edges sharp enough to cut, tool grooves running in parallel lines where generations of hands shaped the mountain into purpose.

Water seeps constantly from the stone itself. The rock weeps through invisible pores, a slow bleeding that fills each terrace to its carved lip before spilling down channels cut with obsessive precision. The channels are the finest work here—barely finger-width but elaborately braided, creating a lacework of controlled flow between levels. The sound is subtle: not the rush of waterfalls but the persistent trickle of a thousand tiny streams finding their ordained paths.

No moss softens these edges. No algae clouds the water. The pools are clear as air, revealing floors of fitted stone blocks—some white, some pale blue, some the faint pink of dawn—arranged in patterns that shift from terrace to terrace. Spirals give way to radiating stars give way to interlocking hexagons. The patterns continue underwater, suggesting the pools were meant to be filled, that the water is not incidental but essential to whatever purpose drives this place.

The construction is total. Even the "natural" outcroppings between terraces reveal themselves as assembled things: dry-stacked towers of fitted stone that buttress the pools, their surfaces carved with shallow cups and grooves that channel condensation back into the system. The morning mist—and there is always morning mist here, regardless of the hour—beads on every surface, runs down every carved channel, feeds the endless circulation.

At this height, the air is thin and cool, carrying the mineral taste of wet stone. The brightness comes not from any visible sun but from the sky itself, which has the quality of illuminated pearl, casting no shadows but revealing every texture in the carved surfaces. The light seems to emanate from the water too, each pool holding its own shallow radiance.

The scale reveals itself slowly. What seems at first like a hillside installation extends beyond sight in both directions, following the mountain's contours. The terraces number in the thousands, perhaps tens of thousands, each one unique in its floor pattern but identical in its dimensions, in the angle of its walls, in the depth of its water. The discipline of it is overwhelming—how many hands, how many years, to transform an entire mountain face into this rigid order?

Between the terraces run narrow walkways, their surfaces grooved for grip, worn smooth in the centers from countless passages. Stone tools lie abandoned on some ledges—hammers and chisels of the same white rock, their handles worn to fit specific hands. Some terraces contain more elaborate constructions: small pyramids of stacked stones sitting in the shallow water, their peaks just breaking the surface. Others hold carved blocks arranged in careful formations, half-submerged, their tops polished to mirror-brightness.

The density presses in despite the open sky. Every surface is worked. Every space is designated. Even the air feels occupied by the rigid geometry of the place, by the relentless intention carved into every stone. There are no resting places for the eye, no natural forms to break the pattern. The few variations—a terrace left dry, its floor covered in carved symbols; a pool filled with black water instead of clear; a section where the stone has been polished to translucency—feel like words in an unknown language rather than relief from the repetition.

The water cycle is visible and eternal here. Condensation on stone, seepage through rock, careful flow down channels, evaporation from pool surfaces, mist, condensation again. The builders captured a mountain's worth of water and set it in permanent, slow circulation. The pools never overflow their bounds, never run dry. The system maintains itself through carved precision and the mountain's endless weeping.

In certain lights—and the light does shift here, though slowly, like breathing—the water in the pools appears to hold depth beyond their shallow basins. Looking straight down, one might see not the patterned floor a hand's breadth below but something vast and dark, as if each pool were a window rather than a container. But these moments pass, and the water returns to its simple clarity, holding nothing but light and the reflection of that pearled sky.

The quiet is not absence but presence—the small sounds of water finding its way, the subtle ping of droplets, the whisper of mist condensing. Sometimes, rarely, a sound like singing comes from the pools themselves, though whether it's wind across water or some property of the carved stone resonating with the slow flow, no one could say. No one is here to say. The tools suggest recent work, but the silence suggests abandonment, and both suggestions feel equally true, equally false.

This is architecture as landscape, or landscape reformed into architecture so completely the distinction loses meaning. Every stone was placed. Every surface was shaped. Every flow was intended. The mountain has been translated into something else, something between monument and machine, temple and system, its purpose as clear as water and just as difficult to grasp.
