The Breathing Plateaus stretch across half a continent, a vast highland of crystalline frost-meadows that pulse with their own peculiar metabolism. At this altitude, the air itself has frozen into something else — not quite solid, not quite gas, but a bright medium that carries both light and life in ways that shouldn't be possible.

The vegetation here defies understanding. In the perpetual cold, massive fungal forests have evolved to photosynthesize from the peculiar radiation that floods these heights. Their flesh is translucent green-gold, packed so densely that walking means pushing through curtains of delicate, regenerating tissue. These aren't plants as lowlanders know them — they're something between coral and cloud, growing in terraced shelves that follow the plateau's young, sharp edges. The mycelial networks below run kilometers deep, mining heat from some vast industrial process far beneath.

For there is industry here, though not built by any hand. The plateau itself is a machine — or perhaps the machine is what created the plateau. Deep thrumming rises from crevasses where the earth's newest faces show: fresh rock that steams despite the cold, extruded in perfect hexagonal columns by whatever enormous processes grind below. The sound is constant, a bass note that makes the frozen air shiver and ring like struck glass.

Water exists in peculiar pockets — not as lakes or streams, but as wandering bubbles of liquid that float through the fungal forest, each one a perfect sphere the size of a house. The fauna has evolved to follow these nomadic oases. Creatures pour through the growth in brilliant, chattering rivers of fur and scale and chittering wing. They're small individually — nothing larger than a rabbit — but they move in such densities that they form super-organisms, flowing masses that reshape the fungal forest as they pass, pruning and propagating in patterns that repeat across thousand-kilometer scales.

The animals themselves are wrong in fundamental ways. They have too many joints, or their bones are on the outside but soft, or they phase in and out of visibility as they move between the fungal fronds. Their calls blend into a constant bright roar — not aggressive, but overwhelming in its sheer biological enthusiasm. Billions of throats singing metabolic songs, cooling themselves in the bright, cold light that seems to come from the air itself rather than any sun.

The newest parts of the plateau are still sharp as glass, their edges not yet softened by the patient frost-cycles that will, over centuries, gentle them into the rolling highlands that dominate the interior. In these young zones, you can see the process that builds this place: geometric extrusions of rock that emerge at imperceptible speeds, crackling with some internal energy that makes the fungal forests grow even denser, even more vigorous, as if feeding on the earth's industrial fever.

Sometimes the floating water-spheres collide with the superheated rock faces. The explosion of steam creates temporary weather systems — clouds that snow upward, bright ice crystals that hang in the air for hours before settling into the fungal mass, where they're absorbed and channeled into the next cycle of growth.

The whole plateau breathes on a schedule measured in months. Inhalation: the fungal forests compress, the animals burrow deep into the living walls, the water-spheres sink toward the warm crevasses. Exhalation: everything expands, reaches upward, the sound grows louder as billions of creatures emerge to feed on the glowing spores that fruit only in these moments. The industrial heartbeat below never changes, but everything above follows its patient rhythm.

There are no paths here, no clearings, no spaces that aren't claimed by something living or growing or slowly extruding from the earth's mechanical depths. Even the air is full — spores, scaled wings, floating seeds that look like tiny geometric crystals, and always that bright, cold light that makes everything seem to glow from within. The eye has nowhere to rest, the ear no quiet to find. This is a place where emptiness has been abolished, where life has found a way to pack itself into every available dimension, thriving on the intersection of geological industry and biological impossibility.

The horizons here are myths. The density of growth means you can rarely see more than a dozen meters in any direction, but you can feel the vastness — in the way the ground's vibration suggests massive scales, in how the weather changes as you climb the fungal terraces, in the subtle shifts in the fauna's impossible songs that hint at regional dialects across distances that would take lifetimes to cross.
