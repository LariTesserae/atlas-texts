The Crystal Tundra spreads endlessly beneath a pale, luminous sky that never quite dims. Here, at the crown of the world, the air is so thin and dry that each breath feels like swallowing glass dust, yet somehow the vast fields of translucent vegetation thrive.

These are not plants as we know them. Rising from beds of powdered mineral snow—a substance that falls not as water but as microscopic fragments of atmospheric salts—great forests of silicon-based organisms stretch their crystalline fronds toward the light. They grow in slow spirals, each branch a prism that splits the harsh radiation into manageable wavelengths their chemistry can digest. The tallest reach forty meters, their bases thick as ancient oaks, their bodies singing faintly in the wind—a barely audible chiming that carries for kilometers in the sparse air.

Between these glass forests, meadows of lower growth spread in patches across the high plateau. Carpet-forming entities that look like frozen soap bubbles cluster together, their surfaces iridescent and constantly shifting as they photosynthesize through pure mineral exchange. They exhale traces of noble gases that create visible streams in the bright air, flowing like ghost rivers down the gentle slopes.

The ground itself is ancient, worn smooth by millions of years of particle winds. Beneath the loose mineral snow lies a hardpan of fused salts, cracked into polygonal patterns the size of city blocks. In the cracks, deeper root systems penetrate down to reservoirs of liquid ammonia, the only "water" this world knows, locked far beneath in pressurized chambers that occasionally vent through fumaroles, creating temporary oases where the vegetation grows thick as jungle.

The scale defies comprehension. Standing on any of the broad ridges, you can see the gentle curve of the world itself, the horizon bending away in all directions. Weather systems visible from a thousand kilometers away crawl across the surface—not storms of water but electromagnetic phenomena, aurora-like cascades that sweep across the tundra, causing the crystal forests to fluoresce in waves of blue and green. These ion storms are how nutrients move here, carried on charged winds that deposit trace elements across the vast distances.

Movement catches the eye rarely but meaningfully. A lone crawler—something between animal and mineral, with a body of linked segments that look carved from smoky quartz—picks its way through a distant grove. It feeds on the electromagnetic discharge that builds up in the larger growths, moving perhaps a meter per hour on hundreds of hair-thin legs that test each surface before committing weight. These are the only "animals" here, and seeing one is an event that might not repeat for months.

The silence is profound, broken only by the occasional crack of thermal expansion in the crystal growths or the whisper-soft sound of mineral snow settling. Even the ion storms pass overhead in eerie quiet, their light-shows unaccompanied by thunder. But if you press your ear to one of the great trunks, you can hear the deep subsonic groaning of growth—a process that takes decades for each meter of height, as patient as continental drift.

Time moves differently here. The bright sky neither sets nor rises but slowly pulses through subtle variations of intensity over periods of many days. The vegetation responds to these cycles, opening and closing their prismatic organs in waves that roll across the landscape like the slowest ocean. In the spaces between the groves, where the mineral snow lies deepest, nothing disturbs the surface for years at a time except for the artistic patterns traced by the wind—spirals and ridges that rebuild themselves endlessly, never quite the same twice.

This is a place of contradictions held in delicate balance: lush but lifeless-looking, bright but cold, vast but intimate in its scattered details, ancient but somehow eternally fresh as each surface regenerates through its slow mineral cycles. A world that found its own way to bloom without water, to live without warmth, to sing without sound.
