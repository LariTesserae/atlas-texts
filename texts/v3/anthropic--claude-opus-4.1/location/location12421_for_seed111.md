The Chimney Rookery rises through perpetual twilight—a volcanic throat of fresh black basalt climbing nearly vertical for eight hundred meters, its interior walls still sharp with cooling fractures that weep condensation in thin, irregular streams. The throat is perhaps forty meters across at its widest, narrowing and bulging like a massive trachea carved from stone.

Every available surface churns with life. Millions of pale cave swifts roost in impossible density, their bodies pressed so close together they form a living wallpaper of feathers and rapid heartbeats. The birds hang from the smallest ledges, from crystal formations of mineral deposits, from their own hardened droppings that have built up into grotesque architectural buttresses over mere decades—this place is young, the volcanic vent having only recently gone dormant enough for colonization.

The sound is overwhelming—not from individual calls but from the sheer mass of tiny movements. Wings rustle against wings in continuous whispers that combine into a roar like distant surf. Claws scratch microscopically against stone. The occasional cascade of droppings patters down through the darkness, striking ledge after ledge. Every few hours, triggered by some invisible signal, sections of the colony explode into brief, deafening flight before settling again.

Water beads constantly on the walls from temperature differentials between the warm exhalations of so many small bodies and the cool volcanic rock. It runs in threads down the stone, collecting in small pools on irregular ledges where the birds drink in careful, competitive clusters. These pools never grow large—the moisture either evaporates in the body heat or drips further down into the darkness below where even more birds crowd the narrowing throat.

Strange fungi grow in the guano deposits—bioluminescent only when disturbed, they create brief constellations of sickly green light when birds land or take off. These blooms provide the only illumination beyond the pale disk of sky far above, itself often obscured by the endless circulation of bodies.

The birds themselves have adapted oddly to this vertical world. Their feet are grotesquely elongated, their wings shortened to knife-like appendages better suited for climbing than flying. They move more like geckos than swifts when not airborne, and when they do fly, it's in tight spirals that follow the thermal columns rising from the mass of bodies below.

Occasionally, something larger moves through the colony—cave centipedes three meters long that navigate the walls with horrible grace, feeding on eggs and weakened birds. The colony responds to these intrusions with coordinated defensive flights, thousands of birds creating vortexes of wings that can disorient even these primitive predators, sending them tumbling into the depths.

The ecosystem feeds on itself in tight cycles. The dead fall to become food for larvae in the guano below. The larvae pupate and emerge as moths that the birds catch. The circle turns slowly but inexorably in this natural cathedral of wings and waste, where the sheer density of life creates its own weather—updrafts of ammonia and methane, downdrafts of cooler air, occasional "rainstorms" when condensation overwhelms a section of wall.

At the very bottom, should anything reach it through the gauntlet of bodies, pools of water have collected—ancient rainwater mixed with condensation, thick with dissolved minerals and the chemistry of decay. Things move in those waters, but in the darkness, with the endless rain of organic matter from above, they've never needed eyes to find their food.

The entire chimney breathes with a slow rhythm—warming during the day as the sun heats the black rock walls, cooling at night, this patient circulation driving the moisture up and down like blood through a vast vertical vein, while the million hearts beating within create their own faster pulse, their own strange music in the darkness.
