The scaffolding stretches upward into brightness that has no source — a luminous void where the sky should be. This is a city built vertically, layer upon layer of industrial framework rising from what might have been a mountain plateau, though no natural stone remains visible beneath the accumulated construction.

Metal catwalks thread between towering condensation farms, their surfaces perpetually beaded with moisture harvested from the cool air. The farms are geometric forests of copper pipes and aluminum fins, each one singing faintly as temperature differentials pull water from nothing. Droplets gather, merge, and fall in irregular rhythms into collection channels that wind downward through the levels like exposed veins. The entire structure breathes moisture — not rain exactly, but a constant cycle of condensation and evaporation that keeps every surface slick.

Between the water-farmers, factories have grown into and through each other over decades of slow expansion. Their walls are riveted steel and clouded glass, their windows glowing with the soft orange of furnaces that never fully sleep. Production lines snake through multiple floors, sometimes piercing straight through buildings that were built around them rather than relocated. Conveyor belts carry unidentifiable components between processing stations, moving so slowly that their motion is barely perceptible — a patient industrial metabolism.

The fauna here are wrong. Pigeons with too many wings roost in the girders, their bodies flickering between positions as if existence itself hiccups around them. Cats prowl the catwalks but occasionally walk through solid barriers, reappearing on the other side without acknowledgment. In the collection pools, things that might be fish school in perfect mathematical patterns, their scales reflecting colors that shouldn't exist in this spectrum of bright industrial light.

Every surface that isn't actively maintained sprouts a thin fuzz of oxidation and pale lichen — the only vegetation that survives here, feeding on metal ions and condensed pollution. These patches spread slowly across neglected walls, creating maps of green-grey that chart the city's zones of abandonment and renewal.

The density is overwhelming but static. Buildings crowd against buildings, pipes thread through pipes, walkways cross over and under in a three-dimensional maze that has grown too complex for any single mind to hold. Yet everything moves at geological speed. A crane might take a full day to rotate. The factories' rhythms are tidal, their production cycling through phases that take weeks to complete.

Sound barely travels here despite the density. The industrial processes are muffled, dampened by the moisture in the air and absorbed by the layers upon layers of construction. Occasionally, a steam valve releases with a soft hiss, or metal settles with a distant groan, but mostly there is only the quiet patter of collected water and the subsonic hum of machinery that runs too slowly to properly hear.

At certain angles, in certain lights, the geometry becomes impossible. Stairs lead up and down simultaneously. The same factory floor exists on multiple levels. Workers — human-shaped but moving with the same flickering uncertainty as the pigeons — operate machines in spaces that overlap without touching. The bright light that suffuses everything casts shadows that fall in directions that don't align with any physical source.

This is a place built beyond sense, where industrial logic has consumed itself and reformed as something else. The city builds itself from its own substance, recycling and reprocessing in cycles that span years. New structures emerge from the digestion of old ones, sharp-edged and clean, already beginning their slow transformation into the next iteration.

The horizon, when glimpsed through gaps in the scaffolding, reveals more of the same — industrial towers stretching outward across what might have once been peaks and valleys, all of it bright and damp and wrong, all of it growing with imperceptible patience toward a purpose that perhaps nothing living remembers.
