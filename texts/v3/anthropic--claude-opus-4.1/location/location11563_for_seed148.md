The city hangs inverted in a cavern of breathing wood.

Above—or below, depending on how you orient yourself—the root-ceiling spreads like neural pathways, each tendril thick as a subway tunnel, pulsing with something that might be sap or might be thought. The trees grow downward from somewhere beyond the stone, their canopy forming the floor of this inverse world. Walking means stepping across leaves the size of shipping containers, their surfaces drum-tight and resonant with each footfall, releasing spores that drift *up* toward the city suspended in the hollow.

The settlement itself defies expectation—a Victorian industrial town built perpendicular to gravity, its brick factories and iron bridges jutting sideways from massive trunk-pillars that serve as both foundation and feeding ground. Steam engines run on fungal combustion, their pistons drawing fuel from the perpetual decay that films every surface. The machines have learned to breathe with the trees, their mechanical rhythms syncing to the slow arterial pulse of the cambium highways.

In the vast spaces between structures, there is mostly nothing—caverns of humid air where condensation forms and falls in brief, localized rainstorms that water both the canopy-floor and the inverted rooftops. The emptiness makes the city feel like scattered islands in a three-dimensional sea, connected only by iron catwalks that spiral around the trunks and by the elaborate pulley systems the inhabitants use to navigate between gravity wells.

The fauna have adapted to this perpendicular existence. Millipedes the length of trains process along the vertical bark highways, their thousand feet gripping surfaces that shift between rough and smooth without warning. Moths with wings of living leaves pollinate the factory smokestacks, which have evolved chlorophyll chambers. In the darkness—for very little light penetrates here save the bioluminescent fungi that the workers cultivate in glass batteries—creatures navigate by echolocation that sounds like whispered mathematics.

The geometry itself is still forming. Streets sometimes end in walls of fresh cambium that weren't there yesterday. Buildings phase partially through trunks as the trees grow, creating rooms that exist half in architecture, half in heartwood. The surveyors have given up mapping; they say the city dreams new districts into being while they sleep, that the root-mind above thinks in urban planning.

Sound travels strangely here—mostly it's quiet save for the distant hum of engines and the occasional groan of growing wood. But sometimes the entire cavern resonates with frequencies below hearing, and the inhabitants feel rather than hear the conversation between the mechanical and the botanical, a dialogue that makes their teeth ache and their compass needles spin.

The workers live in row houses built into hollow trunks, their windows opening onto vertical streets. They've learned to sleep while hanging, to cook on stoves that pivot with the shifting gravity, to ignore when their tools occasionally sprout leaves. Their children play in the empty spaces between buildings, swinging on vines between orientations, their laughter echoing in the cathedral-vast gaps where reality forgets which way is supposed to be up.

At the edges of perception, where the darkness becomes absolute, the rules break down entirely. Machines flower. Trees compute. The rain falls upward and somehow still waters both canopy and city. The inhabitants have stopped trying to make sense of it. They simply live, suspended in their impossible metropolis, manufacturing things that might be products or might be seeds, never quite sure if they're the workers or the crop.
