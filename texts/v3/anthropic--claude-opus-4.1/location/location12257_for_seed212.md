The city breathes.

Not metaphorically — the entire settlement pulses with vegetative respiration, its streets and towers grown rather than built, woven from living wood that never died, never was cut, never stopped its slow arterial flow of sap and sugar. What appears at first glance to be architecture reveals itself as a vast colonial organism, a single plant or perhaps a congress of plants so thoroughly intertwined that individual identity has long since dissolved into municipal unity.

The sound hits you first — a roaring susurrus of ten million leaves shivering in winds that shouldn't exist, photosynthesis accelerated to an audible frequency, the crackling growth of bamboo-quick expansion playing out across every surface. It's the sound of a forest compressed into fast-forward, seasons cycling in minutes, growth and decay and regrowth layered into a constant organic static that makes conversation impossible, thought difficult.

The streets are root systems inverted skyward, braided into walkways that undulate and split and merge according to some botanical logic that almost makes sense if you don't think about it too hard. Buildings fruit from these thoroughfares like galls or seed pods grown monstrously large, their walls a latticework of trunk and vine so densely woven that light filters through green-tinted, transforming everything within into underwater scenes. Windows are gaps where the growth forgot itself. Doors are sphincters of overlapping leaves that part when approached — but only sometimes, and never for the same person twice in the same way.

Water beads on every surface despite no visible rain, condensation from the massive transpiration cycle that turns the air thick and cloying. It pools in the cup-shaped growths that serve as fountains, in the hollow joints of the great bamboo-things that might be lampposts if they held anything besides more vegetation. Sometimes a fine mist drifts through the streets, exuded from pores in the road-roots, carrying the green smell of chlorophyll and something else, something that makes your vision shimmer at the edges.

Because this is where the reality breaks down — in the edges, in the transitions. Watch too long and you'll see the buildings breathing in reverse, exhaling carbon dioxide, inhaling oxygen. The leaves track movement that isn't there, turning to follow the paths of people who walked these streets yesterday, or tomorrow, or never. Sometimes a building will fruit with doors, hundreds of them, opening onto the same room from different angles, from different times. A market square exists in twelve locations simultaneously, its vendor-trees selling produce that grows directly from their branch-tips — fruit that tastes like memories, vegetables that whisper when you bite them.

The density is overwhelming. Every surface sprouts something. Moss carpets grow from other moss carpets. Vines spiral around vines around vines in fractal helices. The air itself seems thick with spores and pollen, with the possibility of growth, until breathing feels like swallowing seeds. In the market-plazas, in the junction-groves where the root-roads converge, the vegetation packs so tightly that navigation becomes three-dimensional — residents climb as often as they walk, swinging from transit-vines that grow between destinations, anticipating need.

There are no animals here, not anymore, not exactly. But the plants remember them. Sometimes a tree will suddenly sprout feathers instead of leaves, or a vine will pulse with a heartbeat that sends ripples through its length. The city's immune system consists of thorn-wolves that are more thorn than wolf, racing through the undergrowth-infrastructure to prune back infections of non-native growth. They have eyes made of seeds and teeth made of bark and they dissolve back into the walls when their work is done.

The warm air shimmers with growth-heat, fermentation-heat, the temperature of massive metabolism. In the denser districts, where the plant-mass reaches critical biological density, the temperature spikes and the growth accelerates until time itself seems to thicken, minutes stretching into hours while the vegetation races through generations. These temporal eddies drift through the city like weather systems. Step into one and you might emerge seconds later to find the street-roots have grown over your feet, or hours later though only a heartbeat seemed to pass.

At the city's heart — or perhaps its root, or perhaps its seed — stands something that might once have been a temple or might still be becoming one. Its spires are trees growing in perfect spirals, their bark inscribed with patterns that shift when observed, words in the language that plants might use if plants used language. Inside, if inside means anything here, space follows non-Euclidean paths through hollow trunks and across bridge-branches that span distances they shouldn't span. Rooms exist nested within other rooms like Russian dolls made of cellulose and chloroplasts. The altar, if it is an altar, is a flowering that never stops, blossoms opening onto blossoms opening onto blossoms, infinite recursion of petals that makes the mind ache to follow.

The city has no edge, exactly. It simply becomes less dense, less organized, less city and more forest until suddenly you realize the root-roads have become mere paths, the building-pods have shrunk to normal fruit, the sound has diminished from roar to whisper. But turn back and the transition reverses — what was forest becomes city again, but never quite the same city you left. The geometry has shifted. The streets have grown new intersections. That market square that was in twelve places is now in thirteen, though one of them exists only on Tuesdays that fall on the thirty-first of the month.

This is a place where architecture is agriculture, where urban planning follows the logic of seasons and phototropism, where the city itself is the only citizen that matters. It tends itself, grows itself, consumes itself and regenerates in an endless cycle of municipal metabolism. The buildings migrate slowly across the landscape, following some vast pattern of nutrients in the soil below. What seems permanent is merely slow; what seems solid is merely dense.

And through it all, that sound — that overwhelming rush of biological noise, growth and breath and circulation amplified to deafening levels. It's the sound of a city that never stops building itself, cell by cell, leaf by leaf, in a reality too vigorous and verdant to remain stable, too alive to ever be still.
