The hollow rings with the sound of ten thousand breathing chambers.

You stand at the edge of what was once a factory floor, though that was geological ages ago. The space is no larger than a village square, bounded by walls of living wood so ancient they've forgotten they were ever separate trees. Their trunks fused millennia past into a single organism, bark flowing together like cooled wax, creating a cathedral of vegetable flesh that has swallowed every trace of the original structure save for suggestions—a right angle here, the ghost of a doorframe there, all softened beneath centuries of cambium.

The floor itself is root and moss, a spongy mat three feet thick that has consumed whatever machinery once stood here. Sometimes, when you step in certain places, you feel the suggestion of metal deep below, but the plant-mass has digested it all into itself, incorporating iron and copper into its circulation. The moss breathes audibly, each square meter exhaling through specialized pores with a soft, mechanical wheeze—the factory's ventilation system reimagined in cellulose and chlorophyll.

Above, the canopy has grown into the precise negative space of what must have been industrial architecture. Branches follow the phantom lines of vanished catwalks and conveyor systems, their paths too regular for nature yet too alive for manufacture. The leaves up there are enormous, each the size of a desktop, and they've evolved a peculiar behavior: they rotate slowly on their stems throughout the day, not following sun but maintaining some ancient production rhythm, their movement generating a constant low thrumming, a bass note that makes the air itself feel thick.

Water collects in pockets where the root-floor dips—not pools but blisters in the plant's skin, translucent and taut. Touch one and it trembles; the entire floor-organism responds with a shiver that travels outward in rings. The moisture never quite forms proper rain here, but condensation drips constantly from the breathing pores above, each drop ringing against the leaf-drums with metallic precision.

The fauna have evolved to match the paradox of the place. Beetles the size of fists wear shells that look like riveted metal but are actually keratin mimicking industrial forms. They tend vast fungal gardens in the hollow spaces between root bundles, their cultivation as methodical as any assembly line. Moths with wings like oxidized copper rest on the walls, their caterpillars boring perfectly circular holes through dead wood with the patience of drill presses. Everything here works in shifts—day fauna and night fauna exchanging the space with clockwork regularity, their calls overlapping at dawn and dusk in a cacophony of biological machinery.

The strangest inhabitants are the wire-worms—whether animal or plant no one can say. They grow like vines but move with purpose, stringing themselves between high branches in configurations that mirror electrical diagrams, growing thicker where they cross, pulsing faintly with bioluminescence when the beetles below complete certain tasks in their gardens.

The air tastes of rust and chlorophyll, metal and sap. The temperature stays forever cool, regulated by the mass of living tissue and its constant respiration. Nothing changes here—hasn't for a thousand years—yet everything moves, processes, cycles. The factory continues its work, though what it produces now is only more of itself: an endless manufacture of living space, the industrial transformed into the biological so completely that distinguishing between them becomes impossible.

Stand still long enough and you can feel it through your feet—the deep pulse, maybe heartbeat, maybe the ghost of turbines, that keeps this ancient hybrid space running on its settled schedule, neither natural nor artificial but something that transcends both, locked in its perfect, breathing stasis.
