The plateau city spreads across weathered limestone terraces, each level connected by rusted cable cars that shriek and groan through the humid air. Water seeps everywhere despite the elevation — not falling as rain but rising as vapor from the porous rock itself, condensing on every surface, dripping from corroded gutters and industrial pipework that has grown barnacle-thick with mineral deposits over decades of exposure.

The stone breathes moisture. Ancient volcanic tubes beneath the plateau act as massive steam vents, their pressure regulated by a sprawling network of brass valves and iron manifolds installed a century ago. The entire city rumbles with the deep bass note of this geological respiration — a sound felt in the bones, punctuated by the sharp hiss of pressure releases and the grinding of ore crushers in the mineral processing plants that rim each terrace.

Vegetation struggles here. Tough wire-grass grows in the gaps between paving stones, and occasional stunted palms rise from courtyards where the steam is thickest, their fronds yellowed and sparse. Most organic life has been replaced by a peculiar oxidation that covers everything in a rust-orange patina — not quite alive, not quite mineral, growing in slow crystalline patterns along the wet walls.

The buildings rise in clusters with wide spaces between them, as if the city fears crowding itself. Each structure stands on stilts or pillars to let the moisture flow beneath, their facades stained with long streaks of iron oxide and copper verdigris. Industrial chimneys puncture the skyline at irregular intervals, venting not smoke but pure white steam that rolls down the plateau's edges like slow waterfalls.

The sound never stops. Beyond the geological breathing and industrial processing, there's the constant percussion of condensation — millions of droplets falling from millions of surfaces, creating a static roar that drowns out human voices. The inhabitants have adapted with a language of gestures and lights, and sensitive equipment is housed in sound-dampened chambers lined with volcanic foam.

At the plateau's edges, the city's drainage cascades over ancient limestone lips in a dozen mechanical waterfalls — not natural flows but engineered spillways that power turbines on their way down. The water glows faintly green-yellow in daylight, tinted by the minerals it's absorbed in its journey through the breathing stone.

Strange economies have evolved here. Water miners lower themselves on cables into the steam vents to scrape valuable mineral deposits. The processing plants run day and night, their rhythmic pounding adding to the din as they separate rare earths from the constant flow of moisture-borne sediment. The ore crushers' vibrations travel through the rock itself, sometimes synchronizing across the city in moments of uncanny harmony that make windows rattle and puddles dance.

The few animals that remain have evolved past recognition — pale geckos with overlarge toe pads that can grip wet surfaces, their ear openings sealed against the moisture. Moths with wax-coated wings that repel the condensation. A species of nearly-blind pigeon that navigates by echolocation, their calls cutting through the lower frequencies of the city's roar.

The city's expansion follows the moisture. New districts are built where the steam wells strongest, while abandoned quarters slowly dissolve back into the limestone, their ruins becoming honeycombed with erosion. Time moves slowly here — a building might take a decade to show real decay, a street fifty years to become impassable. The inhabitants think in these longer cycles, planning infrastructure for centuries of slow dissolution and renewal.

At dawn and dusk, when the temperature differential is greatest, the whole city becomes shrouded in fog so thick that the cable cars must cease operation. In these brief quiet hours, with the crushers paused and visibility reduced to meters, the plateau becomes almost silent except for the dripping. The city seems to exhale, releasing the day's accumulated moisture in one great sigh before the cycle begins again.
