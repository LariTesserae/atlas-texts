The cistern city breathes with ten thousand lungs.

Carved from limestone over generations, this warren of water-catch chambers extends three stories below the desert floor. Each vault, no larger than a village square, connects to its neighbors through arched passages worn smooth by centuries of barefoot traffic. The builders—long since absorbed into the stone's memory—hand-chiseled every surface into shallow channels and collection grooves that guide precious condensation toward central pools. Clay pipes, fired in kilns that burned the last of the surface forests, snake through walls like veins, carrying trickles between chambers according to ancient distribution laws carved into the entrance stones.

The air hangs thick and close, heavy with the breath of crowds and the sweet-rot smell of the pools where salamanders the color of raw clay breed in astounding numbers. They slip between the carved gutters, their soft bodies creating a constant wet susurrus against stone. People press between the chambers—water carriers, eel fishers, children chasing pale crickets that sing in deafening choruses from cracks in the walls. The acoustic properties of the vaulted ceilings amplify every sound: footfalls become drumbeats, whispers become shouts, and the constant drip-music of condensation becomes a thunderous percussion that never ceased.

Mushroom farmers tend sparse gardens in designated chambers, their pale crops growing on beds of imported dung and cloth scraps. The fungi barely sustain themselves in the humid darkness, yielding just enough to supplement what the eel-catchers pull from the deeper pools. Bioluminescent algae, cultivated in shallow wall-niches, provide the only light beyond what filters through the entrance shafts—a greenish glow that makes all faces look drowned.

The economy of space forces intimate density. Sleeping shelves are carved directly into corridor walls, separated only by hanging fabrics. Workshop chambers overflow with the constant activity of maintaining this entirely artificial ecosystem: women weaving reed mats from the sparse plants that grow near the light shafts, men pressing oil from cave fish, children scraping mineral deposits from the walls to trade with surface dwellers for necessities. 

Water rights determine everything. Each family owns specific collection points, marked with carved symbols rubbed smooth by generations of touching. The water tribunals meet in the largest chamber, their debates echoing through the entire complex as they adjudicate disputes over flow rates, contamination, and the ancient horror of water theft. The punishment for disrupting the carved channels is exile to the surface—effectively a death sentence.

The salamanders own the true city. They navigate the hundred unnamed passages too small for humans, breed in the forgotten cisterns where the builders' tools still rest in mineral-locked stillness. Their populations surge and crash with the rains above—when storms come, their mating cries overwhelm even the usual cacophony, a sound like sheets of leather being torn in rhythm. The wise listen to the salamanders; when they flee upward, floods follow.

Strange adaptations emerge in the permanent twilight. Children born here develop enlarged pupils and acute hearing. Some families exhibit webbed fingers after generations of diving in the deeper pools. The old stories speak of those who ventured too deep and returned changed—gills behind their ears, eyes that reflect light like a cat's, an inability to surface without pain.

The city's metabolism never slows. Night and day lose meaning when your sky is stone. The water cycle drives everything: condensation gathering, careful collection, distribution according to the carved laws, evaporation from the pools, and the endless wait for the next rain to percolate through the limestone above. Even sleep happens in shifts, as someone must always tend the collection points, guard against theft, maintain the delicate balance that keeps this dense press of humanity alive in their constructed cave.

At the deepest point, where the original builders first broke through into the natural aquifer, an ancient pump still operates. Built from fitted stone and sealed with tree resin, it requires six people to operate its massive wooden beam. They work in shifts, forever raising water from the deep dark to the merely dark, their rhythmic chanting keeping time. The pump has never stopped. The city believes that if it does, the limestone will remember it was once dry, and reclaim what was taken from it.

The boundary between built and born blurs here. The limestone itself has been so thoroughly carved, channeled, and shaped that it's impossible to say where the natural cave ended and the cistern began. Even the ecology—the salamanders, the blind fish, the phosphorescent algae—has been so thoroughly cultivated and contained that wildness itself becomes another kind of architecture. Everything that lives here lives by design, even as that design frantically adapts to support more life than it was ever meant to hold.
