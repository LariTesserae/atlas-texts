The alpine tundra stretches across a vast plateau near the roof of the world, where the air is so thin that water cannot decide whether to be ice or vapor. Here, at nearly four thousand meters, the morning fog rolls up from invisible valleys below, condensing against the cold rock faces and creating a perpetual state of dampness despite the sparse precipitation.

The geology is young and raw—this plateau thrust upward only a few million years ago, and the bedrock still shows the violence of its birth. Massive slabs of metamorphic stone lie tilted at improbable angles, their surfaces dark and slick with moisture that seeps from microscopic fractures. Between these monoliths spread fields of frost-shattered rubble, the rocks barely beginning their long journey toward becoming soil. In the low places between ridges, water collects in shallow depressions, forming networks of alpine pools no deeper than an ankle, their bottoms lined with a primitive mud that freezes and thaws daily.

Vegetation clings to existence in scattered patches—cushion plants that grow no taller than a thumb, their roots probing deep into the scree for stability and moisture. Patches of arctic willow spread horizontally across the ground, never rising more than a few centimeters, their branches following the microtopography of the stones. In the wettest areas, where meltwater seeps constantly from buried ice lenses, carpets of moss and sedge form spongy mats that squelch underfoot, bright green against the gray stone.

The fauna here defies the harshness. Pikas dart between the rock piles, their sharp chirps the primary punctuation in the thin air's silence. Herds of wild yak move slowly across the distance, their heavy bodies dark spots against the pale landscape. Above, lammergeiers circle on thermals that barely exist in the cold air, while snow leopards pad silently along ridgelines, nearly invisible against the mottled stone. In the wet places, surprising clouds of midges rise in the brief warm hours of afternoon, and specialized spiders weave webs between rocks to catch them.

The strangest element is the remnant technology—not built structures, but the detritus of abandoned industry scattered across this wilderness. Rusted cable drums lie half-buried in scree slides. Segments of pipeline, their original purpose forgotten, channel meltwater into unintended watersheds. An entire diesel generator, its housing cracked by decades of freeze-thaw cycles, has become a den for pikas. These artifacts are being slowly digested by the landscape, their metals oxidizing and leaching into the primitive soils, their forms breaking down under the patient work of ice and gravity.

The weather here operates on multiple scales. Daily, the fog rises and falls with a tidal regularity. Weekly storm systems bring snow even in summer, which melts within hours of the sun's return. The constant freeze-thaw cycle works at the rocks with patient persistence, cracking them along crystalline boundaries, preparing the raw material for some distant geological future when this place might support more than its current severe simplicity.

Sound travels strangely in the thin atmosphere. The crack of splitting stone carries for kilometers. The yaks' occasional lowing seems to come from nowhere and everywhere at once. Most of the time, though, there is only the subtle whisper of water moving through its various states—trickling, freezing, sublimating directly from ice to vapor in the dry wind.

At dawn and dusk, when the oblique light catches the moisture in the air, the entire plateau glows with a pearl-like luminescence, neither bright nor dark but something in between, as if the landscape exists in a perpetual state of becoming, not quite formed, not quite dissolved, suspended between creation and entropy in the cold, wet heights where life persists through sheer biological stubbornness.
