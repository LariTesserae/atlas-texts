The Canopy Cities of the Desiccated Reach spread across a highland plateau where the last moisture fled ten thousand years ago. Here, at this elevation, the air has thinned to something that barely sustains combustion, yet the vegetation thrives through chemistries that abandoned water long before memory began.

The plants are everything. They rise in twisted columns of lignified tissue, each trunk a spiral of chambers and hollows that began as drought adaptation and became architecture itself. These aren't trees anymore—haven't been for millennia. They're substrates, their bark petrified into something between wood and stone, their interiors honeycombed with passages that pulse with synthetic bioluminescence. The vegetation extends for kilometers in every direction, but sparsely, each massive growth separated by expanses of bare mineral ground that glitters with salts no rain will ever wash away.

Cities nest within these plant-towers like parasites that learned symbiosis. The settlements thread through the hollow trunks and spreading crown-structures, their buildings grown rather than built—crystalline pods that the plants extrude when fed the right combination of rare earth elements. The technology is biological but follows no evolutionary logic Earth would recognize. Walls breathe without lungs. Floors digest waste and convert it to structural mass. The infrastructure spreads through the plant tissue in networks of engineered capillaries that transport information as chemical gradients.

The fauna moves constantly through this vertical world. Not animals exactly—more like motile portions of the ecosystem that never fully differentiated from their plant origins. They flow up and down the trunks in rivers of segmented tissue, feeding on dead cells and depositing nutrients, their movement generating a constant thrumming that reverberates through the hollow chambers. Some have developed crude eyes, clusters of photosensitive cells that track the dim bioluminescent patterns the plants use to communicate across the expanses between them.

Above, the sky is the color of old copper, thick with suspended organics that filter the distant sun to a perpetual twilight. The atmosphere itself participates in the ecosystem—spores and chemical signals and chains of carbon molecules that serve as both food and data, drifting between the scattered tower-plants in slow rivers of information.

The sounds never cease. The fauna's movement creates a base note, a grinding hum that rises and falls with their feeding cycles. The plants themselves sing—vibrations through their hollow cores as gases move through specialized chambers, a form of communication that predates the cities by eons. And the city-tech adds its own layer: ultrasonic pulses of data transmission, the whisper of molecular assembly, the crack and hiss of chemical reactions in the processing nodes.

Everything here is ancient, even the newest growth. The ecosystem reached this configuration so long ago that erosion has worn away the memory of what came before. The plateau itself is the stub of a mountain range that predated multicellular life, ground down to this highland pan where the last stand of this peculiar biology makes its home. The plants grow and die and regrow in the same spots, each generation building on the mineralized remains of the last, creating spires that are part organism, part geology, part archaeological record.

The settlements sprawl through maybe a dozen of these plant-spires, connected by gossamer bridges of engineered silk that the fauna spin between crowns. The cities feel both dense and empty—packed with technology so advanced it's invisible, yet sparse with actual inhabitants. The population tends their plant-hosts with rituals that might be maintenance or might be worship, feeding rare minerals into root systems, adjusting the chemical balance of growth chambers, ensuring the slow cycle of renewal that keeps this ancient system functioning.

At the edges of perception, stranger things occur. Places where the plants have begun to compute, their chemical networks achieving something like consciousness. Zones where the fauna briefly coalesce into larger organisms before dissolving again. Chambers deep in the oldest trunks where the technology has evolved past its creators' intentions, pursuing purposes that no longer align with human categories of thought.

The whole landscape pulses with a slow, deep life—not growing anymore, not dying, but simply persisting in this final form it found when the water left and the world changed and adaptation became architecture became ecosystem became something beyond naming.
