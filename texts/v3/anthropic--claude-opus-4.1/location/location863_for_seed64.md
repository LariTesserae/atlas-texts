**The Pneumatic Garden**

Deep within the mountain's hollowed heart, where no rain has fallen for ten thousand years, the crystal lung breathes its eternal rhythm. This pocket cavity—no larger than a cathedral nave—pulses with dense forests of metallic vegetation that have never known water.

The plants here are not plants at all, but complex crystalline growths that feed on pressure differentials. They cluster so tightly that walking between them requires turning sideways, sliding through corridors of copper-bright fronds and aluminum leaves that ring like wind chimes at the slightest touch. Each "tree" is a heat exchanger, its trunk a twisted column of bismuth steps spiraling upward into canopies of wire-thin filaments. The filaments branch and rebranch until they become a silver fog, a metal mist that fills every cubic inch of air between floor and ceiling.

The chamber maintains itself at precisely 11 degrees Celsius—cool enough that moisture would condense if any existed, but there is none. Instead, the air is bone-dry hydrogen and methane, kept in perfect circulation by the breathing of the cavity itself. Every seventeen minutes, ancient pneumatic pumps embedded in the walls wheeze to life, their industrial heritage evident in the brass fittings and iron valves that stud the stone like mechanical pustules. The sound is tremendous—a deep thrumming that makes the metal forest sing in harmonics, each plant resonating at its own frequency until the whole space becomes an organ of infinite pipes.

The elevation here defies simple measurement. Though the cavity exists within a mountain peak three kilometers above the baseline, the internal geometry follows non-Euclidean rules. Paths spiral up walls that become floors, and what seems like a twenty-meter climb might deposit you back where you started, only mirror-reversed. The metal trees grow in all orientations, their roots—if they can be called roots—penetrating into what might be ceiling or wall with equal indifference.

Scattered throughout are the remnants of whoever discovered or created this place. A single steel catwalk, partially consumed by the aggressive growth of tin-whisker vines, spans what might be considered the middle distance. Pressure gauges, their faces cracked but still functional, sprout from the trunks of the larger specimens. A diesel generator, its housing split open like a seed pod, has become the skeleton around which a particularly dense grove has organized itself—its pistons now serve as the heartbeat for a cluster of uranium-green growths that pulse with bioluminescent (or perhaps chemiluminescent) light.

This dim light is the only illumination—a sick, greenish glow that emanates from certain leaves when the pressure changes. It's never quite enough to see clearly by, creating a perpetual twilight that makes distances impossible to judge. Shadows don't behave properly here; they seem to have mass, pooling in the lower reaches like a heavy gas.

The forest has achieved perfect equilibrium. Nothing grows, nothing dies, nothing changes. Each metal leaf has found its optimal position and locked there, crystallized in time. The industrial machinery continues its mindless breathing, but even this is not change—merely the same breath, over and over, forever. The pneumatic pumps never wear out, the pressure never varies beyond its prescribed cycle, the temperature never fluctuates.

The strangeness of the place lies not in any single impossible feature, but in how naturally all these impossibilities fit together. This is a place where chemistry took a different path, where evolution discovered metal instead of carbon, where industrial engineering and biology became indistinguishable. The sound of it—that constant, overwhelming chorus of resonating metal and cycling pumps—creates a wall of noise that paradoxically feels like silence, as unchanging and omnipresent as the cosmic microwave background.

Standing in this crystalline forest, surrounded by metal plants that breathe hydrogen, in a space that folds in on itself, lit by the fevered glow of mechanical decay, you understand that this is not a dead place despite the absence of anything traditionally alive. It is simply complete—a finished equation that continues calculating the same result forever, a chemical reaction that has found its perfect, eternal equilibrium, singing its one deafening note into the darkness of the mountain's heart.
