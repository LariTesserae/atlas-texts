The terraced city clings to the mountainside like lichen on stone, its pale buildings carved directly from the slope's native limestone. Each level rises perhaps twelve feet above the next, connected by worn stairs cut into the rock face itself. The settlement is no larger than what five hundred souls might call home, yet its structures crowd together with an ancient urban density—narrow alleys thread between buildings whose walls nearly touch, creating a maze of shadows even in the middle hours.

Rain comes twice a week, always at night. The limestone drinks it greedily, channeling water through grooves worn over centuries into cisterns carved beneath each terrace. During the day, moisture seeps through the porous stone, beading on walls in the permanent coolness. The air tastes of wet rock and the faint mineral tang of dissolved calcium.

Between the buildings, in courtyards no wider than a modest room, stunted olive trees grow from deep stone planters, their roots threading through drainage holes to seek the cisterns below. Wild thyme and oregano sprout from cracks in the pavement, the only other green in this vertical world of grey and white stone. The vegetation seems placed by careful intention generations ago, now maintaining itself in perfect sparse balance.

The architecture speaks of primitive masonry perfected through repetition—blocks fitted without mortar, held by weight and precision alone. Wooden shutters, dark with age, frame glassless windows. The roofs are flat terraces themselves, accessible by exterior stairs, used for drying what little grain the mountain provides. Clay pipes, fired in kilns long cold, snake along walls to carry precious water from cistern to cistern.

Sound here is swallowed by stone. Footsteps echo briefly in the narrow passages then die. Voices carry only from terrace to terrace when raised deliberately. Even the wind, constant at this elevation, whispers rather than howls as it threads through the purposeful gaps left between buildings for ventilation. Occasionally, a raven calls from the crags above, its cry sharp against the perpetual quiet.

The settlement exists in a state of completion. No new buildings rise; no old ones fall. The limestone itself has reached an equilibrium with the weather—edges softened to curves, surfaces pocked with shallow cups that hold rainwater for hours after storms, inscriptions in an older alphabet still readable but no longer deepening. Iron rings set into walls for tethering animals have rusted to a stable patina. The wooden doors, though ancient, have hardened to something approaching stone themselves.

At the city's heart, where three terraces converge, a small plaza opens—perhaps forty feet across. A single fountain stands here, fed by an underground spring that emerges through a natural cleft. The water falls six inches into a shallow basin carved with a spiral pattern that creates a barely audible whisper. This is the only constant sound, the only movement in the stillness.

The light here is honest and unchanging in quality—the mountain's elevation means thin air and sharp shadows. Morning sun strikes the eastern faces of buildings in stark geometry. By afternoon, the western walls glow while the alleys pool with blue shadow. The limestone itself contributes its own pale luminescence, as if holding and gradually releasing the day's light.

Small gardens tucked behind buildings contain raised beds of dark mountain soil, imported generations ago and carefully maintained. Here grow small, hard vegetables—turnips, onions, hardy greens—in precise rows. Each plant stands separate from its neighbor, the space between them carefully calculated for the scant rainfall.

Birds are few—the occasional raven, sometimes a hawk circling far above. Lizards sun themselves on the warm stone, disappearing into cracks at the slightest vibration. At night, bats emerge from somewhere beneath the lowest terrace, their ultrasonic calls just at the edge of perception.

The boundaries of this place are absolute. Above: bare rock and scree rising to peaks lost in afternoon clouds. Below: a steep valley where the mountain's base disappears into morning mist. The city exists in its narrow band of possibility, neither growing nor shrinking, a permanent expression of what human habitation becomes when carved from stone and sustained by discipline across centuries. It breathes slowly, moisture in and out of limestone pores, but otherwise holds itself in perfect, patient stillness.
