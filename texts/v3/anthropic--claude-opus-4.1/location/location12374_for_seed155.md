The Canopy Mills of Verlassen stand like broken teeth against the mountain shoulder, their rust-eaten turbines tangled in miles of living wood. This was a city once—you can still trace the boulevard grids in the root patterns overhead, where the great scaffold trees have woven themselves into a breathing ceiling two hundred feet above the factory floors. Their trunks, each as wide as tenement buildings, grow in perfect rows that follow the old municipal planning, but their canopy has long since fused into a single organism that filters the high mountain light into a green cathedral dimness.

The mills themselves are being digested. Slowly, over centuries, the wood-boring vines have hollowed out the brick smokestacks and turned them into vertical gardens. The factory floors sprout with ferns that have learned to metabolize machine oil. In the foundries, where furnaces once roared, phosphorescent moss colonies have built elaborate terraced cities that mirror, in miniature, the industrial architecture they're consuming. The moss glows brightest where metal touches plant—a chemical reaction no botanist has adequately explained.

The sound never stops. It's not machinery anymore, though sometimes you'd swear you hear pistons in the rhythm. The scaffold trees pump water up from the deep mountain aquifers through pneumatic vessels in their heartwood, and the whole canopy throbs with hydraulic breathing. During the morning condensation, when mountain fog rolls through the mill yards, the trees sweat through specialized bark glands, and the droplets ring against the remaining metal infrastructure like a thousand orchestras tuning up. The root networks have learned to vibrate the old steel girders at specific frequencies. They sing the factory songs now—work hymns in the language of sap pressure and cellular respiration.

Rain comes twice a week, precisely, though no one remembers who programmed that schedule into the trees' genetic memory. The canopy opens specific channels to funnel water into the old cooling towers, which have become vertical wetlands where rare salamanders breed in the eternal twilight. These are the only animals left—translucent creatures that navigate by echolocation through the dripping mechanical forests of pipe and vine.

The streets exist in vertical space now. The original roads are buried under sixty feet of accumulated plant matter—centuries of fallen leaves compressed into a springy, petroleum-scented peat. But the scaffold trees grew their branches to preserve the traffic patterns, creating aerial boulevards where the old tram lines used to run. Maintenance catwalks spiral up the trunks, built by the last human inhabitants before they left, or became something else, or simply forgot they were separate from the plants. The walkways are wood now too—the trees learned to extrude them like shelves of fungus, complete with handrails of woven bark.

In the former commercial district, where the canopy is thickest, reality follows different rules. Streets intersect with themselves. A warehouse door opens onto its own roof. The trees here grew according to non-Euclidean architectural plans they seem to have extracted from the factory computers before consuming them. Walking through these quarters, you might find yourself approaching the same intersection from four different directions simultaneously, or discover that the fire escape you're climbing is also, somehow, a root system burrowing down into the mountain.

The power still flows—not electricity, but something else. The trees figured out how to photosynthesize the industrial waste, converting heavy metals into a kind of nutritional current that runs through specialized cambium networks. At night, you can see it pulsing under the bark like bioluminescent veins. The old transformer stations have become neural nodes where the forest thinks its slow thoughts. Sometimes, in the relay gardens where the switching equipment used to be, the air itself turns solid, and you can climb the crystallized atmosphere like a ladder into places that shouldn't exist.

The city spreads for miles along the high mountain plateau, but distance means nothing here. The trees have grown space itself into loops and folds. You might walk for hours through the factory districts and find yourself back where you started, or take three steps through a supply closet and emerge in the suburbs. The settlement obeys a geography of chlorophyll and rust, where proximity is determined by chemical gradients rather than physical measurement.

At the edges, where the city thins into normal forest, you can sometimes find the boundary zones—places where the industrial plant-mass is still deciding what it wants to become. Here, saplings grow in perfect mechanical timing, their leaves turning with the precision of clock gears. The bark patterns reproduce circuit diagrams from memory. These young trees are the city's future, spreading its organized impossibility further into the mountain, one perfectly aligned root at a time.

The loudest sound comes from the central mills, where the trees have preserved the factory whistles but repurposed them as respiratory organs. Every twelve hours, they exhale the day's photosynthesis in a chorus that echoes off the high peaks—a shift change whistle for a workforce that no longer exists, calling all the vanished workers home to their transformation.
