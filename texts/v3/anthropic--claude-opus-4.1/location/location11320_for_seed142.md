The plateau city spreads like a dark stain across the ancient tableland, its industrial towers rising from weathered stone that hasn't felt rain in ten thousand years. This is a place ground down to its bones—a vast expanse of primordial rock worn so smooth by eons of dry wind that it reflects the faint starlight like polished obsidian.

The settlement shouldn't exist here, and perhaps doesn't, not in any way that matters to the living. Its factories continue their work in near-darkness, drawing power from thermal wells that pierce down through miles of compressed geological time. The machinery hums at a frequency just below hearing, a vibration felt in the teeth and sternum rather than heard. Steam that isn't steam—some other vapor, heavy and luminous—bleeds from vents and settles in the shallow depressions between buildings, forming pools of glowing mist that never quite dissipate.

The architecture follows no human logic. Buildings lean at angles that suggest they've been here so long they've become geological features themselves, their industrial frameworks fused with the bedrock through processes of heat and pressure and time. Catwalks span between structures at heights that make no sense for workers who might fear falling. The metal has taken on the same worn-smooth quality as the stone, as if the entire city participates in the same ancient erosion.

In the sparse spaces between the industrial clusters, the plateau reveals itself—an infinity of abraded rock extending to horizons that blur with distance and darkness. The surface bears the marks of its age: not cracks or canyons but a terrible smoothness, interrupted only by shallow undulations where harder minerals resisted the wearing-down. These gentle rises and falls create a landscape that seems to breathe in geological time, though nothing here has moved in memory.

The few creatures that persist in this place are things of rumor more than sight—shapes glimpsed between the pylons, movements in the peripheral vision where the factory lights fail to reach. They might be indigenous to this weathered world, or they might be what remains of whoever built this place, evolved or devolved into forms that no longer require water, that feed on heat differentials or electromagnetic fields or the slow decay of industrial infrastructure.

The air itself has weight here, pressed down by the altitude and thickened with particles of stone dust so fine they've become part of the atmosphere. It tastes of copper and chalk. During the darkest hours—which is most hours, under skies where stars shine like distant furnaces through the industrial haze—the only illumination comes from the factories themselves: the glow of smelting operations that never cease, the phosphorescent trace of those strange mists, the occasional arc of electricity between towers.

Sound travels strangely across the worn plateau. The industrial processes continue in near-silence, their noise dampened by the heavy air and absorbed by the porous stone. Occasionally, a deeper sound emerges—a groaning from far beneath the surface, as if the planet's core still remembers what this place was before the water left, before the last root withered, before someone or something decided to build these engines on the bones of a dead landscape.

The city has no visible purpose anymore, if it ever did. Its products, whatever they might be, accumulate in warehouses that are themselves wearing down, their contents becoming geological strata. The entire settlement exists in a state of functional death—still operating but toward no end, maintained by automated systems or by workers too changed to recognize, following routines encoded in the architecture itself.

At the plateau's edges, where the land finally breaks and falls away to whatever lies below, the view extends across similar tablelands, each worn to the same terrible smoothness, some bearing their own dark clusters of industry. The pattern suggests intention, but an intention so old that its purpose has been abraded away like everything else here, leaving only the shape of its ambition impressed into the stone and steel and starlight.

This is a place that has already ended, but continues anyway—a landscape that has reached its final form but refuses to stop existing, where the last processes of an exhausted world play out their industrial liturgy in the darkness between dead stone and distant stars.
