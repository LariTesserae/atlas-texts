The Canopy Engines stretch beyond any horizon you could hope to find, a living industrial complex that has grown itself into being over millennia. Here, in the cool brightness of perpetual afternoon, vegetation has become both substrate and machine.

The trees—if you can still call them trees—rise like cooling towers, their trunks hollow and thrumming with the movement of their own sap through kilometer-high columns. They are sparse giants, each one claiming territory the size of a city, their crowns spreading into vast platforms of interwoven branches that have learned to photosynthesize not just sunlight but sound itself. The leaves are brass-green metallic things, millions upon millions of them, vibrating in frequencies that power the whole system. The noise is overwhelming—not harsh, but total: a chord that never resolves, built from the microscopic friction of leaf against leaf, the deep pneumatic breathing of the trunk-chambers, the ultrasonic communication between root networks that have evolved into something like neural highways.

Between these titan-trees, the space seems empty at first glance, but look closer: the air itself is thick with pollen that has become industrial—microscopic factories that process minerals drawn up from the dry bedrock far below. They drift in sparse clouds, manufacturing complex polymers that rain down as a fine dust, feeding the next generation of growth. No water exists here in liquid form; the trees learned long ago to split minerals for hydrogen, to harvest moisture from dimensions we cannot perceive.

The fauna have evolved as living tools. Beetles the size of locomotives traverse the branch highways, their shells incorporating the same metallic leaves, their movement generating power that feeds back into the system. Bird-things with wings of living solar panels patrol the bright spaces between trees, their cries adding new harmonies to the endless industrial symphony. Massive pollinating creatures—part mammal, part machine, part plant—lumber through the root galleries below, their footfalls sending tremors through the substrate that the trees translate into data.

Everything is always changing here. Branches grow and merge and split apart in real-time, seeking new configurations. Entire sections of canopy collapse and regenerate in different forms within days. The trees wage slow wars through chemical signals, forming and breaking alliances that reshape the landscape. Sometimes a section will achieve sudden coherence and begin manufacturing something incomprehensible—structures of pure sound, gravity-defying seed pods that float upward and never come down, patches of bark that become screens showing images of places that don't exist.

The brightness comes from above and within. The sun, yes, but also the bioluminescent flowering that never stops—each tree crowned with billions of tiny furnaces that burn without fire, converting the endless noise into light and heat and motion. The sky between the crowns crackles with electrical discharge as the trees ground their excess energy into the thin atmosphere.

You could walk for months through the galleries between the root buttresses, each one a cathedral of organic machinery, and never find evidence that anything built this place. It built itself, is building itself, will build itself into something else tomorrow. The industrial revolution here needs no cities, no civilizations. The trees discovered mechanization on their own terms, and they've been perfecting it for geological ages, each generation more efficient, more strange, more incomprehensibly powerful than the last.

The sound never stops. Even in the spaces between spaces, where the air grows thin and the pollen settles into drifts of potential machinery, you can feel it through your bones: the deep tectonic hum of a world that has become one vast, living, thinking, creating machine, powered by its own existence, growing into architectures that no conscious mind designed.
