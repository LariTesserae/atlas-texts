The machinery has been drinking for decades.

In this low basin where warm air settles thick as syrup, the old pumping station has merged with what it was meant to drain. Mangrove-analogues—though these aren't quite mangroves, their bark sweating a viscous amber resin that glows faintly in the filtered daylight—have wrapped themselves through the iron frameworks, their prop roots threading between pistons, their breathing pneumatophores pushing up through cracked concrete floors. The trees grow so densely here that their canopy has become a single organism, branches fused at their touching points, sharing one continuous cambium layer that ripples visibly when the wind moves through.

The pumps still run. This is the strangest part: somewhere beneath the vegetal mass, turbines turn on a slow, geological schedule. Every seventeen hours, the entire grove shudders as ancient diesel generators cough to life, their exhaust filtered through root systems and emerging as a sweet, hydrocarbon perfume from specialized pores in the leaf canopy. The sound is tremendous when it happens—a deep thrumming that makes the water droplets on every surface dance and merge, that sets the resin weeping faster from the bark.

Water is everywhere but never quite water. It seeps up through the root-fractured foundation, already thick with tannins and industrial runoff, creating pools that shimmer with an oily rainbow sheen. The trees drink this eagerly. Their leaves, broad as serving platters and thick as leather, are edged with specialized cells that excrete excess metals as tiny crystalline deposits. When the light catches them right—and the light here is always catching something right, filtered green-gold through the canopy—the entire grove glitters as if studded with broken glass.

The original purpose of the station is obscured but not erased. Massive intake pipes, three meters in diameter, still pierce the grove floor at regular intervals. But instead of draining the marsh, they've become nurseries. Each pipe mouth hosts its own microbiome: specialized ferns whose fronds unfurl in perfect Fibonacci spirals, drinking condensation from the metal; phosphorescent fungi that pulse in rhythm with the distant generators; climbing vines that have learned to conduct electricity through their sap, creating a secondary nervous system that spans the entire structure.

You can walk here, barely. Catwalks and maintenance platforms still exist, though they're more suggestion than structure now. The metal grillwork is visible through the living mass that's claimed it—branches that have grown to incorporate the iron into their heartwood, creating wood that rings when struck. The packed density of growth means moving through the space requires negotiation. You push aside curtains of aerial roots. You duck under branches that drip constantly, not with water but with that amber resin that hardens slowly in the air, creating stalactites of plant matter that will eventually reach the flooded floor and take root.

The fauna that remains has adapted to invisibility. Occasionally, you might spot something that could be an eye reflecting from a hollow trunk, or hear something that might be breathing distinct from the wheeze of the machinery. But mostly, the animals here have learned to mimic the plant-machine hybrid environment. There are things with chitinous shells that look exactly like seed pods until they scuttle. Moths whose wings have evolved the exact pattern of rust on corrugated metal.

The sound never fully stops. Even between the generator cycles, there's the constant drip-tick-hiss of water moving through infinite channels, the creak of branches adjusting their weight, the pop of seed pods opening in the heat, the gurgle of digestion as the trees process their diet of contaminated water. And underneath it all, if you listen carefully, the faint electric hum of sap moving through vines that have learned to conduct current, powering something—bioluminescent flowers, perhaps, or some deeper process we don't yet understand.

The boundaries of this place are clear but soft. Beyond a certain density, the grove simply stops, giving way to more ordinary marsh. But tendrils explore outward constantly. Runners follow old buried pipes. Seeds drift on the warm air, looking for other industrial ruins to colonize. The grove is patient. It has been here long enough that rust and chlorophyll have become a single color, long enough that the distinction between what was built and what grew has lost meaning.

Sometimes, in the seventeen-hour lulls between pump cycles, you can find places where the original tile floors are still visible through gaps in the root mat. They're a institutional green, almost the exact shade of the leaves overhead. It's impossible to tell if this is coincidence or adaptation—if the trees chose this color in sympathy with their substrate, or if the tiles have been stained by decades of tannin rain. Like everything here, the truth is probably both, probably neither, probably something stranger and more symbiotic than we have words for.
