The Weeping Shelves rise in crystalline terraces above the permanent frost line, each level a dense maze of ice-carved channels where meltwater runs in ten thousand temporary streams. This is a city built into the bones of ancient glacial erosion—primitive stone dwellings packed shoulder-to-shoulder along the narrow ledges, their flat roofs serving as the streets for the tier above, creating a vertical labyrinth of habitation that climbs five hundred feet up the mountainside.

The brightness here is painful, almost violent. The wet black stone of the settlement reflects and multiplies the high-altitude sun until the entire cliff face becomes a burning mirror. Ice crystals in the perpetually saturated air create endless refractions—phantom rainbows that shift and vanish with each movement, halos around every shadow. The inhabitants have adapted by wearing strips of translucent fish-skin over their eyes, moving through their dazzling home in permanent squints.

Water is the constant companion and tormentor. It seeps from every crack in the rock face, runs in silver threads down the hand-cut stairs, pools in the carved basins that serve as both cisterns and mirrors. The stone itself weeps continuously—some peculiar property of the mineral causes it to draw moisture from the thin air and release it in slow, steady tears. Every surface glistens. Moss grows nowhere, cannot find purchase in the constant flow, but strange translucent films—part algae, part something else—coat the wettest stones in layers like living glass.

The fauna here defies expectation. Creatures that should not exist at this elevation thrive in the warm microclimate created by the settlement's density and the greenhouse effect of all that reflected light. Lungfish writhe through the temporary pools, breathing air when the water runs too thin. Colonies of something like coral but mobile creep along the drainage channels, filtering nutrients from the constant flow. Birds with too many wings nest in the gaps between buildings, their metabolisms running so hot they steam in the cold air. Salamanders the color of copper oxidation hunt the vertical walls, their bodies generating antifreeze that crystallizes on their skin like armor.

The crowding is absolute. Windows open onto other windows barely an arm's length away. The sound of water trickling through carved gutters mingles with the soft clicking of the salamanders' crystal scales, the wheeze of lungfish breathing, the subsonic hum of the coral-things feeding—yet somehow these sounds layer into something approaching silence, a white noise of life that the ear stops registering. Conversations happen in whispers that carry perfectly through the wet air. Children learn to move silently on the slick stones from infancy.

Stone hammers and copper tools hang from every doorway, constantly in use as the inhabitants wage perpetual war against erosion, rechanneling the water that threatens to undermine their foundations even as it sustains them. They harvest the living glass films with bone scrapers, dry them on the bright roofs, weave them into waterproof cloth. They farm the coral-things in artificial pools, eat the lungfish, trade the crystallized salamander antifreeze to lowland visitors who believe it extends life.

But there is turbulence here beyond the constant motion of water and construction. The settlement rearranges itself. Buildings abandoned for mere days are found relocated—still intact but moved upslope or down, sometimes fused with other structures. New terraces appear overnight, carved by no human hand. The water channels reconfigure themselves, following laws of flow that shouldn't exist, running uphill in places, splitting into probability cascades where a single stream becomes momentarily several, each taking different paths before reconverging.

The brightest days are the worst. When the sun reaches its zenith and the reflections achieve perfect resonance, the settlement begins to duplicate itself—ghost buildings appear in the air, inverted, swimming with reversed waterfalls. Sometimes inhabitants see themselves on distant terraces they've never visited, going about lives they don't remember living. The children born here don't find this strange; they navigate between the real and reflected city with equal confidence, sometimes returning with objects that shouldn't exist, tools of impossible manufacture, fruits from vegetation that never grew on this mountain.

At the heart of the settlement, where the terraces converge into a natural amphitheater, the water collects into a pool that never overflows despite constant input. The surface remains perfectly still while chaos flows beneath—shapes moving in dimensions the eye can't properly follow. The oldest inhabitants say the city grew from this pool, crystallizing outward and upward like slow ice, that they simply carved doorways into what was already waiting to be inhabited. They say one day it will all flow back into the pool and emerge somewhere else, on some other mountain, perhaps already has, perhaps is doing so perpetually.

The smell is of minerals and ozone, wet stone and the peculiar sweetness of the living glass. Your clothes never dry here. Your reflection never looks quite right. The settlement continues to build itself while you sleep, adding rooms no one ordered, stairs that lead to yesterday, cisterns that fill with tomorrow's rain.
