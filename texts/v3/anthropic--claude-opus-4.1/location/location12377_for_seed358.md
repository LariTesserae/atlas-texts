## The Pressing Chamber

You descend through a maintenance shaft into the vast industrial cavity that workers call the Pressing Chamber, though its true purpose was forgotten decades ago when the last engineers fled. The air hits you like a wet cloth—thick, warm, saturated with machine oil and groundwater that seeps endlessly through the bedrock overhead. Every surface weeps: condensation runs in rivulets down the corroded steel pillars, drips from the maze of overhead pipes, pools in the warped metal grating that serves as a floor.

The space extends beyond your headlamp's reach, perhaps two hundred meters across, maybe more. The ceiling hangs low—barely four meters in most places, compressed by the weight of earth above. But the chamber sprawls horizontally through the darkness, a sprawling industrial catacomb that might have once been a processing facility, a water treatment plant, or something stranger. The geometry feels wrong, as if the entire space is slowly folding in on itself, millimeter by millimeter, year by year.

The noise is overwhelming. Not varied or sharp, but a single, crushing drone—the aggregate sound of ten thousand pumps still mindlessly working, pushing water through circuits that no longer connect to anything meaningful. The sound doesn't echo; it simply fills every cubic meter of air until your bones vibrate with it. Beneath this mechanical tinnitus, you hear them: the rustlers. Millions of them.

Pale crustaceans the size of thumbnails carpet every surface in a living, writhing mass. They've evolved here in the decades since abandonment—eyeless, translucent, their shells soft from the constant moisture. They feed on the bacterial films that coat the pipes, on the algae that somehow grows in the weak emergency lighting that still flickers from ancient fixtures, on each other. When you walk, they part like a living liquid, flowing around your boots, immediately filling the space behind you. The density of life here is suffocating—every surface teems, every pocket of space occupied by something scuttling, feeding, dying, being consumed.

Sparse vegetation struggles in this cave-industrial ecosystem. Thin runners of some adapted vine creep along the warmest pipes, following the heat like a bloodstream. Patches of bioluminescent moss cling to joints and seams where minerals leach from corroding metal—not enough to call it green, just occasional spots of sick phosphorescence in the permanent twilight. The plants seem more mineral than vegetable, incorporating rust and copper into their tissues until they gleam dully like circuit boards.

The machinery embedded in this place is both dead and alive. Massive treatment tanks, each the size of a house, still churn with ancient momentum. Catwalks bridge between them, some intact, others collapsed into the shallow lakes of runoff that pool throughout the chamber. Control panels blink with meaningless patterns, their displays long since divorced from whatever processes they once monitored. A few automated systems still function in their broken way—a robotic arm sweeps back and forth through empty air, a conveyor belt cycles endlessly, carrying nothing, depositing nothing.

The water table is so close here you can feel it pressing up through the floor. In places, the grating has given way entirely, revealing pools of black water of unknown depth. The walls occasionally burst with new springs, as if the earth itself is slowly squeezing this place like a sponge. The warmth comes from below—geothermal vents that once powered this facility still breathe their heat up through the foundation, making the water steam, the metal surfaces hot to touch, the air thick as soup.

You realize this place is still becoming something. Not built anymore, not quite ruins—caught in a slow transformation between industrial space and natural cave system. The machinery is being digested by geology, incorporated into a new kind of structure. Mineral deposits already coat the pipes like coral growth. The rustlers are building something too—their accumulated shells form drifts and mounds that might, in a thousand years, become a new kind of stone.

Time moves differently here. The only changes you can perceive are the slow drift of the rustler masses, the occasional groan of settling metal, the patient drip of water working its way through everything. But if you could watch for decades, you'd see this place breathing—expanding and contracting with the seasons, flooding and draining with underground tides, slowly crushing itself into something new.

The sound never stops. Even when you leave—if you can find your way back to the shaft—it follows you. A pressure in your ears that takes days to fade. The rustlers, too, stay with you: in your clothes, in your dreams, a sensation of crawling density that makes all other spaces feel impossibly, unsettlingly empty.
