The plateau breathes steam through its thousand mouths. At this elevation—where the air should be thin and cold—the world has turned itself inside out, creating a suspended jungle of impossible heat.

Stone columns rise from the bedrock like the ribs of some vast, buried creature, each one hollowed and channeled by millennia of patient dissolution. Through these natural pipes, geothermal breath escapes in slow, constant exhalations that blur the boundaries between solid and vapor. The rock itself weeps—not with surface water, but with condensation drawn from the perpetual cloud that both crowns and creates this place.

Vegetation cascades in vertical gardens down every available surface. Root systems penetrate deep into the porous stone, following ancient water channels carved when this plateau was young. Epiphytes cluster in the perpetual mist zones where steam meets cooler air—their leaves adapted to harvest water from fog, their roots never touching earth. Giant ferns unfurl from crevices, their fronds reaching spans that shouldn't be possible at this altitude. Moss carpets everything in vivid emerald, growing thick as fur on the windward faces of the towers.

The heat comes from below—not volcanic, but something stranger. Deep beneath the plateau, a network of natural reactors slowly processes the mountain's mineral heart through bacterial colonies that have never known sunlight. Their metabolic heat rises through the honeycomb rock, creating this elevated greenhouse where tropical plants thrive in what should be alpine cold. The process hums at the edge of perception, a subsonic vibration that you feel more than hear.

Water is everywhere but never quite where you expect it. It sheets down certain cliff faces in predictable patterns, following grooves worn smooth over geological time. It pools in natural cisterns where the rock has cupped itself to catch the constant drip. It hangs in the air as mist so thick that distant towers fade into green-grey suggestions. During the cooler hours before dawn, the entire plateau streams with temporary waterfalls as the night's condensation exceeds what the stone can hold.

Birds nest in the vertical forests, their calls muffled by the moisture-heavy air. They've evolved shorter, broader wings for navigating the dense mist and narrow spaces between towers. Insects thrive in the constant humidity—beetles with shells that channel condensation to their mouths, moths that never need to seek water. Small mammals traverse the plateau through aerial root highways, never descending to the broken ground below.

The industrial presence reveals itself slowly, like recognizing a pattern in static. Those aren't just steam vents—some are extraction points where automated systems tap the bacterial processes below. Collectors, grown into the rock face like mechanical lichen, harvest rare minerals precipitated by the heat exchange. The technology is old, perhaps older than memory, integrated so thoroughly with the natural systems that distinguishing between geological process and ancient engineering becomes impossible. Somewhere beneath, turbines turn in the constant updraft, their electromagnetic fields occasionally causing the mist to shimmer with brief auroras.

The sound-world is soft but layered. The constant whisper of ascending vapor. The irregular drip-music of condensation finding its thousand paths. The rustle of leaves in thermal currents that never quite become wind. Occasionally, a deeper tone—a pipe-organ note when conditions align and the hollow towers resonate with their own circulation.

This is a place between states—between solid and gas, between natural and engineered, between jungle and mountain. The plateau exists in its own microclimate, a sky island where the rules have been rewritten by time, heat, and the slow collaboration between stone, water, and whatever ancient purpose first brought the machines that now dream beneath the green.
