The Furnace Gardens stretch across the plateau in waves of fused vegetation—a vast industrial greenhouse that has long ago transcended its original purpose and become something else entirely. Here, in perpetual twilight beneath kilometer-thick canopies of engineered leaf-matter, the distinction between factory and forest has been erased by centuries of unguided growth.

The plants themselves are the machinery now. What began as hydroponic towers and vertical farms have merged into singular organisms—great pillars of cellulose and chlorophyll that twist upward through the hot, pressurized atmosphere. Their surfaces are ribbed with the remnants of steel frameworks, scaffolding that has been absorbed and digested over generations until metal and wood fiber are indistinguishable. The leaves—if they can still be called that—are broad as sails and thick as mattresses, layered so densely that they form a near-solid ceiling. They photosynthesize not from sunlight but from the heat itself, drawing energy from the thermal gradients that ripple constantly through this sealed ecosystem.

The air is thick, almost syrupy, superheated and laden with spores, pollen, and the chemical exhaust of ten thousand different metabolic processes happening simultaneously. It moves in slow, powerful currents—invisible rivers of heat that snake between the plant-pillars, carrying with them clouds of reproductive material that settle and sprout wherever they land. New growth erupts constantly from old growth, structures building upon structures in an endless cycle of creation and consumption.

The floor—there must be a floor somewhere beneath it all—is lost under meters of accumulated organic matter. Root systems have become indistinguishable from mycorrhizal networks have become indistinguishable from the corroded remains of the original irrigation systems. Everything is substrate for everything else. Walk across what seems like solid ground and you're actually traversing the back of some enormous tuber, or the calcified remains of a tree that fell and was immediately colonized by a thousand other species before it could properly decay.

The industrial bones of the place still function, after a fashion. Enormous turbines, their blades now sheathed in bark and spinning lazily in the thermal currents, generate power that feeds into root-networks adapted to conduct electricity. Pistons push up through the canopy in places, their mechanical motion long ago synchronized with the growth patterns of the vines that encrust them. Steam vents—or are they specialized pneumatophores?—release pressure in regular intervals, creating brief clearings in the spore-clouds before they fill again.

Sound here is muffled, compressed. The constant low rumble of growth—cells dividing, wood expanding, sap flowing through trunk-pipes wide as subway tunnels—creates a bass note felt more than heard. Occasionally something structural gives way with a groan that resonates through the entire plateau-spanning organism. The collapse is immediately followed by a rush of new growth to fill the void, a green thunderclap of vegetation racing to claim the newly available space.

The darkness is not absolute but it is pervasive. Bioluminescent fruits hang in clusters from the undersides of the great leaf-plates, providing a dim, greenish glow that shifts and pulses with the metabolic rhythms of their parent plants. Some of the older mechanical systems still spark with electricity, creating brief auroras of blue-white light that illuminate the endless colonnade of plant-pillars before dying away. In certain places, where the vegetation has grown into the shape of focusing lenses, the heat itself becomes visible—shimmering orange mirages that dance between the trunks like ghost-fires.

There are paths through this place, or what might generously be called paths—corridors where the growth is marginally less dense, where the air moves a little more freely. These passages shift daily, hourly, as new growth pushes in from the sides and old growth collapses from above. Navigate by the sound of the turbines, by the pattern of electrical discharge, by the temperature gradients that map out the hidden architecture beneath all this green.

At the edges of the plateau, where the land drops away into whatever lies beyond, the plant-mass doesn't stop but rather cascades over the sides in great waterfalls of vegetation. These hanging gardens sway in the external winds, their motion propagating back through the entire structure in slow, building-wide undulations. The boundary between inside and outside has become meaningless—the Furnace Gardens are their own sealed world, a terraforming project that has terraformed itself into something unrecognizable.

And through it all, the sense of ancient turbulence—this is not a peaceful garden but a slow-motion explosion, a biological reactor that has been in constant meltdown for so long that meltdown has become its stable state. Every surface seethes with growth, every growth is immediately colonized by new growth, every death is instantly transformed into life. The industrial age never ended here; it simply changed kingdoms, from mechanical to botanical, and continued its relentless production.
