The root-city breathes beneath its own weight.

You descend through a maintenance shaft barely wider than your shoulders, past layers of living infrastructure—pipes wrapped in moss-fur that sweats condensation, fungal gaskets swollen tight around joints, vascular bundles thick as your torso pulsing with transported fluids. The ladder rungs are slick with perpetual moisture, each one furred with what might be algae or might be the city's own attempt at creating grip.

Twenty meters down, the shaft opens into the production floor.

The space is cathedral-tall but oppressively close, every cubic meter occupied by the city's botanical machinery. Overhead, the ceiling is lost in a canopy of industrial foliage—great pneumatic leaves that compress and release in slow rhythm, breathing for the structures above. Their surfaces glisten with engineered dew that drips in steady percussion onto the equipment below. The sound is enormous—not from any single source but from the accumulated whisper of ten million processes. Steam vents hiss through biological filters. Hydraulic roots groan as they adjust pressure differentials. Somewhere deeper, digestive chambers gurgle as they break down waste into nutrients.

The floor is not a floor but a mesh of interwoven root systems, each strand as thick as industrial cable, forming walkways and platforms at different heights. Everything grows into and through everything else. A processing tank sprouts from the root-mesh like a massive gourd, its translucent walls revealing the slow churn of cultivation medium inside. Beside it, what might have once been a diesel generator has been completely overtaken—its metal housing split open by woody growth, its mechanical parts fused with chlorophyll-stained tissue that has learned to metabolize petroleum into sugar.

The warmth is organic, fevered—the heat of decomposition and synthesis, of ten thousand chemical reactions occurring in plant-cells modified to industrial purpose. Water beads on every surface. It runs in engineered channels along the stems of structural columns that might be trees, might be pipes, have become both. The air tastes green and metallic, thick with humidity and the tang of chlorophyll-processed oils.

You follow a catwalk made of woven cellulose strong as steel, passing banks of cultivation pods where the city grows its own components. Through yellowed membrane windows, you glimpse embryonic pump-hearts the size of washing machines, their tissues pink and translucent, veins already beginning to branch. A cluster of mature filters hangs like fruit, ready for harvest and installation. Everything is precisely organized yet utterly organic—the systematic chaos of a garden that has learned to be a factory, or perhaps the reverse.

The industrial plant-flesh presses in from all sides. Ducts made of modified bamboo carry heated air through spaces too packed with growth to permit any other passage. Electrical cables—if that's what they still are—run like vines along every surface, their copper cores wrapped in bark that occasionally sparks with bioluminescent signals. The few light sources are bulbs grown rather than manufactured, their wan yellow glow barely penetrating the vegetal density, creating pools of visibility in the greater dimness.

At the center of this level sits the primary digester, a chamber the size of a small building, its walls a weave of muscular fibers that contract in peristaltic waves. Fed by a dozen tributary pipes—some metal, some grown, most an integration of both—it processes the district's waste stream with patient efficiency. The sound it makes is oceanic, tidal, punctuated by the wheeze of pressure valves that might be mechanical or might be specialized organs the city has evolved for this purpose.

This is infrastructure as ecosystem, technology as biology—a place where the boundary between grown and built dissolved generations ago. The city cultivates itself, maintains itself, slowly expands itself through these packed chambers where no human was meant to spend time but where someone must occasionally descend to tend the tending systems. The botanical machines require their gardener-engineers, after all, to prune what grows too wild, to graft new connections, to ensure the urban organism's metabolism continues its ancient industrial photosynthesis.

Everything drips. Everything breathes. Everything grows into the wet, warm darkness, packed solid with the living machinery that keeps the city above alive.
