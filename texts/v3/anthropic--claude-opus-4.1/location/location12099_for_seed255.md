The foundry-caverns sprawl beneath forgotten cities, a labyrinth of hand-hewn chambers where the air tastes of copper and wet stone. No sun reaches here—only the occasional shaft boring down through collapsed streets brings wan light that dies within meters of entry. The darkness is absolute but for the forge-glow, orange-red emissions from primitive smelting pits that dot the expansive underground like constellation maps drawn in ember.

Water seeps everywhere, though never in torrents. It beads on the low stone ceilings, runs in finger-wide channels carved by generations of picks and chisels, pools in deliberate basins hammered from the rock floor. The moisture carries minerals from the world above, depositing them in pale curtains of flowstone that the inhabitants chip away and reshape, incorporating the natural accumulation into their endless construction. Steam hisses where water meets the furnace-heated walls, creating pockets of fog that roll through the passages.

Everything here has been touched by tools, yet the tools themselves are crude—stone hammers, bronze chisels, implements of fired clay and beaten copper. The original cave system, whatever it might have been, has been completely consumed by this primitive architecture. Walls are carved into shelving, floors pounded flat and inlaid with fitted stones, ceilings buttressed with pillars of stacked rock mortared with mineral paste. Even the stalactites have been hollowed into tubes that channel the dripping water into a vast network of carved gutters and catch-pools.

The sound is overwhelming—a constant industrial percussion of hammering, chipping, grinding. The acoustics of the modified caves create strange amplifications and echoes, so that a chisel strike in one chamber might boom like thunder three chambers away. Beneath this din runs the subtler chorus of dripping, trickling, flowing water, and the wheeze of the bellows, the crack of heated stone, the scrape of metal on metal.

What sparse life exists here defies conventional biology. Patches of pale, crystalline growths spread across the walls where the moisture is thickest—not quite mineral, not quite fungus, feeding on the metal-rich water and the heat differentials. These formations pulse faintly, their surfaces rippling with patterns that follow the rhythm of the hammering, as if the constant vibration has become part of their life cycle. Occasionally, something moves in the peripheral darkness—primitive creatures adapted to this constructed environment, their bodies incorporating fragments of worked stone and metal, neither fully natural nor fully made. They feed on the crystalline growths and the metal shavings, their chittering adding another layer to the cacophony.

The space extends far beyond what any single viewpoint can encompass. Chambers open into chambers, connected by passages both deliberate and accidental. Some zones are dense with activity—forges clustered together, their heat creating convection currents that pull the cool, damp air through the passages. Other areas lie temporarily abandoned, their half-finished constructions weeping water, tools left precisely where they were set down, waiting for workers to return and resume the eternal building.

The entire landscape pulses with industry despite its primitive technology. New sections are constantly being excavated and shaped, while older sections are torn down and rebuilt. The inhabitants—whoever or whatever they are—seem driven by an imperative to continue constructing, to transform every surface, to impose intentional form on every cubic meter of space. Yet their work is never complete; water seeps through their mortared walls, depositing new minerals that must be carved away or incorporated. The crystalline growths push through their fitted stones. The rare creatures gnaw new passages that must be blocked or widened into proper tunnels.

In the deepest sections, where the sound of construction becomes a physical pressure and the darkness seems to have weight, the architecture grows strange. Chambers spiral into themselves, stairs lead up to meet their own bases, doorways open onto the same room from different angles. The primitive tools have shaped something beyond primitive understanding—a built environment that challenges the physics of the space it occupies, where the constant moisture might drip upward, where the forge-light bends around corners that shouldn't exist.

The air moves constantly, pulled by the temperature differentials between the furnace zones and the cold seeps, carrying the taste of metal and mineral, the spores of the crystalline growths, the fine dust of endless construction. This is not a dead place but furiously alive in its own way—a vast, dark workshop where the distinction between natural and constructed has been erased by millennia of patient, primitive, and relentless making.
