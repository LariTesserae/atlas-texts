The birthing pools of the thermal shelf lie scattered across a plateau of still-cooling stone, each depression lined with a membrane of crystallized vapor that formed when the rock first broke through yesterday—or was it last week? Time moves differently here, measured in condensation cycles rather than days.

Steam rises constantly from the black basalt, creating a perpetual warm fog that limits visibility to perhaps thirty meters. The rock itself sweats—beading with moisture that runs in temporary rivulets before evaporating again, leaving behind thin deposits of minerals in branching patterns like frozen lightning. The plateau stretches perhaps the size of a small plaza, bounded by drops into mist on three sides and a rising wall of newer stone on the fourth, still glowing faintly orange in its deepest cracks.

Scattered across this steaming expanse, temporal anchors puncture the rock at irregular intervals—slender pillars of something that might be metal or might be calcified light, each no thicker than a wrist, extending upward until they disappear into the fog. They hum at a frequency just below hearing, a vibration felt in the bones. Where they meet the stone, reality pools and eddies, creating zones where water flows upward in slow spirals before falling again as warm rain.

The fauna here exists in multiple states simultaneously. Creatures phase in and out of visibility—transparent arthropods the size of housecats whose shells fill with condensation until they become visible, then empty again in sudden explosive dispersals of mist. Their movements create the dominant sound: a constant percussion of clicking and hissing as superheated moisture meets cooler air, punctuated by deeper thrums when the larger ones move through the temporal eddies.

A few stubborn lichens have begun to colonize the oldest surfaces, spreading in geometric patterns that follow the mineral veins. They glow faintly bioluminescent in the steam, creating brief constellations when the fog thickens. Where they grow too close to the anchors, they crystallize and shatter, their fragments beginning the cycle again.

At the center of the plateau, someone—or something—has arranged seven of the fallen anchor fragments into a rough shelter, each piece still maintaining its reality-bending properties. Inside this makeshift structure, rain falls upward in a perfect column, never reaching the continuously dry circle of stone beneath. The only other sign of intelligence: a series of shallow grooves carved into the rock, collecting the mineral-rich runoff into patterns that might be writing or might be a map of the condensation cycles.

The air tastes of copper and ozone. Sound behaves strangely—the constant rainfall creates a white noise that somehow grows louder the further away you stand from its source. The chittering of the phase-creatures echoes from directions that don't exist, arriving before the creatures themselves become visible.

This is new land, still deciding what it wants to become. The stone continues to rise imperceptibly from below, adding perhaps a centimeter each day, while the temporal anchors sink at nearly the same rate, maintaining their precise heights above the surface. The ecosystem building itself here operates on principles that mundane physics can only partially describe—thermodynamics works differently in the anchor zones, entropy flows backward in the rain columns, and the fog sometimes shows shadows of structures that won't exist for another hundred years.

At the western edge, where the plateau drops away, the heated air creates a permanent updraft. The phase-creatures ride these thermals, flickering in and out of existence as they rise, their clicks and hisses dopplering into something almost like language. Sometimes they return carrying tiny fragments of frozen cloud, which they deposit near the anchors where the fragments immediately sublime into more fog, perpetuating the cycle.

The whole place pulses with a rhythm—stone cooling, water condensing, creatures phasing, anchors humming—like a vast exposed organ learning to breathe.
