The city spreads across the salt pan like a fever dream, its towers and tenements rising from crystalline mud that never quite dries. In the near-darkness, buildings appear as geometric interruptions against the flat expanse — concrete and carved stone structures that sweat constantly in the oppressive heat, their surfaces slick with condensation that tastes wrong on the tongue.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It seeps up through foundation cracks, beads on every surface, hangs thick in the air, but never pools properly. The ground stays perpetually damp, a mixture of mineral crust and dark mud that squelches between the paving stones. The few stunted plants that survive here — twisted succulents with flesh like wet leather, sparse grasses that grow in perfect spirals — drink not from roots but from the air itself, their surfaces covered in thousands of tiny mouths that open and close with a rhythm no one has successfully measured.

The architecture defies consistent geometry. A tower might have seven floors when counted from the north, nine from the south. Doorways lead to rooms that shouldn't fit within their buildings' footprints. The urban planning follows dream-logic: main thoroughfares that circle back on themselves after exactly three miles, every time, even when they clearly diverge. Public squares where the echo arrives before the sound that made it.

No animals move through these streets — no pigeons on the geometric rooflines, no rats in the sewers that run backwards during certain hours, no insects in the sparse vegetation. The city's inhabitants built this place, or perhaps it built itself around their need for it, but they are the only living things that move here besides the plants. Even bacteria seem reluctant, and things decay too slowly, mummifying in the mineral-heavy air.

The darkness is thick, almost tactile. Not an absence of light but a presence unto itself — it has weight, texture, different densities in different districts. Street lamps burn but illuminate nothing beyond their own glass globes. Windows glow from within but cast no light outward. The sun, when it rises (and it doesn't always rise when it should), is a pale disk that provides heat but no brightness, like a fever you can see.

The city makes its own weather. Columns of hot air rise from certain intersections, creating slow cyclones of mist that travel prescribed routes through the districts. Sometimes it rains upward, droplets lifting from the saturated ground to disappear into the dark sky. The wind occasionally flows through solid walls while leaving curtains untouched.

In the industrial district, factories process materials that don't exist — their smokestacks release memories of smoke, their drainage pipes leak the suggestion of chemicals. The machinery inside operates on principles that work only here, where causality has agreed to local amendments. Workers clock in with timepieces that run backward and are paid in currency that changes denomination based on the observer.

The residential areas cluster in impossible densities — apartment buildings that share the same space but not the same time, their residents passing through each other's walls on different days of a week that sometimes has nine days, sometimes four. Gardens grow on rooftops where the sparse vegetation takes forms that shouldn't survive: geometric cacti that weep constantly, singular blades of grass that cast shadows of entire meadows, bonsai trees that are larger than the buildings they sit upon.

At the city's heart lies the civic center, where the municipal buildings engage in slow architectural drift, exchanging facades and floor plans over the course of decades. The courthouse passes judgment on crimes that haven't been committed yet. The library loans books that write themselves as they're read. The hospital treats conditions that don't have names, symptoms that manifest as alterations in local physics rather than biology.

Water mains run beneath the streets, but they carry something that isn't quite water — it has water's properties only when observed directly. The sewers drain upward into holding tanks that exist perpendicular to normal space. Storm drains open onto dry riverbeds that flood with memories of ancient rivers, their ghost-waters real enough to drown in but leaving no moisture behind.

The heat never breaks. It presses down like a physical weight, making the air shimmer even in the darkness. It's the heat of fever, of delirium, of spaces between sleep and waking. It rises from the earth itself, from the city's foundations that extend down through the salt pan to something that might be bedrock, might be bone, might be the crystallized remains of an older reality.

The quiet is not peaceful but expectant. Footsteps make sounds that arrive minutes after the walker has passed. Conversations happen in whispers that carry for miles or shouts that die inches from the speaker's lips. The city's machinery hums at frequencies just below hearing, felt in the teeth and sternum rather than heard.

This is a place that has always been here, even before it was built. A city that grew like mineral deposits in the dark heat of the earth's fever dreams, where the only truth is that truth itself is negotiable, and the only constant is the slow, wet pulse of a reality learning to breathe in different ways.
