The steam cavern breathes slowly, perpetually. 

Warm moisture beads on every surface of the low-ceilinged chamber, a space no larger than a village square carved from dark volcanic glass. The rock has been worn smooth by centuries of condensation—water that rises from hidden thermal springs below, meets the curved ceiling, and weeps back down in endless cycles. The obsidian walls glisten black and wet, their surface broken only where primitive chisel marks show someone once tried to widen certain passages, to carve shallow collection basins, to shape this natural sauna into something barely more habitable.

The space is packed with steam-smoothed formations—bulbous pillars where mineral deposits have built up drip by drip, creating a forest of slick columns that crowd the chamber. Between them, the floor undulates in frozen ripples of accumulated minerals, white and gray against the black glass, everything coated in a film of warm water. You must turn sideways to pass between the formations; there is no straight path through this dense maze of wet stone.

In the perpetual twilight, fed only by the faintest phosphorescence in the mineral deposits, you can make out crude stone bowls placed beneath the most reliable drips. A few worn grinding stones sit abandoned on natural shelves. Someone lived here once, or used this place, collecting the pure distilled water, perhaps conducting some ritual in the warm damp. But they left only these simplest tools, now themselves becoming part of the geology as minerals slowly engulf them.

The only sound is water—not flowing but gathering, releasing, gathering again. A drop forms, holds, falls. Another. The rhythm never changes because nothing here changes; the temperature holds steady, the steam rises and falls at the same rate it has for a thousand years. Even the rare blind crawling things that navigate the wettest corners move so slowly they seem like additions to the stone, pale and translucent, more mineral than animal.

The air tastes of iron and salt. It fills your lungs heavy and wet, like breathing inside a mouth. This is a pocket of deep time, a warm womb in the rock where the same moment repeats endlessly, where someone's ancient water bowls still fill and overflow and fill again, where the distinction between built and natural has been erased by the patient work of condensation and geological time.
