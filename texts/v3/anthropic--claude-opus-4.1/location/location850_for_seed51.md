The membrane between water and air has forgotten its purpose here.

Droplets the size of fists drift upward through the dim atmosphere, their surfaces trembling with equations that shouldn't balance. They rise from the sodden ground—a carpet of burgundy moss that extends for kilometers, each strand as thick as a thumb, all of them weeping a viscous fluid that tastes of copper and ozone. The moss grows in terraces that were carved by no river, shaped by no tool, yet worn smooth by millennia of something's passage.

Sound rolls across this expanse in visible waves. Not metaphorically visible—the air itself buckles and pleats, creating ridges of compressed moisture that race outward from wherever the moss is feeding. The feeding makes a sound like cathedral bells played backward underwater, a deep thrumming that you feel in your molars before you hear it. When these sound-waves collide, they spawn brief columns of inverted rain, pillars of water that fall upward for exactly seventeen seconds before dispersing into fog.

The fog never fully clears. It hangs at chest height, dense enough that you could write your name in it and watch the letters hold their shape for minutes. Through this perpetual mist, you glimpse the lattice—a structure that both is and isn't there, depending on the angle of observation. It appears to be made of crystallized probability, each strut existing in multiple states simultaneously. The lattice extends up from the moss at irregular intervals, creating a forest of mathematical impossibilities that hum with the frequency of thought itself.

Where the lattice touches the moss, time pools. You can see it collecting in shallow depressions—yesterday's rain still falling, tomorrow's growth already decaying, the present moment stretched thin as silk. Small creatures should live here, but instead there are only their possibilities: the suggestion of movement in your peripheral vision, tracks that appear mid-stride, the echo of calls from throats that never formed.

The vegetation clusters around these time-pools, growing in spirals that follow non-Euclidean curves. Ferns unfurl in five dimensions, their fronds existing in places your eyes can't quite track. Fungal blooms the color of static electricity spread across the moss, pulsing with bioluminescence that casts shadows in directions that shouldn't exist. The plants photosynthesize darkness, drawing sustenance from the spaces between photons.

Above, the sky has given up pretending to be sky. It's a ceiling of liquid mercury held in place by surface tension alone, occasionally releasing drops of itself that fall with the weight of collapsed stars. These drops never reach the ground. The lattice catches them, absorbs them, transforms them into that same singing that reverberates through the moss. The sound builds and builds until reality develops stress fractures—thin lines in the air where you can see through to somewhere else, somewhere where the rules are different, where water remembers how to be ice and the moss grows downward into theoretical soil.

The whole landscape pulses with a metabolism that operates outside conventional physics. Each breath you take tastes of iron and probability. Each step forward might also be a step backward, depending on which version of now you're experiencing. The moss continues weeping its copper tears, the lattice continues its impossible geometry, and somewhere in the fog—always just out of sight—something vast is breathing in frequencies that make the water forget which way is up.

This is a place where nature has evolved past the need for atoms, where ecology runs on pure information, where the boundary between "is" and "might be" has worn thin from eons of gentle, persistent erosion.
