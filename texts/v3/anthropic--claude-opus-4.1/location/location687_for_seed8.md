The saltpan stretches wide beneath a hazy, pearl-white sky. This is an ancient place where the last waters of a primordial sea still seep and weep through the earth, refusing to fully surrender even after geological ages of recession.

The ground is a mosaic of dried mineral crusts and shallow pools, each no deeper than an ankle. These scattered waters are warm to the touch, heated by the sun-baked earth beneath them and holding that warmth through the humid nights. The pools connect through a network of barely-visible channels that snake between elevated patches of crystallized ground — white and grey polygons of salt-earth that crack and re-form with infinite patience as the moisture below pushes upward, evaporates, and leaves its mineral ghosts behind.

Vegetation clings to the margins of this expanse in isolated clusters. Tough, succulent plants with waxy blue-green leaves grow in loose colonies where the salt concentration drops just enough to permit life. These sparse botanical islands — some only a single twisted shrub, others a small gathering of reed-like grasses — create the only vertical elements in an otherwise horizontal world. Their roots run deep and lateral, following underground veins of fresher water that percolate through layers of ancient sediment.

The fauna here moves in abundance despite the sparse shelter. Clouds of brine flies rise and settle in waves across the shallow waters, their collective movement creating a constant, droning hum that fills the air. Long-legged birds — hundreds of them — wade through the tepid pools on stick-thin legs, their beaks probing the silty bottom for invertebrates. They call to each other in harsh, carrying cries that echo across the flat expanse. Small crustaceans swarm in the warmest pools, visible as orange-pink clouds beneath the surface.

The landscape bears the weight of deep time. Every ridge, every depression has been worn down to its essential form. The few rock outcroppings that punctuate the saltpan are smooth, rounded humps of darker stone, polished by millions of years of wind and mineral-laden moisture. They weep constantly — condensation from the humid air collecting on their cooler surfaces and running down in thin streams that leave white mineral traces like frozen tears.

The air itself is thick, almost chewy with moisture and salt. It carries the egg-smell of sulfur from deeper springs that occasionally bubble up through the mud. Sound travels strangely here — the bird calls, the insect drone, the occasional splash of something larger moving through the shallows all seem both near and far simultaneously, the flat acoustics playing tricks with distance.

Changes happen here, but slowly. The pools migrate over decades, following imperceptible tilts in the underlying geology. The salt polygons grow and shrink with the seasons. Sometimes, after the rare but heavy rains, the entire plain becomes a shallow mirror for weeks, reflecting the sky so perfectly that birds lose themselves between two heavens. But these are patient transformations, and mostly the saltpan exists in a state of dynamic equilibrium — wet and dry, living and mineral, ancient and somehow still becoming.

At the edges of vision, the salt plain gradually rises into low, eroded hills — just suggestions of elevation, really, worn down to gentle swells. These mark the boundaries of this in-between place, this neither-sea-nor-land that has found its own way of being. The horizon shimmers with heat mirages even in the early morning, making the edges of the world seem uncertain, as if this pocket of primordial landscape exists slightly outside of fixed geography, a place that has been ending for eons but never quite completes its conclusion.
