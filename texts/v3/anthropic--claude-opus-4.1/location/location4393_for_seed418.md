## The Drowning Cistern

Deep beneath the weight of an ancient city, where foundation stones have compressed into something halfway between architecture and geology, there exists a forgotten reservoir. The entrance is merely a gap where settling has pulled two massive blocks apart — a fissure barely wide enough for a body to squeeze through, weeping perpetually with condensation that tastes of minerals and time.

Inside, the space opens like a held breath. Perhaps thirty feet across, no more than eight feet high, the cistern presses down with the accumulated mass of millennia above. The original builders carved it from living bedrock when the stone was young, but now their careful edges have softened to suggestions. Water seeps constantly through the porous ceiling, not as drops but as a universal perspiration, coating every surface with a film that never quite gathers enough to fall.

The floor is submerged — six inches of blood-warm water that has nowhere to drain. It moves with the slowness of decades, circulating in patterns too gradual for human perception. The water is perfectly clear but appears dark, tinted by the black stone beneath and the absence of meaningful light. Only the faintest glow filters through hair-thin cracks above, just enough to distinguish shape from shadow.

What vegetation exists has evolved beyond recognition. Thin films of something between algae and lichen coat the lower walls in patterns like tidal marks, though no tide has ever reached here. These growths are sparse, patient things — each colony separated by spans of bare, sweating stone. They photosynthesize from the memory of light, from the few photons that wander down through the city's bones. Some patches are translucent, others bioluminescent in waves so faint they might be hallucination. When disturbed, they release spores that drift in the humid air like ash from burned paper.

But it is the fauna that commands this space. They cluster on the ceiling in breathing masses — creatures that were perhaps once bats, or insects, or something else entirely before the cistern changed them. Their bodies have elongated into something serpentine, with too many joints that bend in too many directions. They hang in dense colonies, pressed against each other for warmth in the constant tropical heat, their pale bodies translucent enough to show dark veins pulsing beneath.

They barely move. Their metabolism has slowed to near-standstill, breathing perhaps once per minute, hearts beating with the rhythm of seasons. But they are everywhere — covering perhaps two-thirds of the ceiling in living stalactites. Their waste has built up over centuries into smooth, organic pillars that rise from the water like melted candles. These structures are honeycombed with passages where smaller things live — creatures that might be their young or might be different species entirely, all of them pale and soft and wrong.

The sounds here are almost subliminal. The constant seep of water through stone creates a white noise so steady it becomes silence. Occasionally, one of the ceiling-dwellers shifts, producing a wet leather sound that carries too well in the enclosed space. Their breathing, when noticed, is unsettling — hundreds of tiny lungs expanding and contracting in slow, accidental synchrony. Sometimes there are clicks, or whistles pitched so low they're felt more than heard, as they echolocate in dreams.

The air is thick as soup, saturated beyond saturation. It clings to the lungs, makes breathing feel like drowning in reverse. The temperature never varies — flesh-warm, fever-warm, the exact heat of something alive. Mist forms and dissipates in patterns that suggest air currents too subtle to feel. Sometimes the mist takes shapes that seem intentional before dissolving back into formlessness.

The walls tell stories in their erosion. Water has found every weakness, carved channels that branch and merge like veins. In places, the stone has been worn so thin that the pressure of the city above has pushed through in bulges like blisters. Some of these have cracked, leaking not water but a thick, mineral paste that hardens into formations resembling bones or roots or reaching fingers.

Time moves differently here. The city above has rebuilt itself a dozen times, but the cistern remains, forgotten except by the water that feeds it and the things that have adapted to call it home. It is a pocket of deep time, where evolution has taken strange turns in isolation. The creatures here are no longer quite animals — they've become something between biology and architecture, living components of the cistern itself.

In the center of the space, barely visible beneath the dark water, lies a drain that no longer drains. It's clogged with centuries of accumulated life — not dead, but dormant, waiting with the patience of stone for conditions that may never come again. Sometimes bubbles rise from it, carrying scents that shouldn't exist — sweet decay, metallic flowers, the breath of things that were never quite alive.

This is a place that has finished becoming, settled into its final form like sediment in still water. Yet it persists, breathing slowly in the dark, a hidden organ in the body of the ancient city, performing some function that no one remembers but that continues nonetheless, eternal and forgotten and inexplicably, impossibly, alive.
