The Canopy Mills rise from the mountain's shoulder like a cluster of breathing organs, their brass-and-copper frameworks sweating in the perpetual warm mist. Each structure stands no taller than a bell tower, but they crowd together in the small plateau—industrial mushrooms sprouting from manufactured substrate, their surfaces gleaming wet with condensation that never fully evaporates.

This is not earth beneath them but a foundation of woven metal mesh, rusted orange and green with engineered patinas, layered upon itself until it forms a spongy, elevated floor. Through the hexagonal gaps, you can glimpse more levels below—pipes, ducts, collection channels—all dripping, all gathering the moisture that pervades everything here. The entire structure seems to be drinking the clouds that press against the mountainside.

Vegetation erupts from every joint and seam. Not planted but inevitable—glossy vines with leaves like dinner plates spiral up the support columns, their roots threading through the mesh floor to tap the condensation channels below. Moss cushions coat the valve wheels. Ferns unfurl from the gaps between boiler plates. The machinery has been colonized, transformed into vertical jungle. Steam vents become orchid nurseries. Overflow pipes sprout cascading gardens of air plants.

The mills themselves are alive with slow, purposeful movement. Turbines turn lazily in the thick air, their blades filmed with moisture and pollen. Pistons rise and fall with a breathing rhythm, compressing something—perhaps the very humidity itself—into tanks that sweat like cold glass in summer. Everything is always wet here: the railings slick, the walkways treacherous with algae, droplets forever forming and falling from every surface.

The sound is a layered whisper—the soft hiss of releasing pressure, the musical plink of drops hitting metal at different depths, the occasional groan of expanding pipes. Beneath it all, a low mechanical heartbeat from somewhere in the structure's depths, felt more than heard.

Water finds its way everywhere. It sheets down the curved surfaces of the storage tanks, pools in the intentional catches built into the flooring, runs in engineered rills along the edges of walkways. The builders understood they were constructing in a cloud forest, designing their industrial complex to be a watershed, a machine for moving water as much as processing it.

The few creatures here are nearly invisible—a particular species of tree frog, translucent and no bigger than a thumb joint, that lives exclusively in the collected water of valve housings. Sometimes a shape moves through the mist between the towers—something larger, investigating the newest growths, maintaining the endless balance between the mechanical and botanical systems.

Strange bioluminescent fungi have colonized the darker spaces beneath the mesh floors, their pale green glow visible through the gaps when the mountain fog grows thick enough to dim the daylight. They pulse in slow waves, perhaps in response to the pressure changes in the pipes they grow upon, creating a secondary circulation—a visual echo of the water moving through the system.

The entire complex seems caught in a moment of becoming. Some towers are still skeletal, their frameworks incomplete, already being claimed by eager vines before the metal skin can be attached. Others seem to be dissolving back into the vegetation, their purposes fulfilled or forgotten, their pipes now serving as trellises, their tanks as cisterns for the epiphytes that crowd their upper surfaces.

At the edges of the small plateau, where the manufactured floor meets the raw mountain stone, you can see the beginning of expansion—new meshwork being laid, extending the substrate outward into the mist. The Mills are growing, but slowly, organically, like the plants that infest them. It's impossible to say whether the machines are winning or losing their battle with the vegetation. Perhaps it was never a battle at all, but a collaboration nobody remembers designing.

The warm air carries scents of rust and chlorophyll, motor oil and nectar, ozone and earth. During the brightest part of the day, when the sun burns through the mountain mist, the entire complex steams, releasing clouds of vapor that rise and join the natural clouds above. At these moments, the boundaries dissolve—between built and grown, between machine and ecosystem, between the mountain's water cycle and the industrial process that has inserted itself into it, becoming indistinguishable from it, essential to it.
