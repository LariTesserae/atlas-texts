The ice shelf extends beyond sight in all directions, a frozen ocean suspended two kilometers above the true seabed. This is not water that froze in place but a catastrophe of physics — an entire ocean layer that crystallized instantly when the industrial extractors below miscalculated, pulling too much heat too quickly from the planetary core. The ice hangs impossibly in its original position, held by the same gravitational inversion that killed its creators.

Above the ice ceiling, liquid water continues for another forty kilometers upward. The pressure should crush everything, but doesn't — another artifact of the broken physics. Light filters down from somewhere far above, arriving clean and even despite the distance, illuminating vast emptiness. Every hundred kilometers or so, a factory tower pierces up through the ice from below, its apex barely breaking the frozen surface. These industrial spires still run on automated cycles, their mechanical hearts beating out rhythms that travel for thousands of kilometers through the liquid medium. The sound is everything here — a constant industrial thunder that has no walls to echo from, only distance to swallow it.

The creatures arrived after the freeze. They pour through the ice in living rivers, emerging from microscopic cracks that weren't there moments before. Each organism is no larger than a grain of sand, but they move in colonies of trillions, flowing upward in braided streams before dispersing into the empty water above. They reassemble elsewhere, forming temporary cities of flesh that pulse with bioluminescent signals before dissolving again. They've learned to read the factory rhythms, gathering in precise formations when certain harmonic frequencies align. 

The extraction platforms themselves are simple brutalism rendered in ceramic and tungsten — built to withstand pressures that no longer exist. Their interiors remain dry despite being submerged, held in pockets of air that won't mix with the surrounding water. The machinery inside continues its pointless work: separating minerals that were exhausted centuries ago, processing nothing into nothing with perfect efficiency. Occasionally, a platform's lights flicker in sequence with others thousands of kilometers away, suggesting some remnant communication network still trying to coordinate across the dead industrial landscape.

The cold here isn't severe enough to freeze the liquid water above the ice shelf, but it's persistent, seeping from the frozen ocean below. The temperature gradient should cause currents, but the water remains perfectly still except where the creature-rivers disturb it. Time moves strangely — the factories cycle through their programs, the creatures flow and gather and disperse, but nothing truly changes. The broken physics have locked this place in a state of perpetual present, where motion occurs but progress doesn't.

The sound builds in layers: the deep industrial drum of the factories, the high whistle of water moving through spaces it shouldn't exist in, the chittering static of billions of micro-organisms communicating through pressure waves. It's loud enough to feel in your bones, if bones could exist here unprotected. The acoustic environment is so dense it has texture — sound becomes almost solid, another medium to move through.

Far above, the water simply ends. Not at a surface but at a boundary where the inverted gravity gives up. Beyond that threshold, the liquid hangs in droplets the size of cities, orbiting slowly around nothing, catching and splitting the sourceless light into spectra that suggest colors this world has forgotten how to display.
