The canal quarter presses against itself like a mouth full of too many teeth.

Water seeps everywhere here — not in great torrents or clear streams, but in the persistent weeping that comes when stone and timber are packed too tightly to breathe. The buildings lean into each other across narrow waterways, their foundations sunk into what was once solid ground but has become something between earth and marsh. Wooden walkways cross and recross the channels at irregular heights, some barely a handspan above the dark water, others stretching between third-story doorways. The planks are soft with perpetual damp, green-black with the kind of moss that never quite dries.

This is new construction built over older mistakes. You can see it in the sharp edges of recently-split stone lintels, the fresh tool marks on timber posts that already show the first blooming of rot. The settlement grew too quickly here, driven by some necessity to cluster — perhaps the ground beyond these few blocks is worse, or perhaps there's something about this spot that draws gathering despite its unsuitability. The builders work with bronze tools and sealed-pitch methods, raising structures that will last a generation at most before sinking further into the substrate.

Between the buildings — and there is almost no between, only the thinnest slivers of space — peculiar gardens grow. Not gardens exactly, but vertical territories claimed by the sparse vegetation that thrives in permanent shadow and dripping water. Pale fungi ladder up the walls in careful cultivation, tended by residents who harvest them with curved knives from windows and doorways. Thin vines of something that might once have been edible beans have evolved to need almost no light, their leaves reduced to scales, their roots threading through the mortar between stones.

The air tastes of mineral water and slow decay. Sound travels strangely here — muffled by the density of structures but carried clearly along the water channels. You hear the scratch of a bronze saw several streets away threading through the walls, the splash of someone emptying a bucket, the creak of buildings settling against each other. At dawn and dusk, when the light comes in sideways through the narrow gaps between rooflines, the whole quarter fills with the sound of dripping as condensation runs off every surface.

Small fish navigate the channels — silver-pale things no longer than a finger, moving in darting schools through water too murky to reveal its depth. Once there were more animals here; you can see the remnants of coops built into upper stories, the worn grooves where larger beasts were tied to posts now below the waterline. Now there are only the fish, the occasional water rat, and the chittering things that live in the walls — some hybrid of crab and insect that feeds on the fungi and has learned to tap messages through the wood at night.

The residents have adapted their primitive metalworking to this wet world. Bronze doesn't rust like iron would, but it still turns green-sick in the constant damp. They've learned to alloy it differently here, adding something — tin, perhaps, or stranger metals pulled from the seeping ground — that makes it resist the water better but gives it an oily, rainbow sheen. Their tools are specialized: long-handled items for reaching across channels, hooks and poles for managing life without solid ground, intricate drain-chains and gutter systems hammered from thin sheets.

The packed density creates its own weather. Water evaporates from the channels, condenses on the underside of bridges and walkways, drips back down in an endless cycle. Some corners never fully dry. Some doorways weep continuously from the pressure of moisture trapped in the walls. The newest buildings show attempts to work with this rather than against it — channels carved into doorframes to direct the flow, floors that slope toward drainage points, windows placed to create air currents that might, on rare dry days, pull some of the dampness out.

At the quarter's heart sits a structure that might be a temple or might be a mistake — a building whose foundation cracked while settling, opening a gap that leads down to older foundations, and below those to something else. The water that pools there is different from the canal water. It's clearer but wrong, refracting light in ways that suggest different properties. The residents have built around it rather than over it, leaving a small courtyard-like space, one of the few open areas in the quarter. They've hung bronze mirrors on poles around its edges, though whether to reflect something away or concentrate something inward is unclear.

This is a place of constant small movements — the shift of buildings against each other, the rise and fall of water levels, the slow growth of the fungi gardens, the endless work of residents patching, draining, reinforcing, adapting. It's young geology inhabited too soon, urban ambition pressed into a space that wants to be marsh, primitive technology stretched to meet impossible demands. The sound of dripping water is so constant it becomes silence, broken only by the splash of daily life navigating its wet, compressed existence.
