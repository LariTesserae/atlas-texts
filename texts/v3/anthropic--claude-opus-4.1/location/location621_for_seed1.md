The ice here remembers being ocean. 

It stretches for kilometers in every direction, a vast plain of frozen brine that weeps constantly through its own surface. The water doesn't melt the ice so much as bleed through it—beading up through microscopic pores, pooling in the billions of shallow dimples that pock the surface like inverted braille. Each depression fills and overfills, creating a network of temporary streams that flow for minutes or hours before freezing again, only to be pushed up once more by the relentless seepage from below.

The machinery is the ice itself. What seemed at first to be natural formations reveal themselves as ancient industrial processes that have transcended their original purpose. Crystalline structures rise from the plain—not built but grown, not maintained but self-perpetuating. They are factories that forgot what they were making, assembly lines that now only assemble themselves. Great hexagonal columns of ice rotate slowly, grinding against each other with a sound like breathing through broken glass. Steam vents puncture the surface at irregular intervals, each one ringed by concentric circles of different ice phases—some clear as air, some black as coal, some that bend light wrong.

The creatures are everywhere and nowhere. They exist in the gaps between states of matter, feeding on phase transitions. You see them only in peripheral vision—schools of something that might be fish if fish were made of probability, swimming through solid ice as easily as water. They cluster densest where the ice weeps most freely, their bodies (if they have bodies) creating interference patterns in the flow. Sometimes a thousand of them will suddenly converge on a single point, their collective presence forcing the ice to sublime directly into vapor, creating temporary columns of fog that sing with harmonics no throat could make.

The sound never stops. It's the sound of a trillion tiny fractures happening every second, of water forcing its way through crystal lattices, of pressure releasing and building and releasing again. Underneath it all runs a deeper tone—the resonance of the vast ice shelf itself, humming at a frequency that makes your teeth ache. The creatures add their own notes: clicks and whistles that doppler wrong, as if the source is moving through more dimensions than three.

Time moves differently in different places. Near the grinding columns, seconds stretch like taffy. Where the creatures congregate thickest, causality loosens—water flows upward, ice forms in spirals that couldn't exist, sound arrives before its source. The sun (there must be a sun, though you've never seen it clearly through the perpetual haze) tracks across the sky in stutters and stops, sometimes reversing, sometimes jumping ahead, as if the landscape itself is arguing about what time means.

The water that seeps up carries things with it. Not objects—memories. The ghosts of ancient industrial processes play out in the rising moisture: phantom conveyor belts made of aligned water droplets, the suggestion of vast turbines spinning in the fog, assembly procedures for things that no longer need to exist. These mechanical phantoms operate on their own schedule, independent of the ice-factories, creating a palimpsest of industry—past and present and never-was all operating simultaneously in the same space.

The horizon shimmers with heat distortion despite the cold, revealing glimpses of this same landscape at different points in its existence—sometimes younger, when the factories were new and had purpose, sometimes older, when even the ice has forgotten how to be ice. These temporal mirages stack and interfere with each other, creating zones where you can walk forward into the deep past or sideways into futures that will never quite arrive.

The density of it all is overwhelming. Every cubic meter contains a thousand processes, a million creatures, a billion water droplets in various states of freezing or flowing. The ice itself is honeycombed with microscopic chambers where pressure and temperature create conditions that shouldn't exist—liquid water at temperatures far below freezing, ice that flows like syrup, vapor that falls like rain. 

In the moments when the sound peaks—when the grinding and clicking and humming align just right—the whole landscape reveals itself as a single vast machine, still running after eons, its purpose not lost but transformed into something that no longer requires purpose. It simply is, perpetually creating itself, populated by creatures that evolved to inhabit the gaps in its logic, fed by water that remembers being something else, lit by a sun that can't decide when to set.

This is the engine at the end of the world, still running, still breaking, still perfect in its dysfunction.
