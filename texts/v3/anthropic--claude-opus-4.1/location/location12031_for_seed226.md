The Singing Terraces rise from clouds like a city still being born from the mountain's dreams.

Here, at this elevation where the air thins and cools, moisture clings to everything—not quite rain, not quite mist, but a perpetual wetness that beads on stone and wood alike. The settlement sprawls across natural rock shelves that emerged from recent geological upheaval, each terrace a different age. The newest ones still groan and settle, their surfaces rough with sharp edges that haven't yet learned the language of weather. Water weeps constantly from cracks in these infant cliffs, creating thousands of temporary waterfalls that appear and vanish with the shifting of stone.

Between these vertical planes of dripping rock, the builders have woven their primitive city. Wooden walkways span the voids—some as wide as canyons, most merely generous gaps where the mountain hasn't quite decided its final form. The construction is all timber and rope, bamboo and woven reed, materials that can flex when the stone shifts its weight. Buildings perch on the stable patches like careful birds, their foundations adjustable, their walls deliberately incomplete to let the constant moisture pass through.

The vegetation here is aggressive in its lushness, as if racing against the stone's uncertainty. Moss carpets every horizontal surface within hours of its creation. Vines cascade from the upper terraces in green curtains so thick they've become load-bearing walls for some structures. Giant ferns unfurl from crevices, their fronds creating natural roofs that the inhabitants have learned to cultivate rather than clear. The plants grow with unusual speed here—you can watch them claim new territory when the rocks shift and create fresh soil pockets.

The emptiness is profound despite the settlement's presence. Buildings cluster along the terrace edges but vast spaces yawn between them—aerial voids where clouds drift through what should be neighborhoods. A single rope bridge might span a hundred meters of nothing to reach the next inhabited shelf. The architecture acknowledges this emptiness, building small and tight, leaving room for the absence that dominates the space.

The sound is overwhelming but not harsh—water falling at different heights creates a layered symphony that never stops. The newer waterfalls boom and crash, while the older ones have worn smooth channels that sing higher notes. The wooden structures creak and sigh. Stone grinds against stone in bass notes felt more than heard. The inhabitants have developed a sign language for detailed communication, though they shout cheerfully across the voids for simple messages, their voices joining the water's constant chorus.

Few animals dare these unstable heights, though occasionally you'll spot a sure-footed climber—something between goat and spider—picking its way across the vertical gardens. Birds are rare; the air currents are too unpredictable where hot breath from the earth's recent ruptures meets the cool mountain atmosphere.

The strangeness reveals itself slowly. The terraces aren't random—they're growing in a spiral pattern, each new shelf emerging slightly higher and turned a few degrees from the last, as if the mountain is constructing a vast stairway. The water that weeps from the rocks is sweet, mineralized, almost syrupy. It leaves crystalline deposits that the inhabitants harvest with wooden scrapers, a primitive technology for gathering what might be the mountain's own circulation system. Some say the settlement isn't built on the mountain but within something still becoming itself, and they're merely the first witnesses to its slow birth.

Time moves strangely here. The geological shifts that should take millennia happen over seasons. The settlement adapts with patient urgency—nothing is permanent, everything is renewable, and the builders work with the assumption that their grandchildren will inhabit an entirely different architecture of stone, though the wooden bones of the city might remain. They build for change, not permanence, in this vertical marsh where the mountain weeps itself into being.
