The Furnace Garden breathes inside its crystalline lung.

You discover it by accident — a thumb-sized crack in the basalt cliff face that exhales warm air tasting of copper and chlorophyll. Squeezing through requires abandoning most of your equipment, compressing your body through twenty meters of volcanic tube that narrows and widens like a throat. The passage ends abruptly. You tumble into amber light.

The chamber spans perhaps forty meters at its widest point, though its geometry defies simple measurement. The walls are fresh obsidian, still bearing the ripples and flow patterns of their recent formation — days old, maybe hours. They haven't had time to fracture or weather. The volcanic glass catches and multiplies the light from above, where a membrane of fused silicon stretches across what might have once been an opening to the sky. This natural greenhouse ceiling glows with trapped solar radiation, pulsing with a slow arterial rhythm that takes minutes to complete each cycle.

The vegetation shouldn't exist. In this waterless cavity, sealed from rain and rivers, lush growth carpets every surface that isn't vertical glass. But these aren't plants as biology knows them. They photosynthesize through direct mineral exchange, their root systems threading into the volcanic substrate like circuitry, extracting silicon, sulfur, iron. Their leaves — if that's the right word — are crystalline lattices that shimmer between states of matter, sometimes appearing as solid jade-colored sheets, sometimes as gaseous clouds that drift and reconvene. They grow in scattered clusters with wide spaces between them, each colony claiming its own thermal vent in the floor where superheated gases whisper up from the planet's depths.

The industrial presence reveals itself slowly, camouflaged by its integration with the pseudo-botanical growth. What first appear to be metallic vines are actually pipes, hair-thin and numerous, running beneath the translucent ground like veins. They pulse with something that isn't quite liquid — more like organized heat, packaged entropy being shuttled from the deep vents to collection nodes that bloom like geometric flowers among the crystal foliage. These collectors are perhaps three centimeters across, perfect dodecahedrons that rotate slowly on invisible axes, harvesting energy through principles that make the air around them bend wrong.

The machinery has no maker, no obvious purpose. It might be the landscape's own dream of technology, manifested through the same impossible chemistry that allows forests to flourish without water. Or perhaps the distinction between built and grown has no meaning here. The pipes seem to feed the plants. The plants seem to power the pipes. Both are still becoming, still settling into their forms like cooling metal finding its crystal structure.

Time moves strangely. You watch one of the plant-clusters and see it hasn't grown, hasn't moved, hasn't changed. You look away, tend to your equipment, look back — it has relocated three meters, its roots now threading through entirely different rock. The industrial components migrate too, their configurations shifting between observations like puzzle pieces rearranging themselves when no one's watching. The only constant is the slow thermal pulse of the ceiling membrane, breathing light in and out, in and out.

The silence here is thick, punctuated only by occasional crystalline chimes when the pseudo-leaves brush against each other in currents of air that shouldn't exist in this sealed space. Sometimes there's a deeper sound — a groan from the fresh rock as it continues to cool and contract, still deciding what shape it wants to hold.

The heat is dry and mineral, like standing inside a kiln. It rises from below in visible columns that twist DNA-like through the sparse space. Where these thermals meet the silicon ceiling, they create interference patterns that look almost like writing, almost like circuit diagrams, almost like maps of river systems that will never flow here. The patterns last just long enough to suggest meaning before dissolving.

You realize the space itself is only partially formed — not damaged or incomplete, but still in the process of becoming real. The corners where walls meet don't quite resolve. Distances remain negotiable; the far wall is thirty meters away, then twenty, then forty, depending on how you approach it. The industrial elements are manufacturing the space as much as operating within it, their geometric collectors determining which angles can exist, their pipe networks establishing where "here" ends and "there" begins.

This is a prototype reality, a test chamber where physics experiments with alternatives to water-based life, where geology dreams of becoming technology, where lush ecosystems prove they never needed to be possible in order to exist. The Furnace Garden is less a place than an ongoing argument between different versions of what a place could be, and it hasn't yet decided who wins.
