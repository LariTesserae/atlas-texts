The Plateau of Nascent Cities spreads across forty thousand square kilometers of raw, weeping stone. Here, at the edge of the thermosphere where air thins to mathematical abstraction, the first urban organisms are teaching themselves to exist.

The rock itself is young—catastrophically young. It emerged only centuries ago when tectonic convulsions thrust this shield of granite and obsidian into the high atmosphere. The stone has not yet learned how to be stone. It weeps constantly, a cold perspiration that beads and streams across surfaces that still remember being molten. These waters gather in ten thousand temporary pools, never staying long enough to carve proper channels, always shifting as the rock continues its slow crystallization into final form.

The cities grow like colonies of mechanical coral. They began as seed-structures no larger than pinheads—self-replicating architectural embryos scattered by the Commonwealth's terraforming winds. Now they've reached the size of districts, though spread so sparsely across the vastness that you could walk for days between them. Each city-organism builds itself from the substrate up, transmuting wet rock into programmable matter through processes of molecular gastronomy that operate at the edge of physics.

The sparse vegetation exists only where the cities permit it—engineered mosses that can photosynthesize in the thin air, their chlorophyll modified to work with the peculiar light that filters through the stratosphere. These moss meadows spread in precise geometric patterns around each urban node, dark blue-green against the gleaming black rock, breathing out just enough oxygen to create microclimates of almost-breathable air.

The fauna thrives in paradox. Vast flocks of atmospheric jellies drift between the city-nodes, their bodies translucent membranes filled with harvested hydrogen, tentacles trailing for kilometers through the thin air. They feed on the electromagnetic emanations of the growing cities, clustering wherever construction is most active. Below them, in the wet margins where stone meets moss, colonies of silicon beetles process raw rock into construction paste, their populations booming as the cities' appetites grow.

Engineered ravens, their lungs replaced with pressure exchangers, hop between the pools carrying messages and small repair components in their talons. They've evolved a language of ultrasonic clicks that the cities can hear and respond to with harmonics in their growing walls.

The cold is absolute but irrelevant. The city-organisms generate their own heat through controlled fusion at the molecular level, creating zones of warmth that pulse outward like slow heartbeats. Steam rises constantly where this heat meets the weeping stone, creating localized weather systems—tiny hurricanes that spin for minutes before dissipating, ice-fog that forms and vanishes in waves.

The infrastructure builds itself in real-time. Watch for an hour and you'll see transit tubes extend like reaching fingers across kilometers of wet rock, their surfaces rippling as they optimize their molecular structure for the specific mineral content of the stone they're crossing. Communication towers assemble from the ground up, their fractal antennae adjusting to frequencies that barely exist in normal space. Power grids web outward in patterns that mirror neural networks, learning the landscape as they expand.

In the quiet—and it is quiet here, sound muffled by the thin atmosphere and absorbed by the wet stone—you can hear the cities thinking. It's a subsonic hum, felt more than heard, the processing power of a billion billion nanomachines calculating the optimal shape for existence in this harsh nursery.

The settlements follow no human urban planning. Streets spiral in non-Euclidean curves that make perfect sense to the quantum processors governing growth. Buildings bud from other buildings, sharing walls that exist in multiple states simultaneously. Public spaces are caves of crystallized air, their boundaries defined by magnetic fields that hold warmth and thickness against the hostile altitude.

Sometimes, rarely, structures fail. The technology is so advanced it occasionally forgets how to be technology, reverting to pure mathematics that can't quite sustain itself in physical space. These failures leave behind geometric ghosts—patches where the laws of physics remain slightly edited, where water flows upward or light bends wrong.

The scale defies comprehension from ground level. Stand in one of the city-nodes and you see only the immediate district, maybe a kilometer of actively growing urbanization. But rise high enough—carried by the jellyfish fauna or lifted by the cities' gravity-neutral transit pods—and the pattern becomes clear: a network of glowing nodes scattered across the dark, wet plateau like neurons in a vast brain that's just beginning to form its first thoughts.

This is not a finished place. It's a landscape in the process of deciding what it wants to become, where advanced technology and raw geology negotiate the terms of coexistence in the thin, cold air at the roof of the world. The cities will eventually merge, the fauna will adapt further, the stone will finish crystallizing. But for now, it remains in this state of ambitious becoming—wet, cold, sparse, and alive with the patient violence of creation.
