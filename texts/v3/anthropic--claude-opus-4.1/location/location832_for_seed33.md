The Hollowing stands three kilometers above what was once a sea floor, though no water has touched this place for longer than memory. The city—if city is still the right word—spreads across a vast plateau of crystallized salt and mineral deposits that shimmer with their own faint phosphorescence in the perpetual twilight. 

Buildings rise like enormous dried sponges, their surfaces pocked with millions of holes where something essential has been extracted or simply ceased to be. These structures appear grown rather than built—calcified remains of some vast colonial organism that learned to mimic architecture before forgetting what architecture was for. They stretch upward for hundreds of meters, tapering into delicate lattices that should not be able to support their own weight, yet do, according to rules that shift subtly from district to district.

The air here is thin and dry, pulling moisture from lungs and skin within minutes. It tastes of copper and ozone, though there is no metal here, no storms. The coolness is absolute—not the absence of warmth but its active negation, as if heat itself has been edited from the local physics. Breath does not fog. Ice does not form. Things simply become less, molecularly speaking, the longer they remain.

Streets—or the suggestions of streets—wind between the hollow towers in patterns that restructure themselves according to the observer's expectations. Walk expecting a grid, and perpendiculars will assert themselves until you look away. Follow curves, and spirals emerge, tightening toward centers that never quite arrive. The pavement, where it exists, is a single seamless sheet of something between glass and bone, worn smooth by no footsteps anyone remembers.

The few creatures that persist here are architectural in nature—living buttresses that migrate slowly between buildings, providing structural support where needed before moving on. They resemble enormous pill bugs carved from clouded quartz, their segments grinding against each other with sounds like distant bells. Smaller things—geometric mice, perhaps, or angular interpretations of spiders—occasionally flicker at the edges of vision, existing more as suggestions than substances.

The light comes from nowhere and everywhere, a gray luminescence that casts no shadows because shadows require a coherent relationship between light source and object, and such relationships have grown tentative here. Time moves in eddies and pools. A falling piece of debris might hang in the air for hours, then complete its descent in an instant. The wind, when it comes, carries the sound of conversations that haven't happened yet, words in languages that lost their meanings before they were spoken.

At the city's heart—or what residents once called its heart, back when there were residents who called things anything—stands the Inverse Cathedral. Its spires point downward into solid rock, its nave exists as negative space, its windows are absences that reveal more absence beyond. Prayer wheels turn inside its non-walls, powered by the differential between what is and what should be, their rotation generating the quiet hum that serves as the city's only constant sound.

The Hollowing is not abandoned. Abandonment requires a before and after, a presence that became absence. This place exists in continuous unbecoming, each moment slightly less than the last, asymptotically approaching a zero it will never quite reach. The city maintains itself through this reduction—streets stay clear because debris forgets to accumulate, buildings remain standing because collapse is too definite a state.

Water exists here only as an argument between molecules. Plants appear sometimes as two-dimensional shadows on walls, their growth patterns following non-Euclidean geometries that hurt to perceive directly. The rare rainfall evaporates before touching ground, leaving only the memory of moisture in the air.

In certain districts, the breakdown becomes more pronounced. Architecture begins to quote itself—a doorway might open onto the same doorway, smaller, which opens onto itself again, recursing into invisibility. Stairs lead up and down simultaneously. Rooms contain themselves. The few surviving street signs point to locations that exist only while being sought.

The city breathes, after a fashion. Twice each day—though days here are measured by consensus rather than any celestial movement—the hollow buildings resonate together, producing a chord that can be felt in the bones rather than heard. This is when the geometric mice emerge, when the living buttresses quicken their pace, when the rules relax enough for things to happen that wouldn't otherwise.

Those who study such places say the Hollowing represents a kind of urban enlightenment—a city that achieved such perfect self-knowledge that it began to transcend its own existence. Others claim it's a wound in reality that learned to organize itself. Both may be true. Neither may be true. Truth itself has grown negotiable here, subject to the same patient erosion that has worn away everything else.

The horizon shimmers with the ghosts of other possible cities, variations on the theme that flicker in and out of probability. Sometimes, rarely, they overlap with this one, and for a moment the Hollowing remembers what it was like to be whole. Then the moment passes, and the city continues its slow, dignified dissolution into the perfect emptiness it has always been approaching.
