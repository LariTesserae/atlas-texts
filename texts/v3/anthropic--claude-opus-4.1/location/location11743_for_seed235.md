The city grows from the mountain's bones like a vast coral, its terraces and towers carved from the living rock itself over unknowable ages. Here, at the edge of the world's ceiling, the air burns thick and wet, each breath a drowning in reverse. The altitude should freeze, but instead the atmosphere seethes with a fevered heat that makes no sense—as if gravity itself has turned traitor, pulling warmth upward to pool in these impossible heights.

Water weeps constantly from every surface. The stone sweats. Buildings cry mineral tears that streak their faces with deposits of salt and copper-green. The air is so saturated that distances blur, the furthest towers of the city dissolving into a shimmering haze where architecture becomes indistinguishable from the volcanic peaks that cradle this settlement. Moss and vines have conquered every vertical surface, hanging in curtains so thick they've become load-bearing walls themselves. Ancient trees burst through rooftops, their canopies merging into a second city overhead—a green metropolis of interwoven branches where orchids bloom larger than doors.

The sound never stops. It comes from everywhere and nowhere: a roaring like the ocean trapped inside a shell, magnified beyond reason. The city's stone resonates with it, every building a tuning fork struck by invisible hammers. Windows hum. Doorways moan. The vegetation rustles without wind, adding its whisper to the din until conversation becomes impossible, until thought itself must shout to be heard inside your own skull. Yet nothing moves. No visible force creates this cacophony. The leaves that shiver with sound hang perfectly still. The water that weeps from stone faces falls in straight lines, undisturbed by any breeze.

Streets—if they can be called streets—snake between structures that obey no architectural logic. A tower base might be narrower than its crown. Staircases climb external walls to doors that open onto solid stone. Bridges arc between buildings that lean away from each other, the spans somehow longer than the distance they cross. The geometry hurts to follow with your eyes, as if space has been folded and refolded until cardinal directions become suggestions rather than facts.

Through the green cascade of vegetation, through the mineral-sweating walls, you can glimpse the settlement's true scope. It spreads across the entire shoulder of the mountain, terraces descending and ascending simultaneously, a city that climbs both up and down from wherever you stand. In the steam-hazed distance, other peaks wear their own crown cities, connected by possibilities rather than paths—you could walk for days toward them and arrive, or stand still and find yourself there.

The plants that smother this place grow in impossible abundance. Fruit trees bear constantly, their branches breaking under the weight of produce that rots and regrows in the same moment. Flowering vines bloom in colors that shouldn't exist at this altitude—violent purples, electric oranges, blues that pulse with their own light. The vegetation doesn't just grow on the architecture; it's part of it. Living buttresses. Walls of woven roots. Ceilings of interconnected canopy that drip constant condensation like rain that never quite falls.

Time moves strangely here, or perhaps doesn't move at all. The water that weeps from stone never depletes. The fruit that rots regenerates instantly. The buildings, ancient and weathered, show no signs of continuing decay. They've reached some terminal state of erosion and stopped, like a photograph of ruins that can erode no further because they exist in only a single moment, stretched endlessly.

In the lower terraces, where the city grows denser, buildings merge into a single organism of stone and vegetation. Doorways open into other doorways. Windows look out from inside rooms that shouldn't share walls. The sound grows louder here, a physical pressure that makes the wet air feel solid. Gardens burst from every possible surface—rooftop, wall-face, growing from cracks in the streets themselves. Trees thrust through buildings at angles that suggest the structures were built around them, or perhaps through them, occupant and architecture existing in the same space without conflict.

No animals. No birds despite the fruit. No insects despite the flowers. The pollination happens through impossible means—perhaps the sound itself carries life from bloom to bloom, or the constant weeping water serves as a medium for vegetal reproduction. The absence of fauna makes the abundance of flora feel aggressive, predatory, as if the plants have won some ancient war and now claim everything.

Above it all, the peak vanishes into clouds that glow with their own heat, lit from within by something that is not quite fire, not quite sun. The city climbs toward this luminous obscurity, its highest reaches lost in brilliance that burns without burning, a light that casts no shadows because shadows, too, have abandoned the laws they once obeyed.
