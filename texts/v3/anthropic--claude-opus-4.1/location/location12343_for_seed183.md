The Singing Flats stretch between impossible mountains, their peaks inverting at certain angles of observation, folding back through themselves like paper origami that has learned to breathe. Here, at this extreme elevation where the air should be thin, it runs thick as syrup—supersaturated with moisture that refuses to behave as water should.

The ground is naked stone, scoured clean of anything that ever tried to root here. Obsidian flows frozen mid-cascade create terraces that step down, up, and sometimes *through* each other, their surfaces weeping constantly with condensation that runs upward as often as down. These tears of stone feed into pools that hover at knee-height, perfectly circular, their surfaces vertical planes that ripple with their own weather systems. Touch one and your hand passes through nothing; look away and look back, and it might be frozen solid, or filled with mercury-bright liquid that reflects skies from other seasons.

Throughout this mineral maze, the remnants: stone cairns that rebuild themselves when no one watches, each rock worn smooth by centuries of handling by things that no longer exist—or never did. Simple shelters of stacked slate lean against boulders that weren't there yesterday and won't be tomorrow. Iron tools, primitive hammers and wedges, lie scattered where they were dropped, their surfaces sweating rust that crawls across the stone in fractal patterns before evaporating into copper-colored mist.

The brightness here defies logic. With no sun visible through the perpetual pearl-gray moisture that clings to everything, light arrives from below, from within the rocks themselves—a glacial luminescence that throws shadows upward and makes the constant dripping water glow like chains of diamonds. Sometimes the light pulses, and when it does, the shadows of things that aren't there dance across the condensation-slick stone: the shadows of towers, of forests, of creatures with too many joints.

Sound builds and releases in waves. The dripping water creates a base rhythm—millions of drops striking stone, each impact ringing like a tuning fork. But the stones themselves sing, a deep thrumming that rises from the bedrock, punctuated by sharp cracks as temperature differentials split the air itself into sheets that shatter and reform. When the wind comes—and it always comes sideways here, never from above or below—it screams through natural pipes in the rock formations, creating harmonies that make your teeth ache and your vision blur.

Movement everywhere: rivulets of water racing uphill, mists that spiral in helixes, stones that imperceptibly migrate across the terraces, leaving wet trails that spell out words in languages that were never spoken. The abandoned structures shift when unobserved—a cairn that was three stones high becomes seven, becomes three again. The primitive rope bridges strung between outcroppings fray and reweave themselves, their knots evolving into increasingly complex patterns that shouldn't be possible with simple cord.

In certain spots, usually near the deepest pools, reality wears thin as wet paper. Stand too long in these places and you might see through to other versions of this same landscape—one where great cities of glass rise from these stones, one where the water runs red, one where everything is exactly the same except you're not in it. The transitions between these glimpses are marked by a sound like tearing metal and a taste of copper in the supersaturated air.

The wet stones remember everything and nothing. Handprints appear in the condensation, evaporate, reappear elsewhere. Crude pictographs etched into rock faces change their stories when the light shifts—hunters become the hunted, circles become spirals, human figures grow extra limbs or lose their heads. The tools left behind work on their own sometimes, the hammers tapping out rhythms that match no heartbeat that ever was, chisels carving symbols that hurt to perceive directly.

At the valley's heart, where multiple terraces converge in geometric impossibilities, stands a single structure more deliberate than the rest: a round tower built from stones that don't match any geology visible here. It has no door, no windows, just smooth wet walls that rise perhaps twenty feet before ending abruptly, as if the top portion exists in a direction the eye cannot follow. Water runs constantly down its surface, but the water comes from within the stones themselves, bleeding out through invisible pores. The sound here is loudest—not just the physical cacophony of dripping and wind and cracking stone, but something else, a vibration that registers in your bones as almost-words, almost-music, almost-screaming.

This is a place that has been broken and reformed so many times that breaking has become its natural state, where the wetness is not water but the landscape itself weeping at its own impossibility, where primitive tools wait patiently to build things that cannot exist, where the brightness illuminates nothing except the fact that seeing itself might be just another form of erosion.
