The Singing Geysers of the Drowned Plateau

You arrive at what was once a mountaintop, now barely a hundred feet above an endless expanse of warm, mineral-thick water that swallowed the world below. The plateau is no larger than a village square, its edges sharp and newly broken where chunks have recently calved into the sea. Steam rises in lazy columns from the water's surface, which maintains the temperature of blood.

The sound hits you first—a bone-deep thrumming that makes thought difficult. Every few seconds, another geyser births itself through the plateau's thin crust with a shriek that could shatter glass. These aren't ordinary geysers. They sing as they rise, each one a different pitch, creating accidental harmonies that shouldn't exist in nature. The water they expel doesn't fall back down properly. It hangs in the air too long, forming temporary sculptures of liquid that twist against physics before collapsing.

The rock here is young volcanic glass, black and sharp enough to draw blood, riddled with fresh holes where new geysers have punched through. Between eruptions, you can hear the water moving beneath the crust—not flowing, but crawling, as if it has intentions. The few hardy succulents that cling to the rock face grow in perfect spirals, their flesh translucent and pulsing with the rhythm of the water table below.

Scattered across this precarious ground are structures that might have been shelters once—or might be something the geysers are building. Pillars of mineral deposits that accumulated too quickly, in patterns too deliberate. One resembles a doorway. Another, a throne. They grow and dissolve with each eruption cycle, never quite the same twice.

The air itself is wrong here. You can see sound waves rippling through the dense steam. Sometimes the mist forms faces—not human ones, but expressions nonetheless. The water refuses to behave like water should. It climbs up surfaces when it should run down. It pools in mid-air. Small waterspouts dance across the plateau's surface with no wind to drive them, their movements too purposeful, too curious, as if investigating the few visitors brave enough to stand here.

A single fish—if it is a fish—occasionally breaks the surface of the surrounding sea. Its scales are mirrors that reflect things that aren't there. Or perhaps they reflect things that are there, but that you shouldn't be able to see. It never surfaces twice in the same spot, and its appearances coincide with moments when the geysers sing in perfect unison, which happens rarely but inevitably, as if the entire system is counting toward something.

The plateau shrinks daily. You can measure it by the fresh fracture lines, by the mineral deposits that mark where yesterday's edge used to be. This place is dying and being born simultaneously, each geyser eruption both creating and destroying, the water patient and inexorable as it reclaims what was once mountain, transforming solid earth into its own strange liquid empire.

The sun, when visible through the steam, looks too large and too close, as if the water in the air acts as a lens. But that's not quite right either—sometimes there are two suns, sometimes none, sometimes just the suggestion of light with no source at all. The shadows here fall upward as often as down.

This is a threshold place, where the world's rules grow soft and negotiable, where water remembers being stone and stone dreams of flowing. The deafening song of the geysers might be communication, or calculation, or simply the sound of reality trying to decide what it wants to be.
