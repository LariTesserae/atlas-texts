The cold water stretches endlessly in all directions, a manufactured ocean that has forgotten its makers. Beneath the surface, the landscape reveals itself as a drowned infinity of terraces—ancient agricultural steps carved from some primitive coral-concrete, now softened by millennia of submersion until their edges run together like melted wax.

The vegetation grows in thick mats along these underwater plateaus, a carpet of primitive kelp-analogues that never evolved to reach for sun. Instead, they spread laterally in dense sheets, their fronds heavy and leathery, colored the deep blue-black of old bruises. They photosynthesize from some faint chemical luminescence in the water itself—a barely perceptible glow that emanates from the substrate, too dim to pierce the darkness above but enough to sustain this sprawling aquatic prairie.

The terraces descend in irregular steps, some a hand's breadth, others dropping away into chasms twenty times a person's height. All were built, shaped by ancient hands using simple tools—you can still find the marks where stone chisels bit into the original material, though softened now like thumbprints in old soap. The builders worked this entire world, transforming every natural surface into these cultivation platforms. They must have worked for generations, possibly centuries, with nothing but muscle and primitive levers, reshaping the planet's crust into this vast agricultural machine.

Now the water fills it all. The flood came so long ago that the terraces have achieved a kind of geological peace with their submersion. The edges have eroded into gentle slopes. Sediment has collected in the corners, forming new micro-terrains. The vegetation has woven itself into the structure so thoroughly that root and substrate can no longer be distinguished—everything has become one continuous, living architecture.

The water itself is strange—denser than it should be, thick with dissolved minerals leached from the built substrate over eons. It moves slowly, almost viscously, in currents that take years to complete their circuits around the submerged continents. The pressure is wrong too; at these depths, which should be crushing, the water maintains a peculiar buoyancy, as if the entire ocean exists in a state of semi-suspension.

Sound travels poorly here. The vegetation absorbs vibrations, and the terraced geometry breaks up wave patterns. Occasionally, a deep groaning emanates from far below—the settling of some massive structural element, a buttress or foundation slab finally yielding another millimeter to time. These sounds take minutes to fully manifest, rising from subsonic frequencies into the barely audible range before fading back into the ambient quiet.

The built nature of this place extends beyond the visible terraces. Probe anywhere into the substrate and you find more construction—channels for irrigation, storage chambers now flooded and silted, networks of passages too small for any person but perhaps meant for water or air. The entire planet appears to be artificed down to its core, though built with such primitive methods that each stone was placed by hand, each channel carved with simple tools over lifetimes of labor.

In some places, the vegetation has grown so thick that it forms secondary floors—false bottoms where detritus collects and new ecosystems establish themselves. These bio-layers compress over time, becoming something like peat, adding new strata to the already complex vertical geography. You can push through them and find hollow spaces beneath, pockets where the old air still lingers, transformed now into something unbreathable but chemically fascinating.

The darkness is nearly absolute. Only that faint substrate glow and the occasional phosphorescent discharge from the vegetation's reproductive cycles provide any light. Eyes are useless here; navigation happens by current, by pressure, by the subtle taste of different water layers. Time moves differently in this darkness—without day or night, only the slow breathing of the tides and the patient growth of the vegetation mark duration.

Despite the vast scale—continents of these terraces extending beyond any possible survey—the space feels intimate, enclosed. The water presses close. The vegetation brushes against everything. The terraces create countless small worlds, each depression and plateau its own universe of slow accumulation and gradual transformation. It's a place that has found its final form, not through death but through a kind of patient metamorphosis, where the built environment and the forces of time have negotiated a permanent truce.

This is architecture become geology, agriculture become ecology, an entire constructed world that has aged into something almost but not quite natural—held in cold suspension, growing ever more itself with each passing century.
