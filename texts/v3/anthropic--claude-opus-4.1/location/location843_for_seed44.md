The district breathes through its iron lungs.

Steam rises perpetually from the canal network that threads between the root-buttressed factories, where mangrove-descended trees have been coaxed over generations into living industrial architecture. Their trunks, thick as silos, twist upward for three or four stories before spreading into canopies that merge seamlessly with corrugated metal roofing. The bark weeps constantly—a engineered trait that keeps the factory floors cool through evaporation while feeding the boilers with collected condensate.

Everything here is damp and green-black. Moss fills every joint between the bio-industrial hybridization, creeping across the riveted iron plates that reinforce the living walls, softening the harsh geometry of steam pipes with velvet curves. The air tastes of iron and chlorophyll, machine oil and sap. Water drips everywhere: from the underside of elevated rail lines that run on wooden tracks grown from modified cypress, from the leaves that form living gaskets around pressure valves, from the condensation that beads on every surface in the humid warmth.

The factories themselves process plant matter—the district feeds on its own flesh, harvesting the aggressive kudzu-like vines that attempt to overtake the machinery every season, pulping them, fermenting them, distilling them into the alcohols that fuel the generators. The sound is constant: the churning of mechanical roots that function as pumps, drawing water up from the shallow water table; the hiss of steam vents opening in programmed sequences; the deep groaning of tree-machines as their living frames flex under the weight of industry; the splashing in the canals where automated barges pole themselves along on telescoping mangrove prop roots.

At ground level, boardwalks of woven root systems create streets barely wide enough for two people to pass. The human residents—for there are apartments tucked into the upper reaches of the factory-trees—navigate by memory through the green twilight. Electrical cables, sheathed in living vine, provide power to the few lights needed in this photosynthetic city, their bulbs nestled in flowers that open and close on industrial schedules.

The whole district is perhaps three city blocks in footprint, but it rises and descends through multiple levels of walkways, production floors, and drainage sumps. It's a closed ecosystem that has found its balance—the plants feed the machines, the machines cultivate the plants, the water cycles through both biological and industrial processes, and the entire mass slowly, slowly grows outward at perhaps an inch per year, adding new rings to its trunks, new capacity to its boilers, new depth to its root systems.

In the morning, when the fog rolls in from the surrounding wetlands, the entire district becomes indistinct, its edges blurring into the marsh it emerged from. Only the rhythmic puffing of steam and the occasional groan of a heavy branch reveal that this is not simply another grove of ancient trees, but something that has learned to digest iron and exhale industry, one patient breath at a time.
