**The Singing Cistern**

Deep beneath the sprawling mud-brick city, where forgotten maintenance shafts pierce down through compacted earth and ancient construction, there exists a hollow no larger than a modest barn. The space breathes with impossible vegetation—thick curtains of rootless vines that have never known water, their surfaces like polished copper wire, drawing sustenance from the tremendous sound that floods this place.

The plants grow in spiraling colonies along the curved walls of what was once a cistern, long since drained and abandoned when the old aqueducts failed generations ago. Their leaves—if you can call them leaves—are paper-thin membranes of crystallized sap that vibrate constantly, creating harmonics that layer upon the primary sound: a bone-deep thrumming that rises from somewhere far below, perhaps from the city's forgotten foundations, perhaps from the earth itself. The noise is overwhelming, a physical presence that makes the air itself feel thick and sluggish.

The vegetation pulses with bioluminescent veins, casting everything in sharp, moving shadows despite the bright fungal colonies that coat the ceiling like inverted fields of tiny suns. These fungi, too, feed on sound—you can see them swelling and contracting with each wave of acoustic pressure, their light fluctuating in patterns that hurt to follow with the eyes.

The cistern's floor, what little of it remains visible between the hanging gardens of wire-vines, shows signs of primitive modification—rough-carved channels that once directed water now serve as resonance chambers, amplifying and shaping the sound into different frequencies. Crude metal tools lie scattered about, their surfaces polished smooth by constant vibration. Someone has been trying to tune this place, to shape its terrible music with hand-hammered bronze plates and carefully positioned stones.

The heat here is wrong—not the heat of depth or decay, but the waste heat of pure kinetic energy as every surface vibrates itself warm. The air shimmers not with moisture but with standing waves made visible by the spores and pollen that the sound-eating plants constantly shed. These particles dance in complex mandalas, gathering at the nodes where multiple sound waves cancel each other out, creating pockets of relative quiet where small, pale insects hover—the only fauna adapted to this acoustic greenhouse. They have no wings but swim through the air, riding the pressure waves like fish in an invisible current.

The walls themselves are deeply eroded, not by water but by sound—carved into organic, fluted columns and hollows that make the entire space a vast instrument. Every surface has been worn smooth and then textured again by decades or centuries of acoustic weathering. The original brick and mortar have taken on the quality of bone, porous and resonant.

Above, through gaps where the wire-vines haven't yet grown, you can glimpse the underside of the city—layers upon layers of basements and sub-basements, crude stonework and fired brick, the detritus of a primitive but persistent urban civilization that has no idea what thrums beneath their feet. Occasionally, debris falls through these gaps—pottery shards, worn tools, the occasional bone—only to be pulverized by the sound or slowly incorporated into the bizarre ecosystem.

The strangest thing is how the plants move with purpose, their copper tendrils reaching toward specific frequencies, recoiling from others. They seem to be writing something in their growth, encoding the sound into their very structure, creating a living record of this hidden chamber's endless song. Whether they're domesticated—cultivated by whoever maintains this space with those primitive tools—or whether they've evolved to farm the sound themselves remains unclear.

The sound never stops. It only modulates, rises and falls like breathing, like a heartbeat, like something vast and alive just beyond the range of understanding, using this tiny pocket space as a single resonating cell in an organ too large to perceive.
