The caldera breathes steam.

You stand on a narrow observation platform, one of seven that ring this volcanic throat like jewelry settings. The platform—riveted iron grating over a skeletal frame—trembles with the constant percussion of machinery below. Everything here is vertical: the basalt walls plunge three hundred meters to the work floor, where the heat makes the air shimmer and dance. The diameter spans perhaps two hundred meters across, intimate for such depth, creating an inverse tower open to the sky.

Water weeps continuously from the young rock walls, trickling through fissures that still remember their molten birth. The stone is sharp-edged, unweathered, black as wet ink. These seeping walls feed a thousand small waterfalls that never reach bottom—the heat transforms them to steam halfway down, creating a permanent mist that fills the caldera's lower third. The vapor rises, condenses against the cooler walls above, and begins its cycle again.

Vegetation clings wherever it can. Moss carpets every ledge in electric green. Ferns unfurl from cracks, their fronds reaching toward the light. Vines cascade down the walls in thick curtains, their roots somehow finding purchase in the volcanic glass. Strange orchids bloom in the humidity, their flowers grotesquely large, adapted to catch the industrial waste heat rising from below. The plants grow with tropical enthusiasm but wrong—leaves too thick, stems that spiral, flowers that bloom at night to avoid the noon sun that strikes directly down the shaft.

The industrial works below exist in glimpses through the steam. Brass boilers the size of houses. Networks of pipes that climb the walls like mechanical ivy, bleeding condensation. Turbines that spin endlessly, powered by the marriage of volcanic heat and falling water. The sound is enormous—a constant roar of steam vents, water hammers, grinding gears. It reverberates up the caldera walls, focusing and amplifying until conversation becomes impossible without shouting.

The platforms connect via catwalks—narrow bridges of mesh and cable that span the void at different heights. Some are original to the installation, riveted and solid. Others are repairs or additions, welded crudely, swaying slightly in the updrafts. Thick rubber-coated cables droop between junction boxes bolted directly into the basalt. Electric lights in wire cages mark the walkways, though most of the illumination comes from the sky above and the forge-glow below.

Small shelters perch on the wider platforms—corrugated metal sheds where engineers once monitored gauges, now mostly abandoned. Their windows are milky with mineral deposits from the constant mist. Inside, control panels rust beneath blankets of moss. Logbooks moulder in the humidity, their pages fused together. Only one or two stations remain staffed, their operators arriving for shifts via a freight elevator that descends the northeastern wall—a cage of steel that shudders and groans, guided by rails polished smooth by use.

The fauna that exists here is strange and sparse. Pale salamanders the length of your arm navigate the wet walls, their skin translucent enough to see their organs. Moths with wings like beaten copper cluster around the electric lights, their bodies evolved to withstand the heat. Sometimes a bird enters from above—you can track its panicked spiral as it realizes its mistake, fighting the warm updrafts to escape.

At the bottom, barely visible, the industrial heart continues its slow work. Whatever process happens down there—geothermal power generation, mineral extraction, some fusion of organic and mechanical cultivation—operates on geological time. The operators speak of pressure readings that change over months, of yields measured in decades. The machinery was built to run for centuries with minimal intervention, its brass fittings already green with verdigris, its iron bones accepting their gradual merger with the stone.

The constant wet heat creates its own weather. Clouds form inside the caldera on cool mornings, a perfect cylinder of fog that rises until it spills over the rim like smoke. Rainbow rings appear in the mist when the afternoon sun angles just right. At night, the industrial glow from below paints the steam orange and red, making the entire caldera look like a gateway to some mechanical underworld.

This place feels like a wound in the earth that technology has colonized—not to heal it, but to harness it. The boundary between the natural and built has dissolved in the humidity. Pipes become indistinguishable from vines. Rust and moss share the same surfaces. The industrial roar might be the mountain's own voice, speaking through the machines humanity installed in its throat.
