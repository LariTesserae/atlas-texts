The Woven Plateaus rise in terraced shelves of living architecture, each level a different shade of green-gold-violet where the plant-builders have calcified their bodies into something between wood and coral. These are not trees or vines as we know them, but a single continuous organism that has learned to crystallize parts of itself into permanence while keeping other tissues supple and breathing. The structure spans perhaps three valleys' width, though distance becomes uncertain when every surface undulates with the slow respiratory rhythm of the substrate.

Water weeps constantly from the breathing walls. Not rain, but the exhalation of the vast plant-body as it processes moisture drawn up from aquifers far below. The sound is tremendous — not falling water, but the deep cellular gurgling of transport vessels wide as hallways, the crackling of new growth hardening into buttresses, the whistle of air through stomatal corridors that open and close in tidal patterns. Every surface sweats, beads, streams. Waterfalls braid down through gaps in the structure, but these are internal waterfalls, following channels the organism has grown to direct its own circulation.

The warmth comes from metabolic heat. Stand near any wall and feel the fever-temperature of massive photosynthesis, of cambium layers building themselves atom by atom. The air shimmers with humidity and the sweet-sick smell of continuous growth — resinous, fungal, like overripe fruit and fresh sawdust combined. Spores drift constantly, each one a potential new chamber or bridge, though in this strange equilibrium they only replace what occasionally deliquesces back into protoplasm.

Higher up, where the structure thins, you can see through translucent membrane-windows to other valleys filled with similar architectures — each one a separate but perhaps connected organism-city, their edges touching in places where the chemical signals match. The boundaries between inside and outside barely exist. Corridors open to the sky. Rooms have no roofs, only canopies of active leaf-flesh that part and seal according to some ancient biological programming.

This is architecture without architects, city without citizens — though in the hollow chambers you might glimpse the rare dart of something feathered or furred, creatures that have evolved to live in the gaps between thoughts of this vast plant-mind. They feed on the nectar that beads from certain glands, spread pollen between distant chambers, clean the endless tissues of accumulating debris.

The strangest thing: in certain chambers, the plant has grown perfect imitations of built things — stairs that are really ramps of graduated tissue, windows that are sphincters holding themselves geometrically open, furniture-shapes that fruit from the walls. As if the organism once encountered civilization and incorporated its memory into its genetic architecture, endlessly reproducing forms it doesn't understand but finds aesthetically necessary.

Time moves differently here. The structure appears permanent, yet everything is actually in constant motion too slow for human perception — walls thickening, passages gradually migrating, rooms being born in one decade and reabsorbed three centuries later. Only the water moves at speeds we recognize, endless and musical, painting dark traces down surfaces that dry and darken and wet again in patterns that never exactly repeat.

At night, bioluminescence traces the vasculature beneath the surface — blue-green rivers of light following the water up from roots you'll never see to crown-chambers that kiss the lower clouds. The whole structure becomes a lantern the size of a small city, pulsing with the heartbeat of something too large and too strange to be called alive in any sense we understand, yet undeniably living, undeniably awake.
