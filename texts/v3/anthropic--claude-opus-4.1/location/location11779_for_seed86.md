The limestone breath-caves extend for miles beneath the plateau, a young karst system where the earth broke open perhaps only centuries ago. The ceilings are still sharp-edged from their recent collapse, unsmoothed by time, with fracture patterns that catch what little light filters down through the occasional sinkhole shaft. These openings to the surface world are sparse—perhaps one every quarter mile—creating columns of wan daylight that barely penetrate the constant twilight below.

The caves breathe with moisture. Condensation beads perpetually on the cool stone, fed by the temperature differential between the surface heat and the stable chill of these shallow caverns. Water doesn't flow here so much as seep and drip, creating scattered pools in the limestone hollows that never quite dry, never quite fill. The air tastes of minerals and carries a dampness that clings to skin and fur alike.

This is the domain of the pale moths. They carpet every surface in a living, rustling blanket of wings—millions upon millions of them, their bodies soft and grub-white, evolved for this specific darkness. The sound is constant and overwhelming: a papery thunder of wing-beats that never ceases, echoing through the chambers in waves. They've been here since the caves first opened, their population explosion filling every ecological niche, their caterpillars feeding on the sparse fungi and limestone-adapted lichens that grow in weak patches where moisture collects.

The moths have made tools of a sort—not individually, but collectively. Their accumulated silk over generations has created vast nets and hammocks that span between stalactites, catching water droplets and debris. Their discarded cocoons pile into primitive structures, accidental architectures that channel airflow and create microclimates. When disturbed, sections of the moth-mass lift and resettle like a single organism, their combined wing-beats generating winds strong enough to scatter intruders.

Nothing changes here. The moth population reached its equilibrium decades ago, their numbers precisely balanced with the meager food supply. The same water drops fall in the same places. The same dim shafts of light trace the same paths across the cave floor as the sun wheels overhead. Individual moths live and die, but the mass remains constant, a breathing, rustling permanence in the young darkness of the earth.

The only vegetation consists of those thin films of cave-adapted growth—ghostly pale threads and crusts that survive on minerals and moisture alone. Sometimes a seed drops through from above and sends down desperate, etiolated shoots that yellow and fail in the dimness. Their bodies join the thin layer of organic matter on the cave floor, slowly consumed by the moth larvae and the patient chemistry of limestone.

In certain chambers where the ceiling has collapsed more completely, you can stand in these twilight amphitheaters and watch the moth-clouds cycle endlessly through their space, their collective movement as predictable as tide tables, their sound like an underground ocean that never knew water. The air moves with them, cool and mineral-sweet, carrying spores and wing-scales in perpetual circulation through galleries that stretch beyond the reach of any single light source, vast enough to hold weather systems of living wings.
