The city spreads across the high saddle between two peaks like lichen on stone — sparse clusters of carved-stone dwellings connected by worn footpaths and narrow aqueducts. At this elevation, the air is thin and cool, carrying the perpetual dampness of clouds that roll through the settlement twice daily, leaving everything beaded with moisture that never quite dries before the next wave arrives.

The buildings emerge from living rock, their foundations continuous with the mountain itself. Each structure is topped with elaborate wooden frameworks — not quite roofs, more like skeletal crowns that catch and channel the passing mists into stone cisterns below. These collection systems, woven from mountain bamboo and sealed with rendered fat, create a delicate lacework against the sky. Between the clustered neighborhoods stretch long expanses of terraced gardens, explosively green despite the altitude, fed by an ancient network of channels that redistribute the captured water with mathematical precision.

The vegetation seems impossible for this height — dense banks of flowering vines cascade over retaining walls, and small forests of twisted, silver-barked trees grow in protected hollows between buildings. Their roots crack through paving stones that were laid a thousand years ago, yet look freshly cut. The plants themselves are strange: leaves that fold into tubes to capture mist, flowers that bloom only in fog, moss that grows in perfect spirals up the stone pillars. In the market squares, ancient fruit trees heavy with pale, translucent produce bend over stone benches where no one sits.

Birds nest in every available crevice — clouds of small, violet swallows that hunt the insects drawn to the perpetual moisture, and larger raptors with oil-slick feathers that roost in the wooden mist-catchers. Ground-dwelling creatures scurry between buildings: something like rabbits but with elongated limbs for climbing, and lizards with fur instead of scales that sun themselves on the worn stone steps. In the gardens, you can hear the rustle of larger animals moving through the vegetation, though they remain unseen.

The city feels caught in amber. The same mist rolls through at dawn and dusk. The same water runs through the same channels, wearing the same grooves it has worn for centuries. The wooden frameworks never rot despite the moisture; the stone never crumbles despite the roots that pierce it. Even the sounds are muffled and constant — the distant trickle of water through stone, the whisper of wind through the mist-catchers, the soft calls of birds that never seem to vary their songs.

Tools hang in workshops as if just set down — simple bronze chisels still sharp, wooden mallets smooth from use, looms strung with half-finished cloth that never progresses. The technology is elegant but primitive: water-powered mills that turn steadily but grind no grain, furnaces that maintain perfect temperature but smelt no metal, pottery wheels that spin without potters.

The sharp, young geology of the mountains creates dramatic backdrops — knife-edge ridges and smooth faces of recently fractured stone catch the normal light and throw it back in unexpected ways. The city itself occupies a relatively gentle slope in this violent landscape, a natural amphitheater carved by some geological accident, its buildings stepping down in irregular tiers toward a central plaza where seven channels meet in a fountain that neither overflows nor empties.

There is something dream-like in how the abundance of life exists alongside the absence of change. Fruit never falls from trees. Birds build nests but never lay eggs. The mist brings fresh moisture but nothing ever rots. The city breathes — you can feel it in the movement of air through the streets, in the way sounds carry differently in different quarters, in the subtle shift of shadows as clouds pass overhead — but it never exhales.

At the edges where the settlement meets wild mountain, the transition is abrupt. The cultivated strangeness gives way to raw stone and ice-fed streams, to slopes too steep for even the adapted vegetation to colonize. From these boundaries, you can look back and see the entire city-organism spread across its high perch, a green and grey growth that has found its perfect form and frozen there, abundant and empty, ancient and newborn, as permanent and temporary as the mist that feeds it.
