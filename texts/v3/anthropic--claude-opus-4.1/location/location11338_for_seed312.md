The plateau city spreads like lichen across the mountain's shoulder, ten thousand feet above the world below. Stone dwellings press against one another in dense warrens, their flat roofs serving as streets for the levels above, creating a vertical labyrinth of weathered granite and frozen mortar. Each morning, frost rimes the carved water channels that snake between buildings—ancient grooves that guide the precious melt from occasional snow down through the settlement's tiers.

The air is thin and sharp. It carries little sound beyond the soft scrape of hooves on stone as yaks pick their way along the narrow passages, their breath clouding in small puffs. The city seems to breathe with them—a slow, patient respiration that has continued for centuries. Windows are small, deep-set things, covered with scraped hide or precious glass traded from far below. Smoke seeps from chimney holes, dissipating quickly in the dry cold.

Between the buildings, in courtyards no larger than a small room, sparse grasses grow in protected pockets where snowmelt collects. These tiny meadows are fiercely guarded, their soil built up over generations from yak dung and windblown dust. Prayer flags stretch between the structures, their colors faded to suggestions—once red, once blue, once yellow—now all weathered to the grey-brown of the mountain itself.

The settlement stretches for miles along the high ridge, following the contours of the stone beneath. From a distance, it's difficult to tell where the natural rock formations end and the human construction begins. The buildings seem to have grown from the mountain rather than been built upon it, their walls following existing fractures in the granite, their foundations settled into natural terraces.

Wildlife thrives in the margins. Pikas whistle from the rockfalls at the city's edges, their colonies extending beneath the outer buildings where they've learned to steal grain. Golden eagles circle on the thermals that rise from the sun-warmed stone. In the early morning, before the sun crests the eastern peaks, you might glimpse the ghost-grey forms of snow leopards padding along the highest rooflines, hunting the blue sheep that somehow survive on the vertical cliff faces just beyond the settlement's border.

The scale defies comprehension. What appears from below to be a single settlement reveals itself, once you're within it, to be dozens of interconnected villages, each with its own water source—a spring seeping from the rock, a depression that catches snow, a carved cistern that hoards the brief summer rains. The passages between these nodes are worn smooth by countless generations of feet, hooves, and sledges. Some routes are broad enough for two yaks to pass; others require turning sideways and breathing in.

At the city's heart, where the density reaches its peak, the buildings stack seven, eight stories high, their stone walls tapering as they rise. Here the construction is oldest, the blocks fitted without mortar in a technique forgotten by current masons who patch the ancient work with cruder methods. These central towers sway slightly in the powerful winds that scour the plateau, their movement almost imperceptible except to those who have lived their whole lives within them and know the particular rhythm of each storm.

The technology is stone and wood, leather and wool, bronze and iron worked with patient hammer blows in forges that must burn yak dung and precious imported charcoal. Every tool is worn smooth with use, repaired and re-handled countless times. Nothing is discarded. Broken pottery becomes drainage fill; worn-out felt becomes insulation; even the ash from the fires is carefully collected and mixed with yak butter to waterproof the hides that cover doorways.

In the brief summer, when the snows retreat to the highest peaks, the entire plateau transforms. Sudden carpets of alpine flowers emerge from what seemed barren ground—tiny saxifrages and cushion plants that hug the earth. Marmots emerge from their burrows, their whistles adding to the soundscape. Butterflies appear from nowhere, clouds of them, feeding frantically on the ephemeral blooms. The human inhabitants move differently too, spreading out from the dense winter cores to occupy summer shelters at the edges, following their herds to the high pastures that exist for only a few precious weeks.

But always the cold returns, patient and inevitable. The city contracts again, pulling its life inward, consolidating heat and resources. The young ice that forms in the water channels each dawn grows thicker, persists longer into the day, until finally it remains. The plateau city settles into its winter rhythm—slow, enduring, as much a part of the mountain as the stone itself.
