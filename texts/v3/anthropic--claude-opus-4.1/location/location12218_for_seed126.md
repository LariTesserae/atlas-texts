The Lattice stretches beyond comprehension, a crystalline megastructure of fused silicate that has grown and regrown for aeons until its original purpose dissolved into pure form. Each hexagonal cell spans kilometers, their walls worn smooth as breath by processes that ended before memory began. The material glows from within—not light exactly, but a kind of thermal radiance that physics textbooks would struggle to categorize, casting everything in warm amber permanence.

You stand in what was once perhaps a maintenance shaft, now merely one of countless hollows in this infinite honeycomb. The floor beneath curves imperceptibly, following the structure's own gravity rather than any planetary pull. Here, down means toward the nearest mass of lattice-stuff, and the horizon bends wrong.

Sparse growths cling to junction points where three walls meet—not plants but something between circuit and organism, their copper-bright filaments drinking heat differentials and exhaling faint harmonics too low to properly hear. They spread in slow fractals, each colony taking centuries to advance a meter, building their own small architectures within the greater maze. When you pass close, they contract slightly, a response encoded millions of years ago to some stimulus that no longer exists.

The air tastes of ozone and arithmetic. It's dry as theorem, maintained at precisely 311 Kelvin by the substrate's deep programming. Sometimes a maintenance mite scuttles past—creatures of spun carbon and intent, no larger than your fist, following routes through the structure that only they remember. They pause occasionally to repair nothing, their tools sparking against surfaces that healed themselves long ago, performing phantom surgeries on a body that has outlived injury.

Through the vast cells you glimpse other chambers, other passages, the structure revealing itself in parallax layers. Some sections have collapsed into their own density, forming nodes of compressed matter that hum with stored information. Others have grown baroque with subsidiary constructions—smaller hexagons within hexagons, recursive architecture that serves no purpose except its own elaboration.

The silence here is architectural. Sound exists—your footsteps, the whisper of air through geometries, the occasional crack of thermal expansion—but the scale swallows it all. A shout would travel for kilometers before returning as the ghost of an echo, transformed by its journey through spaces that were never meant for voice.

In the distance, perhaps a hundred kilometers away, a weather system moves through the lattice. Not clouds but a kind of pressure phenomenon, a traveling zone where the structure's internal physics shift slightly. Where it passes, the walls brighten and sing—a deep thrumming that you feel in your bones rather than hear. The copper-bright growths lean toward it like flowers following sun.

You could walk for years here and never find an edge. The structure continues above and below, inward and outward, a built environment so total that concepts like "natural" and "artificial" have lost their distinction. This is simply what *is* now—a warm, bright emptiness densely packed with presence, every surface speaking in the language of something that finished its sentence geological ages ago and now simply persists, active in its stillness, dynamic in its completion.

Somewhere, in chambers you'll never reach, other travelers might be walking. Or perhaps you're alone in this section, this particular billion cubic kilometers of ancient purpose, accompanied only by the maintenance mites and the slow-growing geometries and the patient, permanent light that remembers when all of this meant something specific, before meaning itself grew old and settled into pure being.
