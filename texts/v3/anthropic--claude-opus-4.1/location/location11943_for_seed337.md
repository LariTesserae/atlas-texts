The nascent city spreads through the shallow cavern system like spilled honey finding every depression in rough stone. This is not a place carved with intention but rather occupied as it forms—the limestone still weeping mineral tears that build new columns even as wooden scaffolds rise between them.

The settlement began where the water table kisses the cave floor, creating pools that never quite dry. These scattered basins, each no larger than a market square, provide the only reliable moisture in this emerging underground. The builders have learned to read the stone's slow birth: where calcium deposits grow thickest, there the next chamber will open in a generation's time. They build accordingly, with timber frames that can be moved when the cave reshapes itself.

Wan daylight filters through ceiling fissures and sinkholes, enough to see by but never enough to warm the perpetual coolness. The air tastes of wet rock and sawdust. Wooden walkways span the uneven floor, connecting clusters of structures that crowd every stable surface—workshops, dwellings, storage platforms—all pressed together in the precious zones where the ceiling rises high enough to stand upright. The density is born of necessity; usable space is rare in these forming chambers.

The primitive construction speaks of recent arrival or limited resources. Stone tools chip at limestone edges while bronze implements, precious and few, are reserved for critical joints and fixtures. Rope ladders dangle into lower galleries still filling with flowstone. The sound of mallets on wood echoes softly, then dies in the absorbent stone, leaving long silences broken only by the irregular plink of water drops adding their patient construction to stalactites overhead.

Stunted fungi sprout from cracks where bat guano has accumulated, the only vegetation that tolerates the dim conditions. Occasionally a pale centipede, translucent as the newest flowstone, ripples across a wall—the largest creature most residents will see for weeks. The ecosystem here is still learning what it will become, as embryonic as the cave itself.

The very ground shifts, imperceptibly but inexorably. A doorway that fits today will bind in a year's time. The settlement lives in constant negotiation with geology's slow breath, expanding into new hollows as they open, retreating from galleries that close like healing wounds. This is architecture as process rather than product—a city being born alongside the space that contains it.
