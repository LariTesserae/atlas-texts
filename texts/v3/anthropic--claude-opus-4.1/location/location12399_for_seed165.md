The plateau stretches impossibly thin against the sky, a vast shelf of weathered granite that shouldn't exist at this altitude. Twenty thousand feet up, perhaps more—the air pressure suggests heights that maps insist are wrong. The stone has been carved by millennia of wind into smooth, undulating waves frozen mid-surge, pocked with shallow depressions where condensation gathers in the pre-dawn hours before evaporating into nothing.

Sound travels wrong here. The constant wind—which should be screaming at this elevation—arrives in waves of deep thrumming that bypass the ears entirely, felt instead in the sternum and spine. Sometimes it doubles back on itself, creating moments where the same gust is heard arriving from three directions at once, building into a resonance that makes the plateau ring like a struck bell. The sound never changes, never varies in its pattern, as if this place exists in a loop of its own making.

Scattered across the expanse are structures that might have been cairns once, or might still be—low stacks of fitted stone no higher than a person's waist, spaced with disturbing regularity every few hundred meters. Some have partially collapsed, their stones fused together by processes that have nothing to do with mortar. In certain light, which comes rarely through the perpetual high haze, they appear to be growing from the bedrock rather than built upon it.

The sparse vegetation defies taxonomy. Thin films of something lichen-adjacent spread across the rock in perfect spirals, their edges sharp as drawn lines. They're gray-green in the dim light but sometimes aren't there at all—a trick of perception or something more fundamental. Occasionally, in the protected lees of the stone structures, a twisted shrub no larger than a fist clings to existence, its branches growing in mathematical patterns that hurt to follow with the eye.

Water exists here in temporal pockets. A depression might be dust-dry, then suddenly brimming with perfectly clear water that reflects stars in daylight, then dry again without transition. The moisture doesn't evaporate or drain—it simply isn't, then is, then isn't. Sometimes you can hear it flowing underground in rivers that physics says should have frozen solid at this temperature, at this altitude.

The cold is absolute but wrong. It doesn't deepen with nightfall or ease with what passes for noon in the eternal dim. It exists at exactly the temperature where thoughts begin to crystallize, where breath becomes visible not as vapor but as brief geometric patterns that shatter soundlessly before they can fall.

Movement catches in peripheral vision—perhaps birds, perhaps remnants of birds, perhaps the idea of birds made temporarily manifest. They're gone when you turn to look, leaving only the sensation of having been observed by something that doesn't fully exist in the same dimensional framework as the plateau itself.

The horizon bends. Not the earth's natural curve, but something more acute, as if the plateau is trying to close itself into a sphere but can't quite manage the mathematics. In the far distance, the same cairn you passed an hour ago waits ahead, or perhaps it's a different cairn in the same space, or perhaps distance means something different here where the loudness of silence has worn through reality's fabric like water through stone, patient and eternal and absolutely still.

The place maintains itself in perfect stasis, each element locked in its state—the worn rock never wearing further, the sparse plants never growing or dying, the scattered structures neither completing their collapse nor recovering their original forms. Even footprints in the rare patches of gravel fill themselves in when unobserved, the landscape insisting on its own unchanging continuity against all intrusion.

This is a place between places, thin as the air that barely exists here, loud with the sound of its own impossibility, forever preserving a moment that may never have actually occurred.
