The vertical garden thrums in darkness.

You ascend through it on a rusted cable platform that shudders and groans against its track, the mechanized winch somewhere far below sending vibrations up through the metal that you feel in your teeth. The sound is enormous in this narrow shaft — grinding gears, the splash and gurgle of water through pipes, the pneumatic hiss of moisture delivery systems firing in irregular patterns. Every few seconds, a different sector of the wall erupts with a pressurized mist that beads instantly on leaves and drips back down into the collection channels.

The shaft is perhaps thirty feet across, carved recently from the living rock — you can still see the bore marks spiraling up the stone where industrial drills bit through. But already the walls have been claimed. Terraced growing beds jut out at aggressive angles, each one packed with vegetation that seems to strain against its confines. Ferns unfurl from crevices, their fronds so dense they form curtains. Vines cascade from upper tiers, some as thick as your arm, their roots plunging into hydroponic tubes that gurgle and click with automated nutrient delivery.

The darkness is nearly absolute. What little light exists comes from the occasional maintenance panel — a dim orange glow that barely illuminates a few square feet before being swallowed. In those brief glimpses, you see how violently alive this place is: leaves pressing against each other, fighting for space, stems twisted around support cables, moss coating every surface in a thick pelt. The plants grow by sound and touch and chemical signal here, not light.

The platform jolts, stopping at one of the maintenance levels. Here the cacophony intensifies — steam vents release with sharp whistles, drainage pumps cycle on and off, metal expands and contracts with temperature changes, creating a constant percussion of pings and creaks. The air is thick with moisture and the green smell of chlorophyll working in darkness. Condensation runs down everything in rivulets, pooling in the mesh flooring before draining away through holes that echo with the sound of falling water.

As you rise higher, the temperature drops. The tropical profusion of the lower levels gives way to hardier species — rhododendrons with leaves like leather, alpine mosses, cushion plants that form dense mats. The irrigation system changes too, from those aggressive misting jets to a gentler system of weeping hoses that maintain just enough moisture in the thin air. Ice occasionally forms on the pipes, only to be melted by heating coils that cycle on with a buzzing hum.

The machinery here is purely functional, industrial in its lack of refinement. Exposed pipes run everywhere, bolted directly into the stone. Pumps the size of refrigerators squat on steel platforms, their motors running constantly, pushing water up from some reservoir far below. Everything is painted in that institutional green that's already flaking off, revealing rust beneath. No one built this place for beauty — it's an engine for growth, a vertical farm perhaps, or some kind of botanical experiment running in the heart of a mountain.

Near what must be the top, the shaft opens slightly, and the platform emerges into a cavern — still enclosed, still dark, but vast enough that the sounds change quality, echoing differently. Here the growing system explodes into controlled chaos. Hundreds of growing towers stretch up into the darkness, each one rotating slowly on motorized bases, their hydraulic systems hissing. The vegetation is so dense you can hear it — the rustle of leaves against each other, the creak of stems under their own weight, the constant drip of condensation. Automated pruning systems activate randomly, their cutting blades whirring through the darkness, keeping the growth from choking itself.

The whole space pulses with mechanical life — conveyor belts running somewhere in the darkness, fans circulating air, the distant clatter of what might be a packaging system. It's agriculture made vertical, made dark, made strange. The plants grow in this artificial night, fed by machines, shaped by industry, reaching always upward in the roaring darkness of the shaft.
