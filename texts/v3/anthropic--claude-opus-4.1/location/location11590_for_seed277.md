The karst plateau stretches beyond sight, a vast tableland of weathered limestone riddled with thousands of sinkholes like inverted stars against the pale rock. In this perpetual twilight—whether from dense cloud cover or some quality of the atmosphere itself—the stone appears almost luminescent, a ghost-gray that seems to generate its own dim phosphorescence.

The sinkholes breathe. Not all at once, but in slow, irregular patterns across the plateau's face. Warm, humid air rises from the depths, condensing as it meets the cooler surface atmosphere, creating localized fog banks that drift and dissipate. Each exhalation brings with it the faint mechanical hum of something far below—not quite industrial machinery, but something with that same quality of purposeful, repetitive motion. The sound is felt more than heard, a vibration through the limestone that suggests vast systems operating in the darkness beneath.

Water exists here only in transition. The morning condensation beads on the scattered thornbrush and wire-grass, running down their sparse leaves to vanish into the porous rock. Brief, violent cloudbursts arrive perhaps twice a month, the water disappearing instantly into the sinkholes with a sound like distant waterfalls. Between these events, the plateau remains bone-dry on its surface, though the air always carries that underground dampness, that hint of vast reservoirs moving through caverns below.

The fauna have adapted to this vertical world. Colonies of blind climbing mammals—something between bats and primates—navigate the sinkhole walls by touch and echolocation. Their soft chirping creates a constant, barely audible background texture, like wind through leaves that don't exist here. Larger creatures, six-legged and covered in heat-retaining fur, migrate between sinkholes following some inscrutable pattern. They move in absolute silence across the plateau surface, their padded feet finding purchase on the weathered rock. At the rim of each hole, they pause, lower their elongated necks into the darkness, and drink from moisture that condenses on their specialized tongue-fur.

During the warmest part of the day—though day and night are barely distinguishable in this dim world—streams of insects emerge from selected sinkholes. They rise in perfect spirals, millions of them, their wings catching what little light exists and creating pillars of faint shimmer. The swarms disperse at exactly one hundred meters altitude, spreading across the plateau to feed on the sparse vegetation's microscopic blooms before descending into different holes as the temperature drops.

The vegetation itself defies easy categorization. The thornbrush grows in perfect circles around certain sinkholes, their roots presumably reaching down to some water source. But other holes remain bare for a kilometer in every direction, the limestone polished smooth by millions of years of those humid exhalations. Wire-grass fills the shallow depressions between holes, its metallic sheen suggesting some mineral uptake from the rock. Every plant grows alone, separated from its neighbors by meters of bare stone, as if observing some ancient treaty about territory.

Sometimes, rarely, a deep rumble rises from the largest sinkholes—the ones that span a kilometer or more across. The sound builds slowly over hours, a grinding, mechanical note that makes the smaller fauna retreat to middle-distance. Then, with no warning, it stops. In the silence that follows, a new quality enters the air: ozone, hot metal, the taste of electricity. Whatever industrial process operates in the depths has completed another cycle.

The plateau continues beyond any visible horizon, its surface broken by these countless openings to the world below. In the perpetual dusk, distances become meaningless. A sinkhole that appears nearby might be hours of walking away; the wire-grass meadow that seems infinite might end at an unexpected edge. The only constant is the warm air rising, cooling, condensing, and the patient circulation of life between the sparse surface and the breathing depths.

At the plateau's edges—if edges exist—the sinkholes grow larger and fewer, until finally there are only vast chasms kilometers apart, their depths absolutely black, their exhalations strong enough to create permanent cloud formations that hover like floating islands in the dark air. Here, the mechanical sounds are louder, more complex, a symphony of pistons and gears and processes that might be geological, might be biological, might be something else entirely operating by its own vast logic in the warm darkness below the weathered stone.
