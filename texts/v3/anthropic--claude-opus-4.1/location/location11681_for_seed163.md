The plateau spreads wide beneath a fierce sun, its newly-formed basalt shelves still sharp-edged and unweathered, black rock weeping with constant moisture that runs in thin sheets across every surface. This is young geology, perhaps only decades old, where the earth pushed up and cracked open to create this elevated wetland that extends for miles.

The plants came almost immediately. They grow not from soil—there is none yet—but directly from the wet rock itself, their roots working into every hairline fracture, every pocket where water pools. Massive tree-ferns unfurl thirty feet overhead, their trunks not wood but compressed masses of aerial roots and previous generations of their own dead matter. Between them, curtains of moss hang so thick you could lose yourself in their green depths, each strand beaded with moisture that catches the overwhelming light and throws it back in millions of tiny prisms. The vegetation has become the landscape—there is no distinguishing between the living substrate and what grows from it. Even the rock surfaces bloom with aggressive lichens that glow electric green in the brightness, breaking down basalt grain by grain.

The sound is tremendous. Every surface drips, trickles, seeps—ten million individual water-sounds that merge into a white roar. But louder still are the steam vents, scattered across the plateau where the rock is youngest. They shriek and hiss through the vegetation, sending up columns of vapor that the plants greedily absorb. The industrial remnants make sense now—a few concrete foundations slowly being pulled apart by roots, the rusted skeleton of what might have been a geothermal station, pipes that once channeled this same steam now split open and colonized by ferns. The operation abandoned when the geology proved too unstable, too alive.

The bright light is relentless here above the cloud layer. It pours down through the steam and mist, creating a greenhouse atmosphere that the plants exploit completely. Everything photosynthesizes—even the bark, even the roots where they're exposed. The air itself feels green, thick with oxygen and moisture and the sharp smell of crushed vegetation mixing with sulfur from the vents.

Birds are scarce—you might glimpse a single raptor circling high above, or hear but not see something small rustling through the fern canopy. A few insects brave the hostile acoustic environment. But mostly this is a kingdom of plants and water and stone, still deciding what it wants to become.

The whole plateau pulses with slow transformation. Each rain—and they come often, sudden and violent—cuts new channels through the vegetation, only to have them immediately colonized. The steam vents gradually migrate as the deep heat shifts. The plants build soil particle by particle, their accumulated death gradually softening the sharp edges of the basalt. In perhaps a century, this will be forest. In a thousand years, if the geology holds, it might be jungle. But for now it exists in this raw state, deafeningly alive, blindingly green, neither wholly natural nor wholly industrial but something transitional—a place remaking itself with patient, inexorable force.

The moisture never stops moving. It rises as steam, falls as rain, weeps from rock faces, transpires from a billion leaves, condenses on cool mornings into fog so thick the scattered industrial ruins vanish entirely. Even the air at midday, when the light reaches its most punishing intensity, remains heavy with water. Your clothes never dry here. The exposed metal continues its slow dissolution. The plants continue their total conquest. The sound never, ever stops.
