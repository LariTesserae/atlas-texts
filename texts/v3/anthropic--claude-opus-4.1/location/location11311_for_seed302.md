The plateau rings with water.

Up here, where the air thins and the world drops away on three sides, the stone itself weeps. The rock—a peculiar blue-gray mineral that has no name because nothing here has ever needed naming—sweats perpetually through its crystalline face. Not porous exactly, but somehow permeable to the vast aquifer trapped beneath, pressed up from unknowable depths by geological forces that settled into this configuration eons ago.

The water emerges everywhere at once. It beads on vertical cliff faces like permanent dew, runs in thin sheets across the tilted table of stone that stretches for perhaps three miles in its longest dimension. There are no plants to drink it, no roots to seek it. The surface is naked mineral, polished by ages of this same seepage, carved into shallow basins and runnels that carry the water in braided patterns toward the edges where it simply falls, becomes mist, becomes nothing.

The sound is overwhelming—not from any single source but from the accumulation of water on stone at every possible scale. Each droplet rings against the crystal surface with a clear, bell-like note. Multiply this by millions: the high tinkling of fresh emergence, the deeper tones where streams gather in the worn channels, the white roar of the edge-falls, and beneath it all, a resonance from the stone itself, vibrating with the constant percussion of water. The plateau has become an instrument played eternally by its own wetness.

In the brightest part of day—and it is almost always the brightest part of day here, the high altitude placing this table of stone above most weather, in the realm of unfiltered solar radiation—the water throws light everywhere. Each surface becomes a mirror, a lens, a prism. The air itself seems to ring with brightness, photons colliding with droplets, refracting through the perpetual mist that hangs at the plateau's edges.

The stone beneath shows its strangeness more clearly in this violent light. Formations that might be crystalline, might be organic, might be neither—twisted columns like frozen smoke, fields of hexagonal tiles that fit too perfectly to be natural but show no sign of having been made. The blue-gray mineral has a quality like frozen music, patterns that repeat and vary according to some mathematics that feels almost comprehensible but never quite resolves into meaning.

Where the water pools deepest, in basins worn into perfect ellipses by patient centuries, something lives. Not plant, not animal, not fungus. Translucent films that might be biological, might be mineral precipitate, might be some intersection of the two. They pulse faintly with their own light—barely visible except when the sun angle changes and suddenly they flash like signal mirrors. These colonies, if colonies they are, never grow beyond their basins, never spread, never change. They simply persist, feeding perhaps on minerals in the water, or on the light itself, or on the sound.

Occasionally—rarely—something moves across the plateau that might be fauna. Long-legged things, impossibly thin, that pick their way between the water channels on legs like glass needles. They cast no shadows, or cast too many shadows, or cast shadows that fall in directions that don't match the sun. They might be drinking. They might be listening. They might be something else entirely, performing some function that has no analogue in lower, warmer, quieter worlds.

The water that falls from the edges never stops falling. Far below, hidden in the permanent mist, there might be lakes, seas, another world entirely. But from up here, there is only the falling away, the transformation of water into white vapor into nothing. The plateau is a machine for lifting water into brightness and sound, for holding it in this eternal moment of exposure before releasing it to gravity.

At the few points where the stone rises into peaks or spires above the general level, the acoustic properties intensify. Standing waves form in the air itself, nodes where the sound cancels to near-silence, antinodes where it becomes physically painful, powerful enough to vibrate bones and blur vision. The water here runs upward as often as down, following the pressure waves, defying gravity in silver streams that arc through the air before falling back to splash against the singing stone.

This is not a place that changes. The water has been emerging at the same rate for geological ages. The basins are worn exactly as deep as they will ever be. The sound neither builds nor fades. Even the thin creatures, when they appear, follow the same paths their predecessors followed ten thousand years ago, stepping in the same places, pausing at the same pools, vanishing at the same predetermined points. The plateau exists in a state of perfect dynamic equilibrium, forever thundering, forever bright, forever weeping its ancient water into the void.
