The birthing swamp spreads beneath a permanent ceiling of steam.

This is new land, or land attempting to become — a vast depression where the planet's crust has only recently buckled downward, creating a basin that traps both heat and water. The geology is still soft, still deciding what it wants to be. Rock faces weep continuously with mineral-rich water that emerges at near-boiling temperatures from fissures that formed perhaps only decades ago. These are not yet proper springs; they are wounds in stone that haven't learned how to heal.

The heat is crushing, biological. It rises from below where magma chambers pulse close to the surface, their proximity turning groundwater to steam before it can properly emerge. What does escape as liquid immediately begins to evaporate, creating a boundary layer of fog that hangs at knee-height, then chest-height, then overhead, trapped by temperature inversions that create distinct atmospheric strata. The air itself sweats.

In this prenatal landscape, vegetation explodes with desperate urgency. Giant ferns unfurl fronds the size of sailing ships, their growth audible — a constant rustling, crackling, stretching sound as cells divide at observable speeds. Moss carpets every stable surface in layers three feet deep, each species stacked atop another in a vertical ecosystem of minute gradations in moisture and temperature tolerance. Vines thick as bridge cables loop between emergent tree-things that are more like vertical root systems, drawing nutrients from the air itself, their "leaves" modified into cup-shaped condensation traps that overflow continuously.

The fauna here exists in overwhelming density because the boundaries between individual organisms have begun to dissolve. Clouds of gnats move as single entities, their billions of bodies creating temporary organs in the thick air — here a liver-shaped mass filtering moisture, there a lung-like formation pulsing with collective respiration. Salamander-things carpet the ground so densely they form a living floor, their bodies fused at the edges where they press together, sharing circulation, sharing nervous systems, no longer entirely separate creatures.

Everything here breathes in chorus. The sound is constant — not quite mechanical, not quite biological — a deep thrumming of countless lungs and spiracles and gill-slits all pulling at the soupy air. Underneath this is the percussion of dripping water, millions of droplets per second falling from leaf to leaf to ground, and the bass note of the earth itself groaning as it continues its slow geological birth.

In the eternal twilight beneath the canopy, bioluminescent fungi provide the only light that penetrates the steam. They grow in spreading infections across the bark of the tree-things, pulsing in slow waves that take hours to cross from one end of the valley to the other. The pattern never changes — the same wave, the same timing, as if the entire ecosystem is locked in a single moment of creation, repeating endlessly.

This is not evolution; this is genesis caught in amber. The land hasn't finished forming — pools of water appear and disappear as the underlying rock shifts imperceptibly, creating temporary lakes that last just long enough for ecosystems to establish before draining away through new-formed cracks. But the life adapts instantly, or perhaps doesn't need to adapt at all. It simply is, in overwhelming abundance, as if the planet is testing how much life a single place can hold.

The rules here are still soft, still negotiable. Occasionally, in the deeper pools where the water has collected enough minerals to turn syrupy, you can see things that shouldn't exist — arrangements of matter that haven't decided whether to be animal, vegetable, or mineral. They pulse with their own logic, geometric one moment, organic the next, as if the basic categories of existence haven't yet been firmly established.

The air tastes of iron and chlorophyll, of birth-blood and sap. Every surface seeps. Every shadow moves with the crawling of countless organisms. The density of life here is so great that individual existence becomes academic — everything is touching everything else, sharing heat, sharing moisture, sharing the fundamental chemicals of existence in an economy so tight that death and birth become indistinguishable, matter simply flowing from form to form without ever truly ceasing to be alive.

This is what the world was before it remembered what the world should be.
