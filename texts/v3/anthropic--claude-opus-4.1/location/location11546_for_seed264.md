The salt flats extend beneath a cloudless amber sky, their crystalline surface broken by thousands of shallow depressions where yesterday's rain still pools. Each pool is perfectly circular, about three meters across, their edges rimmed with concentric mineral deposits that shift from bone white to rust orange to a deep volcanic purple. The water in them is unnaturally still, holding the sky's reflection so perfectly that looking down feels like falling.

Between the pools, the ground is a tessellation of hexagonal salt plates, each the size of a dining table, their surfaces etched with delicate fractals where minerals have crystallized over millennia. The plates fit together with mechanical precision, as if laid by some ancient industry rather than natural forces. Walking across them produces a metallic ringing, each footfall setting off a chain of harmonics that travels through the crystalline lattice for hundreds of meters.

The sound never stops. Deep beneath the salt crust, geothermal vents release superheated steam through a network of natural pipes — hollow tubes of fused minerals that honeycomb the subsurface. The steam doesn't escape directly; instead, it resonates through these geological organ pipes, producing a constant drone that shifts between frequencies too low to properly hear and notes that make your teeth ache. Every few minutes, pressure differentials cause one of the larger pipes to release, creating a thunderous boom that rolls across the flats like distant artillery.

The heat rises in visible waves, making the horizon shimmer and dance. In the middle distance, you can make out the silhouettes of thermal columns — natural chimneys of fused salt and mineral deposits that tower thirty meters high, their surfaces glazed to a mirror finish by centuries of steam condensation. They stand alone, separated by kilometers of empty flats, each one singing its own note as superheated air rushes through its hollow core.

There is no life here in any recognizable form. No bacteria stain the pool edges, no algae cloud the water, no lichens crust the mineral deposits. Even the air seems sterile, carrying only the sharp scent of hot salt and sulfur. Yet the landscape feels oddly alive — the way the pools seem to breathe as they evaporate and refill, the way the salt plates occasionally crack and reform along perfectly straight lines, the way certain frequencies make the entire plain vibrate like a struck drumhead.

Occasionally, you spot what might be fauna — or might be mineral formations animated by the constant vibration. Crystalline structures that seem to migrate slowly across the flats, leaving trails of reformed salt behind them. Whether they're alive or just formations being gradually relocated by sonic forces is impossible to determine. They move too slowly to observe directly, but their paths, when viewed from above, form spiraling mathematical curves.

The storms, when they come, arrive without warning. The amber sky doesn't darken; instead, the air simply becomes water for exactly seventeen minutes. The rain falls in sheets so dense you can't see your hand in front of your face, drumming against the salt with a roar that drowns out even the underground pipes. Then it stops, as suddenly as it started, leaving the pools refilled and the air thick with petrichor-less humidity that tastes of hot metal.

At the far edge of visibility, the flats meet a low ridge of black volcanic glass, worn smooth by ages of sonic erosion. The ridge curves away in both directions, suggesting this entire basin might be the floor of some ancient caldera, its original purpose — whether natural or artificial — lost to geological time. The boundary between salt flat and ridge is absolute, as if drawn with a ruler, and the glass surface of the ridge reflects the endless drone back across the flats, creating standing waves of sound that make certain areas impossible to approach without protective equipment.

This is a place that exists in perfect stasis, where every rainstorm replaces exactly what has evaporated, where every crack in the salt heals itself along the same geometric patterns, where the sound never varies in its endless industrial hymn. It could have been like this for a million years. It could remain like this for a million more.
