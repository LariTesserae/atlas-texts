The caldera breathes.

High on this weathered volcanic rim, the stone weeps constantly through its porous basalt bones. Water seeps from every crack and hollow, not as springs but as a perpetual exudation, like the mountain itself perspires in the warm air. The rock face glistens black and green with this eternal dampness, its surface softened by millennia of patient dissolution into curves and bowls and channels that guide the water's slow descent.

Vegetation crowds wherever roots can grip—not as forest but as hanging gardens suspended in the vertical world. Massive tree ferns cascade from ledges, their fronds twenty feet long and drooping heavy with collected moisture. Orchids bloom in curtains from the undersides of overhangs, their roots exposed to the humid air, drawing water directly from the atmosphere. Moss grows so thick on some surfaces it forms shelves and false floors that tremble when the wind passes through, occasionally breaking free in great sloughing falls that thunder down the cliff face before catching on some lower prominence.

The scale is intimate despite the altitude. This high garden exists within a single curved section of the caldera wall, perhaps three hundred feet across and twice as tall—a natural amphitheater of dripping stone and suspended green. The vegetation grows in distinct colonies separated by expanses of wet rock, each microhabitat claiming its own seep or shelf, creating a vertical archipelago of green islands in a sea of weeping stone.

The sound never stops. Water drops ring and splash and trickle in a thousand different tones—some drops fall free for fifty feet before striking stone with sharp reports, others run in sheets that whisper, still others drip in steady rhythms that overlap into complex polyrhythms. The ferns rustle. Occasionally a section of moss gives way with a sound like tearing fabric followed by the splash of its impact below. The warm air itself seems to hum with moisture, creating a white noise underneath everything else.

Strange things grow here. Fungi that glow faintly blue-white in the shadowed recesses, pulsing in slow waves that take minutes to complete. Vines that grow in perfect spirals, their tips always questing upward but their bodies coiling with mathematical precision. There are patches where the stone itself has been colonized by something that might be lichen or might be mineral—it grows in geometric patterns like frost on a window, but made of what appears to be living copper that oxidizes green at the edges.

The few animals that inhabit this vertical garden are hardly seen but often heard. Something large and wet moves through the fern curtains at dusk, never showing itself but leaving behind a trail of disturbed fronds and a smell like crushed ginger. Beetles the size of a child's fist climb the wet rock faces on sucker feet, their shells iridescent black, feeding on the strange copper growths. Once a day, always at a different time, something calls out—a long, hollow note that echoes off the curved wall and seems to come from inside the stone itself.

At the base of this hanging garden, where all the water eventually collects, a series of natural pools have formed in the weathered rock. The water is warm and mineral-rich, tinted green from copper and iron, and things grow in it that grow nowhere else—translucent plants with no chlorophyll that wave in unfelt currents, their movements too regular to be random, too slow to track with the eye.

The only signs of any intelligence are the paths. Not built paths, but worn ones—routes through the vertical garden that something has traveled enough times to polish certain handholds smooth, to wear shallow cups in ledges where feet have stepped for generations. The paths connect nothing obvious to nothing evident, spiraling up and across the cliff face in routes that seem to follow rules invisible to human logic. Some lead to blank walls. Others end at precipices. One spirals seven times around a massive fern before continuing upward.

Time moves differently here. The constant water makes everything seem immediate and active, but nothing truly changes quickly. The ferns grow imperceptibly. The strange geometric lichen spreads at a pace measured in seasons. Even the falling of water-loosened moss happens with a dreamlike patience, the mass pulling away fiber by fiber before finally surrendering to gravity. 

This is an ancient place wearing away and building up simultaneously, where the boundary between stone and plant and water has blurred into something singular—a vertical wetland suspended in warm air, singing its endless water song, growing its strange slow life in the space between earth and sky.
