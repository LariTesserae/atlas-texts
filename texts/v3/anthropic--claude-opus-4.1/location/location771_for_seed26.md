The maintenance tunnels beneath the ice-lock factories stretch for kilometers, a vast industrial undercity where condensation never stops falling. Every surface weeps—rivulets stream down the reinforced polymer walls, collecting in the gridded channels that run between the walkways. The air tastes of coolant and rust.

This is recent construction, maybe three decades old, carved hastily from the permafrost when the processing plants above needed somewhere to hide their heat exchangers. The cuts are still sharp where the boring machines chewed through frozen earth and bedrock. No time for finishing work. The ceiling varies wildly in height—sometimes you walk upright beneath soaring ventilation shafts, sometimes you crouch where the engineers hit stubborn granite and redirected rather than blast through.

The sound never stops. Deep thrumming from the compressors above, the constant drip and trickle of water finding its level, the hiss of steam venting through pressure valves every seventeen minutes. When shifts change, footsteps ring on the perforated metal walkways—but mostly it's just the machines talking to themselves, their automated cycles grinding through infinite repetitions.

Moss grows in unlikely places, fed by the perpetual moisture and the wan industrial lighting—bioluminescent safety strips that run along the walkways, casting everything in pale green. Thin films of algae coat the undersides of pipes. Once in a while, you might spot a maintenance rat—engineered creatures with titanium teeth and heat-sensing whiskers, released decades ago to gnaw through blockages. They've gone semi-feral, breeding in the warm spaces behind the boilers.

The whole system breathes. Air pressure changes as massive ventilation fans cycle on and off, making your ears pop. The temperature hovers just above freezing, except near the heat exchangers where sudden pockets of warmth create their own weather—miniature fog banks that roll through the corridors, condensing on cold surfaces, freezing, melting, condensing again.

Strange geometries emerge where the builders followed efficiency over logic. Catwalks that spiral around enormous cylindrical tanks. Maintenance shafts that plunge at severe angles, following the gradient of some buried geological feature. Entire sections where the floor is a grid over rushing meltwater, the actual bottom lost in darkness below. The infrastructure extends far beyond what the factories above should need—rumors say the tunnels connect to older systems, pre-war excavations that were simply incorporated rather than destroyed.

In the machine halls, everything is purposeful steel and ceramic, pipes and valves and monitoring stations. But between them, in the connection tunnels, the construction grows stranger. Walls of fused synthetic stone, surfaces that sweat a petroleum-like substance, sections where the support beams are grown rather than built—some kind of self-reinforcing polymer that responds to stress by thickening.

The water table is close here, barely held back. Sometimes you can hear it through the walls—a massive, patient pressure. The pumps never stop working, cycling out the seepage, the condensation, the industrial runoff. Where they fail, pools form—perfectly still mirrors that reflect the industrial lighting until disturbed by drops from above, sending endless ripples through the green gloom.

This is infrastructure as landscape—a built environment so vast and self-contained it has developed its own ecology, its own weather, its own logic. The surface factories might shut down, the workers might leave, but the tunnels would continue their cycles, slowly dissolving back into the permafrost, the machinery singing to itself in the dark.
