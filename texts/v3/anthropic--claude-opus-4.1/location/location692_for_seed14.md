## The Abyssal Meadows

Down here, beneath three hundred meters of restless water, the world has worn itself smooth as a worried stone. The seafloor stretches in all directions—not flat, but undulating in ancient, exhausted waves that gave up trying to be mountains millions of years ago. Every ridge has been gentled, every valley filled with the patient accumulation of sediment that falls like snow in the darkness.

The water itself is old and heavy, dense with dissolved minerals that taste of iron and salt and time. It moves constantly but without purpose—great masses of it sliding past each other in invisible rivers, creating shear zones where the temperature drops another degree and the pressure shifts just enough to make your ears ache. These submarine currents braid and unbraid themselves, carrying clouds of disturbed sediment that bloom and dissipate like slow smoke.

What little grows here grows desperately. Bacterial mats spread across the seafloor in patches—thick, rubbery carpets of deep purple and sulfurous yellow that feed on chemical seeps from below. Where the mats tear away in the currents, they reveal the substrate beneath: compressed layers of shell and bone and organic matter packed so densely that individual fragments are impossible to distinguish. It's all become one substance, a kind of biological concrete that extends down for kilometers.

Occasionally, a tubeworm garden erupts from a thermal vent—sparse clusters of pale stalks swaying in the current, their plumes filtering the water for whatever particles drift by. But even these are ancient, their growth measured in millimeters per decade, their tubes thick with their own accumulated history. Between them, the seafloor is littered with the shells of things that no longer exist anywhere else: spiral forms that tried every possible variation before settling on extinction, carapaces built according to geometries that proved ultimately unsuccessful.

The darkness here is absolute except for the ghost-light of bioluminescence—brief, desperate flashes from the few creatures that still patrol these depths. A jellyfish pulses past, its bell nearly transparent, its trailing tentacles studded with photophores that flicker in patterns that might be communication or might be malfunction. A sixgill shark, primitive and patient, glides through the black water, so perfectly adapted to this environment that it hasn't needed to evolve for sixty million years.

But it's the turbulence that defines this place. Not the violent turbulence of surface storms, but something deeper and more unsettling. The water churns with invisible vortices, columns of rotation that form and dissipate without warning. Thermoclines shift like vertical walls through the darkness, bringing sudden changes in temperature that make the water shimmer with differential densities. Submarine avalanches cascade down the continental slope in the distance—silent catastrophes that remake the topology in slow motion, sending pressure waves that arrive as subtle compressions, barely perceptible except for how they make everything feel briefly wrong.

The only evidence of intelligence here is accidental: a stone knife, pressure-welded into the sediment layers, its obsidian edge still sharp after millennia of burial. A scatter of worked bone tools, so degraded they're barely distinguishable from the natural substrate. These artifacts didn't fall from above—they were made here, in some unimaginable past when this wasn't yet beneath the water, or by things that learned to work stone in the crushing darkness. Either possibility feels equally impossible.

The soundscape is a contradiction—simultaneously silent and full of noise. No single sound rises above the threshold of attention, but the cumulative effect is oppressive: the endless whisper of current against sediment, the sub-bass groans of tectonic adjustment far below, the tick and pop of thermal expansion and contraction, the occasional distant landslide that registers more as vibration than sound. It all blends into a kind of anti-silence, a presence of sound that contains no information.

This is a place where time moves in overlapping cycles. The fast time of the currents, constantly rearranging the surface sediments. The slow time of the bacterial mats, spreading and retreating over centuries. The deep time of the seafloor itself, compacting under its own weight, forgetting what it used to be. Everything here is both ancient and immediate, permanent and provisional, settled and unsettled. The water presses down with the weight of eternity, but within it, nothing ever stops moving.
