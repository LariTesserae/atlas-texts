# The Canopy City of the Last Mesa

High above where clouds once formed, atop a mesa worn smooth as river stone by millennia of wind, spreads a city that grows rather than builds. The plateau summit—no larger than a market square in the lowland cities that died ages ago—supports a vertical jungle of immense fungi, their caps layered like the floors of a tower that has learned to live.

The mushrooms are ancient beyond memory, their flesh woody and permanent as stone. The largest specimens rise forty feet, their caps thirty feet across, creating overlapping platforms of living architecture. Between and upon these natural terraces, the inhabitants have woven their primitive city: rope bridges of twisted mycelial fiber span the gaps, wooden platforms nestle in the natural bowls where spore-bearing gills once hung, and hollowed stems serve as vertical passages between levels. Everything is close, cramped, touching—a thousand people live in what amounts to a single grove, their lives layered vertically like sediment.

There is no water here except what the fungi themselves contain, sweated out in careful droplets during the dim morning hours when the sun barely penetrates the canopy. The mushrooms learned, over geological time, to mine moisture from the air itself, from the microscopic dew that condenses on their skin, from some deep chemistry that transforms minerals into liquid. The people harvest this fungal sweat with carved wooden cups, each family tending their assigned surfaces, scraping the precious moisture into communal vessels.

The fauna has adapted to this vertical world entirely. Colonies of blind velvet bats roost in the under-cap spaces, their guano feeding the smaller fungi that sprout like shelf brackets from the great stems. Millipedes the length of a man's arm process the constantly shedding skin of the mushroom flesh, their countless legs creating a constant rustling that never ceases. Web-spinning moths have evolved wingless, living their entire lives on a single cap-continent, their silk harvested for rope and cloth. The air thrums with the sound of boring beetles, fruit flies, and the whisper-flights of the membrane-winged lizards that hunt them—a constant biological static that rises and falls but never stops.

The mesa's edge drops away into nothingness. The erosion that ground down the surrounding land left only this thumb of rock, and the fungi that colonized it so long ago their origins are forgotten. The stone beneath has been completely consumed, replaced by a mass of root-like hyphae so dense and ancient it has petrified into something between wood and rock. The entire structure is more organism than geology now, a living fossil that has achieved its final form.

In the eternal twilight beneath the caps, lit only by narrow shafts of sun and the dim bioluminescence of certain symbiotic molds, the people work their simple crafts. They carve tools from mushroom wood with obsidian traded from travelers who climb the mesa's worn sides. They brew alcohols from spore milk. They train the velvet bats to carry messages between levels. Their hammering and scraping and calling creates a constant industrial murmur that merges with the insect drone into something like the sound of a great machine made entirely of living things.

The city grows denser each generation. New platforms are lashed between the stems, new hollows carved, new bridges spun. The vertical space fills incrementally with humanity and its parasites, predators, and partners. The mushrooms themselves stopped growing eons ago, having achieved whatever mysterious equilibrium they sought. Now they simply persist, neither dying nor truly living, while the warm air rises through their maze-like architecture carrying the sounds of beetle and baby, construction and decay, all of it cycling so slowly that a lifetime might pass without seeing any real change except the gradual filling of every available space with more life, more sound, more density.

At the edges of the mesa, where the caps spread beyond the stone into empty air, children dare each other to crawl to the very lip and look down through the gaps into the hazy nothing below, where the old world wore away to dust and left only this impossible island, this fungal city that forgot it was supposed to need water, this ecosystem so complete and closed that it might persist until the sun itself burns cold.
