The hollow sits thirty meters up in the mountain face, where ancient limestone has surrendered to centuries of patient dissolution. From outside, it appears as nothing more than a shadow among shadows, a dark comma in the cliff's grammar. But step through that narrow mouth and the world inverts itself.

Inside, the cave opens like a lung—vast, ribbed, impossibly empty for all it contains. The walls retreat into brightness that shouldn't exist this deep in stone. Every surface streams with light, not from any sun but from the living carpet that has claimed this space entirely: a phosphorescent moss that burns cold and silver-green, coating every inch of rock in radiance thick as fur. The moss grows in terraces and cascades, in pillows and veils, creating architecture from pure vegetation. It has built false floors that glow underfoot, woven bridges across the cave's deeper splits, formed luminous curtains that partition the space into a dozen smaller chambers within the greater hollow.

The air tastes green and electric. Cool moisture beads constantly on every surface—not quite rain, but a perpetual sweating of the stone as mountain water seeps through hairline cracks above, feeding the moss empire. Sometimes a true drip falls, and where it strikes, the moss flares brighter for hours.

This should be a place of stillness, but it churns with life. The fauna here exists in biblical proportions—not in size but in sheer numbers. Clouds of gnats form and dissolve like thoughts. Pill bugs carpet the moss in traveling rivers, their shells reflecting the vegetal light until they become streams of moving stars. Moths orbit in the upper reaches, their wings transparent as music. Centipedes process along invisible highways. Cave crickets sing in frequencies just below hearing, a subsonic pulse you feel in your bones.

And all of it—every insect, every arthropod—is in constant, catastrophic motion. The ecosystem reorganizes itself moment by moment. Watch any square meter of moss for sixty seconds and you'll witness three wars, five mass migrations, a dozen small extinctions and rebirths. Hunting spiders cause chain reactions of fleeing prey that ripple across the entire cave like wind through wheat. Columns of ants appear from nowhere, accomplish unknowable purposes, vanish into cracks that weren't there before.

The built remnants are so few they feel like accidents: three stones stacked in a cairn near what might be the center, though the cave's geometry resists such easy concepts. A piece of worked wood, soft with rot, wedged in a crevice—perhaps once a walking stick, perhaps a roof beam from some impossible shelter. Scratches on one wall that could be intentional marks or could be the trails of boring beetles. These traces feel overwhelmed, digested by the living place, like the last memories of human presence before the moss-light and the crawling billions inherited this hollow kingdom.

The only sounds are small ones—the tick of falling water drops, the whisper of ten million legs, the occasional soft collapse as the moss readjusts its own architecture. But these quiet sounds never stop. They layer and weave until they become a kind of silence made from infinite tiny noises, a white noise of life that makes the cave feel both muffled and electrically alive.

Sometimes the entire ecosystem synchronizes, as if responding to a signal only the insects can perceive. Every moving thing freezes for a heartbeat, two heartbeats. The moss dims slightly. The cave holds its breath. Then, explosion—everything moves at once, faster than before, a frenzy of reorganization that sends moths spiraling and beetles cascading from the walls like black rain. These storms of activity last minutes or hours, no pattern to their coming or going, the hollow convulsing with its own mysterious purposes.

This is not a place for humans, though humans could survive here briefly, breathing the thick green air, drinking the condensation, perhaps even eating the moss if desperate. But you would always be a guest in someone else's fever dream, a giant stumbling through a city built for citizens the size of rice grains, a slow monument in a world that remakes itself faster than your eyes can follow.

The mountain holds this secret chamber like a cupped palm holds water—carefully, temporarily, aware that eventually all containers fail. But for now, in this high hollow above the world, the bright moss and its trillion subjects build their turbulent paradise, moment by moment, death by death, birth by birth, in the green light that never goes out.
