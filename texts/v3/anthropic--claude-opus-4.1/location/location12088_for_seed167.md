The drowned city spreads beneath you in the warm darkness, its coral-crusted towers rising through layers of murky water like the ribs of some impossible beast. This is not the catastrophic flood of last year or last century—this drowning happened so long ago that the city has become geology itself, its angles softened by millennia of patient currents.

Schools of blind fish move between the buildings in perfect synchronization, their pale bodies creating fleeting constellations against the black water. They navigate by pressure and vibration alone, following routes worn smooth by ten thousand generations of their ancestors. The fish are everywhere—threading through glassless windows, spiraling up stairwells that lead nowhere, nesting in the cavities where wooden doors rotted away before your species learned to write.

The water itself is blood-warm and heavy with life. Not plant life—only occasional strands of some primitive algae wave from cornices like forgotten banners—but the water thrums with invisible animals. Microscopic jellies pulse in their billions. Worms no thicker than hair form living clouds around the towers' foundations. Everything here moves with tidal patience, responding to currents that take hours to complete their circuit through the city's canyons.

The streets below are buried under centuries of organic sediment, a soft blanket of decomposed bodies and filtered nutrients that releases occasional bubbles of gas—methane perhaps, or stranger chemistries—that wobble upward like inverted rain. Where these bubbles disturb the sediment, you glimpse the original stonework: blocks so massive they could only have been placed when this was dry land, fitted together without mortar in patterns that suggest purposes no living mind remembers.

The buildings themselves have evolved beyond their makers' intentions. What were once separate structures have fused together where limestone deposits bridge the gaps. Barnacle-like organisms—not quite animal, not quite plant—have colonized every vertical surface in dense colonies that filter the water with microscopic efficiency. They've grown so thick in places that doorways have become tubes, corridors have become caves. The city has become reef.

Sound barely travels here. The water muffles everything to a low throb—the collective heartbeat of a million filter-feeders, the whisper of currents through broken windows, the occasional crack of settling stone. Even the predators hunt in silence. Long, ribbon-like creatures with too many joints drift between the towers, their bodies rippling with patterns that hypnotize smaller fish. They feed constantly but lazily, just another circulation system in this drowned metropolis.

At what you might call street level—though the streets are buried forty feet below—the pressure would crush unprotected lungs. The darkness is absolute save for the occasional bioluminescent flash, usually a hunting lure or a warning. But the city extends upward through the layers of pressure and darkness until, somewhere far above, the last broken spires might almost kiss the surface, where water meets air and the memory of sunlight turns the black to merely gray.

This is not ruins. This is transformation. The city has been digested and rebuilt by the ocean's slow metabolism, every original surface now host to communities of creatures that think in chemical gradients and hunt by echo. The warm water circulates through it all like blood through an organism that was once a place where beings walked on legs and breathed air and believed their works would last forever.

Somewhere in the deepest foundations, in chambers that were old when the waters came, certain passages remain dry—air pockets held by the architecture's ancient cleverness or by pressures that defy understanding. In these bubbles of the before-time, strange blind things have evolved to breathe both air and water, crossing between worlds through flooded doorways. They feed on the bacterial mats that grow where water meets stone meets air, completing another unlikely circuit in this ecosystem of the drowned.

The city lives, though not as its builders intended. It breathes water now instead of air. Its inhabitants navigate by touch and taste rather than sight. Its streets flow rather than support. And in its warm darkness, ten million small hungers pursue ten million small satisfactions, while the great shape of what was once human intention dissolves a little more each year into something altogether stranger and more permanent.
