## The Vertical Works

The engine-pillar rises through mist, a single colossal shaft of riveted iron and breathing brass that has grown fat with condensation pipes, auxiliary turbines, and maintenance catwalks over untold decades. You stand on Platform Seven-Seven-Ascending, one of hundreds of small staging areas that barnacle the great column as it pierces upward through the cloud layer. The platform is perhaps forty meters across—room enough for the moisture collection arrays, the hanging gardens that drip perpetually onto grated flooring, and the thunderous governor mechanism that never, ever stops.

This high up, the warmth comes in waves from the pillar's core, where ancient furnaces digest something—no one remembers what—in a process that has run so long it has achieved its own equilibrium. The heat makes the abundant vegetation steam. Vines thick as bridge cables have woven themselves through the ironwork, their leaves broad as doors, sweating out the moisture they pull from the condensation systems. Moss grows in hydraulic green cushions wherever the spray from pressure valves has etched the metal soft. 

The sound is everything here. It comes up through your feet—the deep, tectonic grinding of the primary gears somewhere in the pillar's heart. It rattles the air—steam venting, pistons cycling, the crack and ping of thermal expansion in the metal bones. But loudest of all is the governor: a massive flywheel assembly that spins just overhead, its bronze weights whirling outward on worn arms, maintaining some crucial rhythm for systems a kilometer below or above. The noise is so constant it becomes like silence, so present it becomes absence.

Between the platforms—and you can see three others through the shifting mist, each at different heights, each crowned with its own impossible garden—there is nothing but empty air and the occasional transit cable. Birds nest in the machinery, but they're strange here: oil-sheened feathers, beaks evolved to prize apart coupling joints for the grubs that feed on lubricant seepage. They've learned to time their movements to the mechanical cycles, darting between piston strokes, riding the thermal updrafts that belt from exhaust ports.

When the rain comes—and it comes twice daily, when the cloud layer drops to kiss the platforms—everything streams with water. It runs through designed channels, yes, but also through the unplanned routes that centuries have carved: along vine stems, down chains, pooling in the reservoir cells that feed the gardens. The entire structure drinks during these moments, stores what it can, then slowly exhales the moisture back through its industrial pores.

At the platform's edge, where rusted safety rails have long since been replaced by woody growth thick as hawsers, you can peer down through the structure. More platforms descend into haze. Some are dark—their section of the pillar dormant or dead. Others glow with furnace light or arc-lamp brightness. A few have been entirely consumed by growth, transformed into vertical jungle pockets where the vines have won their slow war against the maintenance crews.

But no one maintains anything anymore, not really. The system maintains itself, or perhaps more accurately, has achieved a state where its decay and its growth have locked into balance. The machinery runs because it has always run. The plants grow because the conditions are perfect. The animals live here because their ancestors learned to live here. Even the rain feels scheduled, arriving precisely when the gardens begin to wilt, leaving precisely when the collectors reach capacity.

Far above, barely visible through the layers of mist and distance, the pillar continues its rise. They say it reaches above the clouds entirely, into thin air where the platforms become different things—solar collectors, maybe, or observatories, or something stranger still. Below, it descends until it meets the earth, though what kind of earth could support such a thing is difficult to imagine.

The governor spins. The gardens drip. The empty air between platforms holds nothing but distance and the occasional falling seed, drifting toward whatever platform will catch it, root it, and make it part of the eternal, deafening, warm, and ever-so-slowly changing machine.
