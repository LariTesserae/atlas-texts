The Vertical Works stretch upward through humid air, a manufactured mountain range of rust-bleeding iron and verdigris copper that rises thousands of feet from its sprawling industrial base. Every surface here was made by human hands, though most of those hands have been gone for decades. The original stone was quarried away completely, replaced strut by strut with a skeletal framework of bridges, platforms, catwalks, and towers that grew like coral until no natural substrate remained.

Steam rises constantly through the structure, condensing on the undersides of grated floors and dripping back down through level after level. The drainage systems, built from riveted pipes thick as tree trunks, gulp and gurgle as they channel water through the vertical maze. Some levels flood during the twice-weekly rains; others remain perpetually damp from the fog that rolls through the upper reaches. Puddles collect in the pressed-steel dimples of walkways, reflecting the harsh industrial lighting that burns day and night from caged bulbs strung along endless cables.

The vegetation has conquered every horizontal surface and most of the vertical ones. Vines thick as hawsers spiral around support columns, their weight calculated into the structure's load-bearing requirements by long-dead engineers who knew this would happen. Moss carpets the flooring plates in jewel-green luxury. Ferns burst from pipe joints. Small trees have rooted in the accumulated rust and leaf-mold that fills the corners of platforms, their roots working down through drainage grates to tap into the constant water flow. The plants grow with shocking vitality in the warm, greenhouse atmosphere, fed by the iron-rich runoff and the perpetual light.

The fauna has adapted to this vertical world with remarkable thoroughness. Tribes of monkeys navigate the girders with ease, their calls echoing metallically through the framework. Birds nest in the junction boxes and ventilation turbines, their guano white-washing the lower levels. Beetles the size of thumbs traverse the underside of walkways, feeding on the biofilms that grow in the perpetual dampness. Bats roost in the darker mechanical spaces between floors, emerging at dusk in clouds that briefly darken the industrial lights.

The machines still run, though their original purpose has been forgotten. Enormous turbines spin lazily in the depths, powered by the falling water that the structure collects and channels. Conveyor belts loop endlessly through the middle levels, carrying nothing but accumulated vegetation and the occasional animal corpse. Crane arms sweep through their prescribed arcs once every few hours, their movement so slow that vines grow unbroken across their paths, simply bending and recovering with each passage. The industrial sounds — clanking, humming, hissing — blend into a constant mechanical symphony that the wildlife has fully integrated into their communication, timing their calls to the rhythmic banging of automated hammers that strike nothing.

The scale reveals itself slowly. What seems like a single tower resolves into dozens, connected by swooping cable cars that still run on their ancient schedules and catwalks that span hundreds of feet of vertical space. The entire Works covers perhaps six square miles at its base, tapering as it rises like a manufactured mountain. The upper reaches vanish into low-hanging clouds that perpetually form around the peak, where the temperature differential creates its own weather system.

Changes happen slowly here. Rust advances by millimeters per year. Vines grow and die in decades-long cycles. Platform sections occasionally collapse with groaning surrender, creating new waterfalls and forcing the fauna to adapt their routes. The monkeys have worn smooth pathways on certain beams from generations of use. Paint flakes away to reveal older paint, which flakes to reveal primer, which flakes to reveal the patient advance of oxidation.

The brightest lights cluster at the mid-levels, where ancient sodium lamps cast everything in harsh amber. Higher up, chains of industrial bulbs create pools of white brilliance connected by shadows. The lowest levels glow with the emergency lighting that has somehow never failed — endless strips of green-tinted fluorescence reflected in the standing water that accumulates in the foundation sumps. During the rains, water cascades through the structure lit by all these sources, creating curtains of illuminated droplets that fall through spectrum after spectrum of artificial light.

The place hums with stubborn life, both mechanical and organic, neither quite natural nor fully artificial anymore — a vertical ecosystem built entirely by human ambition and abandoned to find its own equilibrium in the warm, damp air that rises endlessly through its corroding bones.
