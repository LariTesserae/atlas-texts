The valley spreads below you like a vast green circuit board, its terraced slopes thick with luminous moss that drinks electricity from the morning dew. Each terrace catches and holds the moisture differently—some as perfect mirrors of collected condensation, others as soft beds where the moss grows so dense it seems to breathe. The air is cool and clean, carrying the faint mineral scent of wet stone and ozone.

You stand at the rim of this young crater-valley, its edges still sharp where the earth split open perhaps a century ago, maybe less. The stone faces are unweathered, showing clean breaks in the granite where massive forces pulled the land apart. The valley is perhaps three miles across at its widest point, narrowing toward both ends like an eye slowly opening.

Industrial remnants punctuate the vegetation like sleeping giants. A turbine tower rises from a moss-cushioned slope, its blades long since frozen, now serving as perches for vines that glow faintly blue-white in the bright mountain light. The vines have learned to photosynthesize from both sun and the residual current that still trickles through the old infrastructure. Scattered across the terraces are other structures—a pump station crowned with ferns, transformer boxes split open and colonized by cushions of star-shaped succulents that cluster around the copper components, drawing trace metals into their tissues.

The vegetation itself is wrong in subtle ways. Too green. Too geometric in its growth patterns. The moss follows the old electrical conduits buried just beneath the soil, creating flowing patterns of darker and lighter green that map forgotten networks. Trees—or things shaped like trees—grow in perfect hexagonal clusters, their canopies so dense that beneath them the air turns purple-dark despite the brilliant light everywhere else. Their bark sweats a clear fluid during the cooler morning hours, which collects in natural basins formed by their interlocking root systems.

Water moves through this place in whispers. No streams or rivers, but a constant, almost imperceptible migration of moisture. Dew forms on every surface each dawn, beading on leaves that have evolved waxy coatings specifically to channel the droplets toward root systems. By noon, when the mountain sun burns strongest, this moisture has already sunk deep into the spongy earth or been drawn up through hollow stems into storage organs that bulge like botanical batteries.

The silence is profound, almost oppressive. No birdsong, no insect hum. The dense vegetation muffles even the sound of your own footsteps. Occasionally, you might hear the soft pop of a seed pod opening in response to humidity changes, or the nearly subsonic thrum of the old machines still cycling through their phantom loads, but these sounds seem to emphasize rather than break the quiet.

Walking the old service roads—now more green tunnel than path—you find the remnants of what might have been a geothermal plant or research station. The buildings are being slowly dissolved by the patient vegetation. Root acids etch through concrete, breaking it down into nutrients. The moss has developed a taste for insulation foam, spreading across walls in patterns that follow the thermal gradients of long-dead heating systems. In one structure, massive leaves press against windows from the inside, having grown through floor drains to fill the entire volume of what was once a control room.

The few animals that remain have adapted strangely to this quiet, bright, electric garden. Pale salamanders with translucent skin revealing organs that pulse with bioluminescent bacteria. Moths with wings that generate static charge as they fly, drawn to the transformer stations where they cluster on humid nights, their accumulated electricity creating St. Elmo's fire displays that dance across the old insulators. Once, just once, you might glimpse something larger—a deer-like shape standing perfectly still among the hexagonal trees, its antlers branching in patterns that mirror the electrical scarring on the old transmission towers.

Time moves differently here. The industrial ruins decay with impossible slowness, preserved by the cool mountain air and the strange chemistry of the plants that embrace them. The vegetation grows in spurts tied to electrical storms that pass overhead but rarely touch down, the plants somehow sensing and responding to the electromagnetic fluctuations. Seasons blur together in this eternal spring of bright mist and silent growth.

At the valley's heart, where the ground dips lowest, a small lake has formed—perfectly circular, perfectly still, its waters the color of cooling antifreeze. The moss grows right to its edge but never enters. Sometimes, on the brightest days, you can see straight to the bottom where more machinery rests, its purpose obscured by depth and time, its surfaces colonized by something that might be algae but glows too steadily, pulsing in patterns that suggest it's still receiving power from somewhere deep below.
