The Root Cathedral stretches beneath the permafrost for thousands of miles, a hollow world of living wood where gravity seems negotiable and scale becomes meaningless. 

You enter through sinkholes where the surface has given way, revealing glimpses of the pale luminescence below. The air tastes green and electric as you descend, following root-stairs that spiral down through layers of frozen soil until suddenly the earth opens into vastness — a cavity so enormous that clouds form near its wooden ceiling, thin wisps of moisture that catch the pervasive bioluminescent glow emanating from every surface.

The roots here abandoned their trees millennia ago. They grow now in pure architectural ambition, forming buttresses thick as city blocks, vaulted ceilings that span valleys, and delicate lattices that filter the strange light into cathedral geometries. Everything glows with that soft, relentless phosphorescence — a blue-white radiance that pulses gently through the wood's translucent outer bark, bright enough to hurt your eyes if you stare directly at the larger trunks. The light has no source; the wood itself burns cold, converting some unknown nutrient into this ceaseless illumination.

The space between these wooden architectures is mostly emptiness — vast halls of nothing where you could walk for days without touching anything solid. The roots favor the edges, the meetings, the joints between massive structural members. They leave centers hollow, perhaps by design, perhaps by some mathematical principle of their growth. Sound dies quickly in these voids. A shout might carry a hundred feet before the space swallows it.

But the emptiness is deceptive, always changing. Watch any patch of air long enough and you'll see it: rootlets emerging from nothing, growing at visible speed, reaching across gaps to form temporary bridges, scaffoldings, stairs. They build structures in hours that last days or weeks before retracting, dissolving, reconfiguring into new arrangements. The whole cavity reorganizes itself constantly — walls become floors, passages seal and new ones open, entire districts of root-architecture collapse and rebuild elsewhere. Maps are useless here. The only constant is change and that overwhelming light.

Water collects in the joints where roots meet, forming pools that reflect the bioluminescence in painful brightness. Sometimes these pools overflow, creating brief waterfalls that cascade through the void until they find another wooden surface to follow. The moisture keeps everything supple despite the cold — the wood never freezes, maintaining some impossible internal temperature that allows it to flex and grow even as your breath clouds in the chill air.

Scattered throughout are structures that don't belong to the roots — stone cairns, wooden platforms lashed with primitive rope, hollow-log drums covered in stretched hide. Someone lived here once, or perhaps still does, marking territories in this ever-shifting space with simple tools and patient craft. The structures cluster near the stable points, the few places where major root-pillars have remained constant long enough to trust. Charcoal marks on the wood suggest attempts at mapping, at understanding the patterns of change, but the marks are always partial, always abandoned mid-thought as their surfaces shifted away.

You might glimpse movement — something large and furred swinging between distant roots, or schools of pale flying things that navigate the voids between wooden masses, their bodies transparent except for the light they've absorbed, glowing faintly as they pass. But mostly it's just you and the roots and the terrible brightness and the sense that you're standing inside something's dream of architecture, something that builds without plan, without purpose, following only the ecstasy of its own growth.

The turbulence never stops. Even as you stand still, the landscape rewrites itself around you. A root thick as a subway car pushes up through what was solid floor, forcing you to move. Ceiling becomes wall. New chambers open like flowers. The bright light shifts and flows as surfaces reshape, creating a constant aurora of shadows and brilliance that makes your eyes water, makes judgment of distance impossible. You learn to move like the rare creatures here do — quickly, between stable points, never trusting any surface for long, always ready for the world to fold into some new configuration.

Sometimes, in the deepest parts where roots grow so dense they block their own light, creating pockets of relative darkness, you find the old workshops — stone tools half-finished, piles of worked wood, the remains of what might have been looms or instruments. As if someone tried to build a civilization here, in this bright chaos, and gave up or got swallowed or simply moved on, leaving only these scattered attempts at permanence in a world that refuses to hold still.

The cold is constant but not killing, the kind that preserves rather than destroys. Your fingers go numb but never frostbitten. The roots themselves are cool to touch but not icy, maintaining that strange internal warmth that keeps them supple. Sometimes steam rises from the points where new growth emerges, as if the act of creation itself generates heat that the vast hollow spaces immediately steal away.

This is a young world still learning what it wants to be, trying on architectures like clothes, building and unbuilding in a bright fever dream of botanical engineering, too alive to ever settle into mere structure.
