The city breathes steam through ten thousand vents.

Up here on the manufacturing terraces, the air hangs thick with condensation that never quite becomes rain. Water beads perpetually on the oxidized copper gutters and tarnished brass railings, trickling down through layers upon layers of industrial infrastructure built into the mountainside. Every surface weeps—the riveted steel plates of the factory walls, the glass panes of the growing halls, the endless pipes that thread between buildings like root systems made of iron.

This is a vertical city, stacked up the cool slopes of the mountain in terraces that were carved and built over centuries. The original stone has long since disappeared beneath the accumulation of structures—factories growing from workshops, towers sprouting from factories, catwalks and freight elevators and pneumatic tubes creating a three-dimensional maze of connection. Nothing here remembers nature. Even the mountain has become more machine than stone, hollowed out and reinforced, its granite bones replaced with girders.

The vegetation thrives in this eternal dampness, though it grows nowhere that wasn't planned. Vast greenhouse halls stretch along each terrace level, their peaked glass roofs clouded with moisture from within. Through the steamed windows, you can glimpse the green density—industrial cultivation packed tight, every cubic meter producing. Vines have been trained along the exterior pipes for decades, creating curtains of glossy leaves that drip constantly. Moss grows in the mortared seams between bricks, deliberately seeded and carefully maintained. Ferns burst from designated planters welded to the sides of water towers. This is agriculture as infrastructure, botany as engineering.

The sound is not silence but susurration—the constant whisper of water moving through pipes, steam hissing from valves, the low thrum of turbines converting mountain streams into power. Occasionally a factory whistle marks a shift change, or a cargo tram rattles along its elevated track, but mostly the city murmurs to itself in hydraulic whispers.

Everything moves here, though not with chaos but with overwhelming complexity. Crane arms swing loads between platforms. Elevators rise and fall in their iron cages. Conveyor belts carry materials through the sides of buildings and out again. Workers flow along the catwalks in streams that split and merge at junction points. Steam vents open and close in patterns. Even the buildings seem to shift—panels opening for ventilation, awnings extending and retracting, whole sections rotating on massive bearings to catch or avoid the dim light that filters through the perpetual mist.

The light itself is industrial—what little sunlight penetrates the mountain weather gets caught and reflected by millions of wet surfaces, creating a diffuse glow that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere. Electric bulbs burn yellow in the fog, creating halos. The greenhouse glass glows faintly green from the growing lights within. Navigation lights blink red and white along the edges of platforms and the paths of the cargo trams.

The fauna exists only in the margins—pigeons that have adapted to nest in the machinery, their calls lost in the mechanical breathing of the city. Sometimes a rat glimpsed between the pipes. The insects that pollinate the greenhouse crops. But this is a place that has pushed out the wild animals, replaced them with engines.

Water finds its way into everything here. It runs in engineered channels cut into every walkway, feeding into collection cisterns that honeycomb the mountain. It powers the turbines, cools the machinery, feeds the plants, and eventually cycles back up as steam to begin again. The entire city is a water engine, a vast mechanical metabolism that has replaced the mountain's natural watershed with something entirely built yet somehow still alive.

At the highest terraces, where the air grows thinner and cooler, the condensation sometimes freezes on the pipes overnight, creating curtains of ice that workers break free each morning with long poles. At the lowest levels, where all the water eventually flows, treatment facilities process the runoff before sending it back up through massive pump stations that thunder day and night.

This is not a place of earth and stone but of metal and moisture, not of growth but of production, not of quiet but of the endless whisper of steam and water and mechanical life. A city that has consumed its mountain completely, digested it, and rebuilt it as something functional, dense, and perpetually weeping in the cool air of the heights.
