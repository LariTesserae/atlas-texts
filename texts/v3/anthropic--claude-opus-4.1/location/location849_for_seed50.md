## The Singing Cistern

You descend through a crack in the mountain's throat, following the deafening roar that vibrates through your bones. The passage narrows until you must turn sideways, limestone scraping your shoulders, and then—suddenly—it opens.

The chamber blooms before you like a geode's secret heart, perhaps thirty meters across but impossible to measure in the chaos of water and sound. You stand on a narrow ledge of newly-formed flowstone, still soft as clay beneath your boots, watching as the mountain builds itself from the inside out.

This is a drowned womb of rock, a pocket where the summit's meltwater has found a weakness and begun its patient work of hollowing. But something is wrong with the physics here—beautifully, terrifyingly wrong. The water doesn't simply flow; it *migrates* through the air in braided columns, defying gravity in some places while surrendering to it utterly in others. A waterfall crashes upward from a pool below, splitting into a dozen lesser streams that spiral around each other before rejoining and hammering into the ceiling with such force that chips of infant limestone rain down constantly, adding their percussion to the overwhelming symphony.

The sound is everything. It's not just volume but complexity—a thousand frequencies fighting for dominance as water strikes stone at every possible angle and velocity. The chamber resonates, amplifies, distorts. You hear foghorns in the lowest registers where underground rivers punch through temporary openings. You hear glass chimes where droplets strike the few stalactites that have managed to form. You hear something almost like voices, though whether that's pareidolia or some acoustic phenomenon of the chamber's emerging geometry, you cannot tell.

The air itself is more water than gas. You breathe mist so thick it pools in your lungs. The temperature hovers just above freezing—cold enough that ice forms in fleeting crystals on the stone wherever the water briefly retreats, only to melt instantly when the next surge arrives. Your breath clouds and is immediately absorbed into the greater fog.

In this aqueous twilight, lit by shafts of mountain light that pierce through fissures above, you notice the structures. They're easy to miss in the violence of water and stone—small cairns stacked on whatever ledges remain stable for more than a few minutes. Primitive shelters made from fitted stones, no mortar, most of them half-dissolved or freshly collapsed. Someone has been trying to inhabit this impossible place. Stone tools lie scattered where the water has deposited them—crude hammers, something that might be a scraping blade. On one relatively dry shelf (dry being a relative term here), someone has carved symbols into the soft flowstone. The carvings are already blurring, eroding almost as fast as they're made.

The sparse vegetation that manages to exist here defies understanding. Pale, thread-thin plants like albino moss grow in perfect spirals on the ceiling, somehow thriving where the upward waterfalls strike with greatest force. A few aquatic ferns, species you've never seen, grow fully submerged in the aerial streams, their roots dangling in the flying water, their fronds waving in liquid wind. Where the mist is thickest, tiny blue-white flowers bloom directly from the air itself—or perhaps from microscopic spores that have found a way to be simultaneously plant and precipitation.

Movement catches your eye. Through the watery chaos, something swims-flies-glides. You glimpse it only in fragments: translucent flesh, too many joints, moving through water and air with equal ease. It builds too—you realize those cairns aren't human work. The creature (creatures? It's impossible to count in this tumult) is trying to shape this space even as the space shapes itself. You watch it carry a stone through a column of ascending water, place it carefully on a growing pile, only to see the entire structure swept away moments later by a shift in the water's impossible currents.

The chamber is being born and dying constantly. New flowstone formations emerge from the supersaturated water, growing visibly, millimeters per hour instead of per century. But they crack, dissolve, reform elsewhere. The floor (such as it is) is a treacherous landscape of pools that appear and drain in minutes, of stone bridges that crystallize from mineral-rich water only to collapse under their own weight. Nothing is stable. Nothing is permanent. Everything is becoming.

The light changes as clouds pass over the mountain peaks above, and suddenly you understand the true strangeness: this chamber shouldn't exist. At this elevation, with this much water, the cold should have frozen everything solid. But the violence of the water's movement, the constant kinetic energy, the way it seems to generate its own heat through friction and pressure—it maintains this impossible liquid state. You're inside a paradox of physics, a fever dream of geology.

Your ears ring. Your clothes are soaked. The ledge beneath your feet is already different than it was when you arrived, accumulated minerals making it wider but more fragile. You realize you need to leave before the chamber rebuilds itself around you, before you become another primitive artifact scattered in this deafening, nascent cathedral of water and stone.
