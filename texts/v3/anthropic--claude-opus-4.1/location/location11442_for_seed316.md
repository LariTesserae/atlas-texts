The Sounding Flats stretch beyond comprehension, a primordial wetland that has settled into its final form after eons of patient erosion. Water seeps everywhere here—not in rivers or pools, but as a universal dampness that saturates the ancient bedrock just beneath your feet. The stone itself weeps continuously through billions of microscopic pores, creating a surface that is neither truly solid nor liquid, but something between: a vast expanse of glistening, breathing mineral flesh that extends to every horizon.

The scale defies reason. This is not a place within a world, but a world that has become a single place. The curvature of the planet itself is visible in the way the wet stone plain gradually bends away into haze. Weather systems form and die within its boundaries—you can watch storms being born as specks in the distance, growing to mountain-sized thunderheads that crawl across the flats over weeks, dissipating before they reach you.

The sound is everything here. The weeping stone generates a constant roar—not of falling water, but of exhalation, as if the planet itself is breathing out through infinite throats. This base note never changes, but riding upon it are harmonics that shift and warble: the resonance of the whole flat acting as a single vast drumhead, picking up and amplifying every minute vibration. Your heartbeat becomes thunder. Your footsteps trigger cascading echoes that return transformed, minutes later, as alien songs.

The vegetation clings desperately to existence—sparse colonies of something like lichen, but animate, that cluster around the ruins. These growths pulse with their own rhythm, drinking the constant moisture, their surfaces iridescent with oils that help them slide across the wet stone when the sound-pressure becomes too intense. During the loudest episodes, they contract into tight, defensive balls.

The ruins themselves are mere suggestions—weathered nubs of what might have been walls, their material indistinguishable from the bedrock, as if the structures were exhaled from the stone itself in some ancient epoch and are now slowly dissolving back into it. They appear almost organic in their erosion, like worn teeth in an infinite jaw. No two are alike, and their spacing follows no pattern the mind can grasp, though the feeling persists that there *is* a pattern, just beyond understanding.

Reality grows soft here. The constant sound-pressure does something to space itself. Distances become negotiable—a ruin that appears a day's walk away might be reached in an hour, or never. The horizon sometimes inverts, showing you the flats from above while you still stand upon them. The synchronization between sight and sound breaks down; you see your footfall but hear it several seconds later, or before, or from the wrong direction.

The few creatures that exist here have evolved past recognition. They are more like mobile atmospheres than animals—zones of altered pressure and temperature that drift across the flats, visible only as distortions in the air and changes in the omnipresent sound. When one passes through you, your bones resonate at frequencies that make your teeth ache and your vision blur. They seem to feed on the sound itself, growing more substantial near the ruins where the echoes are most complex.

The warmth is oppressive but sourceless—not from any sun (which appears only as a dim smear in the perpetually hazy sky) but from the stone itself, as if the planet's core is very near the surface here. Combined with the moisture, it creates a greenhouse atmosphere that leaves everything slick with condensation. The air tastes of minerals and electricity.

Time moves strangely in the turbulence. Though the landscape itself is ancient and unchanging in its gross features, everything upon it exists in constant flux. The sound-patterns shift. The ruins seem to breathe, swelling and contracting over periods of hours. The sparse vegetation migrates in tidal patterns. Even the wetness of the stone varies in waves that take days to cross the visible expanse. You cannot step in the same place twice—not because the place has moved, but because what constitutes "place" here is always being renegotiated by the roaring, singing, infinite plain.

This is a landscape at the end of its story, yet somehow still violently alive—not with growth or decay, but with a kind of eternal, deafening presence that saturates every cubic inch of space until there is no room for anything but the sound, the stone, and the ceaseless weeping of an ancient world.
