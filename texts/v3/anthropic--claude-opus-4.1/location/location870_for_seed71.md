The plateau stretches beyond comprehension, a worn table of stone that holds entire weather systems in its shallow depressions. This is old rock, so ancient that the mountains it once was have been ground down to a nearly flat expanse, pocked with thousands of circular basins where meteors struck in ages past, each crater now softened to gentle bowls by eons of patient erosion.

The cold here has a peculiar quality — it shifts between different *types* of cold rather than temperatures, as if the concept of thermal measurement has worn thin. Sometimes it's the cold of deep space, sometimes the cold of forgetting, sometimes a cold that exists only in certain geometries of air. Your breath might freeze into crystals that fall upward, or condense into droplets that remember being clouds.

Vegetation thrives in defiance of logic. Thick carpets of something like moss — but more architectural, building itself in terraced cities of green — fill every depression where water collects. These plant-cities grow in precise mathematical patterns, their structures primitive in technology but sophisticated in organization. Wooden aqueducts no thicker than grass blades channel the intermittent moisture between districts. Tiny bridges woven from what might be roots span the thumb-width gorges between moss-towers. The settlements are uninhabited, or rather, inhabited only by their own growth.

Water here behaves strangely. It appears in pockets — sudden wells of perfectly clear liquid that seem to exist at different heights simultaneously, so that looking down into one might show you sky, or another part of the plateau from below, or sometimes your own face looking up. The rains come horizontally, sheets of moisture that travel along the plateau's surface like migrating walls, leaving the moss-cities glistening and the stone dark with wetness that evaporates in spirals.

The scale defeats perception. You might walk for days toward what appears to be a nearby cluster of the moss-cities, only to realize it's a distant forest of them, each individual settlement the size of an actual city. The curvature of the plateau is so gradual that horizons layer upon themselves — you can see tomorrow's weather forming a thousand miles away, watch next week's storms being born in the convection cells that the plateau's own thermal mass creates.

Sound barely carries here, muffled by the thinness of air and the way space itself seems stretched. The moss-cities grow in silence, though occasionally you might hear the crack of their wooden infrastructure expanding, a sound like someone very far away breaking kindling. The horizontal rains approach with a whisper that builds so gradually you don't notice until they're upon you.

The built environment extends beyond the moss-cities. Scattered across the vastness are structures of worked stone — not ruins exactly, but constructions that seem to exist in several states of completion simultaneously. A tower might be both half-built and half-destroyed, its stones arranged in patterns that suggest primitive tool work but achieve impossibly precise geometries. Causeways connect nothing to nothing, their paths describing mathematical curves across the plateau before terminating in mid-air, as if the destinations they once reached have been edited out of reality.

The weirdness accumulates in details: shadows that pool in corners regardless of light direction, forming depths you could reach into. Distances that change based on how you measure them. The way the moss-cities occasionally build perfect replicas of the stone structures, but at microscopic scale, so that kneeling to examine what looks like a patch of unusual growth reveals a complete civilization in miniature, with aqueducts carrying single drops of water through districts measured in millimeters.

Weather systems develop their own ecologies here. Clouds form and dissipate in patterns that mirror the moss-cities' growth, and sometimes the rain falls upward from the plateau into these clouds, pulled by inversions in pressure that shouldn't exist. Ice forms in geometric patterns during certain angles of sunlight, growing into delicate lattices that map invisible currents in the air before sublimating directly into vapor that swirls and rises.

This is a place worn down to its essential nature, so old that even the laws governing it have been abraded into something more fundamental, more strange. The plateau continues, vast and patient, its sparse features separated by distances that make them each feel like the only thing that exists, until you travel far enough to find the next pocket of impossible life, the next primitive-but-perfect construction, the next place where reality has worn through to show something else beneath.
