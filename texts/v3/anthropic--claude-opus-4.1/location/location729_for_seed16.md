The Furnace Garden lies buried beneath the salt-brick city of Khet-Amal, a vast hollow that breathes with its own fevered life. You descend through a crack in the foundation stones, where the builders long ago gave up trying to seal the fissure that opens and closes with the city's weight above. The heat rises immediately—not the dry heat of the streets overhead, but something thick and organic, like entering the mouth of a slumbering beast.

The chamber stretches perhaps forty paces at its widest, though its true dimensions shift in the murky amber light that seeps from phosphorescent fungi clustered along the ceiling's mineral veins. These growths pulse slowly, drinking something from the stone itself—not water, which hasn't existed here for generations, but volatile oils that weep from the rock when the city presses down. The oils feed everything here, this impossible underground jungle that has learned to live on petroleum instead of rain.

Crimson vines thick as ship's rope cascade from the ceiling, their surfaces slick with the same oils they consume. They terminate not in leaves but in hollow tubes that gasp and wheeze, drawing in the superheated air and metabolizing it somehow, exhaling clouds of metallic-tasting vapor that drift through the space like copper ghosts. Between them grow towers of what might once have been bamboo, now evolved into something that builds itself from crystallized tar, segment by segment, their hollow cores resonating with deep thrumming sounds as the air moves through them.

The noise never stops. The plant-things here have replaced photosynthesis with something like echolocation—they sing to each other in frequencies that rattle your teeth, mapping the space, finding pockets of different gases, communicating through vibrations that travel through their oil-wet roots. A constant chorus of clicks, groans, and low harmonics fills the chamber, punctuated by the sharp crack of seed pods exploding in the heat, firing their progeny into the walls where they'll either take root or combust.

The floor exists as a network of primitive walkways, built centuries ago when the first dwellers discovered this place. Rough planks of heat-hardened fungus span between islands of solid growth, where the root systems have formed something like land. The builders knew better than to try to clear the vegetation—it always grows back, and angrier. Instead they've woven their structures into it: simple rope bridges, carved handholds in the trunks of the tar-bamboo, platforms of woven vine suspended in the void between. 

Everything is sparse despite the lushness. The plants grow in desperate clusters around the oil seeps, leaving vast empty spaces between them—dark voids where the sound echoes strangely and the heat pools in invisible layers. You can walk for minutes through emptiness, guided only by the distant phosphorescent glow, before suddenly entering another pocket of fierce vegetative life fighting for its chemical food.

The city above knows about this place but pretends it doesn't. Sometimes a building's foundation cracks and its occupants smell the petroleum breath rising from below. Sometimes the sound-songs of the plants harmonize just right and windows shake three streets away. The primitive tools left behind—bone hooks for harvesting certain pods, stone bowls for collecting the oils, crude masks of treated leather—suggest someone still comes here, someone who's learned which plants produce useful chemicals, which vines can be milked for adhesives, which spores, when burned, keep the worse things that grow in the deepest corners from spreading toward the light.

The heat makes everything shimmer. The vegetation sweats oils that evaporate immediately, creating a constant haze that makes distances uncertain. Sometimes you think you see structures deeper in—more elaborate constructions, maybe temples to whatever process keeps this place alive—but the shifting air and dim fungal light make it impossible to be sure. The plants themselves seem to be building something, growing in patterns too regular to be natural, but too slow to comprehend. 

This is a place between places, neither fully of the city above nor entirely separate from it. It pulses with its own agenda, converting geology into biology through some accident of chemistry that shouldn't work but does. The emptiness between the clusters of growth feels intentional, like the vegetation is leaving room for something, or keeping its distance from something else. In the oil-drunk heat, listening to the endless choir of clicking, breathing plants, it's easy to believe this isn't random growth but cultivation—though what's being grown, and by what gardener, remains mercifully unclear.
