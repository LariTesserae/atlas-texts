The Brass Plateau of the Singing Swarms rises two kilometers above the surrounding badlands, a vast metallic shelf that shouldn't exist. Its surface—smoothed by millennia of wind-polishing—reflects heat in rippling mirages that bend space incorrectly. Sometimes the reflections show places that aren't there. Sometimes they show places that will be there, or were, or might have been if physics had chosen differently at this coordinate.

The air shimmers thick with heat, but it's the wrong kind of heat—dry and electric, tasting of copper and ozone. It emanates not from any sun but from the plateau itself, from the slow industrial fever dream churning in its depths. Deep thrumming rises through the metal ground: the pulse of enormous engines that have no operators, no purpose anyone remembers, no fuel source anyone can identify. They simply run, have always run, will always run, manufacturing nothing but their own continuation.

The fauna here shouldn't survive—there's no water, no vegetation, nothing to sustain biological processes as understood. Yet they thrive in impossible numbers. Swarms of crystalline insects the size of thumbnails fill the air in such density that walking through them sounds like pushing through walls of wind chimes. They're not quite alive, not quite mechanical. Under magnification, their bodies reveal themselves to be intricate clockwork, but dissection finds organs that pulse with something like blood, something like oil, something like liquid light. They feed on the heat differential between the plateau's surface and the cooler air above. They feed on the electromagnetic fields. They feed on the contradictions in local spacetime.

Larger things move among the swarms—angular beings that shift between two and three dimensions as they walk, leaving footprints that are sometimes circles, sometimes infinitely thin lines. Herds of them migrate slowly across the plateau's expanse, following magnetic currents that spiral up from the hidden machinery below. When they call to each other, their voices are industrial accidents—the shriek of grinding gears, the pneumatic hiss of pressure release, the electronic feedback of systems encountering themselves.

The horizon here extends too far. The normal curve of a world doesn't apply; the plateau somehow exists on a different geometric principle. You can see for hundreds of kilometers in the clear, dry air, but distance becomes negotiable. Sometimes a distant ridge is suddenly beneath your feet. Sometimes a nearby formation recedes as you approach, maintaining the same apparent distance no matter how long you walk.

The sound never stops. Beyond the constant drone of the deep engines, beyond the crystalline cacophony of the swarms, there's something else—a kind of tonal mathematics being calculated in sound waves. Harmonics that shouldn't exist overlap and create interference patterns that manifest as brief physical structures in the air: geometric shapes that exist for seconds before dissolving, architectural fragments of buildings that were never built, shadows cast by objects in parallel dimensions bleeding through.

Time moves strangely here, though slowly. The swarms complete their daily migrations over the course of weeks. The angular beings take months to cross from one edge of the plateau to another, though they never seem to move slowly when observed directly. It's only in retrospect that you realize how much time has passed, how the sun has tracked across the sky in ways that don't match your memory of motion.

Occasionally, great vents open in the plateau's surface—perfectly circular holes that appear without warning, exhaling columns of superheated air that carry the swarms up in helical patterns miles into the sky. Inside these vents, glimpsed for moments before they seal themselves again, vast machines can be seen: wheels the size of buildings turning against each other, pistons cycling through movements that hurt to track with human eyes, conveyor systems carrying nothing to nowhere with perfect mechanical precision.

The brass of the plateau isn't uniform. In places it's been worn down to reveal other metals beneath—copper veins, iron intrusions, mysterious alloys that change color based on viewing angle. These exposed areas attract the fauna differently. Some species cluster there in such density they form living mountains. Others avoid them entirely, leaving perfect circles of emptiness in the otherwise overwhelming biological cacophony.

At the plateau's edges, where it meets the lower badlands, reality becomes even less stable. The industrial thunder from below causes the boundary to vibrate, and in that vibration, things slip. Fragments of other worlds appear—a lake that exists for thirty seconds before evaporating into geometry, a forest of metal trees that grows and rusts to nothing in an afternoon, weather systems from planets that don't exist cycling through in fast-forward.

This is an ancient place that runs on principles older than biology, newer than physics should allow. It's a machine dreaming it's an ecosystem, or perhaps an ecosystem that discovered it could be a machine. The slow change here isn't evolution—it's revision, reality debugging itself, trying to resolve the contradictions that keep everything running.
