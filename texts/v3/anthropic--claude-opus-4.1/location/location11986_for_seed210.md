The Breathing Terraces

High in the mountains where the air thins and clouds drag their bellies across volcanic glass ridges, there exists a system of natural terraces that should not be. Each platform spans perhaps thirty meters—small enough that you can see across to where the next one begins, large enough to hold what grows there.

The terraces are young basalt, their edges still sharp enough to draw blood, unweathered by time. They step down the mountainside in perfect mathematical progression, but no intelligence shaped them. They simply emerged this way from the last eruption, as if the lava remembered some impossible blueprint as it cooled. Water seeps constantly from cracks in the black stone—not flowing, but weeping upward, beading on the underside of overhangs, condensing from air that shouldn't hold this much moisture at this elevation and temperature.

The vegetation grows in discrete clusters, each one identical. Not similar—*identical*. The same broadleaf ferns unfurling at the same angle, the same moss patterns spreading in the same fractal branches, the same flowering vines twisting leftward with the same number of leaves. A grove of twenty plants here, then empty wet stone for fifty paces, then the exact same grove again. And again. The repetition becomes nauseating once you notice it, like a glitch in perception itself.

Between the plant clusters, the fauna congregates. Too much fauna. Thousands of glass-winged insects hover in perfect grid formations, never landing. Salamanders the color of fresh coal emerge from cracks by the hundreds, their movements synchronized—all turning their heads at once, all breathing in unison. Birds that shouldn't exist at this elevation circle overhead in precise geometric patterns, their calls creating harmonics that make your teeth ache. The abundance is wrong; there isn't enough vegetation to support them all, yet they neither hunt nor starve.

The sound is constant—not quite natural, not quite mechanical. It's the noise of ten thousand small things breathing in rhythm, of water forcing itself through stone in regular pulses, of wings beating in frequency patterns that create standing waves in the mist. Underneath it all thrums something that might be machinery, might be the mountain's own circulation, might be both. Steam vents hidden among the ferns release pressure at exact intervals, their exhalations timed like a factory's rhythm, though no pipes or mechanisms are visible.

The light here disobeys. The sun, when glimpsed through the perpetual mist, appears to strobe—not clouds passing over it, but the light itself flickering like a failing bulb. Shadows fall in directions that don't match any sun position. Some terraces remain dim even at apparent noon, while others glow with sourceless twilight at midnight. Time moves wrong; water drops hang in the air for seconds before remembering to fall.

The most disturbing element is the stillness beneath all the motion. Despite the abundant life, the seeping water, the breathing steam, nothing actually changes here. The ferns never grow beyond their current unfurling. The salamanders emerge but never depart. The insects hover but never progress across the space. It's not death or frozen time—everything moves, constantly, vigorously—but all the motion adds up to perfect stasis. A waterfall of activity that never empties its source or fills its basin.

Sometimes, rarely, you might glimpse something like a gear or a piston formed from the basalt itself—not carved but naturally crystallized into mechanical shapes. These formations pulse with the same rhythm as everything else, suggesting that perhaps the mountain itself is some vast engine that grew rather than was built, its industrial heartbeat driving the endless repetition of life above it, caught forever in a productive cycle that produces nothing, changes nothing, begins nothing, and never, ever ends.

The air tastes of copper and ozone. The rocks hum with sixty-cycle current that has no source. The moss grows in hexagonal patterns that tessellate too perfectly. This place is both utterly natural and fundamentally impossible, a glitch in the world's programming where organic and mechanical, growth and stasis, abundance and emptiness have collapsed into a single, breathing, weeping paradox carved into the young mountain's side.
