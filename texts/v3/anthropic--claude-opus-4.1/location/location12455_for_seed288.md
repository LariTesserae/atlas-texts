The Mycelial Metropolis spreads beneath the world's crust, a vast urban sprawl that has grown without water for ten thousand years. Here, in these cool caverns that stretch for miles beneath the surface, an ancient city thrives on different principles than those we know above.

The buildings are not built but grown—towering fruiting bodies of specialized fungi that have evolved beyond any need for moisture. These pale structures rise like skyscrapers from the substrate floor, their surfaces tough as stone, pocked with windows that were once pores, now expanded into dwelling spaces. The architecture has the settled quality of something that found its final form millennia ago. Streets are not paved but worn into existence, compressed mycelial mats that have hardened into roadways through countless generations of traffic.

What appears at first to be emptiness between the fungal towers reveals itself as intentional space—broad plazas where the substrate has been kept deliberately clear, creating breathing room in this underground expanse. The city planners, whoever or whatever they were, understood the need for openness even here. The sparseness is architectural, designed.

The vegetation—if we can call it that—is everywhere despite the absence of water. Thick fungal forests carpet the walls and ceilings, their pale filaments creating hanging gardens of white and gray. Bioluminescent varieties provide what little light exists here, a perpetual twilight glow that emanates from certain cultivated patches. The fungi have adapted to metabolize minerals directly from the stone, breaking down rock into nutrients through acids and enzymes, creating their own slow erosion that has, over eons, carved out these cavern systems into their current ancient stability.

The sound is overwhelming—not from any single source but from the accumulated noise of the ecosystem itself. The fauna here click and scrape and sing in frequencies that echo endlessly off the stone. Beetles the size of dogs traverse the fungal towers, their chitin shells rattling. Blind pale things that might once have been mammals chirp in ultrasonic chorus from their roosts in the ceiling gardens. The fungi themselves contribute a constant sussuration—the sound of billions of hyphae growing, pushing through substrate, releasing spores. It's a white noise that becomes thunderous in its constancy, the sound of life processes that never learned to be quiet because here, in the eternal dim, sound became the primary sense.

The city's infrastructure is entirely biological. Transport arteries are hollowed-out fungal tubes through which the beetle-beasts carry goods and passengers. Communication happens through chemical signals released by modified mushrooms, creating an invisible network of pheromone messages that drift through the still air. Waste processing occurs in designated digestion pits where specialized decomposer colonies break everything down to base minerals, which are then redistributed through the fungal networks that lace through every structure like blood vessels.

Time moves differently here. Changes happen, but slowly—a new building might take a century to fully fruit, a road decades to properly compact. The inhabitants, whatever they are, must think in different timescales. Perhaps they are the fungi themselves, achieved some form of collective consciousness. Or perhaps they are something else entirely, adapted to this waterless world where all moisture is recycled internally, where all chemistry happens in non-aqueous solutions within sealed biological systems.

The edges of the city fade gradually into wild fungal plains, then to bare stone corridors that lead to other settlements, other mycological metropolises in this vast underground network. The scale suggests this is not unique—that perhaps the entire planet's subsurface has been colonized this way, transformed into a hidden civilization that thrives on principles entirely alien to the water-based world above, if any such world even exists anymore.

In the ancient quiet loud with life, in the dim bioluminescent twilight, in the cool, dry air thick with spores and chemical signals, the city continues its patient existence, neither growing nor dying, but simply being—a testament to the possibility of urban life without any of the elements we consider essential, yet teeming with its own version of abundance.
