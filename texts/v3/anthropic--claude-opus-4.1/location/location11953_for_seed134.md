The chamber floods and drains with the mountain's breathing—a geological lung that draws in the sea through porous volcanic stone, holds it suspended for seven hours, then exhales it back through hidden channels. You enter during the held breath, when water fills the space to just below the basalt ceiling, leaving only a hand's width of air where strange gases collect and shimmer.

The water here is wrong. Too clear, too heavy, with a viscosity like warm glycerin that makes movement both easier and more disturbing. It carries no salt, though the sea feeds it. Instead, it tastes faintly metallic, charged with dissolved minerals from the young rock that hasn't yet learned to resist erosion. The stone walls still show the columnar jointing of recent cooling, their hexagonal patterns precise as honeycomb but the size of temple pillars.

Creatures cluster here in impossible density. Not fish—nothing with bones could tolerate the water's chemistry. Instead, translucent organisms like living industrial machinery pulse and contract in the liquid medium. Some are colonial beings made of thousands of tiny pumps and filters, their bodies resembling the innards of combustion engines rendered in jellied flesh. Others are singular entities with copper-bright organs visible through their transparent skins, processing the mineral-rich water through biological factories that click and hum with mechanical precision.

The sound is overwhelming—not the muffled quiet of normal submersion but a cacophony of industrial noise transmitted perfectly through the dense medium. Clicking like ten thousand typewriters. Hydraulic gasps. The whine of organic turbines spinning in chest cavities. A deep thrumming that might be heartbeats or might be the mountain's own peristalsis as it prepares for its next exhalation.

Bioluminescent panels flicker along the creatures' flanks in patterns that mirror circuit boards, casting green-gold light that reveals the chamber's true scale—no larger than a modest catheral's nave, but packed with life from floor to ceiling. They hang in the water at all angles, some inverted, some sideways, oriented not by gravity but by electromagnetic fields generated by iron deposits in the basalt. Where creatures cluster too thickly, their combined heat creates convection currents that spiral lazily upward, carrying smaller organisms in helical paths.

The walls themselves are not passive. Young stone weeps constant minerals into the water through microscopic fissures. In places where the concentration grows too high, it precipitates out as metallic frost—copper and zinc and stranger elements forming delicate fractals that grow and dissolve over the course of hours. The creatures graze these formations with mouths like industrial grinders, incorporating the metals into their bizarre physiologies.

During the exhale phase, everything changes. The water drains through vents that open in the floor with mechanical precision, and the creatures collapse into dormant configurations, their bodies compressing into dense packages that lock into hexagonal recesses in the walls. For twenty minutes, the chamber stands empty except for puddles of that wrong water slowly evaporating into metallic mist. The air smells of ozone and hot copper. The stone steams.

Then the mountain inhales again, and the sea rushes back transformed, carrying a new generation of creatures or perhaps the same ones, reformed. No one has stayed long enough to determine which. The electromagnetic fields make most equipment useless, and human observers report headaches, tinnitus, and the persistent sensation that their blood has been replaced with something heavier, more conductive.

The locals have a word for this place that translates roughly to "the machine-womb" or possibly "the factory-dream." They insist it's natural, that the mountain has always breathed this way, but they also claim the creatures inside are less than a century old, born from the runoff of mining operations higher up the slope. Both things cannot be true, yet somehow, in this dense liquid space where biology has learned to mimic industry, or industry has emerged from biology, both things are.
