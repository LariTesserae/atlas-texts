The Nascent Peaks rise from absolute darkness, their substance still deciding what it means to be solid. This is geology in its birth-throes—not ancient mountains worn by time, but mountains being dreamed into existence by forces that haven't yet settled on the laws they'll follow.

The peaks themselves are warm to approach, radiating a dry heat that pulses like a massive heartbeat through stone that shifts between states. One moment a ridge is crystalline and sharp as fresh obsidian; the next it flows like cooling tar before suddenly fragmenting into hexagonal columns that immediately begin growing upward, adding another hundred meters to an already impossible elevation. The air is thin here, would be difficult to breathe if breathing mattered in this place where biological rules haven't been written yet.

There is no water—not even the memory of water. The chemistry here runs on different principles: currents of metallic plasma seep through porous rock faces, pooling briefly in depressions before evaporating into charged particles that drift upward like inverse snow. These particles gather into brief, unstable auroras that provide the only light—dim purple-green sheets that writhe across the peaks before collapsing back into darkness.

The darkness between these light-moments is profound, not merely the absence of illumination but something that seems to eat photons, to unmake the very concept of visibility. Yet even in this darkness, the mountains' turbulent growth can be felt through vibrations in the stone, heard as deep groans and sharp cracks that echo across the sparse valleys between peaks.

Scattered across this emerging landscape are structures—or perhaps proto-structures—that seem less built than precipitated from the chaos. A spiral of fused mineral that might have been trying to become a tower before the local physics changed mid-construction. A perfect sphere of some amalgam metal, thirty meters across, hovering exactly seven meters above a surface that keeps trying to grow up to meet it. What could be walls, or could be natural formations mimicking walls, arranged in patterns that almost make sense before the next upheaval rearranges them.

The scale is vast but comprehensible—from certain vantage points (though these shift constantly) you can survey perhaps fifty square kilometers of this birthing landscape. Mountains thrust up like seeking fingers, some reaching three, four, five thousand meters before collapsing or merging with neighbors or simply deciding to grow sideways instead. Between them, valleys form and reform, their floors covered in a dust of microscopic crystal shards that reorganize themselves into different patterns every few hours, as if testing configurations.

There is no life here—not as biology understands it. But there are patterns that might be trying to become life: geometric formations in the crystal dust that replicate themselves before dissolving, streams of the metallic plasma that follow each other in ways that suggest primitive communication, even the mountains' growth showing signs of responsiveness to each other, competing for space or sometimes synchronizing their upheavals.

The sound, when it comes, arrives in waves: long silences broken by sudden cacophonies of creation—the shriek of stone splitting, the bass rumble of entire ridgelines shifting phase, the high singing note of those metal spheres vibrating in harmony with some unheard frequency. Then silence again, but pregnant silence, the kind that makes you aware of your own heartbeat, if you have one.

This is a place being born, but being born wrong, or at least being born different. The rules are still wet, still malleable. Physics here is more suggestion than law. The warmth that radiates from every surface speaks of energy not yet settled into stable forms, potential still deciding what it wants to become. Walking here—if walking is possible, if the ground stays solid long enough—would be like moving through someone else's dream of what a world might be, before they've decided whether worlds need things like water, or life, or logic.

The horizon, when visible in those brief aurora moments, shows only more of the same: peaks rising and falling like waves in a storm-tossed sea of stone, the occasional geometric anomaly suggesting intelligence without revealing intention, and everywhere that sense of emergence, of something vast and strange coming into being according to principles that haven't been fully written yet.
