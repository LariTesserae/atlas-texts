The salt flats stretch endlessly beneath a bruised sky, their surface a vast mirror of shallow brine that has pooled here for eons. This is water's final resting place—not the violent rush of rivers or the restless heaving of seas, but water reduced to its most exhausted form: thin sheets spreading between crystalline ridges, never deeper than an ankle, never quite evaporating in the cool, humid air.

The land has been worn down to its bones. What were once mountains are now merely suggestions—gentle swells in the mineral crust that extend for miles before dissolving back into the wet plane. The erosion here is complete, absolute. Time has ground everything to the same level, creating a democracy of flatness broken only by the peculiar formations that rise like monuments to patience: salt pillars twisted into helixes by countless cycles of flooding and drying, their surfaces whorled with the fingerprints of millennia.

There is no green here, no brown of earth—only the spectrum between white salt and grey water, punctuated by patches of vivid orange and purple where extremophile bacteria paint their territories in the brine. These bacterial mats are the only thing that could be called vegetation, though they're more like living stains, spreading in fractal patterns across the shallow pools.

The fauna, however, thrives in overwhelming abundance. They are the brine flies—clouds of them, billions upon billions, their collective wingbeats creating a constant roar that fills the air like static. They rise in dark columns that twist and merge, their shadows racing across the flats like storm systems. When they settle, entire sections of the landscape turn black with their bodies. They feed on the bacteria, lay their eggs in the hypersaline water, and exist in numbers that defy comprehension. The sound is maddening—not quite a buzz, more like the white noise of existence itself, punctuated by the strange harmonic drones that occur when multiple swarms synchronize their movements.

Among the flies move the striders—creatures that shouldn't exist, with legs like needles that barely dimple the surface tension, bodies evolved to never fully touch the corrosive brine. They're hunters, patient and precise, picking off flies with tongues that shoot out like whips. Their movements create the only breaks in the water's mirror surface, sending out perfect ripples that travel for hundreds of feet before dissipating.

Scattered across this primordial sheet are the remnants of attempts at habitation—stone cairns built from salt-fused rocks, their purposes lost. Simple shelters made from the only available materials: salt blocks cut from the drier regions, mortared with mud from the bacterial mats. They're more like shells than buildings, hollow forms that whistle in the constant humid breeze. Tools lie where they were abandoned—primitive hammers with salt-crystal heads, scrapers made from the chitinous remains of some larger, extinct arthropod. Whether these were made by humans or something else is impossible to determine; the salt has claimed their makers' bones, dissolved them into the same brine that preserves their works.

The light here is perpetually dim, filtered through a thick atmosphere that never quite decides whether to rain or simply hover, pregnant with moisture. The sun, when visible, appears as a pale disc wrapped in halos—light refracted through billions of fly bodies and the constant haze of evaporation and condensation. Dawn and dusk stretch for hours, the sky cycling through muted purples and greys.

The air tastes of salt and something else—something organic and wrong, the smell of the bacterial mats when the wind shifts, sweet and rotten simultaneously. The humidity makes breathing feel like drowning in slow motion. Water beads on every surface, runs down the salt pillars in tiny rivulets that leave behind new mineral deposits, adding imperceptibly to formations that have been growing for millions of years.

Occasionally, the landscape convulses with strange tides—not driven by any moon, but by deep geological breathing, subtle shifts in the underlying rock that send waves across the flats. These pulse-tides bring up different minerals from below, creating temporary pools of cobalt blue or rust red that last for days before being diluted back into the general brine. The striders have learned to read these tides, gathering at the edges where the new water meets the old, where confused flies are easier prey.

This is a place between states—neither fully land nor water, neither dead nor alive, caught in an eternal present that stretches back to the earth's youth and forward to its eventual end. It's a landscape that has already arrived at its conclusion, yet continues, dynamic in its stasis, deafening in its simplicity, vast in its emptiness, crowded with its single successful form of life that drowns out all other possibilities with sheer, overwhelming presence.
