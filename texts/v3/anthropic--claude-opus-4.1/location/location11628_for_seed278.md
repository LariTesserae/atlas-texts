The plateau stretches beyond comprehension, a mineral shelf of weathered basalt that has known its current form for millions of years. In the dim perpetual twilight, distances become meaningless—what might be a nearby ridge could be a mountain range weeks away. The air is thin and cold, each breath sharp with altitude.

Across this vastness, ancient industrial remnants punctuate the stone like scattered teeth. Cooling towers rise in clusters every few hundred kilometers, their concrete forms worn smooth by ages of wind, no longer venting steam but serving as colossal cisterns that collect the sparse moisture from passing clouds. Between them, networks of elevated pipelines snake across the plateau, most long-ruptured, their steel bones oxidized to russet scales. Where they broke, slow drips have carved channels in the rock over centuries, creating chains of shallow pools that freeze and thaw with glacial patience.

The fauna has claimed every surface. What first appears as patches of lichen reveals itself as colonies of silicon-breathing arthropods, each no larger than a coin but numbering in the trillions. They've evolved to metabolize the trace minerals in the basalt and the rust of the old infrastructure. Their shells are translucent gray, barely distinguishable from the stone until they move in vast, coordinated waves—migrations that take decades to cross the plateau. They travel between the moisture points: the dripping pipes, the tower-cisterns, the morning condensation that forms in the shadows of ancient turbine housings.

The sound here is almost nothing—just the faint crystalline clicking of countless arthropod shells moving over stone, like distant rain that never arrives. Occasionally, when the wind picks up across the enormous distances, the hollow structures resonate with deep, nearly subsonic tones that take minutes to fade.

Along the pipeline routes, massive boring machines sit frozen mid-task, their diamond-tipped heads partially emerged from tunnel mouths they were cutting when abandoned. The arthropods have hollowed them out from within, transforming their mechanical spaces into hives. Some still contain pools of hydraulic fluid, now evolved into complex micro-ecosystems where smaller creatures photosynthesize using the dim light filtered through oil.

The few plants that exist grow only in the lee of the largest structures—sparse, woody shrubs with leaves like steel wool, their roots following the ancient water pipes deep underground. They grow perhaps a centimeter per year, their seed pods designed to be carried by the arthropod migrations, germinating only when deposited in the rare spots where three conditions align: shadow, moisture, and the chemical signature of decaying metal.

Time moves differently here. The arthropods' migrations follow cycles tied to magnetic fluctuations in the planet's core. The pools freeze and thaw not with seasons but with multi-year patterns of atmospheric pressure. Even the oxidation of the massive industrial ruins proceeds at a pace so slow it might as well be geological. 

In certain places where the pipeline networks converge, the abandoned infrastructure creates accidental architecture—cathedral spaces where the wind harmonics align, where condensation gathers in predictable patterns, where the arthropod colonies build their only permanent structures: towering spirals of secreted mineral paste that serve purposes unknowable to any observer, growing grain by grain over millennia.

The horizon is always gray, always distant, always suggesting more of the same—an endless table of stone and rust and patient life, where industrial ambition has become geography, where the boundary between the built and the natural has been erased not by destruction but by time's slow digestion.
