The salt flats stretch beyond sight, a vast mineral crust that formed only centuries ago when the ancient sea retreated through cracks in the world's foundation. The ground is polygonal—sharp-edged hexagons and septagons of crystalline deposit, each plate the size of a cart wheel, fitted together like a puzzle drawn by fever. Between these plates, dark water seeps constantly upward, never quite pooling, always filming the surface with a skin that tastes of copper and forgotten chemistry.

No plants grow here. No plants *can* grow here. The salts are wrong—not sodium but something else, something that builds its crystals in spirals that hurt to follow with the eye. Where the water evaporates fastest, these deposits climb upward in delicate towers no thicker than a finger, some reaching knee-height before their own weight brings them down. They regrow within days, the only quick movement in this patient landscape.

The air is cool and thick with moisture that never quite becomes rain. Instead, it condenses on every surface in beads that roll together, growing heavy, creating a constant percussion of droplets falling back into the cracks between plates. This is the sound that fills the space—millions of drops per minute, a white noise roar like static between radio stations, punctuated by the occasional crack of a salt tower collapsing or a plate settling deeper into its bed.

Scattered across this expanse are the shells. That's what the locals who avoid this place call them, though they're not calcium or chitin or anything that was ever alive. Smooth basalt domes, each one different, erupted from below when the flats were young. Most are small—a person could step over them—but every few hundred meters rises a larger one, hollow, with openings that face seemingly random directions. The wind plays across these openings, adding low drones to the water's static. Inside, the walls are glazed with the same spiral salts, but here they've formed patterns that look almost intentional, almost like writing, if writing could exist before language.

The few creatures that cross this place are translucent. Jellyfish-things, though they float through the humid air rather than water, trailing tendrils that collect the condensation. They move so slowly they seem like mirages, and perhaps they are—no one has ever touched one. They appear most often at the boundaries between temperature zones, where the cool air meets pockets of warmth rising from deeper cracks. Birds avoid the flats entirely. Insects turn back at the edges.

At the horizon in three directions, low mesas mark the boundaries of this depression, their faces still sharp and unweathered, cut clean when the ground here dropped. To the fourth direction, the flats continue past the curve of the earth. Sometimes, on the clearest days when the condensation thins, observers claim to see structures out there—tall things that might be natural salt formations grown immense, or might be ruins of something that should never have been built from mineral at all. The sound grows louder in that direction, they say. Not more drops falling, but something underneath the white noise. A rhythm, perhaps. Or breathing.

The water that seeps up everywhere is fresh enough to drink, if you can stand the metallic aftertaste and the way it makes your thoughts feel slippery for hours afterward. It never runs out. It never increases. It maintains its steady weeping through the crystalline ground, feeding the spiral towers, feeding the floating jellies, feeding the patterns that grow inside the basalt shells, feeding whatever process makes this place hum with its constant, almost-musical static.

Time moves strangely here. The sun crosses the sky, but shadows don't quite track its movement properly. Compass needles spin lazy circles. The only reliable measure of duration is the growth of the salt spirals—always the same rate, always upward, until they fall.
