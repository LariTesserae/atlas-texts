## The Singing Hollow

Deep beneath the frost-locked earth, where ancient roots have carved their patient channels through bedrock, there opens a cavity no larger than a barn. You must crawl to enter it, following the trickle-sound that builds to thunder as the passage narrows, then suddenly releases you into vastness—though a vastness contained, a pocket of impossible space where sound becomes architecture.

The hollow breathes with water. Not a flood, not a stream, but the eternal seepage of mountain-melt through limestone galleries above, each drop's journey taking decades to arrive. The walls weep continuously, their surfaces slick with bacterial films that gleam faintly phosphorescent in the absolute darkness—not enough to see by, only enough to suggest the presence of surface, of boundary, of form. These films grow thick as velvet in places, forming hanging curtains of living slime that trap moisture and release it in irregular rhythms, so the air itself seems to pulse between desert-dry and fog-thick.

But it is the sound that rules here. Every drop that falls—and thousands fall each minute—strikes a different surface: stone worn to varying thinness, pools of different depths, carpets of moss, the occasional rusted tool left by whoever once worked this place. The acoustic physics of the hollow transform these individual notes into a continuous roar that has no source, no direction. It comes from everywhere at once, a white noise so complete it becomes tangible, pressing against eardrums and chest cavity alike. The sound has worn smooth channels in the soft limestone, carved by nothing but centuries of its own echo.

Vegetation thrives in this sunless cacophony. Pale ferns unfurl from every crevice, their fronds thick and waxy, growing toward no light but toward minute temperature differentials, toward the memory of warmth from the world above. Rootless orchids hang in the void, their aerial tendrils catching moisture from the air, their blooms—unseen, unpollinated—releasing perfumes that will never find their intended insects. Moss grows in three-dimensional clouds, suspended on frameworks of its own dead generations, creating soft baffles that do nothing to dampen the omnipresent sound.

The scattered evidence of human presence feels almost accidental: iron spikes driven into walls at irregular intervals, their purpose forgotten; a wooden platform, now mostly rotted, that might have been a workspace or shrine; clay vessels that collect specific drips, their placement suggesting someone once mapped the acoustic properties of each falling note. These artifacts feel less like ruins and more like the hollow has been slowly digesting them, incorporating them into its fundamental architecture.

The fauna that does exist here has evolved beyond sight entirely. Blind cave fish surface in the deeper pools, their lateral lines so sensitive they navigate by the sound-shadows cast by stalactites. Pallid centipedes, each as long as a forearm, move in perfect silence against the roar, their bodies dampening vibrations as they hunt by touch. Occasionally, a lost bat spirals down from the upper passages, its echolocation useless in the acoustic chaos, doomed to exhaust itself against the echoing walls.

The cold here is patient, geological. Not the sharp bite of winter but the steady, underlying chill of depth, of removal from solar influence. It preserves things: the wooden platforms decay at a crawl; iron rusts but never quite crumbles; the phosphorescent bacteria maintain their glow for centuries after death. Even sound seems preserved here, each echo joining an eternal chorus that began when the first water found this void.

Time moves differently in the hollow. The slow dissolution of limestone, the gradual accumulation of flowstone, the patient growth of eyeless ferns—all these processes continue at their near-imperceptible pace while the sound roars on, unchanging, a constant present tense that makes past and future irrelevant. Sometimes the water flow increases—spring melt far above—and the pitch shifts slightly higher. Sometimes it decreases—drought years—and the harmony deepens. But these changes happen over months, years, too gradual for any visitor to perceive.

This is a place between places, a forgotten organ in the earth's body where water and sound have found their own metabolism, their own logic. It exists because erosion discovered a particular weakness in the stone, because ancient roots opened the first channels, because the precise shape of the cavity creates this acoustic phenomenon. It needs nothing from the outside world except the steady donation of water, and in return it offers only its sound—a roar that no one hears, a song that plays for the blind fish and the pale ferns, for the rust and the moss, for itself.
