The city breathes steam through ten thousand vents.

High on the volcanic plateau, where the rock runs with mineral veins of sulfur-yellow and arsenic-green, the settlement spreads like a growth of geometric coral. Every surface weeps. The air itself sweats, condensing on the warm basalt towers that rise hundreds of feet into the perpetual haze. There are no plants here—never were—only the endless black glass of ancient lava flows, polished by centuries of thermal moisture into mirrors that reflect nothing but gray.

The builders understood water's nature here: how it rises as vapor from the deep thermal aquifers, how it clings to every surface, how it runs in sheets down the grooved facades they carved. The city is all channels and cisterns, gutters and collection pools. Steam turbines turn lazily in the depths of every district, their brass teeth clicking in the wet heat. The sound carries strangely through the heavy air—mechanical whispers that seem to come from everywhere and nowhere.

Between the towers: vast emptiness. The streets are boulevards wide enough for small weather systems to form. You can walk for twenty minutes through curtains of warm drizzle without seeing another structure, following the elevated stone causeways that span the shallow thermal marshes. The water below is the color of rust, stained by iron deposits, too hot and mineral-rich for any life as we know it. Sometimes it bubbles. Sometimes it simply steams.

The few creatures that exist here are wrong in fundamental ways. Silicate shells that inch across the wet walls, feeding on mineral deposits. Things with too many joints that skitter through the steam vents, vanishing before the eye can properly focus. In the collection pools, movements that might be automated cleaning systems or might be something older, something that learned to live on sulfur and heat before the city ever rose.

The light never quite arrives. The sun is a suggestion behind layers of vapor, a source of warmth more than illumination. The city generates its own dim glow—phosphorescent minerals in the building stones, excited by the constant moisture. Electrified streetlamps burn orange through the haze, their filaments struggling against the thick air. In the industrial quarters, forge-light escapes through ventilation grates, painting the steam in brief moments of hellish red.

The factories process what the earth gives up: rare minerals dissolved in the thermal waters, crystallized and refined in vast evaporation chambers. Centrifuges spin in warehouse-caverns, separating elements by weight. The work is largely automated—chains and pistons and gear-trains that maintain themselves, or are maintained by things that prefer not to be seen. The human workers, what few remain, wear suits of waxed canvas and brass, their faces hidden behind glass masks that fog constantly.

At the city's heart stands the Condensation Cathedral, a massive structure of black volcanic glass whose every surface is carved with microscopic channels. Water runs down its faces in perfect sheets, collecting in the reservoir below where the city's drinking water is stored. The sound it makes is almost musical—a thousand tiny waterfalls harmonizing in frequencies that make the teeth ache.

The weather here is internal, personal. Each building creates its own microclimate. Step from the street into a dwelling and the temperature might drop twenty degrees, the humidity shifting from tropical to merely damp. The wealthy live in towers tall enough to rise above the worst of the steam-layer, though even there, the walls weep constantly, and mold—or something like mold, something crystalline and patient—grows in corners where the cleaning machines cannot reach.

This is a place that exists in the space between states—neither fully liquid nor gas, neither truly alive nor dead, neither entirely built nor entirely geological. The city and the plateau have grown together over centuries into something singular, a humid machine for living that runs on heat and pressure and the strange chemistry of a world that took a different path.

In the early morning, when the thermal cycles slow and the steam thins slightly, you can sometimes see across the entire plateau—tower after tower receding into the gray distance, each trailing its own column of vapor, a forest of breathing stones reaching toward a sun that never quite burns through. The sight induces a particular vertigo, a sense of looking at something that shouldn't exist but insists on it anyway, perpetually dissolving and reforming in the wet, hot air.
