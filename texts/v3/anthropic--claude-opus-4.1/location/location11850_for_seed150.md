The vertical city grows from ice.

In this dim gorge between peaks, where glacial melt weeps constantly down black stone walls, an entire civilization has sprouted from plant matter alone. The settlement clings to a single frozen waterfall, perhaps three hundred feet high, its ice milky green with trapped algae. Around this central column, the plant-city has woven itself into existence.

There are no buildings here, not in any conventional sense. Instead, massive fungal shelves spiral up the ice in terraces, each one carpeted thick with sphagnum moss that has learned to grow in organized rectangles—rooms without walls. The moss is so dense it springs back underfoot like mattresses. Between these living floors, hollow-stemmed bamboo-like grasses have grown in perfect vertical bundles, forming pillars that support the next level up. The plants grow with an architectural intentionality that seems impossible, yet here it is: corridors of woven vine, stairs of hardened root systems, bridges of interlaced branches dripping with condensation.

The cold should kill everything, but the plants generate their own warmth. You can see it in the constant mist that rises from their packed masses, in the way frost forms and melts and forms again on their outer leaves while their cores pulse with metabolic heat. The vegetation is so dense that walking through the lower levels means pushing through curtains of hanging rootlets and air-roots, breathing in the humid green smell of chlorophyll working overtime in the permanent twilight.

Water is everywhere but never pooling. It sheets down the outer vine-walls, trickles through the hollow grass-pillars with a sound like whispered conversations, drips from every surface in a rhythm that becomes the city's heartbeat. The plants drink it all, process it, breathe it out as fog that makes the air thick enough to taste.

At the settlement's highest reaches, where the ice waterfall narrows to a point, luminescent lichens paint the frozen surface in patches of ghostly blue-green. This is the only light beyond what filters down gray and weak through the mountain mist. The lichens pulse slightly—a slow breath of brightness that takes minutes to complete—and by their glow you can make out the shapes of the few creatures that live here: transparent beetles the size of thumbs that tend certain plants, pruning them into shape, and occasionally a white salamander moving through the water channels within the walls.

The strangest thing is the coordination. When wind comes howling down the gorge, entire sections of the plant-city will shift and lock together, vines tightening, leaves overlapping like scales, the whole structure becoming aerodynamic. When the temperature drops further at night, flowering vines close their blooms in unison, trapping pockets of warm air. The city breathes, adjusts, repairs itself. Dead matter is immediately consumed and reformed. New growth follows patterns that maintain streets, maintain spaces, maintain what can only be called neighborhoods of different plant architectures.

At the center of it all, the frozen waterfall creaks and sings, its ice so old and compressed it's turned the blue-black of deep ocean. Sometimes, looking at it through the green filter of all that living mass, you swear you can see shapes in the ice—root systems, maybe, penetrating impossibly deep into the frozen water, drawing something out that feeds this whole impossible place. The sound they make growing, these ice-drinking roots, is like the crack of joints in winter, barely audible under the whisper of moving water and the soft respiration of a city made entirely of vegetation, thriving in the cold.
