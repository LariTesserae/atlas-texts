## The Clicking Galleries

Deep beneath a salt crust that hasn't seen rain in geological ages, a warren of fresh-cut chambers extends through warm mineral deposits. The walls are sharp-edged, almost crystalline, carved by something that left perfectly hexagonal bore-holes the width of train tunnels. These passages intersect and diverge without apparent pattern, creating a three-dimensional maze perhaps a quarter-mile across, compressed into overlapping levels separated by tissue-thin floors of compacted mineral.

The air tastes of copper and static electricity. A dim phosphorescence seeps from the walls themselves—not quite light so much as a lessening of dark, a bruise-colored glow that makes distances hard to judge. Industrial detritus dots the passages: corroded gear assemblies fused into the walls like fossils, pipes that branch and rebranch until they become capillary networks of metal threading through the stone, the occasional turbine still spinning despite no apparent power source, its blades worn to knife edges.

But it's the inhabitants that make the space truly alien. They cluster everywhere—ceiling, walls, floor—packed so densely that the surfaces appear to writhe. Metallic beetles the size of housecats, their shells patterned with circuit-board traces that pulse with bioluminescent signals. They've evolved (or been designed) to feed on the mineral walls, their mandibles containing microscopic boring equipment that sounds like ten thousand tiny jackhammers when they feed en masse. The noise is constant, deafening—a chittering, grinding, clicking symphony that makes thought difficult.

The beetles never age, never die, never change their routines. They bore precise channels following mathematical patterns, extracting trace metals that they incorporate into their shells, which grow in elaborate spirals and fractals. When too many accumulate in one chamber, they simply begin eating through to create new spaces, the warren gradually expanding downward and outward but never upward, as if obeying some hardcoded instinct about the deadly dry surface above.

Occasionally you find a factory node—a chamber where ancient machines have partially merged with the beetle colony. Here, the creatures deposit refined metals into hoppers that feed into still-functional presses and lathes, producing components for unknown machines. The beetles incorporate these parts into their own bodies or cement them into the walls in patterns that might be functional or might be purely instinctive art. Steam vents hiss from cracks where the industrial machinery's waste heat meets pockets of mineral moisture, creating a dense, warm fog that tastes of iron.

The entire space exists in perfect, horrifying equilibrium. The beetles breed at exactly the rate they accidentally crush each other in the packed tunnels. The machines produce at exactly the rate the beetles consume. The bore-holes expand at exactly the rate the mineral walls regenerate through some unknown geological process. Nothing changes. Nothing ever will. The clicking is eternal, the warm darkness absolute, the density of life overwhelming yet entirely purposeless—a pocket universe running on autopilot long after its designers have vanished, leaving only their evolutionary algorithms and industrial infrastructure to grind on forever in the suffocating deep.
