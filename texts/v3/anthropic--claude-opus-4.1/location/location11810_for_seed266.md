The Bone Terraces rise in endless steps toward a sun that never moves, each plateau a calcified shelf of what was once ocean floor, now lifted miles into dry air. The stone is porous coral limestone, cream and grey, riddled with the fossil chambers of creatures that lived when this was seabed—now their hollows whistle faintly when the thermal winds pass through, a susurration that travels for hundreds of miles across the ascending plateaus.

Cities nest in the sheltered corners where terrace meets terrace wall, built from the same coral stone they rest upon, carved directly into the cliffs or assembled from precisely cut blocks that blur the boundary between geological and architectural. The buildings spiral up the vertical faces, connected by iron catwalks and cable cars that traverse the gaps between levels. Steam vents from geothermal plants deep in the rock, the only moisture in this desiccated atmosphere, quickly dissipating into the warm, thin air.

The fauna have adapted to this vertical desert through strange symmetries. Sail-backed climbers with vacuum-pad feet traverse the cliff faces in slow migrations that take months to complete. Their metabolism runs on iron oxidation—they graze the rust-red bacterial films that grow where ancient pipes have broken through the stone. Thermal-riding arthropods the size of dinner plates drift between terraces on wings of translucent chitin, barely moving their limbs, carried by the predictable updrafts. In the fossil chambers within the rock, colonies of something between ant and architect have built cities of their own from secreted mineral paste, their structures glowing faintly with bioluminescence through the porous stone at night.

The industrial infrastructure threads through this vastness like veins of darker stone. Pneumatic tubes run for thousands of miles through tunnels bored straight through the terraces, carrying messages and small goods between the scattered city-nodes. Mining elevators descend through the geological layers, seeking rare earth elements concentrated in specific strata. The factories are built into natural caverns within the terraces, their machinery powered by the temperature differential between the sun-baked upper surfaces and the cool depths. They process the stone itself—extracting chemicals from the ancient coral, manufacturing ceramics, producing the strange proto-plastics that form when certain fossil deposits are pressure-treated.

The scale defies comprehension. A single terrace might stretch beyond the horizon, wide enough that weather systems form and dissipate across its surface—dust devils, thermal columns, even dry lightning storms that flicker between the iron-rich zones. The climb from one major level to the next could take days by cable car, weeks on foot through the carved switchback roads. Looking up from any city, the terraces above fade into atmospheric haze, each one slightly warmer than the last as they climb toward the stationary sun. Looking down, if you dare peer over the edges, the lower levels descend into shadow and cooler air, where different creatures dwell, where the cities grow denser and more ancient, where the fossil record tells of earlier oceans, earlier worlds.

The quiet is profound. Sound doesn't carry well in the thin atmosphere, and the vast spaces swallow noise. The cities themselves generate only a low industrial hum—the factories run on pneumatic pressure and thermal cycles rather than combustion engines. Conversations happen in whispers, in sign language, in the coded pulses of light between towers. The only persistent sound is the wind through the fossil chambers, that endless whispered note that changes pitch with the temperature, with the time of day that never changes, with the slow breathing of a world that turned its ocean to stone and lifted it toward an unblinking sun.

At the boundaries between terraces, where the rock faces are sheer and tremendous, strange ecosystems flourish. The bacterial films form patterns like circuit boards, metabolizing the minerals, creating zones of electrical potential that the sail-backed climbers navigate by. Flying creatures nest in the fossil chambers, their guano forming nitrate deposits that the cities mine for chemical feedstock. The industrial and the biological have found an equilibrium here, neither truly alive nor dead, both following the patient cycles of a world that measures change in geological time, where a city might stand for ten thousand years and still be considered new against the ancient lifted seafloor, where the only rain is the slow settling of dust from the terrace above.
