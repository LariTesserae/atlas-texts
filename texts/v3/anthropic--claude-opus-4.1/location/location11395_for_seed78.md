# The Singing Canopy

You emerge into darkness that breathes. Above—though "above" hardly captures the sensation—stretches a living vault so vast that it generates its own weather systems, its own gravity of presence. This is the understory of something that defies the word "forest."

The trees here began their reach skyward ten thousand years ago, perhaps more, their trunks the width of city blocks, ascending into blackness. But these are not trees as you know them. They are hollow towers of living wood, their cores given over to emptiness, their walls riddled with apertures that sing in the high mountain winds. Each trunk rises five hundred meters before the first branches emerge, and those branches themselves are ecosystems, highways, worlds.

The darkness is not absolute—it pulses with bioluminescent blooms that drift like snow from the invisible canopy above. These are seeds, or spores, or something between, each one glowing with a pale green light that dies after an hour of falling. They descend through air so thick with moisture it feels like breathing through silk. The condensation gathers on everything, beading on the fur-like mosses that coat every surface, dripping in irregular rhythms that echo through the hollow trunks.

The sound is overwhelming. Not from any single source, but from the accumulated chorus of distance and space. The hollow trees act as massive organ pipes, resonating with wind from the peaks above the canopy. Lower frequencies you feel in your bones—the groaning of wood that has carried mountain storms for millennia. Higher notes come from smaller apertures, from the breathing holes that pocket the bark, each trunk a woodwind instrument played by altitude and pressure differential. And threading through it all: the calls of things that live here in the eternal twilight.

Movement catches your peripheral vision constantly. The fauna here have evolved for a world of vertical territories and vast empty spaces between wooden cliffs. Gliding membranes are common—you glimpse something the size of a bear launch itself from a branch overhead, its skin stretched taut as it navigates the void, steering toward a trunk a quarter-kilometer away. Colonies of climbing things flow up and down the bark in rivers of fur and claw, their whistles and chirps adding layers to the symphony. In the depths of the hollow trunks, you hear larger things moving, using the internal spaces as highways between altitudes.

The vegetation is omnipresent and architectural. Beyond the titan trees, every surface bursts with epiphytic gardens. Vines thick as bridge cables span the spaces between trunks, sagging under the weight of their own ecosystems. Some have calcified over centuries into rigid walkways that the climbing creatures use as bridges. Bracket fungi emerge from the bark in terraces, each one supporting pools of condensation where aquatic plants grow in aerial ponds. The air itself seems thick with floating gardens—masses of unrooted plants that drift between the trunks, photosynthesizing from the dim light, their trailing roots catching nutrients from the humid air.

Occasionally, you find traces of intention, of construction. Platforms of woven branches, ancient and overgrown but unmistakably made. Stone tools embedded in bark where they were forgotten centuries ago, now half-absorbed by the growing wood. Hollows carved into the living trunks, expanded into small chambers with windows that frame the voided darkness. Steps cut into the rising roots, worn smooth by generations of passage. The builders might still be here, might be some of those distant calling voices, or might have become something else entirely in their adaptation to this vertical world.

The cold seeps through the moisture, a mountain chill that the living cathedral cannot quite keep at bay. Ice forms in the highest reaches you can see, creating frozen waterfalls where the condensation streams down bark channels. Your breath mists constantly, mixing with the spore-light and the drifting fog that moves in its own slow weather patterns through the colony of trunks.

As your eyes adjust further, you realize the true scale. This is not a forest but a single organism, perhaps, or a colony so integrated that individuality has lost meaning. The roots below must stretch as deep as the crowns reach high. The hollow spaces within the trunks might all connect somewhere below or above, a network of wooden caves that spans continents. The canopy, impossibly distant above, might be breaking through the atmosphere itself, the trees breathing in space while their roots drink from underground seas.

The dynamism is slow but constant. Wood groans and shifts. Vines grow visibly if you watch for an hour. The drifting gardens follow air currents that change with the temperature, rising during what must be day far above, sinking into the depths during the night. The climbing rivers of creatures pulse with circadian rhythms. Somewhere, always, something is calling to something else across the dark volumes, the sound taking full seconds to cross the spaces between trunks.

You are standing on a shelf of fungus the size of a tennis court, a thousand meters above the actual ground, and a thousand meters below the first real branches. In every direction, the titan trunks disappear into singing darkness, their hollows breathing with the mountain wind, their surfaces seething with quiet life, while ghosts of light fall like snow through spaces that dwarf comprehension.
