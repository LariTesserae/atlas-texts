# The Drowned Works

The factory floor extends beyond sight in all directions, a vast industrial prairie submerged beneath forty meters of clear, cold water. This is no ruin—the machines still run, their purpose never interrupted by the inundation that claimed them a mere decade ago.

Chrome pistons the size of grain silos punch upward through the water in synchronized rows, their motion creating rolling compression waves that travel for kilometers before reflecting off distant walls. The sound carries differently here—not muffled as you'd expect underwater, but transformed into a penetrating industrial heartbeat that vibrates through flesh and bone. Every seventeen seconds, the nearest piston complex fires, and the water itself becomes a solid thing, a traveling wall of pressure that sets schools of silver fish spinning like metal shavings around a magnet.

The machinery is pristine, protected by some coating that repels both rust and the peculiar algae that manages to grow in thin, desperate films on the catwalks and service ladders. These sparse green threads wave like smoke in the artificial currents, the only vegetation in this drowned industrial savanna. They cluster most densely around the heat exchangers, where warm water rises in broad columns toward the surface far above.

Between the massive machinery sprawls emptiness—great vaulted spaces where the water is so clear it seems like air, until a shaft of sunlight from some distant skylight catches the suspended particulate and reveals the medium. The factory floor is a grid of smooth concrete channels, each precisely two meters deep, that once guided automated transport. Now they serve as highways for the rays—modified mantas that evolved in the mere ten years since the flooding, their bodies flattened to slip through the geometric spaces, their movements perfectly timed to the rhythm of the machines.

Octopi nest in the control boxes, their soft bodies compressed into the spaces between still-functioning relays. They've learned the patterns—which buttons stop the grinding gears long enough to let them pass, which levers redirect the warm water flows that bring nutrients up from the processing levels below. They tend their territory with methodical intelligence, cleaning specific sensors, maintaining clear paths through their particular sectors of the infinite factory.

The fish have organized themselves by function. Cleaners patrol the intake grilles in shifting clouds. Hunters wait in the pressure shadows behind the pistons, ambushing prey disoriented by the percussion waves. Builders—a species of small bass that no one can explain—collect metal shavings and wire fragments, constructing elaborate nests in the dead spaces between machines, their architecture growing more complex with each generation.

Above it all, barely visible through the clear water, the factory ceiling is itself another floor, with inverted catwalks and machines that mirror the ones below. The original builders constructed in three dimensions, every surface utilized, and the flooding has only revealed the true scope of their ambition. Maintenance submarines patrol established routes between the levels, their automated systems still following protocols for a factory that never stopped production, merely changed medium.

The cold is constant—nine degrees Celsius, regulated by the massive cooling system that still functions seven levels down. It cycles the entire ocean volume of this place every thirty days, pulling water through filters the size of buildings, past treatment systems that remove everything but the essential minerals that keep the new ecosystem alive. The engineers, whoever they were, built for eternity. The flooding was not catastrophe but transition.

In the eastern districts, newer construction continues. Spider-legged welding robots walk the floors on stilts, their work unimpeded by the water, building new production lines according to plans that account for the aquatic environment. They build with drainage in mind now—channels and sluices and pressure locks, as if preparing for the water to recede, or perhaps to flood even deeper.

The sound never stops. Pneumatic hammers punch out their rhythm. Conveyor belts run their endless loops, carrying nothing but the fish that ride them like moving sidewalks. Warning klaxons sound shifts that no human workers answer, but the fauna responds—entire populations migrating between sectors according to the industrial schedule, feeding times determined by when the grinders run, breeding seasons triggered by the monthly maintenance cycles when certain sectors power down.

This is not decay. This is adaptation. The Drowned Works continues its purpose, modified but unbroken, its vast industrial metabolism incorporating the ocean that claimed it, the creatures that colonized it, the new physics of production under pressure. Whatever it manufactures now in its deepest levels, whatever emerges from its submarine loading bays, whatever its purpose—it continues, patient and eternal, a mechanical reef of impossible scale, breathing with the tides of its own making.
