**The Singing Cavity**

Deep beneath the earth, in a pocket no larger than a cathedral nave, lies a world that has forgotten water. The space breathes with ancient slowness—a hollow carved not by rivers but by the patient chemistry of roots seeking minerals through eons of darkness.

The walls are vegetation, but not as the surface knows it. These are the deep-dwelling ligneous masses that gave up photosynthesis before the continents settled into their current shapes. They grow as wood grows, but following no tree's logic—spreading in laminar sheets that have compressed and recompressed over millions of years until they've become something between plant matter and architecture. The texture is smooth as worn leather, warm to the touch despite the cool air, with a grain that spirals in patterns too complex for the eye to follow.

What appears empty at first reveals itself slowly. The cavity is mostly air—vast amounts of it held in a space that shouldn't exist, sustained by the respiration of the plant-walls themselves. They exhale oxygen drawn from mineral sources, inhale carbon dioxide, completing a cycle that needs no sun. The atmosphere tastes metallic and sweet, thick enough to feel but thin enough that sound carries strangely.

And there is sound—always sound. The plant-architecture groans and sings as it grows, a deep thrumming that rises from below hearing into bass notes that shake the sternum. Each growth ring adding itself to the ancient mass creates a new harmonic, a resonance that builds and builds. The civilizations that found this place—so long ago their stone tools have become fossils embedded in the living walls—carved the plant-mass into resonating chambers. Their city is here, but it is a city of voids: careful absences hollowed into the vegetation, rooms and passages that amplify the growing-songs into complex acoustic patterns.

The builders are long gone, but their urban plan persists in negative space. Corridors branch according to forgotten social logics. Chambers of different sizes create different tones. Some spaces were carved to cancel sound, creating pockets of relative quiet where perhaps work was done or rest was taken. Others focus the harmonics into pure, overwhelming drones that might have served ritual purposes.

The plant continues its ancient metabolism, slowly consuming the carved spaces, slowly creating new ones as parts of itself compact or hollow. A room that existed a thousand years ago might now be half its original size. A new bubble might be forming where minerals concentrate, the vegetation pulling away from toxic deposits, creating accidental amphitheaters.

In the absolute darkness, the sound is everything. It maps the space for anyone who learns to listen—each echo revealing distance, each resonance describing shape. The plant-walls themselves are faintly warm, their slow chemistry generating just enough heat to create convection currents that move through the carved passages like breath. These air currents carry spores—not for reproduction, but for communication. Chemical messages that take days to travel from one end of the cavity to another, coordinating the growth, ensuring the whole remains stable despite its constant, glacial transformation.

Sometimes, in the deepest chambers where the original builders dug down to the plant's oldest core, you can find their tools—stone implements worn smooth not by use but by the slow digestive processes of the vegetation growing around them. The plant-mass here is so ancient it has crystallized in parts, becoming something like wood opal, still alive but mineralized, still growing but at a pace that makes stone seem hasty.

The entire space pulses with a rhythm measured in years—expansion and contraction as the plant responds to deep geological shifts, to changes in mineral availability far below, to its own internal clocks that remember seasons from before the sun looked as it does now. This is architecture as organism, city as instrument, civilization's remains becoming part of a metabolism so patient that its end state might not arrive before the heat death of the universe.

In the corner frequencies of the constant song, some say you can hear patterns that repeat—acoustic fossils of the builders' voices, preserved in the way they carved the spaces to resonate with their own speech. But this might be projection, the human need to find meaning in what might simply be the sound of something ancient becoming more itself, forever, in the dark.
