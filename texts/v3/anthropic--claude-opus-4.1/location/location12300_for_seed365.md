The Hanging Gardens of the Moth-Shepherds rise from a shoulder of mountain where the clouds snag and tear. Seven hundred feet of vertical city, built into living rock that still shows the sharp edges of its recent upheaval—some cataclysm or emergence that left these cliffs raw and weeping with mineral springs.

The settlement climbs in terraces, each level connected by rope ladders and carved handholds slick with perpetual mist. Stone dwellings grow directly from the cliff face, their walls continuous with the mountain, hollowed by patient generations using bronze chisels and water-wearing techniques that leave surfaces smooth as eggshell. Between the sparse clusters of homes, vast curtains of vegetation cascade—flowering vines as thick as a person's waist, their roots burrowing deep into cracks where snowmelt pools. Mosses carpet every horizontal surface in electric greens and copper browns. Ferns unfurl from unlikely crevices, some species growing nowhere else, adapted to the precise altitude and moisture of these vertical gardens.

The fauna here defies expectation. Moths the size of dinner plates rest on walls and leaves, their wings patterned like lichen, like stone, like bark—a thousand variations of camouflage. But there are hundreds of species beyond the moths: glass-bodied beetles that click and tick in barely audible chorus, maintaining the rhythm of the settlement. Pale salamanders that never touch ground, living their entire lives in the aerial root systems. Birds with four wings that hover more than fly, feeding on the nectar of climbing flowers. Small primates with opposable digits on all four limbs, their fur growing in patterns that mirror the vegetation they hide within.

The inhabitants have built their lives around this vertical ecosystem. They harvest silk from cocoons the size of melons, weave bridges from living vines, cultivate the bioluminescent fungi that provide the settlement's dim, blue-green glow during the long mountain dusks. Their tools are simple—bronze knives, wooden shepherding sticks, ceramic vessels that catch the constant drip of condensation—but their knowledge of the cliff's ecology is encyclopedic. Each family maintains sections of the garden, knowing which moths pollinate which plants, when the silent-winged owls hunt, how to read the patterns of mist that predict the arrival of fog-dwelling creatures from higher altitudes.

The strangeness accumulates in details: stairs carved in spirals that follow no human logic but match the flight patterns of certain moths. Water that runs upward through hollow vines, defying gravity through capillary action so powerful it seems unnatural. The way sounds are swallowed here—a shout dying within meters, while the soft scratch of larvae eating leaves carries across the entire cliff face. Flowers that bloom only in near-darkness, their petals reflecting light that isn't there.

At dawn and dusk, when the mist thickens, the boundaries blur. You cannot tell where stone ends and dwelling begins, where garden becomes wilderness, where the sounds of insects fade into the whisper of water through invisible channels in the rock. The moth-shepherds move through their vertical world with practiced quiet, using whistles pitched below human hearing to guide their flocks, reading the health of their gardens in the shimmer of wings, the placement of eggs, the barely perceptible hum of contentment from a thousand thousand living things.

The city is never still. Vines grow visibly if you watch for an hour. Terraces shift as roots expand. New chambers are constantly being hollowed as old ones are abandoned to the garden. The mountain itself seems to still be settling, creating new springs, opening fresh caves where blind fish appear from some deeper water system. The moth-shepherds adapt, rebuild, replant, their urban planning following the logic of the ecosystem rather than imposing human order upon it.

In the highest reaches, where the settlement thins to scattered hermit dwellings and shepherd shelters, the weirdness intensifies. Here, certain moths grow too large, their wings spanning three meters or more. The plants become carnivorous in subtle ways—sticky roots that trap small creatures, flowers that close around investigating insects with deliberate slowness. The mountain speaks in groans and sighs that might be settling rock or might be something breathing in the depths. The shepherds who work these heights return changed, their eyes reflecting light like their moths, their movements taking on an insect-like precision, their voices dropping to whispers that carry impossible distances.

This is a place where civilization and wilderness have found an arrangement that belongs to neither category—a living city that breathes with ten thousand species, where the human population tends and is tended by an ecosystem that has evolved past simple nature into something altogether more intentional, more aware, more strange.
