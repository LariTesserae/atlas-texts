In the shallow pools live the Gleaners — creatures that might have been human once, or might become human eventually, or might be something else entirely that merely suggests the human form when glimpsed through the heat-shimmer. They move on all fours mostly, though sometimes they rear up on two legs to taste the air with organs that aren't quite tongues. Their skin has taken on the quality of the place: translucent in patches where the salt has worked its way beneath, opaque and crusted in others, weeping constantly with a clear fluid that keeps them from desiccating entirely.

They number perhaps thirty, perhaps forty — it's difficult to count them as they merge and separate in their movements through the tepid waters. Their fingers and toes have elongated over generations, becoming something between digits and roots, equally suited for feeling through the silty bottom or drawing moisture from the mineral-rich mud. When they cluster together in the deepest pools at midday, their bodies overlap in ways that make them seem like a single organism with multiple heads, all breathing in synchrony.

The Gleaners feed as the birds do, but more patiently. They can remain motionless for hours, their splayed hands buried in the silt, filtering the microscopic life through modifications in their palms — thousands of tiny openings that pulse and sift. What they consume becomes part of the slow transformation of their bodies. Their bones show through their skin in places, but the bones themselves have become porous, honeycombed, more mineral than marrow. Some of the eldest among them can no longer fully close their mouths; their teeth have grown into lattices that constantly filter the humid air.

They build nothing, but they tend things. Each Gleaner maintains a circuit of pools, deepening certain channels with repetitive dragging motions, blocking others with carefully placed clumps of the salt-crust. They seem to be farming the brine shrimp, or perhaps managing the temperature gradients, or possibly conducting some work whose purpose won't be apparent for another thousand years. They coordinate without speaking — or rather, their speech has become a thing of water-sounds: gurgling, dripping, the subtle splash of a hand placed just so.

At night they gather at the weeping rocks, pressing their bodies against the cool stone to absorb the condensation directly through their skin. In these moments they look almost worshipful, but it's more practical than that — or perhaps the practical and the worshipful have become the same thing here. They leave marks on the rocks, not intentionally, but their touch deposits minerals that build up over time into ridged patterns. Someone versed in reading such things might recognize these as records — growth patterns, chemical signatures, a history written in calcium and salt.

The children, when there are children, are born able to breathe both air and water, spending their first years fully submerged in the deeper pools. They emerge gradually, developing lungs in parallel with their gills, never fully losing either capacity. The young ones play games that involve creating perfect circles in the water with their breath, or arranging the empty shells of tiny mollusks in spirals that follow mathematical principles they couldn't name but somehow know.

The Gleaners have relations with the birds that transcend simple coexistence. Certain herons have learned to follow particular Gleaners, knowing that their patient sifting disturbs prey the birds would otherwise miss. In return — though "return" implies too much intention — the birds' guano enriches specific pools, changing their chemistry in ways the Gleaners have come to depend upon. Some of the eldest Gleaners have patches of their backs where the mineral crust has formed into shapes almost like bowls, where smaller birds come to drink the concentrated brine that weeps from their skin.

They dream collectively, if dreaming is the right word. During the hottest part of the day, when even the brine flies settle, the Gleaners slip into a state between sleep and waking. Their minds flow together through the connected waters, sharing chemical memories, metabolic rhythms. In these moments, an observer might notice that the patterns of their breathing match the slow seepage of water through the underground channels, as if they've become part of the saltpan's circulatory system.

When the rains come and the plain floods, the Gleaners transform. They swim then with movements that recall some ancient marine ancestry, their elongated limbs becoming fins, their filtering mouths opening wider to gulp the temporary abundance of fresh-water organisms. For those weeks of flooding, they might travel miles, visiting parts of their territory normally unreachable, mating in the deeper temporary lakes, exchanging genetic material with distant groups whose existence they somehow sense through taste and current.

But mostly they persist in the daily work of living in this in-between state, neither fully aquatic nor terrestrial, neither completely human nor entirely other. They process the ancient salts through their bodies, becoming libraries of chemical history, their tissues recording the slow changes in mineral composition that mark the passage of geological time. They are patients and curators both, waiting for something — or perhaps the waiting itself is the purpose, the persistence its own end, here in this place that has been concluding since before memory but refuses to finish its long goodbye.
