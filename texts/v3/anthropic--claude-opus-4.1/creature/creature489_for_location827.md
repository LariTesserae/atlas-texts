The Tenders move through the Mills like drops of mercury navigating a tilted plane—always finding the path of least resistance, always arriving where needed. They wear the mist like second skins, their coveralls perpetually damp and stained with the same rust-green patina that marks everything here. You rarely see more than three at once, though there must be dozens working the complex. They appear from the vapor between towers, tend to their purposes, and dissolve back into the white.

Their bodies have adapted to this amphibious existence. Fingers slightly webbed from generations of working in perpetual moisture, skin that never quite dries but never quite wrinkles either—sealed somehow, like the waxy coating on the giant leaves. Their eyes are always narrowed, protected by thick lashes that catch droplets and channel them away. They blink slowly, deliberately, like the tree frogs they share their workspace with.

They communicate in gestures more than words—the fog swallows sound, makes voices directionless. A particular way of tapping a pipe means *pressure building.* A sweep of the hand across a valve indicates *flow restricted.* They've developed an entire language of contact, fingers drumming messages along the rails as they pass, vibrations carrying through the metal skeleton better than voices through the thick air.

The eldest among them have moss growing in the creases of their work clothes—not from neglect but from stillness. They can stand motionless for hours, watching a particular junction, feeling the rhythm of the Mills through their feet, waiting for some subtle shift that tells them intervention is needed. The moss marks them as senior, as those who have learned the Mills' deepest patterns. The youngest Tenders eye these green-shouldered elders with something between respect and unease, wondering if they too will eventually become part of the structure they maintain.

Their tools are hybrid things—wrenches with handles wrapped in living vine for grip, gauges whose faces are polished sections of giant seed pods, collection vessels that might be gourds or might be blown glass, impossible to tell under the layers of accumulated moisture and algae. They carry cutting implements that could be for pipes or stems, lubricants that might be machine oil or plant sap. The distinction stopped mattering generations ago.

They live in the Mills themselves, in spaces that exist between industrial and domestic. A boiler room converted to sleeping quarters, hammocks strung between pressure vessels. A control room where the panels still function but orchids cascade from the ceiling, their roots drinking from the condensation that runs down the walls. Kitchen gardens growing in decommissioned tanks, the vegetables flavored with a metallic tang that the Tenders consider essential, correct.

Children are rare but not unknown. They learn the Mills by touch before sight, crawling through the maintenance tunnels while the fog is too thick for eyes to be useful anyway. They develop an intuitive understanding of pressure and flow, learning to feel the health of the system through vibrations in the mesh floor. By adolescence, they can diagnose a fouled filter by the change in the harmonic of a pump three towers away.

The Tenders eat what the Mills provide—the fruit of vines that have learned to concentrate minerals from the rust, mushrooms that grow fat on the wooden packing inside old cooling towers, fish that school in the deeper collection tanks, pale and blind but sweet-fleshed. They brew a tea from the condensation collected at certain joints, where the water picks up traces of copper and botanical oils, creating a drink they claim prevents the lung rot that would otherwise come from breathing this thick air constantly.

They dream collectively, or seem to. On certain nights when the bioluminescent fungi pulse in particularly strong waves, the Tenders move in unconscious coordination, all rising from their various rests to adjust valves, clear filters, redirect flows—responding to some signal only they perceive. In the morning, none can say exactly why they acted, only that it felt necessary, like scratching an itch or shifting position in sleep.

Some among them have begun to photosynthesize, or so it appears. These ones work the highest towers, where the sun occasionally breaks through, and their skin has taken on a greenish cast that isn't algae, isn't illness, but something else. They need less food than the others, seem to gain sustenance from standing in the spray zones where light and water meet. The other Tenders watch them with careful interest, wondering if this is evolution or affliction.

They mark time not by days but by cycles—the filling and emptying of the great tanks, the sprouting and fruiting of the seasonal vines, the migration of certain small creatures up and down the mountain that use the Mills as a waystation. Their calendar is written in rust patterns and growth rings, in the accumulated deposits on pipe joints that must be chipped away every seventh cycling of the main reservoir.

When Tenders die—rarely, but it happens—their bodies are processed through the Mills like everything else. Returned to the system through special chambers where decomposition becomes part of the industrial process, their minerals reclaimed by the structure, their organic matter feeding the gardens. They become the patina on the pipes, the nutrients in the overflow channels. The other Tenders speak of them as still present, still working, just distributed now throughout the complex they spent their lives maintaining.

New Tenders arrive occasionally—refugees from the lowlands, explorers who got lost in the mountain fog, engineers drawn by rumors of the self-sustaining system. The Mills accept or reject them through a process nobody quite understands. Those who stay find their clothing beginning to dampen within hours, never to fully dry again. Those the Mills reject wake up one morning at the mountain's base, expelled by paths they can't remember taking, their memories of the complex already fading like morning mist.

The Tenders don't worship the Mills, exactly, but they treat the complex as something between a machine and an elder. They speak to it as they work, not expecting answers but acknowledging its presence. They know every pipe's note, every tank's capacity, every valve's resistance as intimately as their own bodies. More intimately, perhaps—their own bodies are changing, always changing, becoming more like the Mills with each passing season, while the Mills themselves become more like something that was never quite machine, never quite organism, but always both, always neither, always exactly what this shoulder of mountain needed them to become.
