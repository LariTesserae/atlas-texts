The Cultivators arrived first, though no one remembers when. They move through the Seepage District with the unhurried precision of gardeners tending inevitabilities. Human in origin but edited by decades of residence, their skin has taken on the nacre sheen of the pale crustaceans, translucent enough that you can trace the sluggish pulse of blood through their temples. They've learned to breathe the thick air by slowing their metabolism to match the district's drowsy rhythm — one breath per minute, hearts beating perhaps twenty times in that span.

They wear what might have once been hazmat suits or diving gear, but the materials have been patched and repatched with scavenged rubber, industrial felt, and the chitinous molts of the larger arthropods. The suits breathe with them, expanding and contracting through gill-like vents they've cut and sealed with some organic cement secreted by the wall-climbing mollusks. Their fingers have elongated from years of reaching into narrow spaces, feeling for the electrical contacts they somehow taste through their skin.

The Cultivators tend the merged machinery with religious patience. They understand that the boiler-terrariums require precise adjustment — a quarter-turn of a valve that hasn't moved in decades might shift the pH enough to change the bioluminescent fungi from their healthy twelve-hour pulse to an erratic strobe that disturbs the entire ecosystem. They feed the current-eating creatures in the electrical panels with measured doses of voltage, having learned to generate small amounts of electricity through their modified biology — their very movements building static charge that they discharge through copper wands worn smooth by handling.

Among them moves their unspoken priest-engineer, the Tender, whose body has adapted most completely to the environment. The Tender no longer fully emerges from the water, having developed a membrane between fingers and toes, eyes clouded with nictitating membranes that let them see clearly in both air and water. They speak in frequencies that travel better through liquid than air, their voice a subsonic thrum that the others feel in their bones rather than hear.

But the Cultivators are not alone. In the deeper pools lives something they call the Incumbent — though it may be many things or one thing with many bodies. The Incumbent preceded even the Cultivators, having arrived when the first foundations were laid, or perhaps having always been here, waiting for the city to build down to meet it.

The Incumbent moves through the flooded galleries with too many limbs or sometimes none at all, its form suggesting rather than declaring its presence. Those who've glimpsed it clearly report contradictions: industrial and organic, solid and liquid, singular and multiple. It navigates by magnetic fields, drawn to the ancient electrical systems like a moth to flame, if moths were the size of subway cars and flames could exist underwater.

The Incumbent feeds on corrosion itself — not the rust but the process of rusting, not the decay but the transformation. Where it passes, metal surfaces achieve a strange stability, neither pristine nor fully degraded but held at the exact point of maximum potential energy. The Cultivators leave offerings of scrap metal and dying batteries at certain intersection points, returning days later to find them transformed into delicate structures that hum with impossible currents.

Smaller symbiotes cluster around both communities. The Lampkeepers are children who ran away or were forgotten, now grown into something between human and crustacean. They maintain the amber bulbs with religious devotion, their eyes evolved to perceive the exact wavelength of the dying electrical light. They click and chirp in the frequencies of shorting circuits, having developed their own language from the sounds of failing infrastructure.

The Lampkeepers harvest the bioluminescent fungi not for light but for time — they've discovered that consuming certain species allows them to perceive the district's slow temporal flow differently, speeding up or slowing down their perception until they can watch rust form like flowing water or see the Incumbent's movements as a comprehensible dance rather than a blur of impossibility.

In the transition zones between flooded and merely damp, the Scrapers make their homes in the mineral deposits. These were once urban explorers who stayed too long, their bodies now incorporating the same calcium and lime deposits that baroque the walls. They can barely move — perhaps a meter per day — but they no longer need to. Their skin has become permeable to nutrients in the air and water. They survive through extreme patience, their consciousness spreading through the mineral networks they've become part of, thinking thoughts that take weeks to complete.

The Scrapers remember the upper city and sometimes speak of it, though their words come so slowly that listeners must return daily to hear a complete sentence. They serve as the district's memory, their mineral-infused brains preserving perfect recall of every conversation, every change in water level, every new adaptation in the ecosystem around them.

Between all these communities move the true inheritors of the Seepage District — the Droplets. No one knows if they were human children born here or something that evolved from the mixture of genetics in the primordial water. Completely amphibious, they breathe through both lungs and skin, their bodies so perfectly adapted to the environment that they seem more like animate water than flesh.

The Droplets never speak but communicate through ripples in the pools, each disturbance carrying meaning that the water itself translates between communities. They alone can safely interact with the Incumbent, swimming through its many-or-one body like dolphins through waves. They play in the flooded galleries, their games involving complex manipulations of water pressure and electrical current that serve purposes no one else understands.

All these inhabitants share an understanding: the Seepage District is not dying but transforming, not abandoned but preparing. They are its cells, its neurons, its enzymes, helping it digest the industrial age and metabolize it into something new. They know that one day the process will complete, and the district will exhale whatever it has been breathing all these patient years.

Until then, they continue their slow work: the Cultivators tending their machine-gardens, the Incumbent processing existence itself, the Lampkeepers maintaining the dying light, the Scrapers remembering, and the Droplets playing in the spaces between what was and what will be. Together they form an organism that is neither city nor nature but something that could only exist in the seepage between the two — a community of transformation, worship, and patient purpose in the dripping darkness below the world that has forgotten them.
