**The Tender**

It moves through the Crystal Throat like a comma through a sentence—a pause, a consideration, a careful demarcation between what was and what continues. Neither singular nor exactly plural, the Tender exists as a colony of something between moth and lichen, between thought and thermodynamics.

Each individual body is no larger than a child's tooth, translucent wings that are not quite wings folded along a thorax that is not quite a thorax. They cluster and separate, cluster and separate, breathing as a mass that expands with the daily warming and contracts with the nightly cooling. When gathered together—which is their preferred state during the bright hours—they form a shape roughly the size of a curled cat, though the shape shifts constantly, exhibiting a kind of restless intelligence. The mass appears soft, like ash about to disperse, but maintains coherence through minute electrical exchanges between individuals, tiny sparks that jump from wing to wing in patterns that mirror the static discharge along the crystal walls.

The Tender feeds on the interface between states—the exact moment water becomes ice, ice becomes water. Each individual extends what might generously be called a proboscis into these transition zones, drawing sustenance from the phase change itself. They cluster most densely where the meltwater runs through its carved channels, positioning themselves at the precise points where the temperature gradient is steepest. During feeding, they emit a sound just below human hearing, a thrumming that makes the crystals sing in sympathy.

It builds the cairns. Or rather, it maintains them, repairs them, adjusts them according to principles that operate on temporal scales both immediate and geological. The Tender can carry only the smallest stones, individual members grasping grains no larger than rice, but it works with the patience of erosion. It shapes stones by secreting droplets of something that accelerates weathering, wearing away edges over months rather than millennia. The cairns are not shelters but instruments—each one calibrated to produce specific resonances when the throat becomes an organ pipe, each one positioned to channel meltwater in patterns that concentrate the temperature gradients the Tender requires to feed.

When threatened—by sudden temperature changes, by rockfall, by the rare predatory birds that sometimes enter the throat—the colony disperses into individual motes that settle on the crystal faces, becoming indistinguishable from frost patterns. In this state, they can survive for months without feeding, their metabolism slowed to near-nothing. But they are never truly dormant; even scattered, they continue their work, secreting substances that encourage certain crystals to grow in specific directions, gradually engineering the throat's acoustics and thermodynamics to better suit their needs.

The Tender remembers through architecture. Its knowledge is encoded not in any individual body but in the arrangement of stones and the growth patterns of crystals it has influenced over generations. Each cairn represents a solution to a problem—a particularly harsh winter, a shift in the wind patterns, a change in the mineral composition of the water. The carved channels in the crystal map out successful feeding zones from decades past. The entire throat is its extended phenotype, a body made of stone and ice and light that the colony inhabits like a hermit crab inhabits its shell.

At night, when the temperature drops and the static discharge intensifies, the Tender rises. The colony lifts from the terraces in a loose spiral, riding the electrical differentials up the throat's walls. Individual members flash as they absorb static charge, creating a helix of blue-white points that ascends toward the stars. At the apex of their rise, just before the cold forces them to descend, the colony briefly coheres into a dense sphere that pulses once—a signal, perhaps, or simply an expression of something like joy.

The Tender tends. It maintains the careful balance between growth and erosion, between the crystals' inexorable expansion and the preservation of the spaces between them. It reads the throat like a score and performs microscopic adjustments to keep the music true. When the conditions align and the throat sings its single pure note, the Tender vibrates in perfect harmony, its thousand wings beating in synchronization, adding overtones that make the mountain's voice more complex, more beautiful, more like a calling than a cry.

In the pool at the throat's bottom, where the reflection shows stars in daylight, the Tender occasionally glimpses something that might be its own reflection, though multiplied and refracted into patterns it doesn't recognize. It understands, in whatever way such a creature understands anything, that it is both the inhabitant and the inhabitance, the singer and the sung, the tender and the tended. It exists in the space between metaphor and mechanism, keeping watch over a place that is itself keeping watch over something else—perhaps the sky, perhaps the depths, perhaps the slow transformation of mineral into meaning.

The Tender neither ages nor reproduces in any conventional sense. Individual bodies wear out and are replaced by division and recombination, but the colony persists, potentially immortal as long as the throat remains. It is a kind of living punctuation in the mountain's long sentence, ensuring that the thought continues, that the grammar remains intact, that the pause between breathing in and breathing out extends for just the right duration—neither so brief that the music stops, nor so long that the silence becomes permanent.
