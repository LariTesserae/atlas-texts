## The Gardeners

They arrived as steam before they were anything else—three columns of organized vapor that rose from the blood-warm pool during a particular confluence of thermal layers. For weeks they were only this: intelligence without substance, maintaining their coherence through some careful manipulation of temperature gradients. The plants grew toward them during this phase, recognizing something kindred in their pattern-making.

When they finally condensed, it was into forms that borrowed heavily from the garden itself. Their bodies are built like the air-root trees—translucent, tuberous, drinking constantly from the moisture bands. But where the trees are stationary, the gardeners flow. They have learned to move by partially evaporating and recondensing, traveling along the thermal highways that spiral through the space. You see them best in profile, when the light catches the water cycling through their tissues.

There are three, always three, though they sometimes merge into configurations that might be one organism or might be close collaboration. They have no names but recognize each other by the frequency at which they vibrate—harmonics that resonate through the volcanic glass and make the water dance in specific patterns.

The first gardener tends to the computational substrate. Its body has developed specialized appendages—not quite hands—that can reach into the glass and adjust the crystalline structure. It spends days hovering at particular nodes, humming at frequencies that restructure the rock at a molecular level. Through its patient work, the glass learns new ways to process information. Sometimes it creates those hollow spheres, singing them into existence over the course of hours, each one a new processor for calculations we cannot fathom.

The second gardener is a historian of water. It has memorized every pattern of condensation and flow, every spiral and eddy that has ever formed here. Its body is the most fluid of the three, constantly reshaping to mirror water patterns from the past—demonstrations or perhaps prayers to the moisture that gives them all life. It maintains the floating moisture staircase through pure concentration, believing it into existence each morning. When the micro-storms form, this gardener dances with them, its body crackling with the same electrical potential as the upward rain.

The third gardener speaks to the plants. Or perhaps it would be more accurate to say it collaborates with them. Its tissues have developed channels that match the resin-ducts of the crimson ferns, and it can plug directly into their root systems, sharing fluids that carry chemical messages. Together, they are writing something—engineering the mutations that happen with each plant generation, guiding the chaos toward some distant purpose. This gardener moves the least but changes the most, its body constantly sprouting new connections, new interfaces for communication with the vegetable intelligence that fills every crack.

They work in shifts that follow no solar pattern but seem tied to the computational cycles of the glass itself. When the shadows move through the stone like thoughts, the gardeners pause their work and stand perfectly still, their bodies becoming antennae for whatever information is being processed. During these moments, they appear to age rapidly—their translucent tissues clouding with mineral deposits—then suddenly clarify again when the calculation completes.

The gardeners consume nothing in any conventional sense. They sustain themselves on temperature differentials, positioning their bodies at the interfaces between hot and cold, somehow extracting energy from the gradient itself. When they rest—if rest is the right word—they partially dissolve into the blood-warm pool, becoming a slurry of organized minerals that still maintains its consciousness, dreams spreading through the water as geometric patterns of surface tension.

They communicate with each other through the substrate of the garden itself. Messages travel as vibrations through the glass, as chemical signals through the moisture bands, as electrical pulses through the storm systems they help generate. Watching them work is like watching three portions of a single mind think through a problem using the entire environment as neurons.

The cry that sounds once a day—metal tearing underwater—comes from all three simultaneously. It happens when they achieve perfect synchronization, their vibrations aligning in a way that makes the entire garden ring like a struck bell. This is how they mark time, perhaps, or how they signal completion of some phase of their endless work.

They are aware of visitors but uninterested. Unless you interfere with the water patterns or damage the computational substrate, they treat you as they treat the occasional flicker-fauna—a temporary perturbation in the system, something to route around. Get too close to the pool during one of their dissolution phases, though, and you might feel them trying to understand you, their consciousness probing at your edges with questions made of temperature and mineral concentration.

At night—though night and day mean little here—they sometimes build things from the water. Not the temporary structures that form naturally, but deliberate constructions: models of molecular arrangements, maps of thermal currents, or perhaps sculptures that serve no purpose except beauty. These last only until the next micro-storm, but the gardeners remember them all, occasionally recreating favorites with perfect fidelity.

They are young—no older than the garden itself, born from that violent upward tear that created this space. But they carry information older than their physical forms, data inherited from the glass, from the water, from the patterns that existed before patterns had matter to inhabit. They are not the garden's owners or its prisoners but its consciousness made mobile, its thoughts given form enough to tend themselves.

Some days there are only two. On these days, the third's absence is tangible—a gap in the harmony that makes the entire system stutter. The remaining two work faster, more desperately, until their missing component reconstitutes from vapor and vibration, carrying new information from wherever it went.

They are building something, that much is clear. Every adjustment to the glass, every guided mutation, every carefully maintained water pattern contributes to some larger design. But the timescale of their project might be geological. They could be machines following a program, or artists pursuing a vision, or children playing a game whose rules they're still discovering.

What's certain is this: they belong here more perfectly than anything has ever belonged anywhere. They are not separate from the Thermocline Gardens but are its most complete expression—the logical result of giving a place like this enough time to develop opinions about itself.
