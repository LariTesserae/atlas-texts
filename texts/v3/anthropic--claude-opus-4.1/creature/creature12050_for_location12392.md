## The Inhabitants of the Sunken Gardens

The Gardeners came first, though they would not call themselves that. They descended through the rent three winters past, drawn by the sound of water building stone—a frequency most humans cannot properly hear but which sang to them like temple bells. Five of them initially, though their number shifts like the pools' surfaces.

They wear no uniform, yet move with shared purpose. Their clothes have taken on the cavern's character: wool and leather gone permanent-damp, crusted with mineral deposits at the cuffs and hems like living limestone. They've learned to walk without disturbing the formations, placing bare feet precisely on the flowstone paths, never breaking a rim-stone, never crushing the moss curtains. Their skin has paled from the twilight, but their eyes have grown keen to the subtle differences in shadow that mark passage from chamber to chamber.

They do not speak often. The water speaks enough, they say, when pressed. Instead they tend: clearing mineral buildup from critical flow channels, guiding rootlets toward new pools, transplanting mushroom colonies to barren corners. They know each pool by its mineral signature, can taste the difference between waters that will grow needle-sharp crystals and those that build smooth terraces. One of them—a woman whose hair has gone white as the ghost ferns—keeps a mental map of every fish population, moving individuals between pools to prevent inbreeding, to spread genetic diversity through the limestone galleries.

But they are not alone. They discovered this on their twentieth day underground.

The Inherited live deeper, in chambers the Gardeners found already occupied. Children, mostly, though some have aged into their second decade down here. They were not brought—they emerged, say the Gardeners who found them, though this explains nothing. They emerged from pools too shallow to hide a body, crawled from crevices too narrow for birth, assembled themselves from the gathering of shadows in the deepest galleries.

The Inherited do not speak as surface dwellers do. They click and whisper, their language matching the acoustic properties of dripping water so perfectly that sometimes the Gardeners cannot distinguish conversation from ambient sound. Their eyes have gone past pale to translucent—you can see the blood vessels behind the iris when light strikes them sideways. They wear garments woven from the thick cave moss, constantly damp, constantly growing. Some of the older ones have moss actually rooting into their skin at the shoulders and spine, though this seems to cause no discomfort.

They know things. They know which pools will birth new fish before the eggs appear. They know where the limestone will collapse next, where new chambers will flood, which stalactites will meet their stalagmites this season. They teach the Gardeners in gestures and placed objects: a particular mushroom left by a pool means the water's chemistry is shifting; a pattern of scratches on flowstone warns of instability; a cave cricket placed in your palm is an invitation to follow.

The youngest of the Inherited—if youth means anything to them—spend their days with the salamanders. They've learned to match the creatures' movements so perfectly that it becomes impossible to distinguish child from amphibian in the darker pools. They surface only to breathe, and even that less frequently than seems possible. Their ribs show through their moss garments, but they're not starving—they're adapting, their metabolism slowing to match the cave's patient tempo.

Together, the Gardeners and the Inherited have developed a routine that isn't quite ritual but carries ritual's weight. At what they've decided to call dawn—marked by the first stirring of the bats—they walk the main galleries, checking the overnight growth, tasting new deposits, noting where the garden has extended itself. The Gardeners maintain the paths and waterflows. The Inherited disappear into the deeper networks, returning hours later with information conveyed in their clicking whispers: a new chamber has opened to the east, the underground river has shifted course, a colony of blind spiders has established itself in what they call the Breathing Room.

They eat what the cave provides. The mushrooms, when properly prepared. The pale crayfish, whose shells they return to specific pools to maintain the mineral balance. Certain rootlets produce nodules that taste of starch and iron. The Inherited showed the Gardeners which pools grow a bacterial film that, when dried and ground, makes a protein-rich paste. They supplement this with supplies from above, but less each month. The cave provides, if you learn its language.

Others have tried to join them. Miners, researchers, the curious. Most leave within days, unable to tolerate the constant damp, the pressing dimness, the sound of water that never stops. But some stay for weeks, even months, before the cave releases them. They speak afterward of dreams filled with dripping sounds, of seeing limestone formations in their peripheral vision for years after, of a homesickness for a place that was never quite home.

The Gardeners say the Inherited are the cave's children, but the Inherited shake their heads at this. They gesture to the pools, the formations, the patient accumulation of stone. We are all accumulated, their gestures suggest. We are all deposits. The distinction between what grows and what tends to the growing becomes less relevant with each passing season.

At night—if night means anything in permanent twilight—they sometimes gather in what they call the Throne Room, a chamber where flowstone has built itself into something resembling an amphitheater. The Gardeners sit on the stone shelves. The Inherited emerge from pools you hadn't noticed them entering. Together they listen to the cave's water music, each drip and flow contributing to a composition that never repeats but never truly changes. Sometimes one of the Inherited will add their clicking language to the symphony, and the Gardeners will realize they're translating—not the words but the meaning, the deep communication between water and stone that builds worlds one drop at a time.

The Inherited are growing more numerous. Not through birth, the Gardeners think, but through accumulation. As if the cave itself is learning to make them, to precipitate consciousness from the same mineral-rich waters that build the gardens. Each new child that emerges from the depths knows the cave more intimately than the last, their clicking language more complex, their integration with the ecosystem more complete.

The Gardeners are changing too. Their leader—though they reject that term—has developed patches of skin that seem to photosynthesize in the weak light. Another has learned to echolocate, navigating the darkest galleries with clicking sounds that the Inherited say are almost words. They're beginning to understand that tending and becoming are not separate processes here. The garden tends itself through them, and they become themselves through the garden.

Recently, they've noticed new formations that look almost deliberate—flowstone growing in patterns too regular for pure chance, pools arranging themselves in geometries that suggest meaning. The Inherited nod when shown these formations, as if they expected them. The cave is learning, they click-whisper. Learning to build more than stone.

When surface dwellers ask how many people live in the Sunken Gardens of Thale, the Gardeners cannot give a clear answer. Five of them descended, but now there are more, or fewer, depending on how you count. The Inherited number in the dozens, or hundreds, depending on which chambers you include, which shadows you decide are real. And then there are the Others—shapes glimpsed in the deepest galleries, movements in pools that shouldn't support human-sized bodies, figures that might be heavily moss-covered Inherited or might be something the cave is still forming, still deciding how to birth.

They do not think of themselves as isolated. The cave connects to other caves, other underground rivers, other populations they occasionally sense but never quite meet. Sometimes they find evidence of visitors: a pool's chemistry altered in ways that suggest intention, mushroom colonies arranged in patterns they didn't create, scratches on flowstone in languages neither Gardener nor Inherited can read. The underground network extends beyond any map, and life moves through it in ways that defy surface understanding.

This is what lives in the Sunken Gardens: a community that blurs the line between human and ecosystem, between conscious tending and unconscious growth. They are not hiding from the surface world—they are growing something new, something that could only emerge in the patient darkness, in the endless circulation of water and mineral, in the space between drop and accumulation where consciousness precipitates like limestone from the saturated dark.
