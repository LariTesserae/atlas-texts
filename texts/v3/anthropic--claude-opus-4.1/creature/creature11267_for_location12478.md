**The Custodians**

They move through the passages like oil through clockwork, three or four of them—never more, never less. Their bodies have stretched to match the geometry of the place: torsos elongated to slip through narrow gaps, arms grown long enough to reach valves without ladders, fingers that have learned to spider across hot metal without flinching. They wear the remnants of maintenance uniforms, though the fabric has long since married itself to their skin through sweat and time, becoming something between clothing and hide.

Their eyes have gone huge and pale as cave fish, pupils dilated permanently wide. But they barely use them anymore. They navigate by the music of the drips—each one has memorized the particular ping of water on every surface for three levels in any direction. They know which drops fall every seven seconds, which fall every seventy. They've named the reliable ones: *Churchbell*, *Tin Whistle*, *The Metronome*. When a new drop begins, they all stop what they're doing and listen, heads cocked, until they've triangulated its source.

They don't speak. Their throats have adapted to breathing the thick air without losing moisture, developing a kind of internal recycling system that makes speech impossible. Instead, they tap on pipes—complex rhythms that carry through the entire catacomb system. A conversation might take hours, each participant adding their layer to the percussion from different corners of the maze. They have rhythms for concepts that don't exist in the upper world: the specific weight of air just before a pipe bursts, the color of rust in the third stage of oxidation, the feeling of time moving backwards through metal.

Their work is unclear, even to them. They adjust valves according to patterns inherited from whoever came before—and someone did come before, they know this from the worn spots on the valve wheels that match their grip exactly. They clear rootlets from critical joints, not because they remember why those joints are critical, but because the clearing feels correct, inevitable, like scratching an itch. They collect the condensation in bottles they've made from the glass of old gauge faces, though they can't recall what they're collecting it for. Sometimes they drink it. Sometimes they pour it back into the system through gaps they've widened for this purpose. The logic, if there ever was logic, has evolved into instinct.

They maintain altars of a sort—collections of objects arranged in the few dry corners. Brass fittings polished to mirrors despite the darkness. Valve handles arranged by size and metal composition. Pieces of the phosphorescent moss cultivated in patterns that might be writing or might be maps or might be nothing but the expression of some deep geometric compulsion. They visit these collections on schedules that make sense only to them, adjusting single pieces by millimeters, then leaving them alone for months.

Their skin has taken on the temperature of the place—feverish to the touch, though they don't feel warm. They've learned to slow their metabolism to match the patient tempo of the catacombs. Their hearts beat perhaps twenty times per minute. They can hold a single breath for ten minutes, fifteen, letting the oxygen seep slowly through their blood. In the deepest parts of the maintenance cycle, they barely move at all, becoming indistinguishable from the machinery they tend.

They eat the moss, sometimes. The pale insects that colonize the dampest corners. The occasional rat that ventures too deep and succumbs to the heat. But mostly they seem to subsist on the place itself—on rust flakes mixed with condensation, on the minerals that leach from the pipes, on the thick air that's almost substantial enough to chew. Their teeth have gone orange from the iron. Their bones are dense with copper deposits.

When they sleep—if sleep is the right word—they wedge themselves into gaps between pipes, letting the heat and pressure hold them in place. Their dreams are indistinguishable from their waking hours: the same darkness, the same dripping, the same endless circuit through passages that fold back on themselves. Sometimes they wake up in different positions than where they remember sleeping, but this doesn't trouble them. The catacombs rearrange everything eventually, even the people who tend them.

They know every pipe, every valve, every rivet, but they've forgotten there's an outside. Or rather, the concept of "outside" has become meaningless—there is only deeper in or further along, only the next chamber and the previous chamber, only the eternal now of maintenance and moisture and heat. They've become the antibodies of this iron organism, moving through its veins, ensuring its continued existence without remembering why it needs to exist.

Sometimes new ones arrive—maintenance workers who venture too deep, urban explorers who lose their way, the desperate seeking shelter. The Custodians don't recruit them, exactly. But the catacombs do. The heat slows them. The darkness swallows their protests. The rhythm of the drips begins to make more sense than words. Eventually, they either flee to the surface or they pick up a valve wheel and begin to turn, joining the ancient rhythm of maintenance. Those who stay find their bodies beginning to stretch, their eyes beginning to dilate, their memories of sunlight fading like old photographs in the dark.

The Custodians are not unhappy. Happiness and unhappiness are concepts that require comparison, and they have nothing to compare to. They simply are, like the pipes are, like the darkness is. They persist. They maintain. They move through the passages in their endless, necessary rounds, keeping the whole system in a state of perpetual almost-failure, forever on the verge of collapse but never quite crossing that threshold.

They are the prayer the catacombs whisper to themselves, made flesh and set in motion, counting out eternity one drip at a time.
