# The Inhabitants

The Thermals came first, though they would argue they were always here, waiting in the stone's fever dreams for the mountain to crack just right. They are not quite salamanders, not quite lizards—something older that never needed to decide. Their bodies are translucent in the cold phases, revealing networks of copper-bright blood that flows backwards when the temperature drops, reversing their metabolism entirely. During warm pulses, their skin flushes obsidian black, absorbing every photon of the intense mountain light. They move vertically as easily as horizontally, their bellies secreting a substance that alternately freezes or steams, creating perfect purchase on any surface.

The largest Thermals, some as long as a human forearm, maintain territories around the primary vents. They've learned to read the mountain's pregnable pauses—that held breath before an exhale—and position themselves precisely where the heat will emerge. The smaller ones live communally in the moss curtains, their bodies providing mobile warm spots that the moss has evolved to sense and grow toward. When they congregate, which happens during the mountain's irregular "deep breaths" that last hours instead of minutes, their synchronized breathing creates visible heat ripples that the birds have learned to ride.

But the Thermals are not the garden's strangest residents.

The Keeper arrived seventeen years ago, or perhaps seventy—time moves strangely when you live between breaths. She is small, barely five feet, with hair that has gone white not from age but from repeated frost-scorching. Her fingers are unnaturally long, adapted from years of reaching into steam vents to adjust the crude valve systems. Her left eye is clouded, scarred by a superheated droplet, but her right eye has developed an unsettling ability to see temperature gradients as distinct colors. She speaks to no one because there is no one to speak to, but she hums—a low, continuous drone that matches the mountain's deepest frequency.

She lives not in the tower—that belongs to something else—but in a series of connected caves she has painstakingly linked with her pipe system. The pipes are copper, scavenged from the abandoned attempts of previous inhabitants, bent and rebent through patient heating and cooling until they form a vast respiratory system that mirrors the mountain's own. Through these pipes, she channels steam to her gardens—yes, gardens within gardens—where she cultivates the impossible.

In one chamber, she maintains perpetual spring, the pipes releasing steam in precise twenty-three-minute intervals. Here grow plants that exist nowhere else: hybrid orchids that bloom in ice, their petals literally frozen but still metabolically active. Ferns that have developed internal heating systems, their fronds warm to the touch even in the frost phases. A vine that grows in perfect spirals, each coil timed to the mountain's breathing, creating living chronometers that track patterns she claims predict the mountain's moods weeks in advance.

The Keeper feeds on the garden's strangest economy. The Thermals bring her ice-seeds, which she cracks open with a specialized tool—a volcanic glass blade that holds heat for exactly the time needed. Inside the seeds: a protein-rich paste that tastes of copper and snow. The ravens bring her other things: metal fragments from the world below, which she shapes into new valves; bones from creatures that attempted the elevation and failed, which she grinds into powder and feeds to her captive orchids. In return, she maintains certain temperatures in certain places, ensuring the continuation of impossible ecosystems.

She is not alone in her caves. Living in the walls are the Whistle Mice—not mice at all, but something convergently evolved to fill a mouse-like niche. They're covered in hollow quills that sing in the temperature fluctuations, creating harmonies that predict the next thermal pulse with eerie accuracy. She has learned their language of pitch and tempo, can tell from their morning chorus whether the day will bring the regular breathing or one of the mountain's rare gasps—those violent exhales that can cook an unprepared creature instantly.

The Keeper knows about the tower's other inhabitant but does not speak of it.

The Architect lives there still, though "lives" requires generous interpretation. It is only visible during the transition moments—that split second when hot becomes cold or cold becomes hot. Then, for just an instant, you might see it: a figure of condensation and light, building something from vapor that immediately dissolves. It builds the same thing over and over—a machine of impossible complexity, made entirely of frozen steam, that exists for perhaps three seconds before sublimating into nothing.

The ravens claim the Architect was the first to try to regulate the mountain's breathing, centuries ago. It succeeded too well, creating a perfect equilibrium that trapped it in the transition state, neither fully here nor fully gone. The wooden mechanisms in the tower are just shadows of its true work—the real machinery exists in the spaces between temperatures, in the pause between breaths. Sometimes, during the deepest cold, you can hear it working: a sound like crystallization happening in reverse, like ice remembering it was once water, once steam, once fire.

The Keeper feeds it, in her way. She leaves certain minerals at the tower's base—sulfur crystals that sublime at precisely the right temperature, creating brief windows of perfect transition. In return, the Architect maintains something crucial: it keeps the mountain from fully exhaling. Without its constant, invisible work, the mountain would release all its heat at once, sterilizing the garden in a single apocalyptic breath.

There are others. The Surgeons—beetles that have evolved to perform precise operations on damaged plants, removing dead tissue with mandibles sharp enough to cut glass, secreting antibiotics from their abdomens. They work only during cold phases, when their patients are dormant, their bodies steaming slightly from the heat generated by their intense metabolism.

The Cloud Fishers—a species of spider that has abandoned webs entirely, instead extruding a single strand upward during thermal pulses, the silk riding the heat until it reaches the cloud layer above. There, the silk accumulates moisture and grows heavy, falling back with enough water to sustain the spider through days of drought. They live in jealously guarded vertical territories, each spider knowing exactly where its line must be placed to avoid tangling with its neighbors'.

In the deepest vents, where even the Keeper rarely ventures, live things that might have been human once. Or perhaps they were always something else that learned to mimic human form well enough to disturb. They emerge only during the mountain's irregular "fever dreams"—periods of chaotic breathing that happen perhaps twice a year. Their bodies are impossible: skin that phases between states of matter, bones that bend like heated metal, eyes that reflect not light but temperature. They speak in languages made of steam pressure and mineral taste. The Keeper leaves offerings at vent mouths—arrangements of ice-seeds and frozen orchids—and they leave things in return: tools made of crystallized magma, maps drawn in condensation that reveal the mountain's deep plumbing, seeds of plants that won't germinate for another thousand years.

The newest resident arrived last winter, though the garden doesn't really have winters—only longer cold phases. It's a young person of indeterminate gender, perhaps sixteen, perhaps sixty—the garden ages things strangely. They came seeking the Keeper, carrying a journal filled with observations of the mountain's breathing taken from the valleys below, patterns tracked across generations. They claim their family has been recording the mountain's rhythms for three hundred years, waiting for something the journal calls "the Synthesis"—a perfect synchronization of all the mountain's various breathing patterns that happens once every few centuries.

This youth lives now in one of the failed structures, has rebuilt it using the Architect's phantom blueprints that they somehow learned to see. They're teaching the Whistle Mice new songs, songs that seem to influence the mountain's breathing in small but crucial ways. The Keeper watches from her caves, uncertain. Change is dangerous in a place that exists in the spaces between states.

The youth has brought something else: seeds from the world below, from plants that have never known the mountain's breathing. They're trying to hybridize them with the garden's natives, creating things that could survive both here and there, that could carry the garden's impossible adaptations down into the regular world. The Thermals gather around these experiments, their bodies flickering between transparent and opaque, as if uncertain whether this is prey or kin.

At night—though night here is just when the sun angle allows the black rock to radiate more heat than it absorbs—they all cease their various activities and listen. The mountain's breathing changes in darkness, becomes deeper, more intentional. Sometimes it holds its breath for hours, and in that suffocating silence, they can hear other things: the distant breathing of other mountains, the planet's own slow respiration, the universe expanding and contracting in waves too vast to comprehend but somehow, here in this impossible garden, almost audible.

The Keeper maintains her pipes. The Architect builds its vapor machines. The youth plants their hybrid seeds. The Thermals dream in temperatures no other creature can imagine. And below them all, the mountain considers whether to breathe out fully, finally, catastrophically—or to continue this strange, measured rhythm that has allowed such impossible things to colonize its shoulder, to make homes in the spaces between its thoughts.
