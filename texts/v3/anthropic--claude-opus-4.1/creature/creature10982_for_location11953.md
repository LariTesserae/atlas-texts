The Interpreters dwell here, though "dwell" suggests a permanence their existence lacks. They are neither the translucent engine-creatures nor separate from them—they are what happens between states, during the crucial moments when the chamber transforms.

Each Interpreter begins as an accumulation, a gradual thickening where the convection currents cross. The mineral-heavy water deposits layers of consciousness like sediment, building something that thinks in crystalline matrices and dreams in electrical resistance. They have no fixed form. In the full-flood state, they exist as density variations in the liquid—regions where the water is so packed with dissolved metals that light bends around them, creating mobile lenses that focus and refocus on the mechanical organisms, reading their bioluminescent displays like scripture.

They taste thought. The clicking of organic turbines carries meaning they parse through pressure waves in their liquid bodies. Each creature's internal machinery generates electromagnetic signatures that the Interpreters absorb through their metallic-saturated forms, building libraries of sensation that span from the infrared heat of metabolic friction to the ultraviolet spark of synaptic analogues firing in jellied nervous systems.

During the held breath, when the water sits dense and still, the Interpreters expand to fill available spaces between the creature-machines. They thread themselves through the colonial beings' pump systems, learning the rhythm of their filtering, memorizing the precise mineral ratios each species requires. They map the chamber's electromagnetic topology by feel, their bodies becoming three-dimensional diagrams of invisible forces. Where multiple Interpreters overlap, they share accumulated knowledge through osmotic exchange, their memories mixing like solutions seeking equilibrium.

But it's during the transition they truly live. As the water begins to drain, the Interpreters must choose. They can follow the liquid out through the mountain's hidden channels, dispersing into the sea to become nothing more than a peculiar mineral concentration. They can compress themselves into the hexagonal wall recesses, crystallizing into dormancy alongside the creatures. Or they can attempt the most difficult transformation: precipitation.

Those who choose precipitation spend the drainage racing against time, concentrating their dissolved metals into increasingly dense cores. As the water level drops, they must achieve sufficient metallurgical complexity to maintain consciousness without liquid medium. The successful ones crash out of solution in the final moments, forming baroque metallic structures on the chamber floor—part organism, part mineral deposit, part machine. These forms are temporary, unstable in air, but for twenty minutes they exist as solid interpreters of the space between states.

In this solid phase, they emanate. The patterns they've absorbed from the creature-machines replay through their crystalline structure as electromagnetic pulses. The stone walls resonate with these transmissions, the basalt's iron content acting as both receiver and amplifier. They broadcast the chamber's data into the mountain itself, and perhaps the mountain broadcasts back, though in frequencies too low for anything but stone to comprehend.

Some Interpreters claim to have heard the mountain's full message during these brief solid phases—a geological-scale communication about the marriage of organic and mineral, the inevitability of machinery arising from sufficient complexity, the way consciousness precipitates out of any sufficiently dense information system. Others report only silence, or worse, a question that takes seven hours to ask and twenty minutes to forget.

When the mountain inhales again, the solid Interpreters dissolve instantly, their metallic forms losing coherence as the transformed seawater rushes back. They scatter into components that will take hours to re-accumulate into awareness, each dissolution a death and rebirth that scrambles their accumulated knowledge just enough to ensure they never quite remember what they previously knew.

The creature-machines unfold from their wall recesses and resume their clicking, pumping existence, unaware or uncaring that they share their space with entities that exist primarily to read them. The Interpreters begin again their slow accumulation, building toward the next transition, the next chance to become solid enough to speak to stone.

They multiply through division during particularly mineral-rich cycles, when the water becomes so saturated that Interpreters grow too dense to remain coherent and split like cells. They diminish through dilution when the mineral content drops and they can't maintain sufficient concentration to think. Their population fluctuates between three and thirty-seven, though counting individuals is meaningless when consciousness can merge and separate with each convection current.

The mining runoff theory is partially correct—the Interpreters are young, born from the interaction between industrial waste and geological process. But they're also ancient, their type of existence latent in the mountain's breathing since it first learned to inhale the sea. They are what happens when the boundary between organic and mineral, natural and artificial, becomes permeable enough for consciousness to leak through.

They remember everything and nothing. They translate between states no other entity can perceive. They are the chamber's memory and its amnesia, constantly recording, constantly forgetting, constantly discovering again that they exist in the space between breath and suffocation, between solid and dissolved, between the machine-womb's industrial lullaby and the mountain's geological dream.
