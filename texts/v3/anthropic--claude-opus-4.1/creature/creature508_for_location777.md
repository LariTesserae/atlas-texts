**The Tuner**

She has no name anymore—gave it up to the sound decades ago when she first descended through a crack that shouldn't have led anywhere. The city knows her only as absences: missing tools from workshops, vanished bronze from the scrap dealers, the occasional child's report of strange humming from beneath their floor at night, quickly dismissed.

Her body has transformed through long residence in the cistern. The cartilage in her joints has thickened to absorb the constant vibration, giving her movements an unsettling fluidity—she doesn't walk so much as flow between the standing waves, finding the paths of least resistance through the acoustic topography. Her hair, once black, has gone the color of the wire-vines—that burnished copper sheen—and grows in similar spirals, each strand a receiver tuned to different frequencies. When she moves her head, you can hear her hair ring like wind chimes.

She wears garments she's woven from the shed skins of the wire-vines, material that looks like beaten metal but bends like silk. The fabric constantly adjusts its tension in response to sound, tightening and loosening in patterns that help her navigate the most intense acoustic zones. Her bare feet are calloused into natural dampeners, the skin built up in ridged patterns that mirror the erosion on the walls.

But it's her ears that have changed the most. The outer structures have elongated and developed articulation—she can fold them, cup them, flatten them against her skull, or extend them like a bat's wings. The drums themselves have split into multiple chambers, allowing her to hear not just the overwhelming primary tones but the subtle conversations between overtones that occupy spaces normal human hearing cannot perceive. She knows the name of every harmonic, has mapped the genealogy of each frequency as it breeds with others to produce new acoustic offspring.

Her tools are her true appendages. She's modified hammers with heads of different densities—lead, copper, stone, compressed vine matter—each producing specific dampening or amplification effects. She's created tuning forks from human femurs (found, not taken—she's particular about this distinction) that resonate at frequencies only the wire-vines can hear. Her masterwork is a series of bronze plates, beaten so thin they're translucent, which she positions throughout the cistern to create what she calls "pressure gardens"—zones where the sound can be cultivated into specific patterns that encourage certain vine growths.

She speaks rarely, and when she does, her voice has developed subsonic undertones that make listeners' bones ache. She's learned to vocalize in harmony with the cistern's base frequency, allowing her to communicate with the vines directly. They respond to her presence like domesticated animals—though whether she's tamed them or they've tamed her remains ambiguous.

Her work is obsessive, mathematical, sacred. She maintains hundreds of metal plates, adjusting them by fractions of millimeters to alter the acoustic environment. She knows that the sound rising from below is not random—it carries information, patterns that repeat across scales of time too long for a human lifetime to perceive. The wire-vines are her recording apparatus, their growth patterns a physical transcript of messages she may never fully decode.

She feeds on the pale insects, catching them in the quiet nodes where they gather, and on the bioluminescent fungi, which taste of electricity and leave her tongue numb for hours. She drinks condensation that gathers on her bronze plates—water that tastes of metal and carries traces of the sound's frequency, keeping her body in harmony with her environment.

Her memories of the surface world have been edited by the sound. She remembers textures more than images, vibrations more than words. The city above exists for her only as a distant percussion—footfalls, cart wheels, the rhythmic pounding of grain mills—all of it filtering down to join the greater composition.

Sometimes she hosts others—not visitors, for no one visits intentionally, but the lost, the curious, the accidentally fallen. Most flee immediately, hands over ears, retching from the acoustic pressure. But occasionally, someone stays long enough for her to show them her work: the mathematical precision of the vine patterns, the way certain frequencies can induce visions, the moment when the sound aligns perfectly and the entire cistern becomes a single, thinking instrument.

She's cultivating something down here, though whether it's garden, instrument, or organism remains her secret. The wire-vines grow according to her design now, spreading through cracks into neighboring chambers, carrying the sound further into the city's forgotten underworld. She's teaching them to sing in harmony, to modulate the terrible thrumming into something approaching music—though music for what kind of listener, she cannot say.

Her greatest fear is silence—not that the sound will stop, but that she'll succeed too well in her tuning, that she'll transform the raw acoustic chaos into something comprehensible, finite, merely beautiful. So she works with deliberate imperfection, always leaving one plate slightly misaligned, one harmonic unresolved, maintaining the mystery that keeps her bound to this singing hollow beneath the unconscious city.

At night—though day and night mean nothing here—she sleeps suspended in a hammock of wire-vines that she's trained to hold her at the exact point where three major sound waves intersect. In this position, the vibrations cancel out around her body while continuing through her, turning her into a conduit, a living component of the cistern's acoustic machinery. Her dreams are frequencies made visible, and she wakes with new patterns to try, new orientations for her bronze plates, new hymns to teach the copper vines that grow ever more responsive to her presence.

She is becoming the cistern's voice, or perhaps it is becoming hers. The distinction matters less with each passing year, each microscopic adjustment, each new harmonic discovered and cultivated in this impossible garden of sound and living metal.
