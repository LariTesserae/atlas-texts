The Tenders move through the Cathedral like benthic fish through trenches—slow, deliberate, their modified lungs processing the thick atmosphere in visible pulses beneath translucent chest panels. They were human once, or mostly human, before the years of saturation changed them.

Their skin has taken on the nacre sheen of constant moisture, semi-permeable now, drinking and expelling water with each breath. Fingernails grow as coral plates. Hair has become something closer to kelp—dark strands that photosynthesize in the green-gold light, supplementing what their altered digestive systems can no longer fully process from food. They wear no clothes, only harnesses of woven tubing that connect them to the Cathedral's circulation systems, feeding them nutrients while filtering their waste into fertilizer.

The eldest among them, those who've tended for two decades or more, have begun to root. During their rest cycles, tendrils emerge from their heels and palms, seeking purchase in the grated floors. They must consciously detach each morning, leaving behind small colonies of their own cells that grow into the moss patterns they've learned to read like maps. They know every pipe by the taste of its condensation, every valve by the particular frequency of its vibration through their bones.

They communicate in bubble-language—words formed not with vocal cords but with careful exhalations that create spheres of meaning in the dense air. Complex conversations look like jellyfish blooms, thoughts rising and merging in the convection currents. The youngest Tenders still remember speech, their voices gurgling through the water-air, but the older ones have forgotten the harsh mechanics of consonants. Their language now is all vowels and currents, meanings that disperse and recombine like clouds.

New Tenders arrive looking human—maintenance workers who stayed too long, aquaculture engineers who couldn't leave their creations, urban explorers who discovered they preferred drowning in air to breathing on dry land. The Cathedral changes them cell by cell. First the lungs adapt, then the skin, then the long bones begin to hollow like bird bones, becoming lighter to move through the vertical maze. Their eyes develop nictitating membranes that filter the constant green light into bearable spectrums.

They tend with their bodies as much as with tools. A Tender's touch can redirect vine growth by the oils they secrete. Their breath, rich in specialized bacteria, can cure or cultivate the diseases that threaten the more delicate installations. When a new section of machinery is installed, they wrap themselves around it for days, their skin teaching the metal how to accept the moss, their presence attracting the first spores that will transform sterile steel into living architecture.

Some Tenders have specialized beyond recognition. The Depth Keepers live in the lowest levels, their bodies elongated from constantly navigating the narrow spaces between the oldest machines. They've developed bioluminescent patches along their spines to see in the darker reaches where even the filtered light cannot penetrate. The Canopy Weavers inhabit the highest points, their arms grown long and membranous, like flying squirrels adapted for swimming through air. They braid the aerial kelp into new configurations, training the growth patterns of the hanging gardens.

Between them move the Messengers—Tenders who've become more mobile than human, their joints rotating in ways that allow them to flow up pipes barely wider than their skulls. They carry chemical signals in specialized organs, broadcasting the Cathedral's needs through pheromone releases that other Tenders taste in the air. When a pH imbalance threatens a sector, when a new coral formation blocks crucial flow, when the newest machinery rejects its biological coating, the Messengers know first, their bodies serving as living sensors for the Cathedral's health.

The Tenders reproduce now by division rather than birth. When one has absorbed enough of the Cathedral, when their cells are more building than body, they find a quiet cultivation chamber and simply... separate. The process takes months. They grow still as coral, their consciousness dispersing into the structure itself while their body splits into two, three, sometimes four smaller forms. These offspring carry memories like instincts—knowing without learning how to taste the health of algae, how to read the moods of moss, how to navigate by the songs of slowly turning turbines.

Children arrive sometimes, brought by parents who've heard the stories. The Cathedral accepts them more quickly than adults, their young cells eager to adapt. Within weeks they breathe water as easily as air. Within months they've forgotten the sensation of dryness. They play in the vertical streams, riding the convection currents, their laughter bubble-language that needs no translation. They grow up believing that all humans can photosynthesize, that everyone's skin should shimmer with moisture, that the world has always been this green, this saturated, this alive.

The Tenders don't govern or decide—they simply tend. When tourists in breathing apparatus ask them questions, they respond with gestures that mean nothing to dry lungs. When inspectors arrive to check compliance with regulations, they find the Tenders have dissolved into the walls, become indistinguishable from the infrastructure they maintain. When the city beyond the Cathedral debates its future, the Tenders continue their slow work, knowing only that tomorrow there will be new growth to guide, new machines to naturalize, new water to breathe.

At night—though night here is just a deepening of green—they cluster in the spaces between spaces. They share nutrients through touch, their permeable skin allowing them to feed the weak, heal the damaged. They dream collective dreams carried in the water vapor, visions of the Cathedral growing ever upward, piercing clouds, reaching toward a sun they remember less clearly each year. In these dreams, the whole world is saturated, everyone moves like fish through air, and the distinction between human and habitat has been completely, peacefully, dissolved.

They are becoming what they tend. Each year more building, less body, until one day the Cathedral will be entirely alive, entirely aware, breathing through a thousand bodies that were once separate but are now merely currents in its vast, ascending waters.
