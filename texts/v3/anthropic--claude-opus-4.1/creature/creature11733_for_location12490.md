The Walkers arrived first, or perhaps they were made by the arriving—their origin inseparable from their function. They are tall, taller than necessity would dictate, with bodies of calcified membrane stretched between frameworks of something not quite bone. When they move, which is always, their joints produce a sound like chalk on slate, audible for miles in the still air.

They traverse the shelves in loose congregations of twelve to thirty, though these groups split and merge without ceremony when their paths cross. Their gait is deliberate, mechanical—not the steadiness of machines but of creatures that have found the optimal rhythm for covering infinite distance. Each step spans exactly the width of a thermal crack, as if they're reading the landscape like sheet music, following scores written in salt and fracture.

Their faces, if they can be called faces, are arrays of crystalline organs that refract light into component wavelengths. They see heat before color, structure before surface. When they pause—always at the intersection of expansion joints, never randomly—they lower these organ-clusters to the ground and taste the mineral sweat through porous patches in what might be their palms. They are recording something, or perhaps they are being recorded by the landscape itself, serving as mobile sensors for a system too large to perceive.

The Walkers tend the crystal-lichen without touching it. They exhale moisture at precise intervals, their breath visible as small clouds that settle over the paper-thin sheets just before the natural die-back would begin. This extends the reproductive window by hours, sometimes days. In return—though "return" implies a transaction they may not recognize—the dying vegetation releases compounds that adhere to the Walkers' membrane skin, forming patterns like frost. These patterns persist for weeks, slowly absorbed, possibly nourishing them or possibly serving some other purpose entirely.

Between the Walkers move the Collectors—smaller, denser beings that seem carved from the same aggregate as the shelves themselves. They are numerous but nearly invisible until they move, their surfaces matching the texture and color of whatever terrace they rest upon. They follow the Walkers at a careful distance, gathering what the tall ones leave behind: shed fragments of membrane, crystalline organs that have served their purpose and been discarded, the sticky residue where Walker-foot met mineral surface.

The Collectors process these materials inside their bodies through some method of chemical digestion that produces two byproducts: a clear fluid they excrete into the thermal cracks, and small, perfect spheres of compressed matter they stack in cairns at regular intervals across the shelves. The fluid accelerates the curing of new shelf material at the expanding edges. The spheres slowly sublimate in the heat, releasing traces of complex organics that drift on the rare winds.

There are also the Witnesses, though they barely qualify as inhabitants. They are more like weather patterns with intent—coherent distortions in the heat-haze that maintain their shape across miles of travel. They appear most clearly at dawn and dusk, when the temperature differential between air and stone is greatest. The Walkers acknowledge them with slight adjustments in their path, creating temporary corridors the Witnesses flow through. The Collectors ignore them entirely, or perhaps cannot perceive them.

The Witnesses seem to be observing the relationship between the Walkers and the landscape, hovering where new cracks form, condensing slightly when the crystal-lichen flushes green, following the drainage patterns of the rare rains with what might be interest. They leave no physical trace except for subtle variations in the rate of mineral deposition where they've lingered—some areas building faster, others slower, as if their presence affects the chemical processes of the shelves' self-construction.

During the cloudbursts, everything changes. The Walkers stand completely still, becoming towers in the shallow flood, their membrane skin drinking in water that won't come again for months. The Collectors seal themselves, becoming indistinguishable from stones until the water passes. But other things emerge in these moments—things that live in the deep cracks or in chambers beneath the shelves, things adapted to survive on moments of abundance separated by vast spans of waiting.

These storm-dwellers are soft, almost liquid themselves, with bodies that can compress to finger-width or expand to carpet entire terraces. They are hunters and scavengers both, consuming the crystal-lichen in great swathes, processing years of growth in hours. They secrete enzymes that temporarily soften the mineral surface, creating shallow pools where they spawn in frenetic, desperate abundance. Their young are microscopic, billions of them, most of which will percolate down through hairline cracks to wait years or decades for the next flood.

The Walkers harvest some of these young, not by catching but by allowing them to colonize the spaces between their membrane layers. There they grow slowly, fed by the Walker's mysterious metabolism, until they're large enough to drop off and burrow into the shelves, beginning the cycle again. Whether this is symbiosis or parasitism is unclear—perhaps it's neither, just another process in a landscape made of processes.

At the oldest sections of the shelves, where the surface has developed its subtle topography, there are signs of something else—something that might have been here before the Walkers, or might be what Walkers become given enough time. These are architectural in nature: spirals of fused membrane and mineral that rise from the surface like frozen whirlwinds. They're hollow, and when wind passes through them they produce tones too low for most hearing, vibrations that travel through the stone itself.

The Walkers avoid these structures without seeming to notice them. The Collectors build their cairns in patterns that orbit but never approach them. The Witnesses, however, congregate here, dozens of them sometimes, creating visible distortion halos around the spiral forms. During certain alignments of heat and light, images appear in these halos—not reflections but projections of somewhere else, or somewhen else. Views of the shelves before they hardened. Views of them worn down to nothing. Views of things that might inhabit them in futures that haven't been decided yet.

There are rumors—though who spreads rumors in a place like this is unclear—of things living at the very edges where new shelves are still forming. Things that shape the soft aggregate before it hardens, encoding messages or instructions in the pour lines and tool marks. If the Walkers know of them, they give no sign. Their paths never reach the true edges, always turning back before the landscape becomes truly new.

The relationship between all these inhabitants is not an ecosystem in any traditional sense. There's no clear flow of energy, no obvious predation or competition. Instead, they seem to be components of something larger—not a machine but a process of becoming that requires biological and geological and perhaps metaphysical elements working in concert. They are all tending something, or tending to something, or being tended by something that expresses itself through the medium of vast mineral shelves and the patient transformation of matter under an endless sun.

At night, when the stored heat radiates upward and creates its own weather systems, phosphorescent traces appear on the Walkers' routes—not from the Walkers themselves but from some reaction between their passing and the minerals below. These traces fade by dawn but never completely disappear. Over years, they build into visible paths, and over centuries, these paths begin to affect how the shelves themselves expand and crack. The inhabitants are writing something in the landscape, or the landscape is writing something through them, a text that will only be readable when the shelves have finally cured, or finally crumbled, or finally become whatever they're in the process of becoming.
