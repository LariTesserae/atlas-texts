The people of the plateau city move like deep-sea divers through air thick as brine. Their skin has taken on a peculiar quality — not quite waxy, not quite scaled, but something between, with a faint iridescence that repels the constant moisture. They've evolved shortened nasal passages and widened nostrils that filter the mineral-heavy vapor. Their eyes, perpetually bloodshot from the steam's minerals, have developed nictitating membranes that blink sideways every few seconds, clearing away the condensation.

They dress in layers of waxed leather and treated sharkskin, garments that have been cured in the steam vents until they're nearly waterproof. The leather develops its own patterns of oxidation — russet whorls and verdigris blooms that mark a garment's age and the districts its wearer frequents. Families pass down coats that have accumulated decades of mineral patina, their surfaces becoming maps of their wearers' lives. Children learn to read these patterns like genealogies.

The water miners are a breed apart — their bodies adapted through generations to the work. They've developed enlarged lung capacities and can hold their breath for nearly twenty minutes. Their fingernails grow thick and ridged, perfect for scraping deposits from vent walls. They communicate through a complex system of tugs on their safety lines, and their children are born with slightly webbed fingers, though whether this is evolution or the minerals' influence, no one can say. They live in communes near the vent openings, their homes built directly over the steam outlets so they wake each morning already acclimated to the heat and pressure.

In the processing plants, the crusher operators have gone partially deaf, but they've developed an uncanny sensitivity to vibration. They work barefoot, feeling the rhythm of the machines through the soles of their feet, detecting irregularities in the crush patterns by touch alone. Their children learn to speak by pressing their throats against their parents' backs, feeling the vibrations of words rather than hearing them. These families live in the industrial quarters, in apartments where the walls themselves pulse with the machinery's heartbeat.

The city's merchants have evolved their own adaptations. They conduct business in covered bazaars where elaborate systems of mirrors and colored glass communicate prices and quantities in flashing codes. Their most valued employees are the "tasters" — individuals who can determine a mineral sample's composition by touching it to their tongue, their palates trained to detect minute differences in metallic content. These tasters abstain from certain foods, maintaining the sensitivity of their taste buds, and their mouths are often stained yellow-green from years of sampling.

A secretive guild of "listeners" has emerged — individuals who claim to interpret the plateau's breathing, predicting where new steam vents will open or which sections of the city will experience structural shifts. They live in the deepest parts of the city, in chambers carved directly into the limestone, their ears pressed to the stone for hours at a time. Some say they've developed echolocation abilities similar to the pale pigeons, though they guard their methods jealously.

The children of the plateau city play games that would seem alien elsewhere. They compete to see who can balance longest on the slick surfaces, developing extraordinary proprioception. They keep pets — not dogs or cats, but the pale geckos, which they train to carry messages across the wet walls. Some children develop a peculiar condition where their hair grows in naturally waxed, forming water-repelling dreadlocks that need no treatment.

The elderly often develop a condition called "stone lung" — their alveoli becoming encrusted with mineral deposits after decades of breathing the heavy air. Rather than being seen as an affliction, it's considered a mark of distinction. These elders move slowly but purposefully, their breathing taking on the same deep rhythm as the plateau itself. They're consulted as living barometers, their labored breathing patterns supposedly predicting atmospheric changes days in advance.

Artists work in oxidation and erosion, creating temporary sculptures from reactive metals that bloom and decay in calculated patterns over months. Musicians have abandoned traditional instruments for percussion devices that work with the city's natural rhythms — drums filled with different densities of water, chimes that resonate only at specific humidity levels. The city's most celebrated performers are the "steam dancers" who perform in the venting clouds, their bodies appearing and disappearing in the billowing white, moving to rhythms only they can feel through the vibrations in the stone.

There are the outcasts too — those who couldn't adapt, whose bodies rejected the mineral saturation. They live in the driest sections of the city, in sealed chambers with dehumidifiers cobbled together from scavenged parts. They venture out only in full-body rubber suits, looking like deep-sea divers in reverse, trying to keep the moisture out rather than in. The other inhabitants view them with a mixture of pity and suspicion, calling them "the dry ones," whispering that they've lost the ability to hear the plateau's voice.

In the cable cars, operators have developed a sixth sense for the machinery's moods. They can feel through the control handles when a cable is about to snap, when a car is overloaded, when the moisture has affected the brakes. They navigate more by intuition than sight in the thick fog, their hands reading the cable's vibrations like braille. Their apprentices spend years just learning to feel the difference between normal operational shuddering and the first signs of mechanical failure.

At night, the inhabitants gather in the communal bath houses — not to wash, for they are never truly dry — but to sit in pools of varying mineral concentrations, their bodies absorbing different trace elements. These gatherings are social, medicinal, and ritual all at once. Conversations happen in gestures and light signals, creating a ballet of communication in the steamy darkness. Couples court by sharing mineral pools, their compatibility determined by how their skin reacts to the same chemical concentrations.
