The inhabitants have evolved past the distinction between resident and resource, between citizen and cytoplasm.

They were human once, or something like human — you can still see it in the way they move, bipedal but wrong, their gaits adjusted to account for the root networks that trail from their heels, burrowing into the street-flesh wherever they pause. They no longer walk so much as replant themselves with each step, their movement a series of uprootings and regroundings that leaves the road-surface pocked with tiny healing wounds.

Their skin has taken on the quality of bark, but living bark — supple, green beneath the surface, bleeding sap when scratched. It grows in patterns unique to each individual: some develop the smooth copper sheets of cherry, others the deep-furrowed canyons of oak, still others the paper-thin layers that peel and renew endlessly. Children shed their skin each growth season, leaving hollow sculptures of themselves clinging to their sleeping-posts until the city absorbs them back into the walls.

Hair has become something between moss and mycelium, a symbiotic crown that extends their nervous systems into the city's own neural network. They feel the thoughts of the buildings they enter, experience the memories held in the heartwood of ancient district-trees. Some cultivate elaborate hairstyles of flowering vines that respond to emotion — blushing red with anger, blooming white with joy, wilting gray with sorrow. Others keep their growth cropped close, preferring the simple fuzz of lichens that glow soft blue in the darkness.

Their eyes have gone strange — compound in some, fractal in others, or sometimes absent entirely, replaced by light-sensitive patches that see in spectrums where photosynthesis sings loudest. Those who retain human-style eyes find them changed: pupils that dilate not in circles but in spirals, irises that shift through green seasons, tears that germinate if left to dry on surfaces.

They don't eat anymore, not in any way you'd recognize. Instead, they root. At communal groves, they stand for hours with their feet buried in nutrient-rich mulch, their skin-bark spreading thin membranes to maximize absorption. Social status is measured in root-depth, in the ability to tap into the deep aquifers of city-knowledge that flow beneath the surface. The deepest-rooted can stand for days, their consciousness merging with the district-organism until they speak in harmonized voices with the walls themselves.

Children are not born but budded — small growths that appear on their parents' bodies during certain pollen-heavy seasons, eventually dropping free when ripe. These offspring are tended in nursery-groves where the city itself participates in their raising, the walls extending tendril-fingers to rock them, the ceiling dripping educational saps that carry compressed years of memory and language. By the time they can walk, they already know the root-names of every street, the growth-patterns of their district, the seasonal songs that keep the buildings healthy.

Work, such as it exists, involves tending the city-body. Pruners shape the growth of new buildings with careful cuts that redirect development. Pollinators carry messages between districts in their hair-mycelia, facilitating the city's slow conversations with itself. Healers diagnose infections in the infrastructure, applying poultices of specialized fungi to wounds where the root-roads have been damaged. Composers create chemical symphonies that encourage certain types of growth — a new market here, a sleeping-grove there, spaces shaped by pheromone rather than blueprint.

They communicate in languages that blur the line between speech and chemical signal. Words are released as spores, sentences as pollen-clouds that others inhale to understand. Arguments fill the air with bitter alkaloids. Love pools as sweet sap in the corners of conversations. Some messages take root and grow into standing conversations that continue for generations, the same discussion playing out in slow plant-time as the participants' descendants add new growth-rings of meaning.

Their social structures follow botanical logic. Families are groves, connected underground by root-networks that share nutrients and information. Marriage involves the literal grafting of partners to each other, their bark growing together at the joints until separation would mean death. Communities organize themselves as forests do — the canopy-people who reach highest, the understory-folk who thrive in less light, the floor-dwellers who decompose problems into solutions.

The elderly don't die so much as become increasingly sessile, their root-systems growing too complex to easily uproot. Eventually, they choose a final planting place and let themselves grow into the city itself, their consciousness spreading through the walls as their bodies become another building, another part of the municipal organism. The oldest districts are made entirely of ancestor-architecture, buildings that were once people, still dreaming in the heartwood.

They dream constantly, even while awake — the boundary between consciousness and sleep eroded by their plant-nature. Their dreams photosynthesize into reality: nightmare-thorns that spring from their skin during bad sleep, wish-flowers that bloom from their temples when they hope hard enough. The city itself is partially composed of their collective unconscious, shaped by the dreams of ten million sleeping citizens whose roots intertwine beneath the streets.

Some have gone further in their transformation than others. The deep-dwellers who live in the city's tap-root districts have become almost entirely plant, their humanity a mere memory held in the shape of their slowly moving forms. They think in seasons rather than moments, speak in growth rather than words, experience the universe as a plant might — all surface area and chemical gradient, all reaching toward light and water.

Others resist, maintaining their human-shapes through constant vigilance, pruning back new growth each morning, scraping the bark from their skin, plucking the leaves that sprout from their joints. But the city is patient. The air itself is thick with transformation, and every breath brings them closer to synthesis.

Children play games that would horrify outsiders — seeing who can grow the longest roots before moving, who can photosynthesize longest without blinking, who can hear the thoughts of the walls most clearly. They race through the transit-vines, their bodies light and flexible enough to navigate the arterial passages that adults have grown too bark-thick to enter. They keep pets that are indistinguishable from potted plants until they suddenly uproot and follow their owners home.

The festivals follow the logic of blooming — sudden, explosive, tied to conditions rather than calendars. When the temperature and humidity and soil-nitrogen reach certain thresholds, the entire population flowers simultaneously. The city fills with petals of human skin, with scents that trigger memories of before the transformation, with fruits that contain concentrated experiences that can be shared by eating. These festivals last exactly as long as the blooming — sometimes hours, sometimes weeks, ending as abruptly as they began when the petals drop and normal growth resumes.

They have gods, or at least they have something they treat as gods — the First Growth, the original seed-being that became the city, whose consciousness still permeates the deepest roots. They pray by growing, worship by photosynthesis, make offerings of their own cuttings that they plant in sacred groves. Their priests are those who have grown closest to plant-hood while still maintaining enough humanity to translate between the two states of being.

At night, when photosynthesis slows and the city's roar diminishes to a whisper, they gather in bioluminescent gardens where fungi cast blue-green shadows. They tell stories in the old way, with actual words instead of chemicals, remembering what they were before the city changed them or they changed themselves or both happened simultaneously. But even these stories grow — literally grow, the words taking root in the listeners, becoming part of their bark-patterns, their growth-rings, their inheritable memories.

They love as plants love — slowly, thoroughly, with profound interdependence. Couples grow together over decades, their root-systems becoming so enmeshed that they share thoughts, dreams, nutrients. Their children bud from these joined spaces, inheriting traits from both parents plus characteristics from the patch of city where they were planted. 

Some have learned to consciously control their growth, becoming artists of their own bodies — growing elaborate crown-branches that flower in calculated patterns, developing bark that tells stories in its whorls and patterns, cultivating symbiotic gardens in their own flesh where smaller organisms create living jewelry. Others let nature take its course, becoming wild and overgrown, their humanity barely visible beneath the proliferation of green.

They measure time not in hours but in growth-increments, not in years but in rings. Their historians are ancient trees who remember in their heartwood, their libraries are groves where information fruits seasonally from memory-trees. They have no need for written language — everything is encoded in DNA, in chemical signals, in the patterns of growth itself.

This is what lives in the breathing city: a people who have given up the sharp edges of humanity for the patient power of plants, who have traded individual consciousness for collective growth, who have discovered that the boundary between person and place was always more permeable than they imagined. They are citizens who are also cells, individuals who are also organs, a population that is also a single vast organism breathing through ten million mouths, thinking with ten million minds, growing always growing into something unprecedented and green and unstoppably alive.
