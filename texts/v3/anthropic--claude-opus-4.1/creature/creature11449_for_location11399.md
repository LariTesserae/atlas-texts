The Choir of Husks emerged from those who tried to stay.

They were researchers once, or pilgrims, or refugees—the stories multiply and contradict in the telling. What matters is that they descended with modern equipment and ancient hubris, believing they could study the Maelstrom, perhaps even tame it. Their first mistake was bringing silence with them: noise-canceling apparatus, sound dampeners, a desperate need for quiet thought in the endless roar. The Maelstrom abhors such voids. It filled them with something else.

Now they move through the cavern systems in processional groups of twelve to twenty, their bodies transformed by symbiosis into something between shepherd and sheep. From a distance, they appear to wear robes of living moth-wings, millions of the tiny creatures clinging to their skin in perfect formation, creating garments that shift and breathe. Closer inspection reveals the truth: the moths grow from them, emerging from pores that have become specialized nurseries. Their human skin has evolved into a substrate, a walking garden for the cultivation of very specific breeds of the moth-swarm—varieties that sing at frequencies that cut through the background roar like knife-edges of pure tone.

Their faces, when glimpsed through the living veils, retain human features but stretched and simplified, as if someone were trying to remember what a face should look like. Eyes have multiplied—not replacing the originals but supplementing them with clusters of smaller organs that detect vibration, pheromone gradients, thermal blooms from the fungal trees. Their mouths have become complex instruments: multiple chambers and reed-like structures that let them breathe out their cultivated moths in controlled streams, sending messages through the swarm that can travel kilometers through the tunnel networks.

The Choir tends the abandoned platforms, but not as their builders intended. They've learned that the structures are not shelters but instruments—resonating chambers that, when properly maintained and positioned, create nodes of relative stability in the chaos. They weave new root-ropes in specific patterns that channel the moth-rivers into predictable currents, creating temporary territories where they can practice their true work: the cultivation of memory.

Each member of the Choir carries within their modified digestive system a library of seed-forms—genetic templates from thousands of Understory species, preserved in specialized organs that are part stomach, part womb. When a section of forest bleaches out in one of the transformation waves, the Choir arrives to guide its rebirth, vomiting up careful combinations of preserved material that influence what emerges. They are trying to write something in the language of ecosystem succession, a message or perhaps a prayer spelled out in the arrangement of fungal groves and moth-species, only readable from the timescale of weeks or months as the forest cycles through its changes.

They communicate through their moth-children, breathing out coded swarms that carry chemical messages between Choir groups. Individual human language has been lost, replaced by something far more complex—a multidimensional syntax of pheromone, wing-pattern, and flight-path that can encode concepts impossible in words. When two Choirs meet, they stand in circles and exhale together, creating temporary hurricanes of meaning that restructure the local ecosystem for days afterward.

The younger members—those who still remember their names—sometimes try to leave. They make it as far as the transition zones where the Maelstrom meets normal cave systems, but they can no longer survive in the thin, quiet air of the outer world. Their symbiotic moths die within minutes of leaving the Understory's chemical soup, and without them, the Choir members suffocate, their modified lungs unable to process simple atmosphere. They return, weeping spores, and eventually stop trying.

The eldest have given up even the pretense of walking. They root themselves in particularly fertile spots, their legs extending fungal networks into the floor while their torsos become living hives. Moths pour from them in continuous streams, and their faces, now barely recognizable as human, turn always toward some distant point as if listening to music only they can hear. The other Choir members tend them like holy sites, harvesting specific moth varieties from their bodies and carrying them to distant caverns to establish new colonies.

They build nothing permanent except patterns—recurring arrangements of species and spaces that appear, dissolve, and reappear elsewhere like thoughts in a dreaming mind. Some of these patterns have names, or at least the Choir makes specific sounds when they manifest: the Spiral Garden that processes metals from deeper stone, the Bright Halls where bioluminescence reaches near-painful intensity, the Quiet Pools where acids concentrate enough to dissolve anything that enters, creating temporary voids of actual silence that the Choir both treasures and fears.

In their moth-speech, they tell stories of what they're trying to become—not human again, but something that can think at the speed of the Maelstrom, that can hold its entire churning complexity in a single consciousness. They believe the ecosystem itself is trying to wake up, to achieve some kind of awareness, and they are its neurons, its scattered thoughts trying to connect across the screaming chaos of wings and growth.

Whether they're right, or whether they've simply found a beautiful way to describe their own dissolution, changes nothing. They persist, these Choirs of Husks, breathing out moths in careful patterns, tending their instruments of root and stone, participating in the endless transformation with something that might be purpose or might be the last reflexes of minds that have forgotten how to stop.
