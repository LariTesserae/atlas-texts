## The Inhabitants

The Accountants arrived first, or perhaps they were always here—their distinction from the original workforce lost to the drowning years. They wear the water like heavy wool suits, their forms suggested rather than seen: a darkness that moves against the current, a depression in the sediment where something just stood. Their bodies, if bodies they have, seem assembled from ledger pages and adding machine tape, gone translucent and fibrous as jellyfish bells. They cluster in the old financial district, passing through walls as if the architecture were merely a suggestion, trailing receipts that dissolve before they can be read.

They tend to the pneumatic tubes with religious devotion, feeding the system an endless stream of capsules containing invoices for work never completed, shipping manifests for vessels that will never surface, careful calculations of losses that compound infinitely in the dark. When they meet—which they do, at the prescribed hours, in flooded conference rooms where the tables have become reef structures—they communicate in clicking sounds like abacus beads, like typewriter keys, like coins falling into deep water.

The Shift Supervisors maintain their routes through the factory floors, though their forms have stretched and ribboned to accommodate the three-dimensional nature of their new patrols. They are recognizable by their luminous armbands—bands of bioluminescent tissue that still display the faded letters of their authority. They have grown additional eyes along their elongated torsos, each one fixed on a different production line, watching the ghost of industry with unblinking vigilance. Their mouths, when they open them, reveal rows of teeth like punch-card holes, speaking in languages of perforation and absence.

In the foundry district lives the Foreman, singular and vast. It has distributed itself through the entire complex, its nervous system running along the old electrical conduits, its sense organs blooming from junction boxes like anemones. It dreams in thermal gradients, thinking in the slow pulse of the bioluminescent furnaces. When it needs to manifest locally, it assembles a body from the available materials—rust flakes and fish bones, copper wire and kelp strands—into something approximately human-shaped but wrong in every particular. It wears a hard hat made of barnacle shell, and carries a clipboard of pressed algae where it records temperatures, pressures, productions that exist only in its distributed imagination.

The Secretary Pool occupies the administrative center, though "occupy" suggests too much solidity. They are a collective entity now, their individual bodies having merged into something like a slime mold that thinks in triplicate, filing itself through the building's pneumatic arteries. They photosynthesize weakly in the phosphorescent light, their surface a living carbon paper that records everything that passes near them. Sometimes they extrude a hand—perfectly manicured, wearing rings of oxidized copper—to stamp documents that immediately dissolve. They gossip in chemical signals, secrets spreading through their mass like tea through water.

The Maintenance Crews have evolved furthest from their origins. Their tool belts have become permanent parts of their anatomy—wrenches growing from hip bones, screwdrivers extending from fingertips that branch and branch again. They've developed a symbiotic relationship with the building materials themselves, their skin secreting oils that prevent further rust, their breath somehow sustaining the impossible mechanical functions. They move in small schools through the pipes and ducts, their bodies compressing and expanding as needed, more liquid than solid. They sing while they work—not in sound but in vibration, harmonics that keep the ancient machinery turning.

In the clock tower lives the Timekeeper, who may have been human once but now exists as a function of the mechanism itself. Its body has spread through the gears and springs like a circulatory system, its heartbeat synchronized to the escapement, its thoughts ticking forward one second at a time. It has prophetic visions of all the moments that have passed and will pass in the drowned district, but can only communicate them through the arrangement of the clock hands, spelling out messages in the angles between hours and minutes that no one else can read.

The children—if children they ever were—school through the district like fish, but fish that remember being human. They wear the remnants of school uniforms, the fabric long since replaced by scales that still hold the pattern of plaid, the suggestion of knee socks. They carry lunch boxes that open onto voids, textbooks whose pages turn themselves, writing out lessons in languages that evolve by the day. They play games that look like drowning in reverse, rising toward the ice ceiling before sinking back into the mechanical depths, laughing in frequencies that only the buildings can hear.

And in the deepest parts, where the industrial basements connect to natural caves, to spaces that were never meant for human occupation, live the Shareholders. No one sees them directly—only their influence, the way certain decisions ripple through the district without apparent source. They communicate through fluctuations in pressure, in the subtle rearrangement of debris, in the careful manipulation of the eternal production that continues without product. They own everything and nothing, existing as pure equity in a market that no longer exists, their portfolios comprised of rust and silence and the slow transformation of one thing into another.

All of them together form a closed economy, a hierarchy of purposes that reference only themselves, a workforce without work, maintaining the memory of industry in the grammar of their transformed bodies. They are what happens when human systems outlive their creators, when the organizational chart becomes an evolutionary tree, when the means of production develop their own ends.
