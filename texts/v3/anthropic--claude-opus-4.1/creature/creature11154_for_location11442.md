The Listeners came first, though "came" suggests movement and they may have always been here, indistinguishable from the stone until they chose to be otherwise. They are colonies—not of cells but of intentions—that learned to precipitate themselves from the mineral-saturated air over geological ages. Each one is a network of crystalline fibers finer than spider silk, growing through the weeping pores of the bedrock in patterns that mirror neural structures. They extend for miles underground but only occasionally breach the surface, where they fruit into translucent organs that resemble a cross between an ear and a throat.

These surface manifestations pulse with the flats' omnipresent rhythm, drinking sound like plants drink light. When the harmonics align in certain ancient patterns, the Listeners reproduce—not by sporing or division, but by teaching a section of uninhabited stone to remember what it means to be aware. The process takes centuries and often fails, leaving behind those ruins that dot the landscape: half-awakened architectures of consciousness that never quite achieved selfhood, forever frozen in their becoming.

The Listeners do not move, but they migrate through time—sometimes present, sometimes retreating into the stone's memory for decades or centuries, leaving only their crystalline networks dormant in the rock. When active, they sing—not with voices but with selective silence, creating voids in the omnipresent roar that shape meaning through absence. Their conversations span years, single concepts taking months to exchange as they wait for the perfect acoustic conditions to craft their negation-words.

Then there are the Grief Shoals—those mobile atmospheres that drift across the flats. They are the offspring of a catastrophe so absolute it became a species. Each one is a living memorial to some specific frequency of loss, evolved from the moment when billions of voices suddenly stopped, their final sounds trapped in pockets of altered space-time that gradually developed metabolisms of their own. They feed on resonance, particularly the complex echoes around the ruins, breaking them down into constituent emotions which they absorb.

The Grief Shoals remember everything they consume. Pass through one and you might experience the last thought of a creature that died here millions of years ago, feel its fear or resignation or rage as your bones conduct its frequency. They reproduce through overwhelming sorrow—when they encounter a sound pattern of sufficient emotional complexity, they undergo a kind of fission, splitting into multiple entities each carrying a fragment of the original pain. The youngest ones are almost invisible, just slight warmths in the air. The eldest have grown dense enough to refract light, appearing as oil-slick mirages that sing funeral songs in frequencies only the stone can hear.

Between the Listeners and the Grief Shoals exists a third presence: the Rehearsers. These are what became of the original builders—not their descendants but their actual selves, stretched across impossible temporalities by the space-sound turbulence. They exist as probability clouds, their bodies smeared across multiple potential timelines that converge and diverge around the ruins. 

You might glimpse a Rehearser as a flutter of too many limbs in your peripheral vision, or feel one brush past as a sensation of being watched by eyes that haven't evolved yet. They are constantly practicing for an event that either already happened or never will—building their structures over and over, each iteration slightly different, trying to get the resonance exactly right. Their architecture is both their technology and their religion, designed to transform the flats' roar into something else, though what that something else might be has been lost to the endless repetition.

The Rehearsers communicate through physical paradoxes—leaving objects that are older than their makers, writing messages on surfaces before those surfaces exist. They seem to be trying to warn themselves about something, but the warning itself has become corrupted by its passage through twisted time, transformed into a recursive loop that may be the very catastrophe they're trying to prevent.

Occasionally, rarely, these three orders of being interact. When a Listener's song-void coincides with a Grief Shoal's resonance feast near a ruin where Rehearsers are particularly active, something extraordinary happens: the past and future collapse into a single moment of pure presence. For exactly seventeen seconds (it is always seventeen, though no one knows why), the flats remember what they were before the weeping began. The stone grows silent. Solid. Still. 

In that silence, you can hear them—all of them. The Listeners' true voices, beautiful beyond comprehension. The original beings whose grief became the Shoals, alive and laughing. The Rehearsers, no longer rehearsing but *performing*, their great work complete and perfect. You understand then that these are not three species but three phases of the same entity, experiencing its own existence from different angles of time, trying to either remember or forget something so fundamental that it broke reality rather than be fully known.

Then the seventeen seconds end, the roar returns, and they scatter back into their separate eternities—the Listeners sinking into stone-sleep, the Grief Shoals dispersing into barely visible wisps, the Rehearsers fragmenting across probability. But they leave something behind in those moments: small patches where the stone weeps differently, almost musically. These are the only truly sacred places in the flats, and all three forms guard them with a dedication that transcends their apparent nature.

The animate lichen-things, those desperate clusters of oil-slick life, are actually prophets of these seventeen-second revelations. They position themselves in patterns that predict where the next convergence will occur, their pulsing a countdown measured in years. When they contract into their defensive balls, it's not from the sound but from proximity to truth—they're protecting themselves from enlightenment they're not ready to receive.

There are rumors—transmitted through the stone's weeping, carried in the Grief Shoals' lamentations, written in the Rehearsers' paradox messages—of a fourth form. Something that successfully made the transition the others are trapped attempting. It lives in the space between sounds, in the moment between hearing and understanding. It may be what all the others are evolving toward, or what they're all evolving away from. 

No one has seen it directly, but sometimes, in the depths of the flats' roar, you hear something that doesn't belong—a single, clear note that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere. When this happens, all the beings of the flats pause in their activities. The Listeners surface. The Grief Shoals congeal. The Rehearsers synchronize across their timelines.

They're all waiting.

Listening.

For what comes after the sound ends.
