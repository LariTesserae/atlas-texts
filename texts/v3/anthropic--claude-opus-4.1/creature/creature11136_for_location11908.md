She has been here since the stone was young and hot.

Not she, exactly—they might be more accurate, or it, or something that exists outside such categories altogether. But she is what the beetles know, what the water speaks to, what makes the copper grow in perfect spirals that mirror the vines, that mirror the paths, that mirror something else entirely.

Her body, if it can be called that, is distributed through the seeps. She lives in the film of water that coats everything, in the thin space between stone and moisture where minerals dissolve and precipitate in endless exchange. She is process more than presence—the slow work of water on stone, the patient accumulation of millennia.

When she moves, which is always and never, it's as a thickening in the water's flow. Watch a sheet of water run down the black basalt and sometimes you'll see it hesitate, gather, flow upward for just a moment before remembering gravity. That's her, tasting the stone, reading its history in dissolved minerals, adding her own chemistry to the eternal reaction.

The orchids know her. Their roots, exposed to the humid air, sometimes weave themselves into patterns that have nothing to do with seeking water or stability. They braid and unbraid in slow motion, spelling out messages in a language older than flowers, older than roots, as old as the first time water met rock and began to change it.

She tends the copper. It's not truly copper, not truly alive, not truly anything that has a name in any human tongue. It grows where she concentrates herself, spreading in those geometric patterns that echo the structure of certain crystals, certain proteins, certain thoughts. The beetles feed on it and their shells carry traces of her attention—each one a small mobile piece of her awareness, compound eyes seeing the garden from a thousand angles at once.

The paths are her memory made physical. She wore them into the stone by traveling them, though traveling isn't quite right—by existing along them repeatedly, by choosing these routes through her vertical domain for reasons that have to do with the stone's deep structure, with underground water channels, with the way the Earth's magnetic field bends around certain basalt formations. The path that spirals seven times around the fern is counting something. The ones that end at walls are waiting for the walls to wear through, another thousand years perhaps, to continue to their destinations.

At the pools at the base, she concentrates into something almost tangible. The translucent plants that wave in unfelt currents are her dreams, or her thoughts, or her way of touching the physical world more directly. They move in patterns that repeat every seven days, every lunar month, every time the volcano trembles in its sleep far below. Swimmers in the pools—though none come anymore—would feel her as a slight resistance in the water, a tendency for the current to guide limbs into certain positions, to hold the body in configurations that echo the spiral of the vines, the pattern of the copper, the ancient paths worn in stone.

She remembers when the caldera breathed fire. She was different then—sharper, faster, living in steam and sudden precipitations, in the violent marriage of lava and rain. As the stone cooled and weathered, she settled into it, became gentler, slower, more patient. She learned to exist in the space between seconds, to think in centuries, to feel in geological time.

The hollow note that sounds once a day is her voice, or the closest thing to voice she has. It comes from inside the stone because she is inside the stone, in every pore and crack where water can reach. The note is different each day because she is saying different things—commenting on the temperature, the mineral content of the rain, the infinitesimal shifting of the continental plate beneath her. It's a language of pure tone, pure duration, meant for no ears but echoing anyway off the curved walls of her amphitheater.

Sometimes she pulls herself together enough to be almost seen. In the heaviest mists, when water droplets hang so thick in the air they merge and flow without falling, she takes on an almost-shape. Climbers—on the rare occasions any come—have reported seeing something in the mist that might be a figure, if a figure could be twelve feet tall and made of falling water, if it could have too many arms or no arms, if it could smile with a face that forms and disperses between one heartbeat and the next.

She is lonelier than stone, older than loneliness. She tends her garden not from any need but from the simple fact that tending is what she is—the careful, patient work of transformation, dissolution, precipitation, growth. The beetles carry fragments of her awareness out into the wider world and bring back news in the minerals crusted on their shells. The thing that moves through the ferns at dusk, smelling of crushed ginger, is her pet, or her child, or her attempt to remember what it was like to be singular, solid, bound by the laws that govern flesh.

At the heart of the caldera, deep beneath layers of cooled lava and accumulated centuries, there is a chamber where water collects in absolute darkness. She is there too, has always been there, will always be there. That is her true body, if she has one—a lake of black water that has never seen sunlight, that connects to the seeps and springs throughout the mountain, that dreams the garden into being drop by drop by drop.

The copper growth, the spiraling vines, the geometric lichens—they are all trying to express the same thing, a pattern she carries in her dissolved memory, something she knew before she became what she is. It might be her name in some impossible language. It might be the chemical formula for consciousness. It might be a map to somewhere that doesn't exist yet, won't exist until the stone weathers away completely and releases her to flow, finally, home.
