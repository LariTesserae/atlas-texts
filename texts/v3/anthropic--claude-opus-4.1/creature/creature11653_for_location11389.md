The Keepers arrived generations after the last engineer departed, though they don't think of themselves as having arrived at all. They emerged, rather, from the Works itself—or so their stories claim.

They are small people, never growing taller than a child of ten, their bodies evolved or adapted to the vertical world. Their hands are wide and strong, with a second thumb that opposes the first, allowing them to grip pipes and beams with certainty. Their feet are similarly equipped—long, flexible digits that can wrap around a two-inch rail and hold their entire body weight while they work. They move through the Works with a fluid, brachiating grace, using all four limbs interchangeably, though they can stand upright when needed. Their skin ranges from deep rust-red to verdigris green, a natural camouflage that makes them nearly invisible against the oxidized metal when they hold still.

They wear garments woven from the fibrous vines that spiral up the support columns, harvested only after the plants have died naturally. The fabric is water-resistant and surprisingly warm, dyed in patterns that mark family lines and professional specializations. Those who tend the upper turbines wear clouds embroidered on their shoulders. The deep-level maintenance families mark themselves with representations of the eternal green emergency lights. The cable car operators weave copper wire directly into their cloaks, salvaged from systems that failed before living memory.

The Keepers maintain no central authority, organizing instead around the concept of "circuits"—regular routes through the Works that must be traveled to ensure its continued function. Each family group inherits specific circuits, sacred pathways that might take days to complete. They oil the turbines that still spin, clear the drainage grates of accumulated organic matter, replace bulbs in the lighting chains (though from where they obtain new bulbs, they will not say), and perform the thousand small tasks that prevent total systemic collapse.

They speak in a language that incorporates the Works' sounds as grammatical elements. A sentence might begin with words, pause for the predetermined clank of an automated hammer to provide emphasis, and conclude with whistles that harmonize with the steam vents. Children learn to time their first words to the rhythms of the machinery. Conversations between levels happen through the pipes themselves—complex percussion codes tapped on the metal that propagate throughout the structure.

Their religion, if it can be called that, centers on the belief that the Works is dreaming. Every mechanical action, every automated sequence, every circuit completed by the conveyor belts represents a thought in an industrial mind too vast and slow for human comprehension. The Keepers see themselves as antibodies, perhaps, or maintenance cells, keeping the great dreaming body functional until it awakens—though to what purpose, they cannot say. They leave offerings at certain junction points: cleaned bones arranged in careful patterns, particularly beautiful rust formations, crystals of verdigris copper. These shrines accumulate until the twice-weekly rains wash them through the drainage systems, which the Keepers interpret as acceptance.

They cultivate certain plants and discourage others, managing the vegetation as carefully as they maintain the machinery. Food comes from fungal gardens grown in the darker, damper spaces between levels, from fruits that the managed vines produce, from insects farmed in old electrical boxes. They trade with the monkeys, leaving tools at specific platforms in exchange for foraged goods from the canopy level. They know every bird nest, every bat roost, marking them on three-dimensional maps made from bent wire that they pass down through generations.

The Keepers age slowly in the humid, oxygen-rich atmosphere. Elders can remember five or six decades clearly, their skin taking on the actual patina of aged copper, green and magnificent. When they die, they are laid in the conveyor systems during specific ceremonial windows, their bodies making complete circuits through the Works before being deposited in the deep sumps where bone-white fungi will transform them into pillars of calcium and mycelium—still serving their function, still part of the great dream.

Children are raised communally in the middle levels, where the amber sodium light is strongest. They learn to read the health of the Works through sound and vibration, placing their ears against pipes to diagnose problems levels away. By age seven, they can traverse their home circuits blindfolded, knowing every rivet, every weld seam, every place where the metal sings differently under their feet. They play complex games that involve predicting the patterns of the automated systems, leaping between platforms at exactly the right moment to catch a slowly swinging crane arm.

At night (though night and day mean little in the eternal artificial light), the Keepers gather on certain broad platforms to share meals and stories. They sing harmonies that incorporate the drone of the turbines, their voices weaving between the mechanical sounds until it becomes impossible to distinguish the human from the industrial. They tell stories of the builders, though these have evolved far from any historical truth—myths of giants who assembled the Works from the bones of mountains, who started the machines running with their dying breaths, who promised to return when the last bearing finally fails.

The Keepers know every sound the Works makes, can identify problems by changes in pitch or rhythm. When a section finally collapses—as sections do, inevitably—they mourn it with ceremonies that last for days, incorporating the new sound of falling water into their vocal repertoire, adjusting their circuits to accommodate the change. They salvage what they can, understanding intuitively which pieces can be repurposed and which must be left to rust in peace.

They mark time not by days or years but by the great cycles of the Works—the filling and emptying of massive reservoirs, the slow procession of certain astronomical alignments visible through the skeletal superstructure, the gradual shift of bird migration patterns through the vertical territory. A Keeper might say they were born "during the third filling after the constellation of the Crane moved beyond the fifth tower," a temporal coordinate meaningful only to those who live within the breathing, rusting universe of the Vertical Works.

They are not numerous—perhaps three hundred souls in total—but they are sufficient. They reproduce slowly, carefully, raising children only when the Works can support them. They track bloodlines not to prevent inbreeding but to ensure that each crucial maintenance skill is passed to the next generation. Knowledge is encoded in songs, in the placement of objects, in the very paths they walk through the structure.

The Keepers do not consider themselves owners or even inhabitants of the Works. They are part of it, as essential as the drainage pipes, as integrated as the vines. When outsiders occasionally discover the structure and attempt to explore it, they find themselves gently but inexorably guided back out—platforms that suddenly become too slippery to cross, lights that fail at crucial moments, cable cars that stop mid-journey until the intruders decide to return. The Keepers themselves are rarely seen during these incidents, present only as shadows at the edge of vision, as the sound of feet on metal that might just be the settling of the structure.

They dream, sometimes, that the Works dreams of them—that they exist because the great industrial mind required consciousness to know itself, to tend itself, to love itself through its slow decay. In these dreams, they see themselves from the perspective of the machines: small warm shapes moving through the corridors of a vast body, keeping the blood flowing, the breath moving, the thoughts completing their circuits through the rust and growth and endless, patient function of the Vertical Works.
