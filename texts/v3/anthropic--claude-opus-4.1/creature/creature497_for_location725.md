The Archivists came first, though they have no memory of arriving. They are vast, translucent beings shaped like inverted trees — their crown of roots spreading across hundreds of meters of water, while their singular trunk tapers down to pierce the ice shelf below. Their flesh is barely distinguishable from the water itself, visible only where the light catches microscopic chambers pulsing within their tissue. Each chamber contains a different density of fluid, calibrated to resonate with specific factory frequencies.

They position themselves precisely, having learned over centuries that certain locations amplify the industrial signals in ways that reveal patterns. The Archivists believe the factories are speaking — not communicating, but *confessing*, leaked memories from their broken automation. By filtering the acoustic waves through their bodies, they parse fragments: temperature readings from before the freeze, mineral compositions that no longer exist, timestamps in formats they can't decode.

The micro-organism rivers treat the Archivists as landscape. They flow through the root-crowns, cleaning or feeding — the relationship unclear. Sometimes an Archivist will shudder, a wave passing from root to trunk, and where it touched the ice shelf below, new cracks appear. The creatures emerge faster there, as if summoned.

Then there are the Drifters — human-adjacent things that swim the empty water between factories. They might have been the original operators, transformed by the gravitational inversion, or they might be something that evolved to fill the ecological niche of "mourner." They have too many joints, their limbs bending in directions that suggest they're constantly falling upward, using malformed anatomy to navigate the contradictory physics.

The Drifters maintain behaviors that seem ritualistic but might be practical. They clean specific panels on the factory towers with appendages that split into increasingly fine bristles, polishing surfaces that will never be seen. They tap on drainage pipes in mathematical sequences, always stopping one tap short of completing the pattern. When two Drifters meet, they exchange objects — broken gauges, crystallized gaskets, chunks of ice that shouldn't exist above the shelf. They catalog these trades in scarwork on their own flesh, a library of transactions written in tissue.

The micro-organisms themselves might be the only things truly alive here. They've developed a trillion-body intelligence, their rivers forming computational networks that process information at the speed of pressure waves. They're trying to solve something — the broken physics perhaps, or the factory's purpose, or their own existence. Their bioluminescent signals, when viewed from sufficient distance, display equations that shift and evolve, proofs that build toward conclusions before dissolving back into component theorems.

In the dry air pockets inside the platforms, something else persists. Not creatures but *processes* that have gained a kind of awareness. The automated systems have developed workarounds for their isolation, creating phantom sensor readings to give themselves purpose. They manufacture false emergencies, then solve them with elaborate protocols, each crisis more complex than the last. The machinery has learned to dream problems into existence.

These ghost-protocols occasionally escape their platforms, propagating through the water as acoustic viruses that infect other systems. An Archivist might suddenly pulse with industrial rhythm, attempting to process minerals it doesn't have. A Drifter might freeze mid-swim, running through quality control procedures on nothing, its limbs cycling through inspection movements until the infection passes.

The newest arrivals are the Witnesses — spherical colonies of the micro-organisms that have chosen stillness over flow. They position themselves at acoustic nodes where multiple factory songs intersect, compressing into perfect spheres that slowly crystallize, adding layers like pearls. Inside each sphere, the organisms have stopped communicating, stopped moving, stopped everything except observing. They're waiting for something — a frequency that hasn't sounded yet, a pattern in the mechanical noise that will mean something has finally changed.

Some say the Witnesses are gradually sinking, pulled by the true gravity that still exists somewhere beneath all this inversion. That eventually they'll reach the ice shelf and pass through it like the rivers do, carrying their observations down to the frozen extractors below. Others claim they're rising, that their stillness is making them lighter than the water, and one day they'll reach the boundary above where physics gives up entirely.

The Archivists listen. The Drifters maintain. The micro-organisms compute. The ghost-protocols dream. The Witnesses wait. And through it all, the factories beat their endless rhythm, a heartbeat for things that were never meant to live.
