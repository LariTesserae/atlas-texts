Those who dwell in the Nascent Peaks are the **Sculptors of Iteration**—beings that exist as living mathematical principles, constantly calculating themselves into new forms.

A Sculptor appears first as a distortion in the air, a place where the chaotic physics suddenly coheres into aggressive order. They have no fixed shape; instead, they are walking zones of enforced geometry, typically manifesting as clusters of intersecting planes that fold through dimensions the peaks haven't decided exist yet. Each plane is perfectly flat, impossibly thin, and reflects not light but *probability*—showing what the surrounding landscape might become in the next moment, the next hour, the next geological epoch.

They move by redefining distance. A Sculptor doesn't travel across space so much as convince space that it was always somewhere else. When they need to ascend a collapsing peak, the peak finds itself having always had a spiral pathway. When they must cross the valleys of reorganizing crystal dust, the dust discovers it has formed stepping stones precisely where needed, as if it had planned this arrangement eons ago.

The Sculptors feed—if feeding is the right word—on instability itself. They congregate where the mountains' growth is most violent, spreading their planar forms wide to catch the waves of uncertainty that ripple out from each upheaval. During these feeding sessions, they emit sounds like breaking bells, like mathematical equations being solved in reverse. The more chaos they absorb, the more complex their geometric forms become, sprouting new planes at angles that shouldn't exist, creating Klein bottles and Möbius strips from their own substance.

They build, but their architecture follows no blueprint save iteration. A Sculptor will begin a structure—perhaps a pyramid of that flowing metallic plasma—then destroy it and rebuild it slightly differently. Then again. And again. Thousands of times, each version marginally distinct, until they find the one iteration that resonates with the local physics in just the right way to become temporarily permanent. These structures serve purposes that would require new verbs to describe—they don't shelter or store or display, but rather they *calibrate* reality around them, creating pockets where the rules hold still long enough for the Sculptors to perform their true work.

That work appears to be cultivation. The Sculptors tend to the geometric patterns in the crystal dust with the patience of gardeners, using appendages that exist only when observed to nudge certain formations toward greater complexity. When the plasma streams show signs of communication, the Sculptors enhance the signal, teaching them new patterns, new ways to flow. They sing to the mountains—not with sound but with resonance, encouraging certain peaks to grow in harmonious ratios, in sequences that follow the Fibonacci spiral or prime number distributions.

They are not alone but they are solitary, each Sculptor maintaining a territory of approximately ten cubic kilometers—noting that the cubic measurement includes dimensions the peaks are still deciding whether to have. When Sculptors meet, they merge temporarily, their planes intersecting and passing through each other in a brief dance that exchanges information in ways that make thought look slow. Then they separate, each taking with them new iterations to try, new patterns to impose on the chaos.

The youngest Sculptors—if age applies to beings that exist partially outside of time—are simple things, perhaps only five or six intersecting planes, their geometry euclidean and comprehensible. The eldest are landscapes unto themselves, their forms so complex they create their own weather systems of probability, their presence causing the nearby peaks to grow in fractal patterns that repeat at every scale. These ancient ones rarely move, having found spots where their very existence stabilizes reality into something almost like permanence.

They communicate through the structures they leave behind—each abandoned iteration a word in a language of pure mathematics. Another Sculptor, finding such a structure, will understand immediately what was being attempted, what failed, what nearly succeeded. They build upon each other's work across centuries that haven't happened yet, their collective effort slowly teaching the Nascent Peaks what it means to have form, to have rules, to be a place rather than just a possibility.

When threatened—though threat here means something like "exposed to too much stability" or "caught in a causality loop"—a Sculptor will collapse all its planes into a single point of infinite density, creating a brief black hole of pure order that forces the surrounding chaos to reorganize itself. Then they unfold again, usually in a completely different location, sometimes in a different time, always with their geometric form slightly evolved from the experience.

They make no sound when moving, but their presence can be felt as a pressure against consciousness, a sense that reality is being observed and judged and gently, inexorably edited. To watch a Sculptor at work is to see mathematics make love to chaos, producing offspring that are neither orderly nor random but something new, something that the universe hasn't had before.

These are the gardeners of impossibility, the architects of maybe, the living processes that shepherd the Nascent Peaks toward whatever they're meant to become—or perhaps toward something better than what they're meant to become. They are not creators but facilitators, not gods but teachers, showing the infant landscape through patient iteration how to be itself, whatever that self might eventually choose to be.
