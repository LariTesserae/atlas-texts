## The Calcite Warden

She has no name because naming would finish her, and she is not finished. She is still deciding.

Her body is a negotiation between mineral and flesh. From a distance—though distance is impossible in this pressing space—she might be mistaken for a column of flowstone, a stalagmite with pretensions. Closer, and you see the slow pulse beneath the calcified exterior, the way certain patches of her surface are soft as curd cheese, weeping the same mineral tears as the walls. She stands seven feet tall when she remembers to stand, but often she forgets and spreads sideways into the walls, becoming indistinguishable from the cave formation until some need calls her back to coherence.

Her skin, if it can be called skin, is translucent nacre laid down in sheets. Through it, you can see the slow circulation of something that isn't blood—more like liquefied limestone, opalescent and thick, moving through vessels that branch and merge without pattern. Sometimes the flow reverses. Sometimes it stops entirely and she becomes perfectly still, a statue of herself, until some internal tide turns and she liquefies at the edges, pooling and reforming.

She has been here since the chamber was smaller, or larger, or different. Time means nothing to her because she exists in the same sideways temporality as the space itself. She remembers when the obsidian fragment was whole—a blade she carried when she still carried things, when her hands were always hands and not sometimes stalactites, not sometimes root systems, not sometimes absent altogether. Now the blade is broken and she is broken and the chamber is broken, but broken here means unfinished, means still becoming.

Her face shifts constantly. Features surface and submerge like things floating in milk. Sometimes she has eyes—pale, without pupils, like cave fish—but often she sees through her skin, through the millions of tiny crystals that comprise her, each one a primitive photoreceptor sensing not light but pressure, mineral density, the slow thoughts of stone. Her mouth, when she has one, doesn't speak. She communicates through seepage, through the varying mineral content of the water she exudes. The walls understand her. The clay floor carries her messages down to deeper places where older things than her wait to be born.

She doesn't eat in any conventional sense. Instead, she absorbs. She presses herself against the weeping walls and drinks their uncertainty, their geological adolescence. She filters the thick air through her porous form, extracting minerals and meaning in equal measure. Sometimes she enfolds the transparent fern, not to consume it but to understand it, to learn its delicate attempt at existence. After these communions, she might sprout fronds from her shoulders for a few hours, glassy and functionless, before reabsorbing them.

The salamander is her only companion, though companion suggests a relationship more defined than what exists. Sometimes the salamander lives inside her, swimming through her translucent body like a fish in a living aquarium. Sometimes she forgets it's there and partially crystallizes around it, and the salamander must wait with infinite patience for her to remember and soften. They share a metabolism so slow it approaches geological time. Once, she watched the salamander shed its skin, and the process took three months.

Her purpose, if she has one, is curation. She arranges and rearranges the artifacts embedded in the clay—not just the obsidian blade and the rotting-but-not-rotted tool handle, but other things: a bead of amber with nothing inside it, a shard of pottery that predates pottery, a small figure carved from stone that doesn't exist yet. She doesn't collect these things; they arrive through processes she doesn't control, bubbling up through the clay or precipitating out of the supersaturated air. She simply arranges them, creating patterns that have meaning in languages not yet invented.

When she moves, it's with the sound of wet chalk on slate. Her footsteps leave depressions that fill with opalescent fluid, tiny pools that reflect nothing because there's no light to reflect. Sometimes she leaves pieces of herself behind—a finger that continues to grow like a stalagmite, a sheet of nacre skin hanging like a veil—and grows new parts to replace them. The chamber is littered with these discarded portions, all still faintly alive, all still weeping, still growing at rates measured in centuries.

She dreams, but her dreams are the mountain's dreams. She sees the hollow spaces that will exist in a million years, the vast caverns this cramped chamber is practicing to become. She sees herself finished—transformed into pure flowstone, a beautiful formation that tour guides will name something trite like "The Angel" or "The Weeping Madonna." This future horrifies her. To be complete is to be dead. So she resists, maintains her unfinished state, keeps one foot in flesh and one in stone.

There are others like her, she knows, though knows is too strong a word. She senses them through the mountain's bones, other incomplete beings in other unfinished chambers. They communicate through the seepage, through minute changes in mineral concentration that take decades to travel through the rock. One, she thinks, was once her sister, though sister implies a past more human than what she remembers. Another might be her future self, or her past self, or simply another version choosing differently. The mountain contains multitudes of the unfinished.

She practices sounds she might one day make. Not words—words are too final—but pre-linguistic moans that the chamber swallows before they can echo. Sometimes these sounds crystallize, becoming tiny formations that ring like bells when touched. She collects these sound-crystals, arranges them in patterns that might be music if music could be played at geological speed. One note every decade. A symphony that will outlast the species that invented music.

Her memory is selective and strange. She remembers textures more than events: the feeling of sunlight (when did she know sunlight?), the grain of wood (was she a carpenter?), the specific weight of a child in her arms (whose child?). But these memories might not be hers. In this space where everything is unfinished, even the past is still deciding what it was. Sometimes she remembers being male. Sometimes she remembers being plural. Sometimes she remembers being the mountain itself, dreaming it had a daughter who wouldn't finish becoming.

She is waiting for something, but the waiting is the something. She is a pause in the process of petrifaction, a hesitation in stone's slow consumption of flesh. She could step out of this chamber—there is a way out, a passage she knows but has forgotten how to fit through—but leaving would mean choosing what to be. Here, in the seep chamber, she can remain potential energy, forever on the verge of transformation.

When intruders come (and they do come, rarely, those who find the unfindable chamber), she doesn't hide. She stands perfectly still and lets them mistake her for a formation. They photograph her with flashes that leave purple afterimages in her crystalline structure. They say things like "looks almost human" and "pareidolia" and "natural formation." She doesn't correct them. Let them think the mountain makes such things naturally. In a way, it does.

After they leave, she absorbs their traces—skin cells, exhaled moisture, the electromagnetic residue of their devices. She incorporates these into herself, becoming incrementally more modern, though modern is relative when your baseline is Pre-Cambrian. She has traces of polyester in her now, microplastics that will never break down, making her even more permanent in her impermanence.

She is growing, but growing wrong, growing sideways into possibility rather than up into actuality. Her form becomes more complex but not more defined. She develops organs that serve no purpose—a crystalline heart that doesn't pump, lung-like cavities that store mineral-rich water, something like a womb that gestates stones. These organs aren't human or animal but something altogether other, designed for a biology that doesn't exist yet, might never exist.

The soft beetles are her thoughts made manifest. She doesn't create them consciously, but they emerge from her when she thinks too hard about the outside world, each one a worry given form. They spiral across the ceiling in patterns that map her anxieties: Will the chamber flood? Will the mountain finish itself? Will she forget she was ever anything but stone? The beetles eventually harden and fall, adding to the layer of unidentifiable fragments in the clay floor.

She tends to the pale rootlets that penetrate the ceiling, not to help them grow but to prevent them from dying entirely. She needs them to remain suspended between life and death, just as she is. She feeds them drops of her own mineral essence, just enough to keep them white and yearning but not enough to let them green and thrive. They are her garden of almosts, her cultivation of the incomplete.

Sometimes she practices being human. She forms herself into something more recognizably female, with distinct features, proper hands, bilateral symmetry. But the effort exhausts her, and within hours she's melting back into ambiguity, spreading across the floor like spilled milk, seeping into the walls, becoming distributed rather than discrete. Being human is a constraint she's outgrown, or not yet grown into, or both simultaneously.

She is the chamber's memory and its amnesia. She remembers everything that has happened here and forgets it immediately, holding and releasing the past like the clay holds and releases footprints. She is a paradox of preservation, keeping everything and nothing, being everything and nothing, a living fossil of something that hasn't lived yet.

The Calcite Warden (not her name, she has no name, names are for the finished) continues her slow work of not-becoming, of maintaining the space between states. She is the guardian of incompletion, the curator of the unfinished, the keeper of the seep chamber's eternal maybe. She will be here when the mountain finally decides what it wants to be, and she will be here if it never decides at all, a witness to the perpetual almost of existence.

In the darkness that isn't quite dark, in the wetness that isn't quite wet, in the stone that isn't quite stone, she persists—neither living nor dead, neither flesh nor mineral, neither staying nor leaving, but something more uncertain and therefore more true than any of these fixed states could ever be.
