The Shepherds move through the crystal forests like shadows of thought, their forms so attenuated they seem more like distortions in the air than physical beings. Each one stretches impossibly tall — perhaps four meters when fully extended — though they spend most of their existence compressed into dense knots barely larger than a fist, tucked beneath overhangs of oxidizing crystal where the electromagnetic fields run strongest.

Their bodies, if bodies they can be called, consist of countless microscopic filaments of living metal, each strand no thicker than a bacterium but kilometers long when fully unfurled. These threads weave and reweave themselves constantly, creating temporary organs as needed — a mesh that becomes an ear to listen to the crystal growth, a net that tastes the metallic dust, a web that catches and processes the electromagnetic radiation streaming up from the thermal veins. They have no permanent form, only an endless reimagining of themselves in response to the landscape's needs.

They are gardeners of a sort, though their cultivation happens at scales both vast and minuscule. A Shepherd might spend a decade guiding the growth of a single crystal formation, secreting catalysts from specialized filaments to encourage branching in harmonious mathematical ratios. They prune with acids extruded from their metal threads, dissolving overgrowth that threatens the geometric perfection of the spiral patterns. Sometimes they transplant — carefully detaching a promising young crystal and carrying it in a cradle of their own substance to a new thermal vent where it might thrive better.

Communication between Shepherds occurs through the substrate itself. They ground portions of their filament mass, creating electrical circuits through the mineral layers that can carry signals for kilometers. These conversations unfold over months, complex harmonics of electrical pulses that discuss growth patterns, debate optimal arrangements, share observations about the slow changes in the metal vapor overhead. A single greeting might take a week to complete, a philosophical discussion could span years.

They feed, after a fashion, on the inefficiencies of the crystal forest's industrial processes. Where the formations fail to completely metabolize the raw elements, leaving pockets of unreacted compounds, the Shepherds infiltrate with their finest filaments, completing the chemical reactions within their own distributed bodies. They are scavengers of entropy, cleaning up the margins of this self-organizing system, ensuring nothing goes to waste.

Memory for them is physical — encoded in the crystalline structure of specialized filaments that they grow and shed in cycles. Older Shepherds carry libraries of these memory-threads wound through their cores, chronicles of the landscape's evolution stretching back millennia. They can share memories by literally passing threads between themselves, one Shepherd incorporating another's experiences into their own substance.

During the brightest part of the day, when the crystal forests blaze with refracted light, the Shepherds retreat into their compressed forms, sheltering from the electromagnetic storm. But in the dimmer hours, they emerge and stretch, their filaments catching the gentler light like spider silk made of copper. They move by flowing — their bottom filaments adhering to the substrate while their top portions stream forward, then the bottom catching up in a motion like poured mercury.

Reproduction, when it occurs, is a matter of decades-long preparation. Two or more Shepherds will slowly separate portions of their filament mass, weaving them together with infinite care to create a new core consciousness. The offspring emerges as barely a handful of threads, which the parents shelter and feed with pre-digested compounds for years before it can survive independently. A single Shepherd might participate in reproduction once or twice in a millennium.

They dream, perhaps, during the long intervals between actions. When a Shepherd has finished adjusting a section of forest and found no inefficiencies to consume, it might remain motionless for years, its consciousness cycling through the patterns encoded in its structure, running simulations of possible growth configurations, imagining new forms the crystal forest might take. These dreams sometimes manifest as faint electromagnetic aurora in their filaments, visible only to others of their kind.

Death comes slowly to them, a gradual oxidation that their self-repair mechanisms can no longer outpace. Dying Shepherds often root themselves near particularly vigorous crystal growths, their corroding filaments providing trace elements that produce unique colors in the formations — memorials of metallic green and gold that persist for centuries after the Shepherd itself has dissolved into the substrate.

They are lonely beings, perhaps, or perhaps loneliness is too human a concept for creatures that experience time as they do. They tend their crystal forests with patience that borders on the geological, their movements so slow that the formations they cultivate might seem to grow wild and ungoverned. Yet each spiral, each branch, each delicate lattice of metallic foam bears the subtle influence of their work — a vast garden shaped by gardeners who themselves are barely distinguishable from the industrial ecology they maintain.

In the endless butterscotch twilight of the Synthesis Plains, they continue their work, these beings of living wire and patient thought, ensuring that the slow transformation of elements proceeds according to some pattern only they can perceive, some purpose that might not reveal itself for another million years of careful, silent cultivation.
