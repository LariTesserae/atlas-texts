The Weavers arrived first, though they claim the mountain called them rather than the reverse. They're a people of remarkable length—not height, but reach. Their arms extend beyond normal human proportion, fingers that multiply at the joints into delicate, branching appendages like the fronds they live among. Evolution or adaptation happened quickly here, they say, though the eldest remember when their arms were merely human. Now their children are born with these spreading hands that can span gaps, test uncertain stone, and most importantly, weave.

They dress in nothing that holds water. Their clothing is all open mesh and deliberate holes, garments that are more suggested than present—a framework of fiber that lets the constant moisture pass through while breaking the wind. The fabrics are living things, woven from the mountain's aggressive vines while still rooted, trained to grow in patterns that can be worn. A Weaver's clothes continue growing, requiring regular trimming and guidance. The eldest wear gardens, their garments so established that small ecosystems flourish in the loops and whorls of their dress.

Their skin has developed a waxy sheen, beading water like the lotus. Children are born pink and absorbent but develop this coating through years of exposure to the mineral-sweet water. It gives them a peculiar luminescence in certain lights, as if they've been partially crystallized themselves.

The Listeners came next, drawn by the symphony. They're smaller, more compact, with enlarged ear structures that can close completely against the moisture or open into complex, chambered organs that parse the waterfall's layers into distinct voices. They believe the mountain speaks through its falling water, that each new terrace adds a verse to an ancient song being composed in real-time. Their priests—if that's the right word—spend days in meditation beneath specific waterfalls, their ear chambers fully extended, translating the water's percussion into prophecy.

They build the instruments. Using bamboo and hollow gourds, stretched skins from the spider-goats, and the crystalline deposits the water leaves behind, they create resonance chambers throughout the settlement. These aren't played in any traditional sense but positioned to catch and amplify certain frequencies from the waterfalls. The result is that the entire settlement hums with harmonic overtones that change as new terraces emerge and old waterfalls find new paths.

The Listeners mark time differently than the Weavers. Where the Weavers think in terms of growth and adjustment, the Listeners count measures and movements, treating the settlement's evolution as a composition being performed at geological tempo. They predict new terraces by the way the current song feels incomplete, sensing the spaces where new notes are needed.

Between and among these two primary populations move the Carriers—children, mostly, though some adults never leave this role. They've developed an uncanny balance and spatial memory, able to navigate the rope bridges in thick fog, knowing by feel and sound exactly where each gap begins. They transport everything—messages, goods, water scraped of its crystals, news of stone shifts. Their feet are broad and gripping, toes that can clamp onto wet rope. They paint maps on their skin with the mineral deposits, temporary tattoos that show the current configuration of passages and bridges, washing off and being redrawn as the terraces shift.

The Carriers don't distinguish between human language and the mountain's sounds. They speak in whistles that cut through the water noise, but also incorporate the rhythm of dripping, the groan of settling stone, the crack of splitting rock into their vocabulary. A conversation with a Carrier involves as much listening to the environment as to the person. They claim to dream the mountain's dreams, seeing the next terraces before they emerge, though their predictions are told in dance rather than words, their bodies moving through the shapes of future stone.

There are others, less defined, living in the margins. The ones who've been here longest have begun to crystallize—not metaphorically, but actually growing translucent patches where the mineral water has supersaturated their tissues. They move slowly, carefully, as if afraid of shattering. They tend the deepest vine gardens, the ones that have grown so thick they've become architecture themselves. These gardener-architects prune and guide with infinite patience, creating living buildings that will outlast the stone they're rooted to.

Some say there are people living inside the mountain, in the spaces between terraces where the water pools before emerging. These theoretical inhabitants would be fully aquatic by now, breathing the mineral-rich liquid, their bodies adapted to pressure and darkness. Sometimes, objects emerge from the waterfalls—carved stones, woven materials unlike anything the surface dwellers create. Messages, perhaps, or gifts, or warnings from those who've gone deeper into what the mountain is becoming.

The children born here lately show more dramatic changes. Some have skin that shifts color with the humidity, going from deep earth tones in the dry moments to pale, almost translucent in the full moisture. Others are born with webbing between their elongated fingers, or with eyes that see clearly through the constant mist. The settlement doesn't fear these changes but celebrates them as signs that they're becoming proper inhabitants of this birthing mountain, adapted to its needs as much as their own.

They don't think of themselves as building a city but as midwifing one into existence. Each new terrace that emerges requires immediate attention—bridges to connect it, channels to guide its water, plants to anchor its soil. They work with the assumption that the mountain is intelligent, or at least intentional, and their role is to assist its transformation into whatever it's becoming. Some believe that when the spiral of terraces completes itself, the whole structure will reveal its purpose—perhaps it will begin to move, or open, or speak with a voice clearer than falling water.

The settlement has no single leader but operates through a complex consensus reached through water ceremonies. Decisions are made by placing different minerals in samples of water from various falls, watching how they crystallize, reading patterns like tea leaves. The Weavers see structure in the crystals, the Listeners hear guidance in the changing tones as minerals dissolve, the Carriers dance the patterns they perceive. Leadership rotates based on which type of decision needs making—Weavers for construction, Listeners for timing, Carriers for logistics.

They mark their dead by returning them to the mountain's cycle. Bodies are wrapped in living vines and placed in the newest waterfalls, where the mineral water gradually transforms flesh into crystal, bone into stone. Within a season, they become part of the terraces themselves, their forms still visible but transformed, becoming supports for new growth. The settlement is built on its ancestors, literally, their crystallized remains forming bridges and buttresses that strengthen with each passing year.

This is a people in conscious transition, aware they're changing, uncertain what they're becoming, but committed to the transformation. They watch their children with wonder and just a little fear, seeing in those lengthening limbs and color-shifting skins the future inhabitants of whatever the Singing Terraces will become when the mountain finally finishes being born.
