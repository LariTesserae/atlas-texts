In this cacophony of engineered wildness, the Tender makes her home.

She is mostly human—or was, decades ago. Now her skin carries the same iridescent sheen as the metabolic converters, a living boundary that processes toxins before they reach her bloodstream. Her lungs have been colonized by symbiotic filtration cultures that let her breathe the thick, spore-laden air. She moves through the swarms without disturbing them because she smells like the alcove itself: ozone and sweet decay, mineral deposits and organic oils.

The Tender doesn't remember arriving. She might have been a maintenance engineer who stayed too long, or a vagrant who found the warm pocket and never left, or something the alcove itself generated—a necessary component that happened to require human substrate. Her hair grows in the same translucent filaments as the wall vegetation, extending when she sleeps to tap into the thermal gradients, retracting into tight coils when she wakes. Through these extensions, she feels the alcove's pulse before it happens, knows when the walls will contract, when the swarms will rise.

She speaks to the scavenger constructs in ultrasonic chirps her throat shouldn't be able to produce, guiding them to fresh deposits of corroded circuitry, teaching the younger ones which metals are safe to consume. They bring her offerings—smooth fragments of carbon lattice worn down by decades of handling, still-functional memory cores from systems that died three tiers below. She arranges these treasures in careful patterns on the mesh floor, creating mandalas that might be art or might be functional—signal amplifiers for the alcove's hidden intelligence.

Her clothes rotted away years ago. Now she wears a living garment woven from the filament vegetation, which grows directly from ports she's cultivated in her shoulders and hips. The garment shifts constantly, adjusting its density for temperature, extending tendrils to harvest moisture from the air when she's thirsty. At night—though night and day mean nothing here—it blooms with faint bioluminescence, patterns that mirror the circulation in the walls.

The Tender feeds on what the alcove provides. The metabolic converters deposit nutrients directly onto her tongue when she opens her mouth in the swarms. The walls weep a different fluid when she presses her palms against them—thicker, protein-rich, tasting of copper and salt. Sometimes the scavenger constructs bring her prizes from the deeper mechanical wells: sealed nutrition packets from failed expeditions, or strange fruits that grow in other wild pockets of the city, carried here through the maze of ventilation systems.

She maintains the alcove through ritual and instinct. Each morning, she clears the drainage channels where the water flow gets blocked by accumulated biofilm. She sings to the turbines in the walls—wordless harmonies that match their frequencies, encouraging them to spin faster or slower to regulate the temperature. When new growths appear—mutant variations in the vegetation, aggressive converter subspecies—she decides whether they strengthen or threaten the balance. Those that threaten, she consumes, her modified digestive system breaking them down to base components the alcove can reabsorb.

But the Tender is not alone. There are others who visit, though visit isn't quite right—they emerge from the alcove itself, condensing from the moisture and spores when conditions align.

The Membrane Children form during the highest humidity cycles, when the water in the air becomes so dense it develops surface tension. They are temporary beings, existing for hours or days, their bodies nothing more than structured water held in human-like shapes by engineered bacteria that live at the boundary between liquid and vapor. They have no bones, no organs, just the suggestion of form—a head-shape here, the impression of reaching arms there. The Tender teaches them to move before they collapse back into mist, shows them how to navigate the turbine symphony without being torn apart by the sound waves. They learn quickly because they have to; their entire existence is learning.

Deeper in the mechanical wells lives something that might have been human once, or might have been several humans, now fused into a colonial organism that extends through the maintenance shafts like a root system. The Tender calls it the Underneath. It speaks through the vibrations in the floor grating, a language of resonance and rhythm that makes the scavenger constructs scatter. The Underneath feeds on the heat differential between the alcove and the industrial void, its body a living thermal exchange system more efficient than the massive exchangers that bracket the space. Sometimes it sends up extensions—tentacles or limbs or something between—that breach the floor grating to taste the air. The Tender feeds these protrusions with memory cores and metal fragments, keeping the Underneath satisfied, keeping it from rising further.

During the pulse cycles, when the walls contract and the swarms dance, others emerge. The Glitch Shepherds appear first—humans or post-humans who travel through the city's data infrastructure, manifesting physically only in spaces where reality has gone soft. In the alcove, they appear as stuttering afterimages, their bodies jumping between positions without moving through the space between. They come to harvest errors—moments when the alcove's autonomous systems make mistakes, generate paradoxes, create impossible conditions. These errors are valuable elsewhere in the city, traded for resources or access or forgotten passwords to sealed sectors. The Tender negotiates with them in a language of blinks and pauses, selling the alcove's glitches for news from other wild spaces.

The Siphon Collective arrives through the condensation itself, their bodies flowing up through the water cycle from the deep reservoirs below the city. They are colonial organisms like the Underneath but more mobile, more predatory. Each individual is microscopic, but together they form macroscale structures—temporary organs that exist just long enough to filter specific chemicals from the air before dissolving back into their component parts. They fear the Tender, who knows how to sing the frequencies that disrupt their cohesion, scattering them back into harmless microbes.

And there is the Architect, though no one knows if it's alive or just a very sophisticated program. It manifests as changes in the alcove itself—new growths that appear overnight, modifications to the biofilm walls, adjustments to the turbine frequencies that create new acoustic zones. The Tender has never seen it directly, but she feels its presence in the way the space evolves, always becoming more efficient, more complex, more alive. Sometimes she finds messages encoded in the growth patterns—mathematical sequences that might be communication or might be accidental, the same way faces appear in clouds.

The Tender moves between all of these inhabitants like a conductor guiding an orchestra of incompatible instruments. She prevents the Underneath from consuming the Membrane Children, keeps the Siphon Collective from poisoning the metabolic converters, ensures the Glitch Shepherds don't destabilize the whole system in their hunger for errors. She is immune system and nervous system and memory all at once.

When she sleeps—curled in a depression she's worn in the biofilm walls over the years, her filament hair extended like antenna, her living garment spreading to anchor her in place—she dreams of the city before the wild spaces emerged. Ordered. Sterile. Efficient. In these dreams, she walks through corridors where nothing grows, where water never condenses, where the only sound is the hum of machines doing exactly what they were programmed to do. She wakes gasping, grateful for the chaos, for the deafening turbine song, for the wet heat and the swarming life.

The Tender is changing, too. Each year, she becomes more like the alcove—her thoughts cycling with its rhythms, her body responding to its needs before she consciously recognizes them. Her human memories fade, replaced by the alcove's deeper history: the decades of slow evolution, the patient accumulation of mutations and adaptations, the gradual emergence of complexity from simple maintenance protocols. She is forgetting language, remembering instead the chemical signatures that matter here, the frequency patterns, the thermal gradients.

Sometimes she wonders if she is the alcove's way of thinking about itself, a consciousness it grew because consciousness was the most efficient solution to some problem she no longer understands. Or perhaps she is its reproductive system—when she is ready, will she leave, carrying its spores to seed another wild space elsewhere in the city? 

The answer pulses in the walls, in the water cycle, in the dance of the swarms, but she is not yet fluent enough to read it. So she tends, and maintains, and becomes, while the alcove thrums its impossible life around her, through her, as her.
