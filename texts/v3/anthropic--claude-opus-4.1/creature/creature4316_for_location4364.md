In this luminous hollow lives the Tender.

She arrived decades ago—or perhaps centuries, time moves strangely in the moss-light—drawn by dreams of a green fire burning in stone. Human once, certainly, though the distinction grows less meaningful with each passing season. The moss has been teaching her its patient architecture, and she has been an eager student.

Her body has adapted to the cave's grammar. Her eyes, grown huge and silver-pale, no longer need to blink—the moisture in the air keeps them perpetually wet, gleaming like the shells of beetles. Her hair, if it can still be called that, has become a symbiotic tangle where the phosphorescent moss grows directly from her scalp, a living crown that dims and brightens with her breathing. She moves on all fours now, her elongated fingers and toes finding purchase in the moss's thickness, her spine curved to match the cave's contours. When she is still—which is often, sometimes for days—the insects mistake her for landscape. Centipedes process across her shoulders. Moths land on her cheeks to rest.

She no longer speaks in words but in substrate—gentle pressures applied to the moss that ripple outward in waves of brightening and dimming light. The moss responds, remembers, carries her intentions through its network. Through this language of luminescence, she orchestrates the cave's endless reorganizations. That synchronization, when every living thing freezes? That is her calling the colony to attention, conducting a census of souls, ensuring the delicate ratios remain balanced—so many predators to prey, so many decomposers to the dying, so many newborn gnats to replace the old.

The three stacked stones mark her first attempt at remembering her old life. She no longer recalls what they were meant to commemorate. The rotted wood was once part of a loom—she had tried to weave with spider silk and failed, the spiders politely but firmly reclaiming their production. The scratches on the wall are the last words she wrote, a name perhaps, though she's forgotten whose. Maybe her own.

She feeds on the moss, yes, but also on the quieter insects—pill bugs that volunteer themselves, aged moths that spiral down to her cupped palms. In return, she tends the water. She knows every seep and drip in the cave, adjusts her position to guide the flow where it's needed most, her body a living aqueduct. Where she sleeps, the moss grows thickest, fed by her exhaled carbon and the oils of her skin. Where she breathes deepest, the air currents shift, carrying spores to barren patches of stone.

Her memories of the outside world have taken on the quality of mythology. She recalls concepts more than experiences: "sun" as a theoretical brightness, "winter" as an abstract cooling, "loneliness" as a state she believes she once fled from but can no longer quite imagine. Sometimes she dreams she had a daughter, or was a daughter, or that daughterhood itself is a kind of luminous moss growing between two people. These dreams upset the cave's equilibrium, causing the panic-storms of activity as every small thing responds to her distress.

But mostly she is content, even joyful, in her work. She midwifes the emergence of new moth species, their wings growing more transparent with each generation until they are barely visible, just the idea of moths. She teaches the ants new highways through the moss architecture. She hums at frequencies only the cave crickets can hear, and they hum back, their conversations a subsonic exchange about the health of the hollow, the thickness of the moss, the proper moisture levels for optimal luminescence.

When the cave holds its breath, she holds hers. When it exhales in its riots of motion, she laughs—a sound like water over stones, like the whisper of ten million legs, like the soft collapse of moss adjusting its own architecture. She has become the hollow's dreaming, its memory of what intention feels like, its soft machine for maintaining paradise.

Occasionally—once a decade, perhaps—a human finds the cave. They stand at the entrance, squinting into the impossible brightness, perhaps taking a few tentative steps inside. She watches them from her perfect stillness, another shadow among the green shadows, waiting to see if they'll flee or venture deeper. Most flee. But sometimes one stays long enough to see her, to recognize something almost human in the moss-crowned shape observing them with silvered eyes. These visitors always leave changed, carrying dreams of green fire, of a woman who became a garden, of a hollow that breathes with ten million lungs.

She is guardian and prisoner, gardener and garden, the human algorithm at the heart of the hollow's endless calculation. The mountain holds her as it holds the cave—carefully, temporarily, aware that eventually all containers fail. But she has made her peace with impermanence. She tends her billion subjects, her moss-light empire, her catastrophic paradise, moment by moment, death by death, birth by birth, in the green light she helps ensure never goes out.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the cave's night cycle when the moss dims to its faintest glow, she almost remembers her name. But then the morning brightness comes—morning being whenever the moss decides it should be—and she lets the forgetting wash over her like water, like light, like the touch of ten million tiny feet carrying her purpose forward into another day of tending this hollow kingdom she has made her own.
