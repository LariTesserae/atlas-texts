The Thermophiles arrived first, though they would bristle at the term—it was coined by the lowland archaeologists who study them from a safe, cool distance. They call themselves simply "the Settled," as if their presence here were as inevitable as the rock's uplift.

They are a people of subtle gradations. Their skin carries a permanent flush, not from exertion but from a lifetime of capillaries trained to dissipate heat. The eldest among them—those whose families have lived here for six or seven generations—have developed a faint translucency to their skin, as if they're slowly becoming compatible with the glass arthropods that share their walls. Their eyes are always narrowed, protected by thick lashes that have grown unusually long, filtering the perpetual glare of steam-refracted light.

Children here learn temperature before they learn colors. A Thermophile child of four can place their palm on a pipe and tell you not just that it's hot, but that it's exactly the 73-degree water from the third-tier condensers, slightly cooler than yesterday, probably due to mineral buildup in the tubes. They play games in the mist, hiding in pockets of temperature differential, their bodies learning to read the air's thermal topography like a map.

The adults move with an economy that borders on the ceremonial. No gesture is rushed, no movement wasteful. They've learned from the rock itself that haste generates only unnecessary heat. Watch a Thermophile engineer at work: she approaches her steam-hammer with a fluid grace, her movements timed to the machine's thermal cycle. She places her hand on the housing—not to test the temperature, but as greeting, acknowledgment. She begins work only when the metal's expansion tells her it's ready. Her apprentice, typically a lowlander brought up for a five-year term, sweats and fidgets nearby, still fighting the environment instead of joining it.

They dress in layers of volcanic fiber—a fabric they process from the mineral deposits that form hair-thin filaments in certain cooling chambers. The material breathes with temperature change, tightening in cool air, relaxing in warmth. Their clothing is always shifting, always adjusting, giving them an oddly fluid appearance as they move through zones of different heat. The garments are uniformly pale, almost colorless—dyes are a lowland luxury, and besides, any strong color would fade in the constant chemical bath of the steam.

The Thermophiles speak softly, their language full of sibilants and whispered consonants that carry well through mist. They've developed a parallel language of pipe-knocks and valve-releases, a percussion dialogue that travels through the city's ceramic nervous system faster than any voice could carry. A conversation might begin with spoken words in a morning gathering, continue through pipe-taps during the working day, and conclude with steam-whistle codes at evening's rest.

Their social structures mirror the thermal gradients they inhabit. The deepest dwellers—those who maintain the volcanic tap-roots that power the city—hold a status both revered and isolated. They surface rarely, their bodies so adapted to extreme heat that normal temperatures feel arctic. They're fed by pneumatic tubes, sending up samples of new mineral formations in exchange for food that's been pre-heated to their comfort. When they do emerge for civic ceremonies, they wear cooling suits of their own design, their faces barely visible through the condensation that forms on their masks.

The mid-tier majority comprises the engineers, the crystal-growers, the pipe-readers. They marry young—the formal ceremony involves the couple jointly calibrating a new pressure valve that will be installed in their shared home, their compatibility measured by how quickly they can find the harmonic balance point. They raise their children communally in neighborhood créches where the temperature is kept slightly cooler, gradually acclimatizing the young to the full range of the city's thermal zones.

At the terraces' edge live the traders, those who deal with the lowland caravans that bring what cannot be grown from stone or condensed from air. These edge-dwellers are regarded with a mixture of necessity and suspicion—they're needed, but their constant exposure to outside temperatures makes them thermally unreliable, their body rhythms slightly out of sync with the city's deep pulse.

The Thermophiles age differently than lowlanders. The constant mineral-rich steam acts as both preservative and irritant. Their skin becomes increasingly crystalline with age, developing a faint shimmer, but their lungs often fail early, clogged with decades of mineral deposits. They've developed a tradition of "stone sleep"—when an elder feels their breathing becoming too labored, they descend to the deepest chambers where the air is so hot and dense it requires barely any lung effort at all. Some live for years down there, sending up occasional messages through the pipe-language, their wisdom sought but their physical presence gradually forgotten.

They dream of depth, not distance. Their stories tell of heroes who descended through the thermal shelf to its molten heart, who negotiated with the planet's core for one more century of stability, one more generation of gentle uplift. Their children don't play at exploring new lands—they play at discovering new chambers, new veins of heat, new passages in the rock that's still revealing itself.

In their art—and yes, they have art, though lowlanders might not recognize it as such—they work in pressure and flow. Their sculptures are functional fountains where the beauty lies in the thermodynamic elegance of the water's path. Their music is played on steam-organs installed throughout the city, each building becoming an instrument when the evening pressure differential reaches its peak. They compose for the city itself, writing symphonies that require a thousand people adjusting a thousand valves in precise sequence, creating harmonics that resonate through every pipe, every chamber, every hollow bone in the settlement.

The Thermophiles have no word for "permanent." Everything is transitional—the rock still rising, the steam still condensing, their own bodies still adapting. They've built their entire civilization on the premise that change is the only constant, that home is not a fixed point but a rate of transformation they've learned to match.

When they die, they're interred in the cooling chambers—bodies placed in ceramic shells and inserted into carefully chosen spots where the rock is still warm enough to fossilize them over decades. They become part of the thermal shelf itself, their minerals returning to the stone, their hollow bones becoming new channels for steam. The oldest cemetery, from the first settlement wave, is now indistinguishable from natural rock formation except for the too-regular spacing of certain cavities, the too-uniform composition of certain mineral veins.

Occasionally, a lowland anthropologist will arrive to study them, wrapped in cooling gear, taking notes on their "adaptation to extreme environments." The Thermophiles are unfailingly polite to these visitors, offering them properly cooled water, showing them the safer, upper terraces. But among themselves, in the whispered steam-language, they express amusement. Adaptation implies they've changed to fit this place. The truth, they know, is more reciprocal—they and the thermal shelf are becoming each other, one heated breath at a time.
