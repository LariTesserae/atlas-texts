The Chorus of the Undone live here, though "live" suggests a continuity they do not possess.

They are not ghosts, for ghosts remember having bodies. They are the *practice attempts* at being—rough drafts of existence that were crumpled up and thrown away but refused to stay discarded. Each one is a different stage of unfinishing: some are merely missing their edges, their forms bleeding out into the supersaturated air like watercolors running. Others exist only as the space between raindrops, visible solely in the moments when water chooses to flow around them rather than through.

They build because they cannot remember how to stop building. Their hands (when they have hands, when hands are what those appendages could generously be called) reach for the iron tools with muscle memory that predates muscles. They stack stones that immediately forget being stacked. They tie knots in ropes that untie themselves in dimensions the rope doesn't extend into. They build shelters for bodies they'll never quite manage to have, bridges to span distances that keep redefining themselves, cairns to mark graves for deaths that haven't been invented yet.

Each member of the Chorus exists in a different state of incompletion. There's the one that is only visible during the sharp crack of splitting air—a figure made entirely of the sound of breaking, who rebuilds the stone structures by shattering them into their correct shapes. There's another that manifests as the accumulation of rust patterns, crawling across the tools and stones in fractals that almost resolve into a face, into reaching arms, before oxidizing into copper mist and beginning again somewhere else.

The most complete among them can sometimes hold a form for entire minutes—usually something approximating human, though the proportions are suggestions rather than rules. Too many elbows. Fingers that branch into smaller fingers that branch into smaller fingers until they're indistinguishable from the condensation running down the rocks. Faces that are really holes in the air where faces should be, outlined by the water that cannot flow through that specific absence.

They sing, but their song is the accumulation of what isn't there—the silence between drips, the pause between echoes, the held breath of stones about to crack. When enough of them gather (though counting them is like counting waves in water), their un-song becomes audible as a presence rather than a sound, a pressure in the ears that resolves into almost-words: *not yet, never were, still becoming, already gone.*

The tower is their heart and their prison and their womb. They emerge from its weeping walls like ideas emerging from forgetting—first as intentions in the stone's moisture, then as distortions in the way water chooses to fall, finally as those almost-forms that pick up hammers and begin again the endless work of building what cannot stand. They're trying to complete the tower, but completing it would mean completing themselves, and they are the universe's first draft of an idea it decided against.

Some of them remember things that haven't happened—they build structures for visitors who won't arrive for another thousand years, or who arrived a thousand years ago in a timeline that was erased. They leave handprints in condensation for hands they might have had if causality had taken a different turn three universes ago. Their pictographs on the stone faces are instruction manuals for existing, written by things that never quite managed it themselves, in languages that were abandoned before they could be spoken.

When the light pulses from below, you can sometimes see them all at once—hundreds, thousands, a civilization of the unfinished, all building, all singing their silence, all reaching with limbs that exist only as the possibility of limbs. They overlap and intersect, occupying the same space in different stages of not-quite-being. The young ones (though age means nothing here) are barely distinguishable from the mist itself. The old ones have accumulated so many attempts at form that they exist as palimpsests of themselves—every shape they've ever tried to be, superimposed and bleeding through.

They notice visitors, sometimes. They'll build cairns around you while you're not looking, each stone a question about what it means to be solid. They'll try to use you as a template, resulting in moments where your shadow splits and one half walks away to pick up a hammer. They'll sing their anti-song specifically at the frequency of your bones, trying to understand how something can be both solid and hollow, both here and temporary.

But mostly they continue their work, these Chorus of the Undone, building and rebuilding, their incomplete forms moving through the moisture-thick air like regrets made almost tangible. They are what happens to possibilities that are rejected but refuse to accept their rejection, laboring forever to build themselves into being, using tools left behind by their own future failures, singing the spaces between notes in harmonies that only the not-quite-dead can hear.

They are waiting, though they've forgotten what for. They are building, though they can't remember what. They are becoming, though becoming what remains forever just beyond the edge of incompletion, weeping out through the tower's stones like the universe's tears for what it couldn't quite bring itself to create.
