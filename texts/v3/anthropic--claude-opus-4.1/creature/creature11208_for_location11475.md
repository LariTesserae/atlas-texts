The inhabitants move through their city like schools of fish through a reef that rebuilds itself daily—fluid, alert, never quite settling into predictable patterns.

They are a people of layers. Everyone wears multiple coats of varying permeability, shed and donned throughout the day as they move between the wet cold of the streets and the humid warmth of the factories. The outermost layers are typically rubberized or waxed canvas, beaded perpetually with moisture, while inner layers trap precious body heat. But it's the middle layers that mark profession and status—engineers wear coats lined with pockets for instruments that measure geological stress, their brass fittings tarnished green; moss farmers sport living garments where carefully cultivated specimens create patterns of bioluminescence; factory workers' clothes are riddled with intentional tears and patches that serve as impromptu ventilation systems, opening and closing via drawstrings as they move between temperature zones.

Their bodies have adapted over generations. Hands are universally thick-fingered and deeply calloused, with a particular grip strength developed from constantly grasping wet metal railings and tools. Everyone develops what they call "vapor eyes"—the ability to see clearly through the perpetual mist, to read the subtle variations in steam density that indicate a pipe about to burst or ground about to shift. Their children learn to walk on slick surfaces before dry ones, developing an distinctive gait—never quite lifting their feet fully, maintaining three points of contact whenever possible, moving with a controlled slide that looks precarious to outsiders but allows them to navigate the treacherous streets at surprising speed.

The social structure mirrors the city's architecture—vertical, dense, multi-purpose. Extended families occupy building columns, with great-grandparents in the basement workshops, parents in the middle manufacturing floors, children in the upper residential spaces. But these family units are porous; specialists drift between buildings following their work—a tube-mason might maintain the drainage systems for a dozen family towers, becoming pseudo-kin to all of them. Marriage contracts include elaborate clauses about geological disruption, allowing for temporary dissolutions if emerging rock formations separate households.

They've developed a complex language of steam whistles and pipe-knocks that carries through the industrial fog better than voices. Each district has its characteristic rhythm—the Foundry Quarter's conversations sound like percussion ensembles, while the Moss Gardens communicate in long, sustained tones. Children learn to interpret the city's mechanical sounds as a kind of ambient information system, knowing from the pitch of a particular factory's hum whether it's hiring, from the rhythm of drainage whether a sector is stable or about to experience a geological event.

Their religion, if you can call it that, revolves around the Emergence. They see divinity in the planet's constant self-creation, reading meaning in the patterns of crystal growth, in the geometry of new rock formations. Their priests are more like industrial shamans—part geologist, part engineer, part prophet—who maintain the sacred boilers that must never be allowed to cool completely, lest the city freeze. They perform divinations by watching how steam condenses on different mineral surfaces, claiming to predict not just geological events but social ones—strikes, marriages, innovations.

The constant rebuilding has created a culture with no permanent monuments, no ancestral homes, no graveyard. Instead, they practice "industrial memory"—encoding their history into the functional infrastructure itself. A family's story might be etched into the pipes they maintain, readable only by those who know how to interpret the wear patterns. Important events are commemorated by incorporating salvaged pieces of collapsed buildings into new construction, so that the city becomes a three-dimensional collage of its own history.

Their children grow up feral and competent, forming loose gangs that know every hidden passage created by the city's constant reconfigurations. They play in the spaces adults can't reach—the gaps between emerging geology and old infrastructure, the narrow channels between buildings where moss grows thick enough to cushion falls. By age six, they can predict ground-shifts by the way arthropods change their migration patterns. By ten, they're apprenticed to whoever can use their particular talents—those with steady hands to the instrument makers, those with geographic memory to the courier guilds, those who show affinity with plants to the vertical farms.

The elderly don't retire so much as sediment. As their bodies slow, they move deeper into the city's stable cores, eventually taking up residence in the massive gear-rooms and engine spaces where someone must always be present to maintain the great machines. They become part of the city's mechanical rhythm, their daily routines of oiling and adjustment as crucial as heartbeats. Some say the oldest among them stop speaking entirely, communicating only through their maintenance patterns, their consciousness merged somehow with the industrial processes they tend.

Food culture revolves around what can be grown in artificial light and processed from mineral-rich water. They cultivate fungus in great underground farms, the fruiting bodies harvested daily and processed into everything from bread to beer. The bioluminescent moss isn't just decorative—certain varieties are edible, prized for their mineral content and slight electric tingle. Arthropods are farmed in warm factory spaces, their shells ground into calcium supplements, their meat pickled in acidic runoff from the metal processing plants. Every meal tastes faintly of metal and moss, a flavor profile outsiders find disturbing but locals can't imagine living without.

Their art is ephemeral by necessity. Musicians compose for the factory rhythms, adding human voices and instruments to the industrial symphony in performances that last only as long as a production cycle. Visual artists work in condensation on cold surfaces, in the arrangement of bioluminescent organisms, in the temporary sculptures formed by routing steam through networks of pipes. The greatest artists are those who can predict and incorporate the city's changes—a sculptor who places a piece precisely where emerging geology will complete it, a composer whose work reaches crescendo just as a scheduled demolition provides the percussion.

They love fiercely but pragmatically. Romance often begins in the rebuilding crews, bonds forged through the shared exhaustion of constant construction. They court with gifts of rare minerals still warm from their formation, with promises of stable spaces in one's family tower, with elaborately engineered drainage systems that spell out declarations when the rain falls just right. Their wedding ceremonies involve the couple jointly maintaining a complex piece of machinery through one full production cycle, their ability to work in harmony determining the marriage's official recognition.

Death, when it comes, is processed like everything else. Bodies feed the moss gardens, bones are ground and incorporated into concrete, personal effects are salvaged and integrated into new construction. They don't visit graves; they visit the infrastructure their loved ones have become—a support beam here, a particularly lush patch of moss there, the specific tone a pipe makes when steam passes through it.

They dream, collectively, of stability—but suspect they would hate it if it came. The constant change has made them who they are: adaptive, ingenious, accepting of loss, always building. In their stories, heroes aren't those who establish permanent kingdoms but those who navigate change most gracefully, who can rebuild faster, adapt better, who find opportunity in every collapse.

When outsiders arrive—traders, occasionally, or lost travelers—the locals watch them struggle with barely concealed amusement. Watch them slip on wet surfaces, flinch at routine ground tremors, fail to hear the conversations carried in steam-whistle. But they're hospitable in their way, finding stable spaces for visitors, teaching them the basic audio signals for "danger" and "all clear," though they know most outsiders will leave as soon as possible, unsettled by a city that refuses to ever be finished, inhabited by people who have made an art of impermanence.

At the shift changes, when thousands move through the streets at once, the crowds flow like water finding its level, each person reading the subtle signs of instability, adjusting their routes instinctively. They move with the particular grace of those born to uncertainty, their faces showing neither anxiety nor resignation but a kind of alert satisfaction—the expression of people who have learned not just to survive but to thrive in constant transformation, who find in their city's endless becoming a reflection of their own refusal to ever quite settle into final form.
