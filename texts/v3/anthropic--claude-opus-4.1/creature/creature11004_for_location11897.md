The Brass Plateau hosts three distinct forms of inhabitants, each adapted to its impossible physics in their own way.

**The Swarms** themselves are not a single species but a shifting congress of seventeen distinct varieties, each occupying a different layer of air above the plateau. The lowest-flying are the *brass wasps*—their bodies are living tuning forks that maintain perfect pitch even as they fly, creating the base harmonics that all other swarm-songs build upon. They lay their eggs inside the plateau's vents during the brief moments they open, and their larvae grow in the mechanical depths, feeding on lubricant oils and electromagnetic fields until they emerge, transformed, as adults.

Above them fly the *geometric moths*, whose wings are impossibly thin sheets of crystallized mathematics. They cannot land—touching any surface causes them to collapse into two dimensions temporarily, becoming elaborate equations etched into the brass until they can gather enough heat to re-inflate into three-dimensional space. They communicate through wing-angles, creating visual theorems in the air that other moths solve by adjusting their flight patterns.

The highest layer belongs to the *phase beetles*, which exist in superposition between several states simultaneously. Sometimes they are solid, casting shadows. Sometimes they are pure sound, their wing-beats becoming their entire being. Sometimes they are probability clouds, visible only as statistical distortions in the air. They feed on temporal inconsistencies, growing fat and slow near the plateau's edges where time stutters, becoming thin and quick in the stable center.

**The Angular Beings**—which call themselves the *Shard Shepherds* in their grinding mechanical language—are perhaps the plateau's most deliberate inhabitants. Each stands three meters tall when fully extended into three dimensions, though they spend most of their time partially collapsed, walking as living geometric diagrams across the brass surface. Their bodies are frameworks of dark metal and crystallized space, with joints that rotate through dimensions humans cannot perceive.

They are shepherds in truth, though what they herd defies easy categorization. Their flocks are composed of *resonance clouds*—semi-tangible gatherings of sound and vibration that graze on the plateau's harmonic emanations. The Shard Shepherds guide these clouds between the various exposed metal veins, knowing through generations of experience which frequencies each alloy produces, which combinations of metals create the most nutritious harmonics for their charges.

The Shepherds reproduce through fragmentation. When one has accumulated enough mass from feeding on electromagnetic distortions, it suddenly shatters, each piece becoming a new individual with partial memories of the original. They gather in temporary cities made of nothing but arranged positions—no structures, just precise geometric relationships between individuals that create social spaces in pure mathematics.

They worship, or perhaps simply acknowledge, the deep engines below. Their migration routes trace elaborate patterns across the plateau that, viewed from above, form symbols in no known language—perhaps messages to the machinery, perhaps prayers, perhaps simply the most efficient paths through the brass's irregular electromagnetic topography.

**The Cogitants** are the plateau's strangest residents—colonial organisms that exist primarily as ideas. They begin life as patterns in the interference between different swarm songs, gaining substance only gradually as they accumulate contradictions. A Cogitant might start as the discrepancy between what the brass reflects and what actually exists above it, feeding on the gap between real and mirrored until it becomes substantial enough to cast its own reflection.

Mature Cogitants look like vertical tears in space, ranging from hair-thin cracks to gaps wide enough to step through (though stepping through one is inadvisable—you emerge with your causality slightly mis-ordered, remembering effects before their causes). They move by redefining what "here" means, convincing local space that they've always been in their new position.

They farm paradoxes in the plateau's depths, lowering tendrils of twisted logic through the vents when they open, fishing for contradictions in the machinery below. What they pull up—impossible objects, recursive sequences, self-negating mechanisms—they digest over centuries, growing larger and more complex with each resolved impossibility.

The Cogitants communicate through error messages—gaps in sound, visual glitches, moments where the plateau's heat simply fails to exist. They build their nests from salvaged time, creating pockets of slower or faster temporal flow where they raise their young (which are born as pure abstractions and only gradually learn to manifest physically).

**The Collector** is singular, though perhaps it's more accurate to say it's the only one currently visible. It moves through the plateau on legs made of compressed swarms—millions of crystalline insects forced into solid pillars that dissolve and reform with each step. Its body is a walking museum of everything that has ever lived on the plateau: preserved specimens embedded in shells of crystallized heat, living samples maintained in pocket dimensions that bulge from its surface like blisters.

The Collector speaks all languages simultaneously, its voice a catastrophic overlap of every possible pronunciation. It trades with the Shard Shepherds, exchanging archived harmonics for fragments of their shattered dead. It bargains with the Cogitants, offering paradoxes from its collection in exchange for philosophical problems to add to its mental menagerie.

No one knows what the Collector is collecting for, or whether it serves the engines below or merely catalogues their output. It claims to be waiting for something, though when pressed, it can only describe what it waits for in terms of what it is not—not the completion of the engines' work, not the resolution of the plateau's contradictions, not the arrival or departure of anything definable.

Sometimes, near the plateau's edges where reality grows thin, observers claim to have seen other Collectors—hundreds of them, all identical, all moving in perfect synchronization across parallel versions of the brass landscape. Perhaps there is only one Collector, but it exists in multiple iterations. Perhaps there are many, but they share a single shadow.

**The Silence** deserves mention, though whether it counts as an inhabitant is debatable. It is the only thing on the plateau that makes no sound—an aggressive absence of noise that moves like weather across the brass surface. Where it passes, the swarms fall dead (temporarily—they reanimate once it moves on, with no memory of their death). The Shard Shepherds flee before it, their mechanical voices cutting off mid-word. Even the deep engines seem to quiet slightly as it passes overhead.

The Silence might be a creature, or a phenomenon, or perhaps a malfunction in the plateau's systems—a moving gap in the constant industrial symphony. It leaves no tracks, casts no shadow, but things change in its wake. New species of swarm appear. The angular beings' migration routes shift. The Cogitants speak of memories they didn't have before.

Some theorize the Silence is how the plateau dreams—a wandering unconsciousness that processes the mechanical ecosystem's experiences, updating its patterns, debugging its biological-industrial hybrid existence. Others believe it's a predator so perfectly adapted that it hunts sound itself, leaving only the visual evidence of its prey's existence.

All of these inhabitants, in their impossible variety, create an ecosystem that operates on principles thermodynamics hasn't discovered yet. They are components in a vast machine, or perhaps they are the machine's thoughts made manifest—subroutines that gained independence, or independent entities that learned to think in mechanical rhythms. The distinction may not matter. On the Brass Plateau, being and function are negotiable, and everything that lives there exists in the space between what should be possible and what simply is.
