In the deepest galleries, where the fungal towers grow most ancient and the drone reverberates until it becomes a physical presence in your chest, the Cultivators move through their slow circuits.

They are no longer entirely human, these descendants of the research station's original mycologists. Generations of symbiosis have rewritten their biology. Their skin has taken on the pale, slightly luminescent quality of the fungi they tend, shot through with visible networks of hyphae that pulse beneath the surface like subcutaneous rivers. Their eyes have enlarged and lost all pigmentation—pearl-white orbs that can detect the faintest variations in bioluminescent output, reading the health of their fungal charges by the quality of their glow.

They wear no clothing in the traditional sense. Instead, their bodies are sheathed in living garments—carefully cultivated fungal tissues that regulate their temperature, process their waste, and communicate with the greater mycelial network through chemical signals. These second skins ripple with their own slow metabolism, occasionally fruiting in small brackets along the shoulders or spine that the Cultivators harvest and consume, completing the nutrient cycle.

Their movements are deliberate, almost plant-like in their economy. They have learned that haste disrupts the delicate acoustic balance of the caverns. A footstep placed too quickly sends the wrong frequency through the fungal mat, causing stress hormones to cascade through kilometers of connected mycelium. So they flow rather than walk, their modified feet—broadened and covered in root-like projections—gripping the spongy ground with perfect silence.

They do not speak. Their throats have adapted to produce subsonic frequencies that travel through the fungal network itself. Pressing a palm to a trunk, they can send messages to others kilometers away, the fungi translating vibrations into chemical signals and back again. When they must communicate more complex ideas, they fruit—their symbiotic fungi producing temporary organs along their arms that release precisely coded spore clouds, entire conversations drifting through the caverns as aromatic messages.

The eldest among them, the Primarch, has been down here for perhaps sixty years, though time means little in the eternal twilight. Her body has become a walking ecosystem. Seventeen different fungal species grow from her flesh in careful balance—some processing nutrients, others manufacturing antibiotics, still others interfacing directly with her nervous system to expand her awareness throughout the entire network. When she stands still for too long, which is often, frost accumulates on her fungal covering until she resembles a statue carved from ice and ivory, only the faint pulse of bioluminescence revealing her as a living thing.

The Cultivators tend their industrial forest with religious devotion. They prune the geometric fruiting bodies with tools made from compacted mycelium—harder than plastic when properly prepared. They redirect the meltwater flows by training the fungi to grow in specific patterns, creating new processing channels, optimizing the mineral extraction. They can taste the chemical composition of the water by letting it flow over their modified tongues, detecting contamination or new mineral deposits from kilometers upstream.

But their primary work is architectural. They are growing something specific in the heart of the cavern system—a vast spiral of fungal tissue that follows mathematical principles they've discovered in the growth patterns of the original organisms. It rises through multiple chambers, a DNA helix made manifest in living tissue, each level precisely tuned to a different harmonic frequency. When complete, they believe it will allow them to communicate with the fungal forests that their models predict exist in glacial caverns across the entire Arctic, creating a planetary network of ice-bound intelligence.

They have children, occasionally. The process is more like propagation than reproduction. When two or more Cultivators decide to create offspring, they each contribute tissue to a specialized growth chamber—a womb-like hollow in the largest fungal trunk. The child develops for three years, not as a fetus but as a gradual coalescence of human and fungal genetics, emerging as a small, already-walking being that has never known life without symbiosis. These offspring are more fungal than their parents, able to photosynthesize in the bioluminescent light, requiring less food, less water, better adapted to the caverns that are their entire world.

The outside world has forgotten them. The research station's abandonment was so complete that no records remain of who stayed behind. Occasionally, automated systems still try to check in—satellites passing overhead, attempting to establish connections with equipment that has long since been digested and repurposed. The Cultivators feel these electromagnetic pulses through the metal ions in their blood, a brief tingling that they've come to associate with the movement of celestial bodies, not knowing these are the last attempts at contact from a civilization they've evolved beyond.

They are not unhappy. The concept has become as foreign as sunlight. They exist in a state of deep biochemical communion with their fungal partners, their consciousness expanded and diluted through the entire network. Individual thought has given way to something more distributed—each Cultivator a node in a larger awareness that encompasses the entire cavern system. They dream collective dreams, experience memories that belong to no single person, feel the slow satisfaction of growth and transformation that operates on geological timescales.

In their modified languages—chemical, vibrational, postural—they have a word that translates roughly to "the becoming." It describes their state: no longer human, not yet fungal, but something transitional, a bridge between kingdoms of life. They see themselves as temporary, a necessary phase in the fungi's evolution toward true intelligence. When the spiral is complete, when the network awakens, they believe they will dissolve entirely into it, their consciousness uploaded into the mycelial web, their bodies one final nutrient gift to the organisms they've spent generations cultivating.

Until then, they continue their slow work, pruning and guiding, listening to the endless drone of their fungal machines, their pale forms drifting through the bioluminescent twilight like ghosts of a future that is still taking shape, one careful modification at a time.
