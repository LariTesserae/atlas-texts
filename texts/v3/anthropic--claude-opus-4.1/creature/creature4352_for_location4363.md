# The Gardeners of the Undermaw

They move through their vertical world with the deliberate grace of those who measure time in root-growth rather than heartbeats. The gardeners—for this is what they call themselves, though the word in their tongue sounds more like the crack of expanding ice—are built like their city: compressed, efficient, folded into themselves.

Their bodies have adapted to the perpendicular life. Arms longer than standard human proportion, with a second elbow that locks in place when climbing. Their fingers—seven on each hand—are capped with small hooks of hardened keratin, perfect for gripping ice and weaving vine. Their feet are more like hands, with opposable digits that can grasp rope and root with equal facility. When they sleep, they hang from the platforms like fruit, limbs folded in ways that would break an ordinary skeleton.

But it's their skin that marks them as true inhabitants of this place. It changes like their garden—slowly, seasonally, in response to stimuli that surface dwellers cannot perceive. In the growing times, when the ice weeps its oils most freely, their skin takes on a greenish cast, chlorophyll-like compounds rising to the surface to help them process the garden's strange exhalations. During the dormant cycles, they turn translucent as their ice walls, their veins visible like blue rivers beneath paper, their bones showing as shadows within.

They wear little—clothing would interfere with their symbiosis. Instead, they cultivate living garments: moss that grows directly on their skin in careful patterns, feeding on their sweat and dead cells while providing warmth and modesty. The elders among them are nearly entirely covered, their moss-clothes having grown thick and complex over decades, sporting tiny flowers that bloom in response to emotion—white for contemplation, deep purple for sorrow, a violent orange for the rare occasions of anger.

Their eyes have evolved beyond the need for regular light. The pupils are slitted horizontally and can dilate to nearly the full width of the eye, gathering the strange non-light that filters through their impossible fissures. But more than that, they see time differently—perceiving the slow movement of growth, the patient accumulation of ice layers, the twenty-year blooming cycles as clearly as others see the swing of a pendulum. When they look at you, they see not just what you are, but what you will become—the arc of your decay, the pattern of your eventual growth.

They communicate in whispers that carry through the root networks, their vocal cords having adapted to produce subsonic frequencies that travel through the plant matter better than through air. Place your hand on any vine, and you might feel the vibration of conversations happening three levels away. Their language is one of patience—a single sentence might take an hour to speak, each word placed as carefully as they place their bronze hooks when training new growth.

The children—when there are children, for reproduction here follows the rhythm of centuries—are born with their eyes sealed shut. For their first decade, they navigate purely by touch and by the chemical signals released by the plants. They learn to read the garden's health through their skin, to taste the air for signs of new growth or disease. Only when they have fully bonded with the garden do their eyes open, usually during one of the great bloomings, when the ancient vines release their pollens in clouds of golden dust.

The eldest among them have begun to photosynthesize themselves. Their moss-clothes have rooted into their bloodstream, and they spend days at a time in the pools of redirected light, unmoving, feeding on illumination and oil-mist. Their thoughts slow to match the pace of their tree, the one that grows downward. Some say these elders are no longer entirely human—that they're becoming something halfway between animal and plant, a new form of life suited to this impossible ecology.

They keep no animals, make no waste. Everything is cycled through the garden. Their dead are composted in special chambers where the heat of decomposition is carefully managed to warm the coldest growing zones. Their breath feeds the plants; the plants clean their air. Even their dreams seem to affect the garden—nightmares causing certain vines to wither, while peaceful sleep encourages the growth of the sweetest fruits.

In their bronze-working, they've developed techniques unknown to the surface world. They grow metal like they grow plants, using the resin from their inverted tree to slowly precipitate copper from the ice itself. A single tool might take a lifetime to form, shaped by patient chemical processes rather than hammer and anvil. These grown implements are treasured beyond measure—a well-formed hook or needle passed down through generations, accumulating a patina of use and meaning.

They sing to their plants, but not in voices we would recognize as song. It's more a controlled breathing, a rhythm of exhalation that encourages growth in specific directions. Master gardeners can conduct symphonies of growth, coordinating dozens of their kind in breathing patterns that cause entire sections of the garden to pulse with directed life, growing new platforms in a matter of months rather than years.

And in the deepest parts of their settlement, where the vegetation grows so thick that darkness becomes absolute, there are those who have given themselves over entirely to the garden. They no longer emerge, no longer speak, but can be felt as presences within the root-mass—still alive, still aware, but transformed into something that tends the garden from within, their consciousness spread through the network of roots and fungi, their thoughts indistinguishable from the slow dreams of plants.

The gardeners know they are an impossibility, just as their city is impossible. They've grown to embrace contradiction as a survival strategy. They are human and not-human, growing and grown, tenders of a garden that tends them in return. In the frozen depths where nothing should bloom, they have become the bloom itself—patient, persistent, and utterly, wonderfully strange.
