The Millkeepers move through the Drowned Mills like thoughts through a sleeping mind — present but not quite conscious, purposeful but not quite deliberate. They are neither fully human nor fully other, having undergone generations of slow adaptation that has left them somewhere between.

Their bodies have lengthened and compressed simultaneously. Torsos stretch to accommodate modified lung structures that process both water and the thick, particulate air trapped in the facility's few remaining pockets. But their limbs have shortened, become denser, fingers now twice-jointed and webbed with a membrane that seems part skin, part industrial textile — as if they've been slowly replacing themselves with materials scavenged from the Mills' endless inventory. Their skin carries a sheen like machine oil, iridescent in the rare shafts of light, and beneath it, their veins branch in patterns that mirror the piping systems they maintain.

They wear the Mills as much as inhabit them. Fragments of machinery have been incorporated into clothing that might also be anatomy — shoulder pieces of corroded brass that could be pauldrons or could be growths, tubes that enter their backs carrying some vital fluid that might be blood or hydraulic oil or something that serves as both. They dress in layers of kelp leather and rubber that has partially dissolved and re-formed around their bodies so many times that removing it would be like peeling away skin.

The Millkeepers do not speak in voices anymore. They communicate through vibrations carried in the pipes — a hand placed on iron, a subtle modulation in the rhythm of their work that propagates through the entire structure. They know each other by the signature of their hammering, the particular way each one turns a valve or cleans a filter. This is a language of maintenance, where meaning is conveyed through the act of keeping things running.

Their society, if it can be called that, is organized around cycles of care. Each Millkeeper tends to a section of the Mills, but these sections overlap and change according to tides both literal and mechanical. On certain days, determined by the convergence of multiple slow-moving gears, they gather in the great turbine halls. Here they perform what might be rituals or might simply be necessary maintenance — synchronized movements that clean specific sequences of machinery in orders that have been passed down through generations, though the original purpose has been lost. They move in unison, their bodies reflecting the mechanical rhythms around them, becoming part of the Mills' vast choreography.

They feed on what the Mills produce, though production and waste have become indistinguishable. The kelp papers pressed by the ancient machines dissolve in water to create a nutritious soup. The seed pods glowing in the chemical tanks can be harvested and consumed, their light continuing in the Millkeepers' translucent bellies for hours afterward. They farm the tube worms, but also clean them, and in cleaning them, feed them, and in feeding them, change them, until the relationship has become so reciprocal that it's impossible to say who is keeping whom alive.

Children are rare and strange. They gestate in the porcelain-lined chambers, tended by the entire community in rotation, growing in artificial wombs that are part of the Mills' original industrial processes repurposed. The young emerge already adapted, already knowing the vibration-language, already able to navigate the impossible tangle of their world. They learn by following, by placing their small, dense hands on the pipes and feeling the knowledge flow through iron and copper and brass.

The eldest Millkeepers have begun to root. They move less and less, choosing favorite spots where they stand for days, their feet spreading into holdfasts, their skin taking on the texture of the coral-crusted walls. They become nodes in the Mills' nervous system, processing information, distributing nutrients, their consciousness spreading through the structure until they are no longer entirely themselves but part of the greater organism. The younger ones tend to them as they would any other vital component, cleaning barnacles from their shoulders, ensuring water flows freely through their transformed bodies.

They dream collectively, when they dream. During the slowest tides, when even the geological pace of the Mills seems to pause, the Millkeepers enter a shared suspension. Their bodies cool to match the ambient temperature, their hearts beat in synchronization with the distant turbines. In these moments, they might remember — or imagine — the time before the drowning, when the Mills produced things that left, that had purposes beyond their own walls. But these memories are fading, each generation remembering less of the before, becoming more of the now.

The Millkeepers are not trapped here, but leaving is inconceivable. They are the Mills' consciousness, its immune system, its reproductive organs. They maintain the balance between growth and decay, between mechanical and biological, between past purpose and present being. They ensure the water continues to change in just the right ways, that the temperature stays within the narrow range that keeps everything functioning, that the slow transformation continues at its proper pace.

Sometimes, rarely, outsiders find the Mills. The Millkeepers observe them with eyes that have developed nictitating membranes like camera shutters, watching but not interfering unless the strangers threaten the balance. They are not hostile, but they are not welcoming either. They simply continue their work, their care, their slow dance with the Mills, as if the outsiders were just another temporary current in their eternal circulation.

This is how they live: in movements measured by tides, in language made of maintenance, in bodies that are becoming architecture, in minds that are becoming collective, in a present that stretches backward and forward until time itself becomes just another substance to be processed, refined, and recycled through the endless, breathing organism of the Drowned Mills of Gethara.
