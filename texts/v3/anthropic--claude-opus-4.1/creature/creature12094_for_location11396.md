**The Understory Millkeepers**

They emerged from the city's maintenance crews three generations ago, or perhaps the city's maintenance crews emerged from them — the stories contradict depending on who's telling them, and the Millkeepers themselves speak of time in cycles rather than lines.

You'll know them first by their suits, if suits is even the right word. The base layer might have started as hazmat gear or diving equipment, but now it's something altogether different. Fungal leather has grown over and through the original material, creating a second skin that breathes for them in the spore-thick air. The surface ripples with shallow channels where condensation collects and flows toward collection bladders at the hips. Their breathing apparatus long ago fused with a colony of oxygen-producing algae that lives in symbiosis with their lung bacteria, the transparent tubes pulsing green-gold with each breath.

They move through their domain with practiced inefficiency, taking routes that seem meandering until you realize they're following the thermal gradients, letting the environment's own circulation patterns carry them. Their gait has adapted to the uncertain footing — a sliding, almost skating motion that keeps their center of gravity low and their hands free to steady themselves against surfaces that might be metal, might be bark, might be something between.

The eldest among them, the Tender-General Morse (though that's a translation of the click-whistle-hum that serves as her name), has been down here so long that her suit has achieved something approaching permanence. Lichen grows in deliberate patterns across her shoulders, encoding shift schedules in its growth rings. Her fingers end in copper-tipped extensions that can interface directly with the root networks, reading electrical resistance like braille. When she speaks, it's in a pidgin of engineering terminology and botanical metaphor that makes perfect sense if you don't think about it too hard.

The younger Millkeepers are easier to recognize as once-human, though they're changing. Their eyes have developed nictitating membranes that filter the bioluminescent wavelengths, letting them read the health of the fungi gardens at a glance. Their gut flora has adapted to process the filtered water that comes from the condensation systems, and they supplement their diet with the fruit of the ductwork trees — dense, oily things that taste of copper and mulch but provide everything a body needs.

They work in shifts that follow the cavern's breathing rather than any clock. When the humidity rises, the Pollinators emerge to tend the moth colonies, their suits studded with brush-like extensions that collect and distribute spores. When the rains come, the Plumbers (though they're more like veterinarians for pipes) wade through the flooded lower galleries, adjusting the root-filtered drainage systems that prevent total flooding. The Readers spend their shifts at the monitoring stations, interpreting the data that flows through vine-transmitted pressure waves, their bodies swaying unconsciously in rhythm with the information.

Children are rare but not unknown. They grow up feral-careful, learning to read the cockroach migrations before they learn to read words, understanding that the Mills are neither safe nor dangerous but simply *are*. They play in the spawning pools, racing the amphibious generators, their laughter echoing weirdly in the acoustic chambers of the generator-trees. By adolescence, they've usually chosen their apprenticeship — or had it chosen for them by whichever system accepts their touch without rejection.

The Millkeepers have their own mythology, though they'd call it technical documentation. They speak of the Great Convergence, when the abandoned industrial infrastructure first began to fruit. They remember the Copper Wars, when competing fungal networks fought for mineral rights until the Keepers learned to negotiate treaties through targeted pruning. They tell stories of the Deep Generators, massive trees that grow in the lowest galleries where no human can survive unmodified, their heartwood-chambers producing enough power to run half the forgotten quarter above.

Their society operates on principles of maintenance rather than progress. Status comes from how well you can read the Mills' needs, how smoothly you can mediate between the industrial and biological systems. They have specialists who've modified themselves for specific tasks — the Syrup-Runners whose blood sugar has adapted to interface with the photosynthetic networks, the Acoustic Engineers who've developed bones dense enough to resonate with the generator-trees' bass notes, the Membrane Weavers who can felt the fungal leather into new patches for deteriorating suits.

They trade with the surface through intermediaries who can still pass for normal humans, exchanging power (siphoned carefully, sustainably from the grid) for the specific nutrients the Mills can't produce — trace minerals, certain proteins, the occasional piece of pre-convergence technology that can be digested and incorporated. The surface world considers them urban legends, or at best, an extremely dedicated union of underground utility workers.

At night (though night and day mean nothing in the constant twilight of the Mills), they gather in the common chambers where the walls fruit with bioluminescent displays. They share meals of processed mulch-cake and ductwork fruit, tend to each other's suits, cleaning the algae tubes and checking the fungal leather for infections. They sing — or rather, they harmonize with the Mills' constant sound, adding human voices to the whistle of modified stomata and the click of mechanical beetles, creating something that might be music or might be prayer or might be simply another form of maintenance.

The oldest among them barely remember the surface, and the youngest have never known it. They are becoming something new, something that exists in the margin between fixing and growing, between human and system. They are the Understory Millkeepers, and they are keeping the lights on in their own peculiar way, one spore-laden breath at a time.
