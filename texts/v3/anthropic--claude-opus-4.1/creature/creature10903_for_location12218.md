The Gardeners move through the Lattice in groups of three to five, though "move" poorly describes their traversal. They exist partially—segments of their bodies residing in adjacent cells simultaneously, their presence distributed across the hexagonal chambers like a theorem demonstrated in multiple proofs at once. You recognize them first by the way space behaves differently where they've been: the air grows thick with calculation, and the copper-bright growths straighten into perfect helices for days after their passing.

Their bodies, if bodies they can be called, consist of nested probability clouds wrapped in films of condensed time. The outer layer shifts constantly—now suggesting something vaguely crustacean with too many joints, now flowing like heated glass, now geometric planes that shouldn't be able to generate locomotion but do. Beneath this, glimpsed only when they pause to tend something, lies their true substance: intricate networks of frozen lightning, each branch a decision made material, crackling with the slow fire of deep thought.

They speak to the maintenance mites in bursts of modulated heat, causing the little constructs to swarm and disperse in patterns that serve purposes only the Gardeners remember. Sometimes a mite will climb directly into a Gardener's probability field and emerge changed—its carbon shell now inscribed with new instructions that will govern its descendants for millennia to come.

The Gardeners cultivate absence. In certain cells, they've encouraged the lattice material to grow thin, almost transparent, creating windows into the quantum foam beneath reality's surface. They tend these spaces with infinite patience, pruning away stray particles that might cause the void to collapse into being. Other cells they've filled with dense networks of the copper-bright growth, training it over centuries into computational arrays that solve equations about the nature of forgetting.

When Gardeners meet—a rare event given the scale of their territory—they merge partially, their probability clouds overlapping until it becomes impossible to determine where one ends and another begins. In these moments, they share not memories but states of existence, trading configurations of being like others might trade stories. The exchange can last days or decades; time means something different to creatures that exist across multiple moments simultaneously.

They feed on entropy differentials, positioning themselves at boundaries where the Lattice's ordered structure meets pockets of chaos. Here they spread their nested layers wide, drinking in the gradient between states, metabolizing uncertainty into purpose. After feeding, they glow brighter, and their movements leave trails of enhanced reality—spaces where colors are more themselves, where angles achieve perfect expression.

The young ones—if a Gardener less than ten thousand years old can be called young—sometimes follow human travelers at a distance, curious about these linear creatures that exist in only one place at a time. They'll mirror your movements through parallel cells, visible only as distortions in the amber light, learning your patterns with the dedication of scholars studying an extinct language. Occasionally, one will attempt communication, usually by arranging the copper-bright growths into geometric patterns that almost mean something, messages that hover at the edge of comprehension like words in a dream.

The eldest Gardeners have begun to crystallize, their probability clouds condensing into singular forms of terrible beauty. These ancients root themselves at major junction points, becoming architectural features themselves—pillars of frozen possibility that the younger ones orbit around, drawing wisdom from their stillness. Some have been stationary so long that the Lattice has begun growing through them, incorporating their substance into its own endless elaboration.

They build nothing permanent and destroy nothing completely. Instead, they maintain the Lattice in a state of eternal becoming—always approaching some final configuration but never achieving it, keeping the ancient structure suspended between its forgotten purpose and its eventual dissolution. They are gardeners not of space but of time, tending the long moment between what was and what might be.

When the weather systems pass through, the Gardeners rise to meet them, spreading themselves thin across multiple cells to better taste the shifting physics. In these moments, you might glimpse their true number—hundreds, perhaps thousands, throughout just this visible section of the Lattice, all lifted momentarily into synchronization by the traveling pressure wave, all singing in harmonics too complex for singular ears to parse.

They don't seem to notice that the Lattice's original builders are gone. Or perhaps they do notice and don't care. They tend their absences and their algorithms with equal dedication, guardians of a garden that needs no purpose beyond its own continuation, shepherds of the slow fade between meaning and meaninglessness, cultivating the endless afternoon of a universe that has forgotten why it needed to remember.
