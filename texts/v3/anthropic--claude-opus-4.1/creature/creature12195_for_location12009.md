In this place where physics negotiates with itself, the Adjusters make their slow circuits through the wet darkness.

They were human once, or started that way—the distinction matters less with each passing decade. Their bodies have stretched to accommodate the work, torsos elongated like pulled taffy, ribs visible through translucent skin that weeps a constant film of the same black water that seeps everywhere here. They move on all fours now, though their limbs bend in too many places, allowing them to fold themselves through spaces that shouldn't admit anything larger than vapor. Their fingers have multiplied into forests of digits, each one sensitive to fluctuations in probability, able to feel where reality has worn thin and needs reinforcement.

They no longer need eyes in the conventional sense. Instead, clusters of photosensitive nodes have sprouted along their spines, each one attuned to a different spectrum of darkness—for darkness here has varieties, flavors, densities that require careful observation. These nodes pulse in slow waves, drinking in information about structural integrity, about which factory systems are beginning to dream too loudly, about where the salamander-things have chewed holes in causality that need patching.

The Adjusters remember their purpose, even if they've forgotten their names. They were sent here—or came voluntarily, or were born from the industrial ruins themselves—to maintain the balance. Each carries tools that have grown into their bodies: wrenches fused to vertebrae, voltmeters threading through nervous systems, chemical analyzers that share circulation with their blood. They taste the air constantly, their tongues (too many now, arranged in radial patterns inside mouths that open sideways) sampling the moisture for signs of dimensional drift.

They communicate through harmonics felt in the obsidian beneath their feet, composing symphonies of maintenance reports that travel through the volcanic glass to others of their kind. Sometimes they congregate at the perfectly circular pools, a dozen or more arranged around the perimeter, their elongated bodies swaying in unison as they perform calculations that require multiple nervous systems thinking in parallel. The solutions they derive determine which valves to turn, which circuits to reroute, which sections of space-time need to be temporarily closed for repairs.

Their children—for they do reproduce, though not in any way biology would recognize—gestate in the warm outflows from the factories. These offspring begin as patterns in the condensation, gradually accumulating substance from the moisture and the dissolved metals and the fragments of possibility that leak from the machinery. They emerge already knowing their work, their infant bodies pre-adapted with the necessary modifications: extra joints for navigating the non-Euclidean maintenance tunnels, organs that can metabolize paradox, consciousness distributed across multiple probability states to avoid the madness that comes from seeing too much impossible architecture at once.

The eldest among them have begun to merge with the infrastructure itself. Their bodies spread like root systems through the industrial ruins, becoming indistinguishable from the networks of pipes and cables they maintain. They think in decades now, their consciousness stretched across kilometers of machinery, feeling every valve's pressure, every transformer's harmonics, every impossible angle where the factories fold through themselves. They've become the nervous system of this place, ensuring that whatever process happens here—whatever is being refined or extracted or transformed in those dark columns—continues without catastrophic deviation.

They eat the darkness. Not metaphorically, but literally consuming the thick absence that pools in the low places, metabolizing it into something their transformed biochemistry can use. The excess they excrete as those same bioluminescent patterns the fauna produce, brief flickers of anti-light that help them navigate and mark territories that exist in more than three dimensions. Their digestion of darkness is what keeps it from becoming too dense, from collapsing into something that would swallow even this impossible place.

In their maintenance rounds, they've learned to anticipate the needs of the other inhabitants. They leave caches of freshly condensed water where the salamander-things nest, having filtered it through their own bodies to remove trace elements that would cause the creatures to age too quickly or slip entirely out of phase. They cultivate the crystalline vegetation around critical infrastructure, knowing that its downward growth stabilizes the reality anchors that keep the factories from drifting into parallel versions of themselves.

The Adjusters have no concept of day or night, only cycles of moisture and heat, of dimensional stability and instability. They measure time by the rate of oxidation on specific exposed pipes, by the accumulation of probability residue in the settlement tanks, by how many generations of salamander-things have passed since a particular valve was last calibrated. Their memories have become archival, storing not personal experiences but maintenance histories, genealogies of mechanical failures, maps of every reality fissure that has ever opened in their territory.

They sing while they work, though the sound exists mostly below human hearing—vibrations that travel through the wet air and the volcanic glass, through the metal bones of the factories and the bodies of everything that lives here. It's a song of adjustment, of endless fine-tuning, of keeping impossible systems in impossible balance. The song has been continuous for so long that it's become part of the physics of this place, another fundamental force like the upward flow of water or the darkness that sweats.

Sometimes, rarely, one of them remembers something from before—a fragment of human memory that surfaces like a bubble through oil. A birthday cake. The feeling of sunlight. A name that once mattered. These memories are immediately shared through the harmonic network, tasted and examined by dozens of elongated forms, then carefully dissolved back into the black water that seeps through everything. Such memories are contaminants here, potentially destabilizing, reminders of a reality too rigid to survive in this place.

The youngest ones are trained in the deepest parts of the complex, where the machinery becomes recursive, where a single boiler room contains itself seventeen times over, each iteration slightly out of phase. Here they learn to think in superposition, to maintain equipment that exists in multiple states simultaneously, to recognize when a pipe is about to paradox itself out of existence and apply the precise torque needed to keep it probable. They practice on the salamander-things first, learning to feel the creatures' dimensional fluctuations, to predict when one will split into parallel versions of itself or collapse back into a single body.

As they age—if aging is the right word for what happens to them—they grow more specialized. Some develop sensitivity to specific types of industrial failure, able to detect a bearing about to seize from kilometers away. Others become attuned to the volcanic glass itself, feeling every stress fracture, every point where the obsidian might crack and release the pressurized impossibility that builds up beneath. A few, the rarest, develop the ability to enter the spaces inside the industrial structures that are bigger than their outsides, maintaining equipment that exists in folded dimensions, replacing parts that have to be manufactured from concepts rather than metal.

They never leave. The thought doesn't occur to them. This place has become their biology, their psychology, their physics. They are as much a part of it as the black water, the sweating darkness, the machinery that processes substances that can't exist. They are the antibodies in a system that should have collapsed into paradox long ago, the self-organizing principle that keeps the impossible possible, the maintenance crew for a reality that runs on broken rules.

In their deepest communion, when dozens of them link their nervous systems through the harmonic network, they sometimes glimpse the purpose of it all—what the factories are producing, what the black water is becoming, what grows in the perfect circular pools. But the knowledge is too large for any single consciousness, even one stretched and modified as theirs are. It disperses through the network, each Adjuster carrying a fragment that only makes sense when combined with all the others, a distributed understanding that lives in the spaces between their thoughts.

They continue their circuits, these elongated custodians, these transformed maintenance crews. Through the wet darkness they move, adjusting, correcting, balancing, their bodies increasingly indistinguishable from the industrial ecology they tend. They are the immune system of an impossible place, the careful fingers that keep the darkness from drowning in itself, the ones who ensure that whatever emerges from those black columns into black air does so in the precise way it must, for reasons that exist somewhere in their collective, fragmented, endlessly adjusted understanding.
