The inhabitants arrive by rail lines that shouldn't be able to climb this high, stepping onto platforms that groan with the mountain's breathing. They are industrial theologians, every one of them—people who gave up on the distinction between faith and engineering generations ago.

They wear thick canvas coats gone silver-green with accumulated moss spores, the fabric having developed its own ecosystem. Breathing apparatus hang from their necks like pilgrim badges, necessary for when the mountain forgets what altitude it's supposed to be. Their hands are always slightly damp from the condensation that beads on everything here, fingers stained with the iridescent oil that leaks from machines processing materials that haven't finished becoming what they'll be.

The first-generation settlers have eyes that don't quite track together anymore—one pupil follows the present while the other has gotten stuck somewhere in the mountain's temporal stutters. They've learned to see around corners that won't exist for another decade, to step over cracks that healed themselves yesterday. Their children were born pre-adapted, with lung capacity that shifts to match the mountain's breathing and inner ears that can balance on surfaces that are becoming something else.

They speak in overlapping dialects. Morning shift workers use future tense for everything, even the past. Night shift speaks only in conditional phrases—might-be, could-have, when-if. The twilight crews who maintain the reality anchors between industrial districts have developed a pidgin of hand signals and harmonic humming that resonates with the mountain's growth frequency. Conversations layer and loop, with answers sometimes arriving before questions, understanding achieved through accumulation rather than sequence.

The engineers wear specialized monocles that let them see the stress patterns in spacetime itself. They map the places where the mountain is trying to fold into itself, marking them with fluorescent paint that only shows up in certain qualities of industrial light. Their apprentices follow behind, adjusting the factory anchors by degrees measured in units they invented last week and will rename tomorrow.

In the sparse zones between buildings, the moss farmers tend their impossible gardens. They've given up trying to understand why certain spores produce vegetables that taste like next Thursday or why some patches grow backwards into seeds. They harvest what grows, when it grows, selling produce at markets that set up in the wide boulevards whenever the geometry permits. Their children play between the legs of the semi-transparent deer, learning games with rules that shift each time the mountain settles into a new position.

The factory workers are philosophers by necessity. They clock in for shifts at machines whose purposes reveal themselves only through repetition—year after year of feeding raw materials into processors, adjusting dials according to patterns felt rather than understood. Some workers have been running the same production line for forty years, making components for devices that won't be invented until their grandchildren's time. They take pride in the precision of their uncertainty, in the craftsmanship of manufacturing impossibilities.

Religious orders have formed around the various industrial processes. The Turbine Monks maintain the massive engines in meditative silence, believing the harmonic frequencies reveal the mountain's true name. The Sisters of Flexible Plumbing follow the water wherever it travels, rerouting the city's pipes in elaborate rituals that are part engineering, part interpretive dance. They've stopped distinguishing between prayer and maintenance schedules.

The children attend schools built on platforms that adjust their height daily. Their textbooks are printed on paper that updates itself as the mountain revises local reality. They learn mathematics that includes imaginary numbers as standard integers, geography classes where they map places that are still deciding whether to exist. At recess, they play in the courtyards where gravity pools, learning to jump in three and a half dimensions.

The elderly don't retire so much as they begin operating on mountain time. They move slowly through the streets, but arrive at destinations before younger folk who left earlier. Some have been around long enough to remember when the mountain was just a hill with ambitions, and they tell stories about the early days when the city only existed on Tuesdays and alternate Fridays. Their memories are considered more reliable than photographs, which here only show what might have been.

Medical practitioners have evolved beyond traditional healing. They set bones that are thinking about breaking, treat infections that haven't decided which species to affect yet. The hospital operates on all seven levels of the city simultaneously, with the same ward existing in multiple locations depending on the patient's relationship with local spacetime. Births are scheduled for whenever the baby decides to have been conceived.

At night—or what passes for night when the industrial glow never quite fades—the inhabitants gather on their moss-covered balconies. They listen to the mountain's growing sounds, feeling the subtle shifts in their foundations, watching the too-winged pigeons spiral in patterns that map tomorrow's weather. They share meals of vegetables that taste like temporal anomalies and meat from animals that exist in several states simultaneously.

They are people who have learned to love uncertainty, who find stability in constant adjustment, who have built their entire civilization on the principle that nothing needs to make sense as long as it works. They maintain their impossible city through collective faith in the practical—if the factories produce what's needed (eventually), if the streets lead somewhere (ultimately), if the mountain keeps growing (inevitably), then life continues.

Some leave, taking the rail lines back down to where physics behaves itself. But more arrive every day—industrial pilgrims drawn to a place where reality's quality control is permanently on break, where you can spend your whole life manufacturing impossibilities for a future that's constantly revising itself. They come to live on an unfinished god, parasites who've learned to photosynthesize from uncertainty itself.
