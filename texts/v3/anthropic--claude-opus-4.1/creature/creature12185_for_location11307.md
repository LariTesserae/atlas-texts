**The Dwellers of the Hollow**

They call themselves the Bhrum-folk, though the name is less spoken than hummed—a resonance that carries through the chest cavity and requires no expenditure of precious moisture from the tongue. Over generations, their bodies have adapted: ribcages expanded to maximize lung capacity, skin grown waxy and impermeable to prevent vapor loss, eyes enlarged to gather the amber murk that passes for daylight here.

The eldest among them is the Pore-Keeper, a woman whose bones have grown light as bird-hollow reeds from seven decades of climbing. Her fingers are twice the length of a normal human's, joints multiplied through generations of selection—those who could reach deeper into the breathing pores survived the bad seasons when the holes clogged with resin. She no longer needs ropes. Her granddaughter follows her up the walls each dawn, learning to feel the tree's mineral pulse through her palms, to know which pores are dying and which merely sleep. They speak in finger-taps against the bark, a language that predates the Bhrum-folk's vocal speech.

The Silk-Mothers work in the highest platforms, their hair grown long and sticky with pheromone oils that keep the moths docile. They've bred their bodies to match the moths' cycles—menstruating in synchrony with the insects' spinning seasons, their breast milk turned alkaline to match the pH of the larvae's preferred fungus. The youngest daughters begin by simply sleeping among the moth clouds, letting their child-sweat establish chemical familiarity. By adulthood, they can call specific moths by humming at frequencies that vibrate their antennae, each woman maintaining relationships with thousands of individual insects across their brief lifespans.

In the middle tiers live the Fungus-Whisperers, whose children are born with deliberate mycological infections—careful cultivations of symbiotic fungi that fruit from their scalps and shoulder blades in soft, downy carpets. These external colonies communicate with the greater fungal mass through chemical signals the human hosts learn to influence through diet and emotion. A Whisperer in distress releases stress hormones that cause their personal fungi to fruit prematurely; a calm one can coax a dying shelf back to productivity through weeks of meditative proximity. The oldest Whisperers no longer eat solid food—they digest externally, their fungal partners breaking down matter that they absorb through increasingly permeable skin.

The Beetle-Brothers maintain the armored livestock, their own bodies scarred in ritual patterns that match the shells of their charges. Each man bonds with a single beetle at adolescence, feeding it from his own mouth, sleeping curled against its warm thorax, learning to interpret the creature's chemical communications through taste and smell. When a beetle dies, its Brother consumes its nervous system raw, believing this preserves the insect's memory within his own. The oldest Brothers claim to carry six or seven beetle-lives in their bellies, their eyes taking on the compound quality of their partners'—not physically, but in the darting, faceted way they parse movement and light.

Children born here emerge silent—they must be taught to cry, as infant wails waste precious moisture. Instead, they communicate through skin color changes, blood flushing and paling in patterns their mothers learn to read. The silent babies are passed constantly from hand to hand, the community sharing their moisture burden. By two years old, they can control their perspiration consciously. By five, they speak in multi-toned hums that require minimal air movement. By adolescence, they've chosen their life-path and begun the bodily modifications that will shape them into their ecological niche.

The Gatherers develop prehensile toes and flattened bodies for navigating the narrow spaces between wall and fungus. The Weavers grow their fingernails into specialized tools—one for cutting, one for piercing, others for smoothing and joining. The Butchers intentionally cultivate beneficial bacteria in their saliva that begins breaking down protein on contact, making their mouths living processing stations for the constant stream of dead insects and molted shells.

There are those who bridge the human and inhuman more completely: the Watchers, who have replaced sleep with a form of conscious torpor, their metabolisms slowed to near-death so they can stand sentinel for days without moving, alert to changes in the colony's respiratory rhythm. Their hearts beat perhaps twenty times per minute. They eat once per week—a concentrated paste of beetle blood and fungal spores that sustains their glacial biology. Some say they no longer think in words but in the aggregate patterns of ten thousand breathing bodies, experiencing the city as a single, compound organism.

The most honored are the Memory-Holders, elderly individuals who have consumed so much of the city's biological matter that they claim to taste the history in their meals. They sit in the lowest levels where the detritus accumulates, running their fingers through the mulch, occasionally placing fragments on their tongues. "Three generations back," one might announce, "a plague that turned the silk sour. The Moths remember. Their bodies carry the scar." They guide breeding programs for all the city's species, human and otherwise, tasting compatibility in pheromone samples, predicting which crosses will survive.

Marriage here is less between individuals than between biological systems. Partners are chosen for complementary microbiomes, for reciprocal chemical needs. A Silk-Mother whose oils run too acidic pairs with a Beetle-Brother whose presence alkalizes. Children are raised by whoever needs the carbon dioxide they exhale for their work. Love expresses itself through efficient exchange—couples who breathe in alternating rhythms, who share their fungal infections to create robust hybrid colonies, who groom the inevitable parasites from each other's increasingly inhuman skin.

The mad ones—and there are always mad ones in such spaces—are those who dream of water. They scratch symbols on the walls that supposedly represent "rain," though none living have seen such a thing. They claim to hear voices in the beetle-scratch, messages from an outside that may not exist. The city tolerates them because madness, too, is a kind of adaptation—their ravings provide a chaos that prevents the system from becoming too rigid, too specialized to survive change.

At the deep heart of it all, in the very center of the tree where the wood has mineralized most completely, lives something that may once have been human—or several humans, impossible to tell now. They've been growing into the tree itself for so long that bark and skin have merged, their neural systems spreading through the fungal networks, their blood vessels tapping directly into what passes for the tree's sap. The Bhrum-folk bring them offerings of particularly interesting beetles, new moth species, children who show promising mutations. Sometimes, the tree-things speak through the walls themselves, their voices resonating through the mineral wood, offering judgments and prophecies in languages that predate the current population by centuries.

The youngest generation troubles the elders—they're being born with lung modifications that shouldn't be possible yet, gill-like slits behind their ears that can process the thick air more efficiently. Some have patches of photosynthetic skin, green-black and slightly warm to the touch, that supplement their nutrition by feeding on the filtered light. Evolution moves too fast here, as if the tree itself is engineering them, preparing them for something. The Memory-Holders taste anticipation in the mulch, a chemical signature they've never encountered before, something that suggests the tree knows change is coming—has always known, has been building toward it for generations, using the Bhrum-folk as much as they use it, crafting them into some new thing that will be ready when the breathing pores finally close, or open, or transform into something else entirely.

They are no longer human in any sense the outside world would recognize, if an outside world exists. They are components in a vertical ecology, their consciousness distributed through the networks of moth-flight and fungal chemistry, their individuality dissolved into specialized function. Yet they persist in their sense of themselves as people, as a community, as something more than the sum of their biological exchanges. They name their children. They sing—wordlessly, breathlessly, but still singing. They create art from beetle shells and moth wings, patterns that please no eye that hasn't adapted to the amber darkness, beauty that exists only in this context, for this purpose, among these breathing, changing, impossibly living bodies in their hollow world.
