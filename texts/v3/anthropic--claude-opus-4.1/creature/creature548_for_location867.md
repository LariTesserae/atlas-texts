The foundry workers arrive before dawn, their bodies already adapted to the district's peculiar rhythms. They move through the half-light with practiced ease, stepping over the tree roots that buckle the walkways, ducking beneath the morning spider webs that span every doorframe. Their uniforms—thick canvas dyed the color of rust—bear the permanent moisture marks of this place, salt-white rings at the collars and cuffs where sweat and steam have dried a thousand times over.

The furnace tenders come from families who've worked these foundries since the beginning. Their children grow up knowing the different qualities of steam by sight alone—which plumes mean the furnaces run true, which suggest a cracked crucible. These workers have developed a resistance to the heat that amazes newcomers; they stand bare-armed beside furnaces that would blister others, their skin darkened not just by their ancestry but by decades of radiant heat. They speak in abbreviated bursts between the machinery's cycles, a clipped dialect that drops unnecessary syllables, saving their voices from the constant competition with industrial noise.

But the district draws others too. Between shifts, the scrap collectors emerge—elderly women, mostly, who've discovered that certain fungi growing on the metal surfaces contain rare mineral concentrations. They harvest these growths with curved knives, filling woven bags with what the workers call "rust flowers." The foundry management turns a blind eye; these women know which valves shouldn't be touched, which pipes run too hot to approach. They've become part of the ecosystem, as essential as the herons that keep the fish populations in check.

The rail workers constitute their own society, living in converted shipping containers stacked three-high along the main track. They've learned to sleep through the rhythmic thunder of passing freight, but wake instantly at any irregularity in the pattern. Their children play on the gravel berms, building kingdoms from discarded machine parts, racing homemade carts along the drainage channels. These families maintain elaborate rooftop gardens on their container homes, growing vegetables in the ash-enriched soil they collect from the furnace cleanouts.

In the administrative offices—a cluster of prefabricated buildings on the district's highest ground—a different class of inhabitant manages the industrial flow. These coordinators rarely touch the factory floors, but they know every steam signature, every shift in the mechanical harmonics that might signal breakdown. They work surrounded by walls of analog gauges and paper charts, preferring these old methods that continue functioning despite the moisture that corrupts digital equipment. Many are former floor workers who've aged into these positions, their bodies no longer able to sustain the physical demands below, but their knowledge irreplaceable.

The night shift brings its own population. Security guards patrol the elevated walkways with powerful flashlights, less concerned about human intruders than about checking that the wildlife hasn't compromised critical systems. They've developed an uneasy truce with the monitor lizards, even naming the largest ones, learning their territorial patterns. These guards carry long poles to redirect snakes away from warm electrical boxes, and keep detailed logs of which sections of the district the wild boar families are currently occupying.

The district also harbors those who exist in its margins. In the abandoned lower floors of the older factories, small communities of squatters have established themselves. The foundry management knows they're there—the smoke from their cooking fires is visible some mornings—but as long as they don't interfere with operations, they're tolerated. These shadow residents know the district's hidden spaces better than anyone: which abandoned tanks collect the cleanest rainwater, which ventilation shafts provide warmth without deadly gases. They've learned to read the industrial schedules, knowing when to move unseen between buildings, when to harvest the edible plants that grow in the thermal zones.

The maintenance crews form the district's nervous system, constantly moving through pipes and ducts, keeping the delicate balance between function and decay. They wade through flooded basements with headlamps, clearing drain grates of vegetation. They scale the smokestacks to repair lightning damage, sharing their high perches with the nesting cormorants who've learned to recognize individual workers. These crews speak of the district as if it were alive, describing its moods, its seasonal changes, its good days and bad days.

Young engineers arrive from the universities, full of plans to modernize and efficiency. Most leave within a year, defeated by the constant moisture, the irregular schedules, the way standard solutions fail in this environment. But a few stay, seduced by the district's strange beauty, by the engineering challenges that require innovation rather than textbook solutions. These converts become the district's fiercest advocates, arguing in boardrooms that this integration of industry and nature represents not failure but a different kind of success.

The ferry operators who bring workers from the residential districts across the delta have their own relationship with this place. They time their routes by the industrial shifts but also by the tides, reading the water levels with generational knowledge. Their boats—flat-bottomed and wide—serve as informal gathering places where workers from different factories share news and gossip during the crossing. The operators' children often work as runners, carrying messages between buildings for those who don't trust the moisture-damaged phone lines.

All of them—workers, scavengers, administrators, squatters—have learned to move with the district's hybrid rhythm. They know when the morning mist will burn off (after the third shift bell), when the fruit bats will swarm (during the evening maintenance window), when the spring floods will force operations to the upper floors (marked by the migration of certain bird species). They've developed a culture that's neither fully industrial nor natural, but something new—a way of living that accepts rust and growth, precision and chaos, as equal partners in the daily work of making things from metal and fire.
