The Keepers arrived long after the builders left, though "arrived" suggests intention where there was only drift. They are tall—three meters when fully extended—but spend most of their existence folded into themselves, their bodies compressed into dense nodes that could be mistaken for basalt formations. Their skin is a living map of mineral deposits, constantly absorbing and secreting layers of silicate that match whatever surface they rest upon. When they unfold to move, which happens perhaps twice in a human lifetime, their limbs extend like telescoping machinery, each joint sealed with a membrane that holds their internal pressure against the thin atmosphere.

They have no eyes in any conventional sense. Instead, their sensory organs are distributed across their skin in patterns that mirror the pipeline networks—branching channels that detect vibration through solid matter, reading the plateau's slow percussion like a language. They feel the arthropod migrations as vast sentences spoken across decades, punctuated by the deep resonances of wind through the cooling towers.

Their consciousness is intermittent. They think only when the atmospheric pressure drops below a certain threshold, which happens in irregular cycles. During these windows of awareness—lasting anywhere from days to months—they perform their work. They maintain selected sections of the ancient infrastructure, not to preserve its original function but to cultivate specific acoustic properties. Using secretions that temporarily soften metal, they tune the hollow structures like massive instruments, adjusting their resonances to frequencies that influence the arthropod migration patterns in ways that won't become apparent for centuries.

They feed on oxidation itself, their metabolism extracting energy from the electron transfer between metal and air. Their feeding grounds are the great ruptures in the pipeline system, where they spread themselves tissue-thin across acres of rusting steel, their bodies becoming translucent membranes that pulse with slow chemical digestion. During these feeding periods, which last years, they are indistinguishable from natural corrosion patterns except for the faint geometric regularity of their spread.

Communication between Keepers occurs through induced oxidation—they leave messages in the pattern of rust on selected surfaces, texts that take decades to write and centuries to fade. Their language has no word for urgency. Their discussions of territory boundaries or mating rights unfold across geological time, arguments begun by grandparents concluded by descendants who have never met.

They reproduce only when three or more gather at the convergence points where the arthropod spirals grow. The process is architectural rather than biological—they collectively secrete a crystalline structure that takes a thousand years to mature, incorporating materials from the arthropod constructions, the decaying infrastructure, and their own shed skin layers. What emerges is not quite offspring but rather a new node of their distributed consciousness, sharing memory and purpose with its creators while developing its own harmonic signatures.

Their memory is mineral. Important events are encoded in deliberately induced stress patterns in the basalt, readable only through direct contact with their sensory channels. Sacred sites exist where the stone has been so thoroughly inscribed that it has become brittle, threatening to shatter under the weight of accumulated history.

They dream during the high-pressure periods when thought becomes impossible. In these dreams, they experience time at the pace of electrons, living entire subjective lifetimes in the space between one oxidation reaction and the next. Some believe these dreams are their true existence, and their waking life is merely the slow process of translating dream-knowledge into the physical world through their patient modifications of the ruins.

The youngest among them—merely a few thousand years old—sometimes experiment with faster modes of being. They've learned to cultivate certain strains of arthropods within their own bodies, creating internal ecosystems that process information at speeds approaching real-time. These hybrid consciousnesses can think and react in mere months rather than decades, but the elders consider this dangerous innovation, a corruption of their essential nature.

Their mythology speaks of the builders not as departed beings but as entities who achieved such perfect stillness that they became indistinguishable from the plateau itself. They believe that consciousness naturally tends toward slower and slower speeds, and that their own thought, sluggish as it seems, is still frantically fast compared to the true awareness that permeates the stone. Their highest aspiration is to one day think so slowly that they sync with the magnetic fluctuations of the planet's core, achieving unity with what they consider the world's deepest mind.

In the places where hydraulic fluid has evolved into micro-ecosystems, the Keepers perform something like agriculture. They carefully regulate the light reaching these pools by adjusting the positioning of metal sheets, influencing the evolution of the organisms within across millennia. Each pool is an experiment in consciousness, an attempt to create new forms of awareness that might bridge the gap between their mineral thought and the even slower cogitation they imagine in the planet itself.

They mark time not by cycles but by completions—the full oxidation of a pipeline section, the total migration of an arthropod colony across the plateau, the formation and collapse of a condensation pattern. Their nearest concept to death is a planned stillness, where an individual chooses a location and simply stops, allowing their body to mineralize completely, becoming a permanent part of the plateau's surface. These chosen sites are selected for their acoustic properties, so that even in stillness, they continue to participate in the vast, slow symphony of resonances that the Keepers have been composing since before the arthropods evolved their current form.

Sometimes, rarely, one will wake from thought to find that dozens of arthropod generations have built structures around and through their resting body. They accept this with the same patience they bring to all things, carefully adjusting their position to accommodate their new symbiotic passengers, becoming architecture for creatures whose entire civilizations rise and fall in the space of a single Keeper thought.
