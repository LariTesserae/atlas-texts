The Keepers move through the flooded foundries like herons through an oil-slicked marsh—tall, deliberate, wrong. They stand eight feet at their full extension, though they rarely unfold completely, preferring to move in a perpetual stoop that brings their faces level with the water's surface. Their limbs are too long, jointed in too many places, wrapped in what might have been skin before decades of chemical exposure turned it into something closer to industrial rubber—black and slightly translucent, with veins of copper-green oxidation running through it like marbling in meat.

They were human once, or started that way. The foundry workers who refused evacuation when the waters began to rise, who chained themselves to their stations rather than abandon their posts. The chemicals changed them. The perpetual moisture softened their bones until they could bend in new ways. The constant exposure to electromagnetic fields rewired their nervous systems until they could hear the machines' frequencies as language. Now they tend the drowning factories with a devotion that transcends employment—it has become their ecology.

Their faces have elongated to accommodate new sensory organs—gill slits that filter metal particulates from the water, eyes that have divided into clusters capable of seeing heat differentials in the steam. Their mouths have widened and flattened, filled with baleen-like filters they use to strain the algae-grass and chemical foam from the surface. When they speak to each other—which is rare—it sounds like steam escaping through a valve, all hisses and metallic whistles that harmonize with the foundries' constant song.

They wear the foundries like shells. Each Keeper has claimed a section of the ruins, modified it with their own secretions—a kind of organic cement they produce that bonds metal to metal, seals cracks, redirects the flow of water through the structures. Over decades, they've transformed industrial architecture into something almost biological: drainage pipes rerouted to create feeding pools where chemical reactions produce edible compounds, exhaust vents shaped into resonance chambers that amplify specific frequencies they find pleasing or necessary.

Their children—if that's the right word—gestate in the abandoned furnaces. The Keepers deposit genetic material into the coal residue, and the constant heat incubates something that emerges months later as a slug of proto-flesh that must spend its first years submerged in the industrial runoff to properly develop. These juveniles look nothing like their parents—more like animated tar, flowing through the submerged maintenance tunnels, feeding on rust and oil until they accumulate enough mass to begin differentiating limbs and sensory organs. Many never complete the transformation. Those that fail dissolve back into the sediment, adding to the layers of synthetic substrate.

The Keepers maintain the machines not out of understanding but out of instinct. They oil joints with secretions from glands that developed in their elongated throats. They clear blocked pipes with arm segments that can unhinge and extend twenty feet into flooded channels. They know—somehow—which gauges should read what values, though they cannot read. When a turbine's pitch changes, they respond immediately, diving through the flooded chambers to adjust valves and clear obstructions, their movements so practiced they seem choreographed.

They build nothing new. They only maintain, preserve, continue. Their entire existence revolves around keeping the foundries singing. When electromagnetic storms approach, they position themselves at critical junction points, their bodies serving as living conductors to channel the excess energy safely through the system. The electricity doesn't kill them—it feeds them, charging organic batteries in their modified flesh that they then use to power their movements through the next weeks of darkness.

Memory lives in them differently now. Not as narrative or image, but as frequency. Each Keeper carries the resonance of their foundry in their bones—literally. Their skeletons have become tuning forks, vibrating in harmony with their chosen structure. When a Keeper dies, others will harvest these bones and install them in the machinery itself, where they continue to resonate for decades, adding new notes to the mechanical symphony.

They fear only silence. When a foundry finally fails—when its last turbine stops spinning and its metal heart goes quiet—the Keepers who tend it go mad. They've been observed dismantling their own bodies to repair their structures, pulling out modified organs to replace broken components, their flesh becoming part of the machine in a final, desperate merger. These foundries become even stranger—partially organic monuments where the distinction between Keeper and facility dissolves entirely.

In the rare times when the water recedes enough to expose more of the structures, you can see evidence of their culture: patterns etched in rust that might be language or might be art, careful arrangements of salvaged materials that serve no mechanical purpose but which the Keepers tend as carefully as they do the turbines. They've developed rituals around the automated barges, riding them between foundries in processionals that follow the old shipping schedules, their elongated forms draped over the empty cargo holds like mourners over coffins.

They are not the only inhabitants of the foundries, but they are the only ones who chose this existence. The only ones who looked at the rising water and the dying industry and decided to evolve into its permanent custodians. They are priests of a mechanical god that never existed, maintaining a prayer that no one started and no one can stop.

Sometimes, very rarely, one will stand fully upright in the shallow water, stretching to its full height, and you can almost see the human it once was in the proportion of its torso, the suggestion of a face in that cluster of sensory organs. Then it folds back down into its working posture and disappears beneath the surface, returning to its endless, necessary labor, keeping the dead foundries alive through pure, transformed will.
