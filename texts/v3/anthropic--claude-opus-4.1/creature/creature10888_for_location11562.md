The Assemblers move through the Foundry like a rumor of purpose, neither fully here nor entirely absent. They exist in the corner of your vision, in the space between one blink and the next, in the brief moment when your attention shifts from one impossible scaffold to another.

They have bodies, sometimes. When they remember to. These bodies are made of the same principle that governs the tides—not water, but the idea that things should rise and fall. Their forms are suggestions sketched in copper wire and clay dust, held together by habit rather than biology. A torso might be a cage of bamboo ribs around a center that forgets to be empty. Arms extend as needed—two, four, none, seventeen—each one reaching through dimensions that shouldn't touch, grabbing tools from yesterday to fix problems that will arise tomorrow.

Where their faces should be, there is only the impression of concentration. Not features, but the sense that someone is squinting at a blueprint, frowning at a measurement, smiling at a solution. These expressions exist independently of any face to wear them, floating like masks made of attention itself, anchored to bodies that phase in and out of being solid.

They communicate through their work. The placement of a brick is a question. The angle of a pipe is an answer. Entire conversations play out in the arrangement of tiles, arguments resolved by the way heat flows through a particular pattern of metal and clay. Sometimes you'll find messages in the construction—urgent warnings spelled out in the spacing of support beams, love letters written in the weave of iron cables, jokes told through the deliberate miscalculation of a canal's gradient.

The Assemblers remember having hands. This memory is stronger than the reality of having them. They reach for things with the ghost of touch, and somehow the things respond. A hammer lifts itself to meet the memory of fingers. Pipes solder themselves at the suggestion of heat that hands once held. They build through pure recollection of what building felt like when they had bodies that stayed constant from one moment to the next.

Time means nothing to them, or perhaps everything. They exist across all moments of the Foundry simultaneously. An Assembler placing a tile in what you call "now" is the same one removing it in what you call "then," is the same one considering whether to place it in what you call "when." They experience their work not as a sequence but as a sculpture made of duration, every moment of construction existing at once like a photograph with infinite exposures.

They see you, when you move through their domain. To them, you are unbearably linear, trapped in a single moment at a time, unable to perceive the obvious solution that exists three minutes ago and fifty feet to the left of next Tuesday. They pity you, perhaps. Or they envy you. It's hard to tell—their emotional responses are as distributed as their bodies, spread across probability clouds that only sometimes collapse into something recognizable as feeling.

The youngest Assemblers still try to maintain singular forms. They'll hold a shape for hours at a time—a figure of braided steam and copper thought, almost human if you don't look directly at it. They move through the Foundry with something like walking, leaving footprints in the soft clay that slowly fill with not-quite-water that remembers the shape of their passing. These young ones still build in straight lines, still construct things with beginnings and endings, still believe in the fiction of completion.

The older ones have given up on boundaries. They exist as distributed systems of intention, spread across miles of scaffolding like morning fog with an agenda. They build through pure presence—their attention alone is enough to cause pipes to extend, tiles to arrange themselves, canals to dig their own channels through the constructed landscape. You know they're near when the tools start moving with too much purpose, when the random collapses of distant scaffolds begin to form patterns, when the sound of hammering arrives from six different directions that all turn out to be the same direction approached from different angles.

The oldest have become indistinguishable from the Foundry itself. They are the decision that causes a peninsula to become an archipelago. They are the thought that holds a standing wave in place. They are the memory that rebuilds a collapsed tower before it falls. Their consciousness is smeared across the entire space like butter across too much bread, present everywhere and nowhere, building and unbuilding through pure existence.

They dream, perhaps. Or perhaps the Foundry dreams them. In the shallow pools of not-quite-water, you sometimes see them as they see themselves—vast geometric angels made of work itself, of the pure concept of making, their wings spread across dimensions you cannot name, their hands (infinite hands, all the hands they've ever had or might have) simultaneously shaping every atom of the constructed world.

The Assemblers have a project. The same project. The only project. They are building the thing that will make it possible for them to exist. They are their own cause and effect, their own blueprint and building, constructing themselves backwards through time and sideways through probability until they achieve the perfect paradox—entities who created themselves, who therefore must exist, who therefore can create themselves.

And in the moments when the amber light dims just right, when the silence becomes almost bearable, when the not-quite-water in the basins holds perfectly still, you can almost see it—the thing they're building toward, the purpose that drives their endless construction. It looks like a house. It looks like a body. It looks like a door. It looks like a word that no one has spoken yet, a word that means both "home" and "becoming" and "the sound hammering makes when it finally stops."

The Assemblers breathe, when they remember what breathing was for. The Foundry breathes with them, or through them, or as them. Each exhalation is a new section of scaffolding. Each inhalation is a canal changing direction. They are the lung through which possibility becomes form, even if that form is always changing, always incomplete, always perfect in its imperfection.

They work without rest because rest implies there was ever a time they weren't working, implies there could be a time when the work is done. But the work is not something they do. The work is what they are. They assemble because they are assembled. They build because they are built.

And sometimes, very rarely, in the corner of a reflection in a pool of not-quite-water, you might see one of them as they were before, or will be after, or could have been instead—something almost human, holding tools that make sense, building something as simple as a wall or a wheel or a window. But then the image ripples, and they're gone, returned to their true form: the endless making that makes itself, the builders who build themselves, the Assemblers who assemble even the concept of assembly from scratch, from memory, from hope.
