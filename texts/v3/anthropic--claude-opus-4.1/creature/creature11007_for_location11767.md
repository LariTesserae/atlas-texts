The Weavers move through their vertical world like living contradictions — too large to be insects, too strange to be anything else. Each stands roughly the height of a deer but built nothing like one: their bodies are segmented tubes of translucent chitin through which you can see fluids pumping in peristaltic waves. Six primary legs, each ending in clusters of finger-like appendages that can splay wide as dinner plates or compress into pointed picks, carry them across the root-mesh floors with disturbing grace. They place each foot with deliberate care, testing the weave's tension before committing their weight.

Their most unsettling feature is the breathing. Rows of spiracles run along their sides — oval vents that open and close in sequences that never quite synchronize, creating a constant whispered wheeze that follows them everywhere. When they exert themselves, these vents flare wide, showing glimpses of feathery internal gills that flutter frantically before snapping shut. The sound of a group of Weavers working together is like wind through a forest of flutes.

They tend the terraces with obsessive precision. Their forward pair of limbs split into countless sub-digits, each tipped with a different biological tool — some secrete growth hormones, others digestive acids, still others produce silk-like binding proteins. You'll see them hunched over junction points where major roots meet, their digit-tools buried deep in the plant matter, making adjustments too subtle for human eyes to follow. They taste everything constantly — their head-segments are mostly mouth, a complex arrangement of palps and chemical sensors that they press against surfaces, sampling the health and intentions of the growth.

The relationship between Weaver and terrace defies easy classification. They're not exactly gardeners — the plants would grow without them. They're not parasites — they give as much as they take. Watching them work, you get the sense of musicians tuning an instrument, making microscopic adjustments that cascade into massive structural changes hours or days later. They'll spend weeks encouraging a single root to grow in a specific direction, then abandon it suddenly when some invisible threshold is reached.

Their colonies — if that's the right word — operate without any apparent hierarchy. Weavers flow past each other on the root-bridges, occasionally pausing to exchange chemical messages through brief touches of their mouth-parts, but they never seem to gather or coordinate in obvious ways. Yet their work shows clear collective intent: they'll simultaneously begin reinforcing platforms that won't show stress for months, or start redirecting water flow patterns across multiple terraces in perfect synchronization.

The young emerge from nodules the adults cultivate in the pillar trees — fist-sized growths that could be mistaken for fruit until they split open and release tiny, translucent versions of the adults. These juveniles spend their first months living inside the root systems themselves, visible only as moving bumps beneath the bark, learning the terrace from within before emerging to work its surface.

They build things beyond the terraces too. Spherical nests of crystallized sap hang in the voids between platforms, connected by guy-wires of silk that vibrate warnings when large creatures approach. Inside these spheres, Weavers undergo some kind of periodic transformation — their shells crack open and they emerge slightly different each time, their tool-digits reconfigured for new purposes, their breathing vents relocated, their chemical signatures altered.

Most disturbing is their reaction to observation. They don't flee or attack — they simply include you in their work. A Weaver might spend hours adjusting the growth around where you're standing, weaving a subtle depression that perfectly fits your seated form, or directing water flow to pool where you typically rest. They read intentions through some chemical or vibrational sense, anticipating needs before they're expressed. Many who stay too long in the terraces report waking to find the roots have grown around them in the night — not trapping, just accommodating, forming perfect supports and shelters they didn't know they wanted.

The eldest Weavers are nearly transparent, their chitin worn thin as paper. These ancients move differently than the others, spending days motionless at critical structural points, their bodies pulsing with the same rhythm as the nutrient flow in the roots beneath them. When they finally do move, entire sections of terrace respond — platforms rotating slowly to track their movement, water flows reversing, new growth erupting in their wake. 

They sing, after a fashion. In the humid quiet of dawn, before the day's heat builds, their breathing vents produce harmonics that resonate through the hollow roots. It's not quite music — more like the terraces themselves are being played as an instrument, with the Weavers as both performers and tuning forks. The sound carries for miles through the green architecture, a deep thrumming that makes the water drops hesitate in midair and causes the bioluminescent waves to pulse in sympathy.

Their dead are never seen. Some say they root themselves, becoming part of the structure they've spent their lives shaping. Others claim they dissolve into the constant water flow, their chemicals absorbed and redistributed through the system. But sometimes, in the oldest sections of the terraces, you'll find roots grown into shapes too deliberate to be natural — spiral staircases sized for their bodies, hollow chambers with perfectly placed grip-holds, viewing galleries that frame distant vistas in ways that feel deeply intentional. Perhaps these are their monuments. Perhaps they're something else.
