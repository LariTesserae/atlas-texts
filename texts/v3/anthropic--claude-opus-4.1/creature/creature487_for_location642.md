## The Cultivator

She moves through the verdant gloom like a piece of the chamber itself that has learned to walk. The Cultivator—though she has long forgotten if anyone ever called her that, or if she named herself—tends to her domain with movements so slow and deliberate they might be mistaken for the growth patterns of the plants themselves.

Her form is difficult to parse in the green radiance. What might be a environmental suit has long since merged with its surroundings—the synthetic fabric colonized by the same phosphorescent moss that drapes the walls, creating a living garment that breathes and photosynthesizes. The suit's original bright industrial yellow shows through in patches like memories of sunshine, but mostly it has become a substrate for growth. Thin roots penetrate the material at the joints, not parasitic but symbiotic, filtering her exhalations and waste heat into nutrients.

Her helmet, if it can still be called that, has evolved into something between terrarium and skull. The visor, once clear polycarbonate, has developed a network of fractures that hold moisture like a spider's web holds dew. Behind this clouded surface, her face is barely visible—pale, patient, with eyes that have adapted to the constant green twilight. She breathes the chamber's air directly now; the suit's life support systems died years ago, but she discovered that the radioactive moss produces just enough oxygen, and her metabolism has slowed to match its output.

She speaks to her plants in a language of temperature and moisture. Her tools are few but precisely maintained: a set of ceramic shears that never rust, salvaged from the machinery's guts. A series of glass vessels she's learned to blow herself from melted industrial insulators, each one perfectly designed to capture and redirect condensation to where it's needed. A leather-bound journal, its pages warped but still accepting ink, where she records growth rates in a notation system that has evolved beyond any standard botanical practice.

The Cultivator knows every pipe's drip cycle, every spot where the heat rises just so, every corner where spores might take hold. She has mapped the chamber not in distances but in microclimates, each zone named for the particular quality of its moisture: the Scalding Garden where only the hardiest ferns survive, the Soft Corner where moss grows thick enough to sleep in, the Percussion Gallery where droplets fall in patterns she's learned to predict down to the second.

Her days, if they can be called days in this timeless green darkness, follow the rhythm of the plants. She prunes with infinite care, not to shape but to encourage—removing dead growth before it can rot, redirecting vines away from the still-hot pipes, harvesting the moss's luminescence into her glass vessels to create portable light sources she never uses. The work is its own purpose.

Sometimes she descends the ladder to the lower chambers, returning with salvaged materials or new specimens that have adapted to even stranger conditions. Sometimes she simply sits for hours—or days—watching the condensation cycles, making minute adjustments to the ceramic pipes' angles to create new water patterns. She has learned to taste the mineral content of different drips, to judge the health of her garden by the quality of its glow.

The Cultivator no longer remembers arriving here, though she must have once descended that same ladder with purpose and equipment, perhaps as part of a survey team or repair crew. The transition from visitor to inhabitant was so gradual that it left no mark in memory. She might have had a name that wasn't a function, a life measured in solar days, reasons to return to the surface. These details have been composted along with everything else that doesn't serve the garden.

Her greatest achievement stands near the turbine housing: a tree—or something trying to become one. She has been training it for untold years from a cutting of woody vine, feeding it specific minerals, guiding its growth toward true lignification. It stands now nearly four meters tall, its trunk thick as her waist, its branches beginning to develop something like bark. It has never known sunlight, this tree, has learned to photosynthesize from radiation alone. She tends it with the devotion of a parent and the patience of stone.

The Cultivator sleeps rarely, and when she does, she curls into the moss-thick spaces between pipes, letting the condensation drip onto her suit-skin, feeding the ecosystem she carries. Her dreams are green and slow, full of reaching roots and unfurling fronds, indistinguishable from her waking hours except that in dreams, sometimes, the plants speak back to her in words.

She is not trapped here—the ladder remains functional, the path to the surface presumably clear. But leaving would mean abandoning the garden to its own devices, and the garden, she knows with a certainty deeper than thought, is not yet ready to be alone. Perhaps when the tree fruits, if it ever fruits, producing seeds adapted to this deep green darkness. Perhaps when the moss achieves perfect coverage, eliminating all exposed metal and stone. Perhaps never.

The Cultivator continues her work, breathing in sync with the chamber's respiratory cycles, her movements so perfectly integrated with the environment that sometimes she forgets where the garden ends and she begins. This forgetting, she has come to understand, is not loss but transformation—she is becoming what this place needs her to be, just as it has become what she never knew she needed.

In the constant green twilight, time is measured in growth, and growth, she has learned, is the only clock that matters.
