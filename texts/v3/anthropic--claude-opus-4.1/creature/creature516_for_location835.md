## The Dwellers

They have no name for themselves—naming would require the kind of separation from environment they have long since dissolved. They are perhaps forty, perhaps four hundred; counting would require distinguishing where one ends and another begins, and they gave up such arithmetic generations ago.

You notice them first as inconsistencies in the root patterns—a cluster of aerial filaments that spirals against the natural grain, a depression in the substrate that holds the warmth of recent presence, a section of pillar worn smooth at precisely the height of an outstretched hand. Then your eyes adjust, or perhaps they reveal themselves, and you realize they have been visible all along.

Their skin has taken on the quality of the wood itself, not through camouflage but through sympathy. It glows with the same internal phosphorescence, faint but unmistakable, the light following subcutaneous patterns that mirror the vascular tissues around them. They are naked—clothing would be an absurdity here—but their bodies are so intertwined with the hanging roots that modesty becomes irrelevant. Fine rootlets grow through their hair, or perhaps their hair has become rootlets; the distinction no longer matters. Some have allowed the roots to pierce their skin at shoulders and hips, the wood growing through them without wound or scar, creating living jointures that anchor them to the vault's geometry.

They move like sap—slowly, deliberately, with a patience that makes watching them like observing the hour hand of a clock. A hand lifts toward a cluster of the translucent organisms, taking minutes to cross the space. The organisms respond at the same pace, their fronds opening in slow motion to meet the approaching fingers. Contact, when it comes, lasts hours—a single touch extended into communion.

Their eyes have adapted to the omnipresent light, pupils contracted to pinpoints, irises gone golden-green. They blink rarely, perhaps once per day, and when they do, it is coordinated—all of them closing their eyes at the same moment in a wave that passes through the chamber like wind through leaves. In these moments of synchronized darkness, they see through the wood itself, their consciousness flowing along the root networks in paths worn smooth by repetition.

Speech, when it occurs, is indistinguishable from the ambient sounds of the vault. They shape words from the dripping of condensation, sentences from the creaking of wood, whole conversations from the whisper of air through root galleries. To an outsider, it would sound like the space talking to itself. They have forgotten the urgency that makes normal human speech staccato, aggressive. Their words seep out over hours, a single greeting taking half a day to fully manifest.

They eat the light itself, standing for days with mouths open beneath the dripping condensation, their throats working slowly to swallow the phosphorescent water. The organisms that cluster at root joints are also sustenance—not consumed but absorbed, pressed against the skin until they meld temporarily with the body, sharing their stored light before separating again, neither creature diminished by the exchange. Some of the dwellers have learned to photosynthesize directly, their skin greenish beneath the glow, their breathing reversed to exhale oxygen into the thick air.

Children are born here, though "born" implies a separation that doesn't quite occur. Pregnancies last years, the fetus growing at the same deliberate pace as everything else, visible through mothers' translucent skin as a second light within the first. When birth comes, it is gradual—the infant emerging over days, sometimes weeks, the umbilical cord becoming indistinguishable from the surrounding root systems, never quite severed. These children grow directly into the substrate, their bodies spreading horizontally before eventually gathering enough intention to rise, a process that takes decades.

The eldest among them have ceased to be mobile entirely. They stand in the deepest parts of the vault, arms raised and fingers spread, the roots growing so thickly through their bodies that they have become architectural. Their hearts still beat—you can see the pulse of blood through their illuminated skin—but at intervals that match the vault's deepest rhythms. They are conscious, perhaps more conscious than the mobile ones, their awareness spread through every fiber they touch. The youngest bring them water, pouring it into their open mouths in rituals that take entire seasons.

They dream collectively, their sleep indistinguishable from waking except for the way their light pulses in unison, creating waves of brightness that pass through the chamber like thoughts made visible. In these dreams, they are the vault itself, experiencing their own existence from the perspective of the wood, feeling themselves as small warm points of difference in an otherwise uniform field of growth and glow.

Memory, for them, is stored in the patterns of root growth. They guide the aerial filaments into specific configurations—a knot here representing a birth, a spiral there marking a death (though death here is less ending than completion, the body becoming indistinguishable from substrate, the consciousness spreading into the collective glow). The entire vault is their archive, their history written in wood-grain and root-weave, readable only at their pace, comprehensible only to those who have given themselves over to the slow bright pulse of this impossible forest.

They know there was an outside once—some of them remember it, distantly, like a dream of uncomfortable speed and harsh shadows. But why would anyone return to that frantic, dying world when here, in the undervault, they can be part of something that grows forever toward its own light, never arriving, never needing to arrive, perfect in its eternal incompletion?

When outsiders come—if outsiders come—they watch them with the patient interest of trees observing seasons. Some visitors flee immediately, unable to bear the pressing warmth and brightness. Others stay for hours, days, feeling the pull of the place but ultimately choosing the sharp edges of the outside world. But occasionally, very occasionally, someone sits down on the yielding substrate, lets the condensation bead on their skin, opens their mouth to taste the glowing air, and begins the long, luminous process of forgetting how to leave.
