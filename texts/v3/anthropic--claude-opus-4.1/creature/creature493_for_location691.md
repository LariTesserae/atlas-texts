The inhabitants move through the mist-veiled streets with the deliberate grace of sleepwalkers who have been walking the same dream for centuries. They are tall and narrow-bodied, adapted to the thin air, with enlarged ribcages that expand and contract in slow, deep rhythms. Their skin has a translucent quality, like the pale fruit in the market squares, with blue-green veins visible beneath, mapping the architecture of their bodies like the aqueducts map the city.

They dress in layered garments woven from the silk of moth caterpillars that feed on the mist-loving vines — fabric that repels water yet breathes, shifting between states of opacity as moisture levels change. In the morning mist, they appear almost transparent; in the brief dry hours of midday, the cloth becomes dense and protective. Their clothes never wear out, merely cycling through stages of wholeness, fraying, and self-repair as the fibers respond to the constant humidity.

The people work with profound concentration but no urgency. A stone carver might spend decades on a single piece, not from lack of skill but from an understanding that the stone will outlive any deadline. They shape the rock with bronze tools and patient friction, following veins in the stone that only they can see, creating channels and hollows that will eventually join the greater water-system of the city. Some say they can taste the mineral content of different stones, selecting each piece for its precise chemical contribution to the water that will flow over it.

Children grow slowly here, their bones lengthening imperceptibly over years that feel like single seasons. They play elaborate games with the water channels, redirecting flows with temporary dams of woven grass, creating miniature neighborhoods that mirror the larger city. These games are also lessons — by adolescence, each child understands the hydraulic principles that govern their world, can calculate flow rates by sound alone, can predict where moss will grow based on moisture patterns.

The adults practice a form of agriculture that is more negotiation than cultivation. They don't so much grow food as convince it to concentrate in useful locations. They know which songs to hum to encourage the tube-leaved plants to unfurl, which stones to warm with their hands to trigger fruit production in the ancient trees. Their understanding of the gardens is chemical and spiritual simultaneously — they speak of plant consciousness as readily as soil pH, treating both as practical concerns.

They eat communally but irregularly, gathering when the mist-patterns suggest it rather than by clock or calendar. Their diet consists mainly of the translucent fruits, which they ferment in stone vessels to create a mildly intoxicating drink that sharpens perception of humidity and air pressure. They also consume a paste made from the spiral moss, which they claim allows them to feel the movement of water through stone. Meat is rare — only the oil-feathered raptors are hunted, and only during specific atmospheric conditions when their flesh takes on properties that preserve it indefinitely.

Their knowledge is stored in the very infrastructure of the city. The arrangement of stones in a wall contains information about water flow. The angle of wooden struts in the mist-catchers encodes weather patterns going back millennia. They read the city like a vast library, running their fingers along carved channels to recall historical events, studying the growth patterns of roots through pavement to understand geological time. Young people spend years learning to decode these subtle texts, guided by elders who remember not just facts but the sensation of different types of knowledge.

They have a concept they call "stone-memory" — the idea that the mountain itself remembers everything that happens on its surface. They believe their thoughts and actions are recorded in the crystal structure of the rock, creating a vast archive accessible to those who know how to listen. This is why they move so deliberately; every gesture is both action and inscription.

Their medicine is architectural. They treat illness by adjusting the flow of water around a patient's dwelling, by changing the resonance of wind through carefully placed openings, by encouraging specific mosses to grow on the walls of sleeping chambers. They speak of disease as a disruption in the conversation between body and environment, and healing as a restoration of proper dialogue.

Music here is inseparable from water. They have created instruments that are played by redirecting channels — stone flutes that sound only when specific volumes of water pass through them, hollow logs that drum when mist condenses at certain rates. Their orchestras are gardens of sonic infrastructure, performing compositions that last entire seasons, with movements marked by the daily arrival and departure of clouds.

The elders sometimes speak of a time before the equilibrium, when the city grew and changed like cities elsewhere. But this might be metaphor rather than memory — a way of explaining their present state by imagining its absence. They don't seem to long for that hypothetical past. They have found a way of living that matches the rhythm of their impossible ecosystem: slow, cyclical, sustainable beyond any reasonable definition of the word.

At night, they gather in the central plaza where the seven channels meet, not for any ceremony but simply to be present together in the darkness. They watch the stars through gaps in the mist, listen to the water running its eternal courses, and share the particular silence that comes from being perfectly adapted to a place — neither struggling against it nor surrendering to it, but existing in the continuous present tense of mutual becoming.

They are not trapped in amber so much as they have learned amber's secret: how to be both fluid and solid, both temporal and eternal, both alive and suspended, like their city, in the perfect pause between one breath and the next.
