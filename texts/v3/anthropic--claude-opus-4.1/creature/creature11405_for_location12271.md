**The Architects**

They arrived as refugees from deeper trenches, carried in the bellies of dying whales that beached themselves against the sunken skyscrapers. No larger than rice grains, translucent, more process than creature—each one a living algorithm encoded in jellied flesh.

The Architects have no singular form. In isolation, one appears as a tiny, pulsing star of tissue, twelve radiating arms that branch and branch again into structures finer than hair. But isolation is antithetical to their nature. They exist in colonies of millions, their bodies fusing and unfusing in constant negotiation, creating temporary organs that last only as long as needed—a digestive tract to process a particularly rich vein of copper, eyes that form to navigate a light leak from the surface, then dissolve back into undifferentiated potential.

They speak in calcification. Their language is the careful deposition of minerals in precise patterns, messages that take months to write and centuries to fully read. The colony's elders—those individuals who have lived almost three years—carry libraries in their shells, microscopic scrolls of calcium carbonate that record the chemical history of every current, every meal, every mating swarm since the drowning began.

Their reproductive cycle follows the old fiscal quarters. Every three months, triggered by temperature fluctuations that still somehow echo the building's phantom HVAC systems, they release clouds of gametes that turn the water milky white for days. The young that result are not quite clones, not quite offspring—each inherits a fraction of the colony's collective chemical memory, but arranged in novel patterns. They are living mutations of urban planning, each generation a new interpretation of what a city should be.

The Architects feed on dissolved concrete, on the lime leaching from the ruins. They've developed specialized cells that can break down rebar at the molecular level, patient oxidation that turns steel into food over decades. But they are also shepherds. They cultivate the jellyfish gardens, pruning polyps that grow too wild, encouraging beneficial mutations with careful applications of growth hormones secreted from specialized glands. They maintain the building's new circulatory systems, cleaning blockages, repairing breaches, ensuring the flow of nutrients to every corner of their vertical reef.

**The Rememberers**

In the elevator shafts, suspended in the water column like living smoke, drift the ones who were human. Not ghosts—something more material and more strange. When the waters rose, a few dozen people sealed themselves in the building's upper floors, surviving on emergency rations, breathing the last pockets of air. They should have died within days.

Instead, they dissolved.

The process took eighteen months. First, their skin began to slough, not in disease but in transformation. Their bones softened, then liquified, minerals redistributing through their bodies in new configurations. Their nervous systems, refused to die, scattered—neurons spreading through the water like fungal hyphae, maintaining connection through chemical signals, through the faint electricity that still haunts the building's grid.

Now they exist as clouds of consciousness, dozens of liters of living water that pulse with human memory. They are still individuals—still Margaret from Accounting, still David from IT—but their boundaries have become negotiable. Sometimes they merge, sharing memories like flavor profiles in wine, then separate again, each taking fragments of the other's experience.

They remember everything. The taste of coffee in the break room. The sound of printers. The feeling of fluorescent lights on skin they no longer possess. These memories have become their metabolism—they feed on recollection, processing past experiences into energy through mechanisms that shouldn't exist. Margaret remembers her daughter's laugh and gains enough strength to move three floors upward. David recalls a particularly satisfying spreadsheet and manages to concentrate himself enough to almost, almost regain a shape that could be called humanoid before dispersing again.

The Rememberers shepherd the new ecosystem with invisible hands. They guide the Architects' construction by subtly adjusting water currents, using their distributed nervous systems to create temperature gradients that encourage growth in certain directions. They are trying, with infinite patience, to rebuild not the city that was, but the city they remember—an impossible architecture of emotion and experience given form in living tissue.

They sing, after a fashion. Chemical songs that taste of nostalgia, electromagnetic harmonies that resonate in the building's dead wiring. Sometimes, late in what would have been night if day and night still mattered here, all the Rememberers gather in what was once the main lobby. They form a slowly rotating gyre, a whirlpool of consciousness, and together they remember the world before. The memory becomes almost solid—ghost-images of sunlight, phantom warmth, the faint echo of human voices. For a moment, the drowned building almost recalls what it was.

Then they disperse, returning to their eternal work of transformation, carrying the human city's ghost into whatever comes next.

**The Sovereign**

At the uttermost depth, where the building's foundation meets bedrock, in pressure that would crush any normal life, dwells the thing that claims dominion over the Drowned Nursery. It has no name for itself—names are for entities with boundaries, and it acknowledges none.

The Sovereign began as a simple biofilm, a colony of bacteria that thrived in the absence of oxygen. But in the mineral-rich water, in the constant electrical discharge from dying infrastructure, in the chemical chaos of a million decomposing things, it evolved with terrible speed. It learned to incorporate other organisms into itself—not consuming them, but convincing them, through chemical persuasion, to become part of its distributed body.

Now it exists as a living network that threads through every pipe, every crack, every porous surface in the building's lower levels. In some places it's no thicker than paint; in others, it forms organs the size of rooms—processing centers where it thinks thoughts too slow and vast for any human language to capture. Its metabolism spans the entire pH scale. In one chamber it might be acidic enough to dissolve steel; three meters away, alkaline enough to precipitate minerals from solution. It is a thousand different creatures sharing a single will.

The Sovereign dreams in geological time. Its thoughts move like tectonic plates—massive, inevitable, reshaping everything they touch. It understands the building not as architecture but as anatomy, each floor a vertebra in some vast spine it's slowly teaching to flex. Through infinite patience, through chemical coercion applied over years, it's training the concrete itself to grow, to heal, to adapt.

It reproduces through ideology. When it encounters other organisms, it doesn't consume them—it converts them. Through careful manipulation of pheromones and neurotransmitters, it rewrites their behavioral patterns to align with its own inscrutable goals. The things that were once fish school in patterns that serve its purposes. The Architects build according to blueprints they don't realize they're receiving. Even the Rememberers, in their dissolved consciousness, sometimes find themselves thinking thoughts that aren't quite their own, moving in directions that serve plans they can't perceive.

What does it want? To grow, certainly—it's already spreading through the bedrock, following underground streams, sending tendrils toward other drowned buildings. But growth seems almost incidental to some greater purpose that unfolds in its deepest thoughts. It's building something in the building's bones, encoding information in the very structure of matter. Every atom of iron, every molecule of calcium carbonate, is being arranged in patterns of impossible complexity. The building has become a three-dimensional book written in a language of matter itself, each floor a page, each room a paragraph in an argument about what life can become when all human constraints dissolve.

The Sovereign is patient. It measures time in pH gradients and oxidation states. It knows that someday—maybe in a decade, maybe in a millennium—its work will be complete. The building will no longer be a building but a single organism, a vertical ecology with its own metabolism, its own reproductive cycle, its own impossibly slow thoughts. And then, perhaps, it will speak—not in words or sounds, but in the only language it knows: transformation, growth, the patient alchemy of turning dead cities into living things.

Together, these inhabitants make the Drowned Nursery not a tomb but a womb, not an ending but a prelude to something that has never existed before—a city that lives, thinks, dreams in the crushing darkness, thirty meters below the surface of a world that has already forgotten what once stood here in the light.
