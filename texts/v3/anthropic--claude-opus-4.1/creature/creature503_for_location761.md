The people of the Weeping Shelves are translucent at the edges.

It begins in childhood—their fingernails clouding like the living glass they harvest, their earlobes gaining a quality of filtered light. By middle age, their peripheral features have taken on the suggestion of water, as if they are always slightly dissolving into the perpetual moisture that surrounds them. The eldest are nearly invisible in profile, existing fully only when viewed directly, their boundaries negotiable with the wet air.

They call themselves the Maintenance, not as a role but as an identity. Each person is born into a specific relationship with erosion—some are Channelers, whose bones resonate with the frequency of running water, able to sense where the flow will undermine foundations days before it happens. Others are Sealers, their saliva hardening into a resin that bonds wet stone to wet stone. The Readers study the patterns in the living glass films, finding in their growth predictions of the settlement's rearrangements, though their interpretations are never quite right, always adjacent to what actually occurs.

Their children are amphibious in temperament. They play in the vertical pools, holding their breath for impossible lengths, their lungs developing secondary chambers that store air like the lungfish they swim beside. These children age in stutters—remaining unchanged for years, then transforming overnight, their growth spurts coinciding with the settlement's mysterious reconfigurations. Some children are discovered to have been born twice, once in the real city and once in its reflection, living parallel childhoods that occasionally intersect. These double-born serve as messengers between states of being, carrying questions down and answers up, though neither quite match.

The adults work in shifts that follow no solar pattern but rather the rhythm of water damage. They sleep in rotation, someone always awake to tend the constant maintenance, and their dreams are communal—what one dreams appears as moisture on another's walls, stories told in condensation patterns that must be quickly channeled away before they undermine the structure. They have evolved beyond the need for privacy, their lives flowing into each other like converging streams.

Their medicine is architectural. Illness is treated by relocating the patient to specific terraces where the water runs differently, where the reflections create beneficial interference patterns in the body's fluids. Broken bones are set in carved channels that direct the flow of healing. The coral-things are cultivated in the blood of the injured, creating symbiotic colonies that prevent infection by consuming it. The salamander antifreeze, when properly prepared, doesn't extend life but rather spreads it out—allowing someone to experience their remaining years all at once or parsed out in careful doses over centuries.

They worship through maintenance, their religion indistinguishable from their constant battle against erosion. Prayer is the careful rechanneling of water. Meditation is the patient scraping of living glass. Their holy texts are written in the patterns of temporary streams, read quickly before they change, each reading different as the water finds new paths. They believe the pool at the city's heart is not a thing but a process—that they themselves are part of the pool's extended metabolism, their civilization a temporary crystallization of its dreams.

The Maintenance speak in liquids. Their language includes not just words but the sound of water over stone, the click of salamander scales, the subsonic hymn of feeding coral. Emphasis is provided by directing water while speaking—a cupped palm channeling flow adds urgency, water allowed to sheet down the speaker's face indicates deep truth. They have seventeen different words for erosion, each describing a specific relationship between water and stone, and their poetry consists entirely of technical descriptions of drainage that somehow convey profound emotion.

They couple fluidly, partnerships forming and dissolving like converging streams, children raised by whoever is upstream at the moment. Their marriages are celebrated by carving new channels that unite previously separate flows, their divorces by the careful construction of division walls that part the water without stopping it. Love is expressed through collaborative maintenance, the ultimate intimacy being to work together on the same erosion problem, four hands rechanneling the same stubborn stream.

The old ones are preparing for submersion. As they grow more translucent, they spend longer periods in the pool at the city's heart, learning to breathe the thick water, to see in its dimensions. They report that there are others already there—the previously submerged, possibly the yet-to-be-born, all engaged in some vast maintenance project whose purpose remains unclear but whose urgency is absolute. They say the city extends downward as far as it climbs up, that what looks like a pool from above is, from below, another sky, bright with the reflection of some other sun.

When the Maintenance die, their bodies are placed in the drainage channels, where they quickly erode into mineral films that strengthen the stone they spent their lives protecting. Their names are carved into the water itself, spoken by the flow, echoed in every temporary stream. The living glass grows thickest where they dissolved, and these patches are never harvested, becoming windows into something that isn't quite memory but isn't quite forgetting either.

They measure wealth in dryness—the rich can afford moments without moisture, chambers sealed temporarily against the flow, though these invariably fail and must be rebuilt. Poverty is perfect wetness, the inability to ever escape the constant seep. Yet the poorest often become the most powerful Channelers, their constant saturation granting them perfect sympathy with the water's will.

Their art is entirely functional—beauty exists only in efficient drainage, in the perfect curve of a channel that speeds flow without causing erosion. Their music is the careful tuning of gutters to harmonize with the natural frequency of falling water. Their dance is the coordinated movement of maintenance crews, bodies flowing around each other in the narrow spaces, never colliding despite the slick stones and restricted passages.

The young ones dream of descending, of leaving the Shelves for the dry lands below, but those who go invariably return, their bodies unable to function without the constant moisture, their minds unable to process solid ground that doesn't rearrange itself, their eyes forever seeking reflections that refuse to appear in the dim, stable light of lower altitudes. They return with a kind of grateful exhaustion, taking up their hammers and scrapers with the relief of those who have learned that maintenance is not a burden but a form of breathing.

At night, when the reflections dim and the ghost city becomes harder to distinguish from the real, the Maintenance gather in their tight quarters and listen to the water fall. In these moments, they claim, you can hear the city thinking, planning its next reconfiguration, deciding which buildings to move, which terraces to add, which of its inhabitants to translate into reflection. They listen with the patience of those who know they are not separate from what they observe, that they are themselves part of the city's slow, liquid thought, maintaining it as it maintains them, each erosion a small word in an ancient conversation between stone and water, structure and flow, the permanent and the perpetually transitioning.

The salamanders, they say, are the only ones who truly understand, their crystalline antifreeze scales clicking out messages in a code the Maintenance are still learning to decipher, their copper bodies navigating between dimensions with casual expertise. The children follow them through the vertical maze, learning secret passages that exist only when wet, shortcuts through reflection that lead to yesterday's water or tomorrow's erosion.

This is how they live: translucent, patient, forever building what dissolves, forever dissolving what builds, their civilization a careful balance between creation and entropy, their bodies slowly becoming indistinguishable from the water they channel, their thoughts flowing in patterns that mirror the drainage they maintain, each generation slightly more liquid than the last, approaching some ultimate confluence where the distinction between inhabitant and habitat finally dissolves, where the Weeping Shelves reveal themselves to be not a place where people live but a process by which the mountain dreams of motion, and the people are simply the part of the mountain that has learned to dream back.
