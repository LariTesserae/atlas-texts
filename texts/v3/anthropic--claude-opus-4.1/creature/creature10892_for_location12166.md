The inhabitants move through the fungal metropolis like living nerves through a brain—necessary, integrated, inseparable from the city that birthed them.

The **Shepherds** came first, or perhaps the city grew them first. They stand twice human height but weigh almost nothing, their bodies a lattice of hardened mycelium wrapped in translucent skin that shows the constant movement of fluids beneath—nutrients pumping from their root-feet to their crown of sensing filaments. They cannot truly walk; instead, they replant themselves with each step, their toe-roots briefly fusing with the substrate to exchange chemical messages before lifting free with a soft, wet sound. Their faces, if they can be called faces, are clusters of sensory organs arranged radially around a mouth that never closes, constantly tasting the air's chemical signals. They tend districts of the city like gardeners, but their tools are their own bodies—secreting selective toxins from their palms to prune aggressive growths, exhaling specific pheromones to encourage certain architectural developments, bleeding nutrient-rich sap from self-inflicted wounds to feed struggling sections.

Between the Shepherds move the **Memorists**, creatures of accumulated knowledge. Each began as something like the flying moth-jellyfish but refused metamorphosis, instead growing larger, heavier, more complex with each year. Their bodies are living libraries—every spore they've ever consumed remains alive within specialized chambers in their flesh, a genetic archive they can access by internal digestion and regurgitation. The oldest have grown too heavy to fly, dragging themselves through the understory on modified wings that have become like hands. Their skin is a map of their experiences, developing unique patterns of spots and whorls for every new species they catalog. They speak to each other in spore-language, puffing out complex chemical messages that younger Memorists scramble to decode and absorb.

The **Hollowers** work in the depths. They are colonial organisms, appearing first as an innocuous slime spreading across a dying fungal trunk. But the slime is merely their dispersal phase. When enough individuals converge, they assemble into something like a serpent made of living liquid, able to compress and extend themselves through the tiniest gaps in the fungal mass. They digest from the inside out, creating the negative spaces that prevent the city from choking on its own growth—tunnels, chambers, vaults. Their excavations follow mathematical principles no one else understands, possibly not even them. Sometimes they hollow out perfect spheres, sometimes spiraling tubes that seem to follow the path of invisible forces. In their wake, new species always emerge, as if the spaces they create call forth specific evolutionary responses.

Above, in the canopy between the great mushroom caps, live the **Weavers**. They exist as clouds until they need to be otherwise—dispersed colonies of thread-thin organisms that can coalesce into temporary bodies when work requires it. They build the city's only non-fungal structures: nets of crystallized proteins that catch spore-storms, bridges of hardened saliva connecting distant mushroom caps, sometimes entire aerial neighborhoods suspended in geometric webs. When threatened or simply done with their current form, they dissolve back into component threads and drift away. Their intelligence is distributed—no single thread thinks, but together they demonstrate complex planning. They seem to predict growth patterns weeks in advance, building structures where they'll be needed before the need arrives.

The **Cultivators** might be the city's most alien residents. They wear bodies like clothing—mobile colonies of specialized fungi that have learned to puppet dead beetle shells, hollow log segments, even the evacuated skins of failed Shepherds. Inside each animated shell is something between liquid and gas, a conscious suspension of enzymes and acids that can dissolve and reconstitute organic matter at will. They farm specific decay patterns, creating elaborate gardens of decomposition that follow aesthetic principles incomprehensible to outside observers. Their gardens often become permanent features of the city—spiral patterns of die-back that create natural roads, or carefully orchestrated cascades of decay that generate specific temperature gradients other organisms depend on.

In the deepest, hottest cores where decomposition runs so fierce the substrate glows with bioluminescent bacteria, the **Thermal Poets** make their homes. They have no fixed form, existing as mobile columns of superheated air given substance by the spores they carry. They can only be seen clearly when they move through cooler areas, their heat creating visible distortion, their spore-body briefly condensing into almost-solid form. They write their poetry in temperature—creating complex thermal narratives across districts that other inhabitants read through heat-sensitive organs. Their poems might be warnings, might be histories, might be prayers to the mathematical principles that govern fungal growth. Sometimes they gather in groups, their combined heat causing local weather—spore-rain, fungal lightning made of chain-reacting bioluminescence.

The **Archivists** live in the spaces between spaces. They have evolved to be perfectly flat, able to slip between pressed fungal bodies like living sheets of paper. Their bodies are transparent except for dark veins that form constantly shifting written languages—they communicate by pressing against each other and letting their vein-scripts interact. They record everything: growth rates, chemical compositions, the brief lives of individual spores. But their recording changes what they observe—their presence between fungal bodies alters growth patterns, their chemical secretions influence evolution. They are historians whose writing of history creates new futures.

Youngest of all the inhabitants are the **Dreamers**, who might not be separate organisms at all but rather a phase that any creature here can enter. When the spore-density reaches certain critical thresholds, when the chemical conversation becomes too complex for normal consciousness to process, inhabitants sometimes root themselves and begin to fruit. Their bodies sprout temporary fungal growths that interface directly with the city's vast mycelial network. In this state, they experience the city's distributed consciousness—if it can be called consciousness. They see/feel/taste the totality of the endless growth and decay, the chemical communications spanning horizons, the deep patterns that emerge from mindless rules followed perfectly. Most who enter this state never fully return. They remain rooted, half-creature, half-city, providing translation between the quick thought of mobile life and the vast slow cognition of the fungal mass.

These inhabitants don't build in the city—they compose it, each species a different instrument in an orchestra that plays a silent symphony of growth and transformation. They are not separate from their environment but expressions of it, as much grown as born, as much place as people. Their interactions create the city's metabolism: the Shepherds directing growth, the Hollowers creating space for it, the Weavers connecting disparate parts, the Cultivators preparing the ground for what comes next.

They reproduce in ways that blur the line between birth and architecture. A dying Shepherd becomes a small district. Memorists divide by literally splitting their archives, each half regenerating into a new whole but with different knowledge. Thermal Poets leave heat-shadows that sometimes spontaneously ignite into new beings. The city builds its inhabitants as surely as they build it.

Their society, if it can be called that, operates on consensus achieved through chemical democracy. Major decisions—whether to allow a new species to establish, whether to resist or embrace a spore-storm, whether to hollow out new districts—are voted on through pheromone release. The process takes days, sometimes weeks, as chemical signals propagate through the substrate, are modified by each participant, and eventually reach something like agreement. There are no leaders, only temporary aggregations of influence when particular expertise is needed.

They mark time not by days or seasons but by growth cycles—the life span of the great mushroom caps, the frequency of spore-storms, the rare moments when the entire city fruits simultaneously in a coordinated release that darkens the sky for miles. Their art is indistinguishable from their agriculture—carefully cultivated patches of bioluminescent fungi that pulse in complex patterns, acoustic gardens where the placement of hollow stems creates wind instruments that play in the faint breezes, scent symphonies that unfold over months as different species bloom in sequence.

Death here is not an ending but a transformation. Bodies are processed back into the substrate within hours, their nutrients redistributed, their genetic material incorporated into the next generation of growth. Some inhabitants plan their deaths like others might plan buildings—choosing specific locations and times to maximize their contribution to the city's architecture. The Cultivators in particular have elevated this to an art form, their deaths creating foundation-patterns for entirely new districts.

They dream collectively. When the spore-density peaks at night (for even here, circadian rhythms persist), electrical activity in the mycelial network increases. The inhabitants, all connected to this network through root-feet or interface organs, share visions of possible futures—architectural innovations that haven't been tried, new species that don't yet exist, sometimes glimpses of other cities like this one growing in distant places, their spore-signals having traveled impossible distances.

This is their world: a place where identity is fluid, where individual and environment merge, where consciousness might be a property of architecture rather than organisms. They are the city thinking about itself, building itself, transforming itself. They are what happens when decay becomes so sophisticated it loops back around to creation, when death becomes so elaborate it becomes indistinguishable from life.
