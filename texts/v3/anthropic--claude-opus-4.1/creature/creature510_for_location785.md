In the makeshift shelter of fallen anchor fragments, the Chronicler maintains her vigil.

She is difficult to perceive completely—not because she phases like the arthropods, but because she exists in too many moments simultaneously. Her form stretches across temporal states: sometimes she appears as a child of perhaps nine years, translucent skin showing the mineral veins beneath like a living geological map; sometimes as an ancient thing, crystalline deposits forming elaborate ridges along her spine and skull; most often as something between, caught in perpetual transformation.

Her hands never stop moving. They trace the grooves in the stone—not carving them but reading them, feeling the future accumulation of minerals that will fill these channels. She tastes the deposits with fingertips that branch into too many joints, each one sensitive to a different temporal gradient. The patterns she reads are both history and prophecy: they describe the plateau's birth from below and its eventual subsidence, the full lifecycle of stone compressed into symbols that exist in multiple tenses simultaneously.

The Chronicler doesn't need eyes in the conventional sense. Her sight organs—if that's what they are—resemble the compound structures of the phase-creatures but filled with condensation that never quite settles. Through these, she watches the shadows in the fog of buildings that will stand here, the ghost-impressions of a city that might exist in a hundred years or might have existed a hundred years ago. The temporal anchors make such distinctions largely academic.

She speaks to the arthropods in their percussion language, mimicking their clicks with joints that dislocate and relocate in rhythm. They bring her things: fragments of frozen cloud, yes, but also paradoxical objects—seeds that sprout backward into themselves, stones that remember being lava, pieces of crystallized time that she carefully catalogues in the pools around her shelter. Each pool serves as a different temporal storage, maintaining its contents at specific points in their existence. In one, a clutch of arthropod eggs perpetually hatches and unhatches. In another, the same water has been falling as rain for three weeks.

Her relationship with the anchors is symbiotic and complex. She tends them like a gardener, clearing away the lichen that would otherwise crystallize and clog their reality-bending properties. In return, they allow her to exist in her multiplicitous state—without their influence, she would collapse into a single timeline and either age to dust in seconds or regress to pre-existence. She knows each anchor's resonance, can feel when one begins to sink too quickly or when another's hum shifts toward frequencies that would shatter the plateau.

The Chronicler feeds on temporal differentials. Where the upward rain meets the fog, where the phase-creatures flicker between states, where the mineral deposits branch in their lightning patterns—these discontinuities sustain her. She positions herself at the convergence points, absorbing the energy released when different timestreams collide. During the plateau's most active periods, when the stone below pushes up more aggressively and the anchors strain to maintain equilibrium, she glows with excess chronoenergy, her child-form and ancient-form superimposed so completely that she becomes a living lens through which past and future can be viewed simultaneously.

She is building something, though whether it's physical or conceptual remains unclear. The patterns in the stone aren't just recordings—they're instructions, possibly for the plateau itself. She's teaching the rock how to remember, creating a kind of geological consciousness that will persist even after the anchors finally sink too deep to function. The phase-creatures are part of this project, their clicking communications encoding information into the very moisture cycles of the plateau. Every droplet of condensation carries data; every upward rainfall is a backup system.

When the fog thickens enough to block all visibility, the Chronicler sings—a subsonic drone that harmonizes with the anchors' humming. The song describes the city she sees in the mist-shadows: its towers of crystallized vapor, its inhabitants who phase in and out like the arthropods, its foundation of conscious stone that remembers every footstep. She sings it into being, or perhaps she sings because it already exists, will exist, has always existed in the temporal eddies around the anchors.

Occasionally, others arrive at the plateau—explorers, lost travelers, things that might once have been human. The Chronicler greets them in whatever temporal state they can perceive, sometimes appearing to them as a child who speaks in future tense, sometimes as an ancient oracle whose words arrive before she opens her mouth. She offers them shelter in her arrangement of anchor fragments, where they can rest in the single dry circle where time moves normally. Most leave quickly, disturbed by the way their reflections in the pools show different ages. Some stay longer, helping her tend the anchors or learning to read the mineral patterns. A very few become like her, stretched across timelines, adding their own voices to the subsonic song.

The Chronicler knows the plateau's lifecycle intimately. She knows that in thirty years or three hundred (the anchors make such measurements fluid), the stone will cool completely and crack, that the thermal energy driving the whole system will dissipate. But she also knows that time moves differently here. Those thirty or three hundred years might be experienced as millennia, or as moments. She is both preparing for that end and preventing it, creating a temporal loop that will preserve the plateau in its moment of becoming.

She is its memory and its prophet, its gardener and its harvest, as multiple and singular as the place itself.
