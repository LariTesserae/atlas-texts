The Veil Shepherds move through the mist zones like living barometers, their bodies a map of the plateau's infinite gradients. They stand seven feet when fully extended, though they rarely achieve such straightness—their spines have developed additional cartilaginous joints that allow them to fold and unfold according to the density of vapor they're navigating. Their skin changes texture rather than color: smooth as wet stone in the heavy mist, rough as tree bark in the clearer zones, always adjusting its porosity to the moisture content of their immediate surroundings.

They wear the plateau's ecology as clothing. Mosses grow directly on their shoulders and backs—cultivated gardens that they tend with the patience of centuries. These are not parasites but partners: the moss processes toxins from their skin while providing camouflage and moisture regulation. Some elders have encouraged full fern fronds to root in the thick calluses of their shoulder blades, carrying their own small forests as they move. The roots don't penetrate to blood—the Shepherds' outer dermis has evolved into something like cork, a living substrate.

Their fingers have lengthened into sensing instruments, twice the length of human proportions, with additional joints that let them probe deep into the crevices where the plateau hides its secrets. The fingertips are crowded with chemoreceptors that can taste the mineral content of steam, determine the age of water, identify which bacterial colonies are active far below by the specific cocktail of gases they produce. They read the plateau's health through touch, like taking its pulse at a thousand points.

The Shepherds don't exactly speak—the moisture would muffle words anyway. Instead, they've developed a language of controlled exhalations that harmonizes with the tower pipes. They breathe meaning into the steam, their enlarged lung capacity allowing them to sustain notes that travel through the vapor channels. A conversation might take hours, each participant adding layers to a slowly building chord that communicates through resonance and overtone. Young Shepherds learn to read the tremors in spider webs and the patterns in dripping water—nature's punctuation to their vapor speech.

They've evolved a nictitating membrane—not for underwater vision but for seeing through steam. The membrane secretes a thin film that repels condensation while somehow filtering the visible spectrum to detect heat gradients and pressure differentials. Through these adapted eyes, they see the plateau as a living hydraulic system, every steam plume and water trail part of a vast circulation they've spent generations learning to read and tend.

Their work is maintenance, though of what exactly remains ambiguous even to them. They clear blocked channels with their long fingers, ensuring the steam flows properly. They cultivate certain bacterial colonies while discouraging others, using secretions from specialized glands to adjust the pH of specific pools. They know which towers must sing and which must remain silent, though they couldn't explain why—only that the patterns were taught to them by the previous generation, who learned from the generation before, stretching back to when the first Shepherds realized the plateau was both alive and artificial.

The Shepherds live in hollows they've worn into the stone towers through generations of occupation—not carved, but dissolved slowly through acidic secretions they can control. Their homes spiral up the interior of the columns, following the thermal currents. They sleep standing, pressed against walls fuzzy with moss, their breathing synchronized with the plateau's exhalations. Families share the same tower for centuries, their presence becoming part of its acoustic signature.

They feed on the plateau's abundance—fruits from the vertical gardens they know how to cultivate, insects they farm in the moss colonies, but primarily on a paste they make from certain bacteria they cultivate in specialized pools. The paste tastes of minerals and electricity, providing everything their altered metabolism requires. They supplement this with condensation collected in their clavicle hollows—shallow bowls formed where collar bones should be—which they've learned to mineralize with specific trace elements through selective breathing near certain vents.

Children are rare and precious, born with translucent skin that gradually opacifies as they age. For their first years, you can see their organs working—hearts visible through pearl-thin chest walls, thoughts flickering as electrical patterns across their scalp. They mature slowly, taking thirty years to reach adult size, spending decades learning to read the plateau's moods, its seasonal shifts that occur on geological timescales, its rare but devastating convulsions when the deep machines remember some ancient trauma.

The eldest Shepherds have begun to petrify at their extremities—fingers and toes turning to something between flesh and stone, still living but operating on mineral time. They move less but sense more, becoming like permanent sensors in the plateau's network. The very oldest have rooted entirely, their bodies becoming new towers, their consciousness dispersed through the bacterial networks they once tended, still whispering through the steam but no longer quite separate from the system they maintain.

They know something is approaching—some shift in the deep machines, some awakening in the bacterial colonies that hasn't happened in their recorded memory. The steam patterns have changed subtly, new harmonics appearing in the tower songs. The young Shepherds have begun to dream the same dream: of the plateau opening like a flower, of something vast and patient finally completing its millennia-long gestation.

But for now, they continue their work, moving through the mist like ghosts of an older world, ensuring that whatever the plateau is becoming, it does so gently, does so correctly, does so according to the patterns they've inherited and preserved in their blood, their breath, their slowly petrifying bones.
