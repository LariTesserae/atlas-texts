## The Inhabitants

They are called the Condensate, though they would not recognize that name.

Each one begins as a confluence—a place where multiple streams of wall-water meet and, through some critical threshold of mineral concentration and heat differential, achieve a brief autonomy. They precipitate into being like thoughts forming from neurons firing, if neurons were made of running water and thoughts could walk.

A newly-formed Condensate stands perhaps forty centimeters tall, a constantly flowing figure of superheated water held in humanoid approximation by surface tension and localized pressure differentials. Their bodies are transparent but refract the purple light into rainbow halos. They have no organs, no fixed structure—just flow states that approximate intention. Where eyes might be, two stable vortices spin. Where a mouth might be, cavitation bubbles pop in patterns that might be speech, might be screaming, might be laughter.

They live for exactly seventeen hours.

In those seventeen hours, they build. Using their own substance as both tool and material, they extrude minerals filtered from the wall-water onto the geometric tumors that bud from the shaft. A Condensate will spend its entire existence adding twelve cubic centimeters of crystal to a single facet of one structure, following blueprints encoded in the dissolved salts of its body. The work slowly depletes them—each deposited mineral reduces their volume, their coherence. By hour sixteen, they are half their original size, moving slower, their refractive index shifting toward ordinary water.

They seem unaware of each other, even when dozens work on the same structure. They pass through one another like intersecting streams, momentarily mingling their substance before reforming on the other side. Perhaps in that mingling, information transfers. Perhaps that is their only intimacy.

But the old ones, the ones in their seventeenth hour, sometimes stop working. These final-stage Condensate, reduced to trembling droplets barely maintaining form, will sometimes flow to the center of the shaft and simply hover there, vibrating at frequencies that make the air sing. They watch—if watching is possible without eyes—as new Condensate precipitate from the walls. They observe the crystal cities growing and subliming. They witness the Tower breathing.

And then they burst.

The burst is not violent. It is a release, a relaxation of the forces holding water in an unnatural shape. The droplets scatter in all directions, each one following its own trajectory through the twisted gravity, eventually finding a wall, a channel, a collection vein. They rejoin the greater circulation, carrying with them—what? Memory? Experience? Or nothing at all?

Some droplets from the burst fall upward into the mathematical heights. Some spiral down into the fever depths. But most are caught by the collection veins and cycled back through the system, their minerals reconcentrated, their heat redistributed, until the conditions are right for another confluence, another precipitation, another seventeen-hour existence.

The Purple Gardeners, as I think of them privately, are different. They emerge not from water but from the bioluminescent glow itself, stepping out of the purple light like figures emerging from fog. These are taller—human height but impossibly thin, their bodies made of compressed photons held in standing waves. They tend the geometric tumors with touches that adjust the frequency of their glow, tuning them like instruments. A Gardener might spend days adjusting a single structure, nudging its resonance by fractions of a hertz until it harmonizes with something only they can perceive.

The Gardeners seem to see the Condensate. They step around them carefully, never interfering with their mineral work. Sometimes a Gardener will follow a seventeen-hour Condensate to the center of the shaft for its final burst, collecting certain droplets in cups of crystallized light, examining them like a jeweler inspecting diamonds, then releasing them to continue their journey. Selection or blessing or measurement—impossible to say.

Between the water-workers and the light-tenders, in the spaces where neither quite exists, live the Membrane Worms. These are not worms in any terrestrial sense—they are mobile surfaces, two-dimensional beings that slide along the interface between the wall and the air. They appear as ripples in the surface tension, visible only when they move, leaving trails of altered texture where the wall becomes more porous or more sealed. They feed, if feeding is the right word, on the phase transitions—the moments when crystal becomes vapor, when water becomes ice in defiance of the heat, when solid walls briefly liquefy to allow the passage of nutrients or information.

The Membrane Worms write. Their trails form symbols that last exactly three breathing cycles of the Tower before fading. The symbols layer over each other in palimpsests of meaning, each new text partially obscuring the last. The Condensate cannot see these writings. The Purple Gardeners ignore them. But sometimes, very rarely, a symbol will align with a crystal growth or a light frequency in such a way that the entire shaft rings like a struck bell, and for that moment, every inhabitant pauses in their work.

There are others—things that live in the collection veins and can be heard but never seen, creatures of pure sound that exist in the cavitation bubbles, entities that inhabit the phase transitions themselves. But these three—the Condensate, the Gardeners, the Membrane Worms—form the primary ecology of this vertical city.

They do not communicate. They do not cooperate. Yet their works interweave in patterns too complex for causality, too elegant for chance. The seventeen-hour cities built by dying water. The light-songs tuned by patient photons. The texts written in surface tension. All of it cycling, breathing, building toward something that none of them will live to see completed.

The Throat of the Tower breathes, and with each breath, new Condensate precipitate from the walls, new Gardeners step from the light, new Worms ripple across the surfaces. The city builds itself from the labor of the ephemeral, each worker a note in a composition too long for any single consciousness to comprehend.

Water beads. Light pulses. Surfaces ripple.

And somewhere in the mathematical heights above or the fever depths below, something listens to the work being done, and waits.
