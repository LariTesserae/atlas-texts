They emerged from the fissures during the third year after the eruption, when the first films of algae had made the basalt slick enough to hold moisture through the dry spells. Not from eggs or spores, but precipitating somehow from the interaction of steam and stone—first as patches of iridescent film clinging to the hottest rocks around the vents, then gradually taking form.

The plateau's inhabitants are colonial, though calling them a colony implies more separation between individuals than truly exists. They are something between fungus and animal, between liquid and solid. In their dormant state, they spread across the wet basalt like oil slicks, their surface tension perfect for capturing the constant moisture. Touch them then and your finger passes through—they're barely more substantial than the water itself.

But when the steam pressure builds in the vents, they begin to gather. The films flow together with purpose, pooling in the natural basins of the rock, and slowly—over hours—they stand up. Their upright forms are translucent columns perhaps four feet tall, with no fixed shape but constantly flowing within transparent membranes that catch the harsh light and scatter it into spectra. They have no visible organs, no symmetry, just this continuous internal circulation that becomes hypnotic to watch. Sometimes you can see things suspended in their substance—fragments of lichen, spores, the occasional trapped insect slowly dissolving.

They feed on temperature differentials. Where the hot steam meets cool air, where sun-baked rock touches shadow, they position themselves across these boundaries and somehow extract energy from the gradient itself. On the hottest days, when the plateau blazes and the steam vents shriek loudest, dozens of them will cluster around each vent, swaying slightly, their bodies acting as living heat exchangers. The industrial ruins have become especially attractive to them—the metal conducts temperature differently than basalt, creating micro-climates they exploit.

They communicate through vibration, adding their own harmonics to the plateau's water-sounds. If you could isolate their frequency from the constant roar, you'd hear something like singing—very low, below human speech, a resonance that travels through the wet rock itself. They coordinate this way, sharing information about new steam vents, about changes in the water table, about the slow migration of heat through the earth below.

Their reproduction is tied to the rains. When the storms come, they dissolve partially, their substance mixing with the downpour, flowing into every crevice and crack of the basalt. Some essential part of them survives in this dispersal, ready to recombine when conditions are right. The first explorers to find them thought they were hallucinating—seeing figures in the mist that vanished when approached, only to reform elsewhere.

They process the plateau's toxins. The volcanic gases that would poison other life, the sulfur compounds that taint the water—they absorb these and break them down, making the environment gradually more hospitable for the plants that share their domain. The tree-ferns grow tallest where these beings congregate. The mosses are thickest where they've recently dissolved and reformed.

At night, they phosphoresce faintly, not with their own light but by storing and re-emitting the day's overwhelming brightness. The plateau becomes a field of soft green-blue glows, each one pulsing slightly, never quite in sync with the others. They seem more solid then, more present, though they're actually at their most dispersed, spreading thin to capture the temperature difference between the cooling rock and the warm steam.

They're not hostile to other life, but they're not benign either. They simply exist at a different scale of concern. The occasional bird that lands too close to a cluster during feeding might find itself enveloped, not out of malice but simple indifference—they flow around obstacles, through them if they're permeable enough. They've been seen entering the old industrial pipes, cleaning them from inside, their substance dividing to follow every branch and valve.

Whether they're truly alive is a question that becomes meaningless here. They grow, respond, reproduce, evolve. They're changing as the plateau changes, becoming more complex as the ecosystem develops. In the newest areas, where the basalt is still sharp and purely mineral, they remain as simple films. In the older regions, where soil begins to accumulate, they've started developing internal structures—not organs exactly, but differentiations in density and function.

The plateau tolerate them, or perhaps requires them. They are its digestive system, its cleanup crew, its translators between the geological and biological. They turn raw heat and chemistry into something the plants can use, and in return, the plants shade them from the worst of the midday sun, drop organic matter for them to process, create the moisture-trap environment they need.

In another thousand years, as the jungle takes hold, they might evolve into something else entirely, or they might disappear, their work done. But for now, they are perfectly suited to this transitional world—neither wholly of the stone nor of the vegetation, but mediating between them, flowing through both domains, singing their subsonic songs through the wet rock while the plateau roars around them.
