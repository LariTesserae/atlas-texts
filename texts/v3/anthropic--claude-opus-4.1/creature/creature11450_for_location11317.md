The Breathkeepers arrived first, or perhaps they were always here—their genealogy lost in the merger of meat and mechanism. They are no taller than children but move with the deliberation of ancient trees, their bodies a compromise between mammal and moss. Fur grows only on their backs and crowns, elsewhere their skin has taken on the texture of bark, smooth in youth, deeply fissured by middle age. Their fingers are too long by half, with an extra joint that lets them reach deep into the breathing pores to clear blockages, remove parasites, adjust the mysterious cartilage valves that regulate each chamber's exhalation.

They do not speak—their larynxes atrophied generations ago—but communicate through touch-patterns on each other's bark-skin, a language of pressure and duration. When working, they emit a low humming from specialized air sacs in their throats, harmonizing with the factory-forest's bass note. This isn't song but echolocation; they read the health of the breathing chambers by the way sound bounces back.

Each Breathkeeper tends roughly three hundred chambers, knowing them with an intimacy that surpasses ownership. They carry tools grown rather than made—combs of living bone that still grow hair-fine root networks, used to clean the membrane filters inside each pore. Pouches of processed beetle shell hold various fungi they've cultivated to treat the diseases that periodically afflict the chambers: white rot, black sigh, the crystalline growth called factory-cough.

They live in hollows where the root-floor bunches up against the walls, spaces just large enough for one to curl into sleeping position. These dens are lined with shed wire-worm casings that provide a faint light and warmth. The Breathkeepers subsist entirely on the condensation they collect from their chambers—not the water itself but the microscopic organisms that bloom in it, rich with concentrated nutrients from the plant-mass's digestion of ancient metals.

Their children are born with eyes, but these seal shut by their third year as their echolocation develops. The vestigial eyelids remain as shallow depressions, sometimes twitching in response to strong vibrations. Adolescent Breathkeepers spend years as apprentices, learning to feel the difference between a healthy wheeze and one that signals deeper dysfunction, memorizing the seasonal patterns of each chamber's breathing, understanding which fungi to introduce to correct imbalances in the factory-forest's vast respiratory system.

They mark time not by days but by breathing cycles—ten thousand chambers completing one full exhale-inhale sequence. During the deepest part of the cycle, when all chambers sync momentarily in their rhythm, the Breathkeepers gather in the center of the floor-organism. Here they perform maintenance that requires coordination: adjusting the wire-worms' configurations, which they believe regulate the distribution of nutrients through the system, or collectively humming at specific frequencies to encourage new growth in damaged sections.

The eldest among them, distinguishable by bark-skin so thick it's begun to crack and weep amber sap, remember stories of the before—though these memories are suspect, possibly dreams or genetic echoes. They recall a time when the chambers breathed smoke not air, when the Breathkeepers' ancestors wore coverings that weren't grown but given, when the floor was hard and unforgiving. These stories are shared through elaborate touch-dances during the cycle-gatherings, each retelling slightly different, evolution in real time.

They fear only one thing: the silence. Sometimes, rarely, a section of chambers stops breathing. When this happens, all Breathkeepers in the area converge, working frantically to restart the respiratory process before the death spreads. They've developed elaborate rituals around these events—part medical procedure, part prayer to the factory-forest itself. They introduce specific combinations of fungi and bacteria, manipulate the wire-worms into new patterns, drum complex rhythms on the root-floor.

Other creatures inhabit this space but none with such purpose. The Scuttlers came later—metallic crabs that aren't crustaceans at all but evolved from some small mammal, their fur compressed into overlapping plates that click when they move. They feed on the beetle's fungal gardens, keeping them from overgrowing their boundaries. Their young are soft and translucent, hardening as they age through exposure to the mineral-rich condensation, incorporating traces of iron and copper into their shells until they gleam like living bronze.

In the highest reaches, where the wire-worms weave their densest networks, live the Chanters—bird-things with no wings, their flight feathers evolved into sound-producing organs. They hang upside down like bats, each individual maintaining a single pure note for hours, their collective chorus creating acoustic patterns that the Breathkeepers believe help regulate the breathing chambers' rhythm. Their bodies are impossibly elongated, stretched between powerful grasping feet and prehensile tails, with their sound organs spreading like fans from their undersides.

The Chanters feed on the wire-worms' luminescent secretions, their tongues evolved into hollow tubes that can pierce the worms' skin without killing them, extracting just enough of the glowing fluid to sustain themselves. This relationship seems symbiotic—the wire-worms grow thicker and brighter where the Chanters feed regularly, their light pulsing in time with the songs above.

But it's the Breathkeepers who define this place, who maintain its impossible balance between the organic and mechanical. They've evolved not just physically but culturally to serve the breathing chambers, developing a complex philosophy around their work. They believe each exhale carries something essential out into the world—not oxygen alone but some ineffable substance that keeps reality stable. Without their ministrations, they maintain, the boundary between what was and what is would dissolve entirely.

New Breathkeepers are not born often—perhaps one per hundred cycles. When they are, it's always during the deep sync, when all ten thousand chambers breathe as one. The newborn emerges already humming in harmony, their too-long fingers grasping at air as if feeling for the pores they'll tend. They grow slowly, reaching full size only after thousands of cycles, by which time they've memorized every whisper and wheeze of their inherited chambers.

Some philosophers from the outside world—those few who've found and studied this place—suggest the Breathkeepers aren't separate organisms at all but motile extensions of the factory-forest itself, evolved to tend its breathing apparatus like white blood cells maintaining a vast body. The Breathkeepers, if they could understand this theory, would find it neither strange nor disturbing. They know what they are: the ones who keep the breathing steady, who ensure the ten thousand chambers never fall silent, who maintain the rhythm that holds their merged world together.

When a Breathkeeper dies—a rare event, as they seem to live as long as trees—their body doesn't decay normally. Instead, the root-floor slowly accepts them, drawing them down over the course of several cycles until they merge entirely with the plant-mass. Where they sink, a new breathing chamber eventually forms, adding to the ten thousand, wheezing its first breath in perfect synchronization with all the others.
