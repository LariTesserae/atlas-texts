The Rootkeepers move through their domain with the peculiar grace of those who have learned to navigate by texture rather than sight. They are perhaps a dozen, perhaps fewer—difficult to count as they appear and vanish into the vegetal density, sometimes indistinguishable from the growth they tend.

Their bodies have adapted. Not through any deliberate modification, but through generations of work in this humid depth. Their fingers are long, with additional joints that weren't quite there in their grandparents' hands—better for working in the tight spaces between root-bundles, for feeling the pulse in a vascular conduit without having to see it. Their skin carries a permanent sheen, not of sweat but of a waxy secretion that prevents the constant moisture from causing rot. It gives them a silvered look in the bioluminescent light, like fish glimpsed in murky water.

They wear little—the heat makes anything beyond necessity unbearable. What clothing they have grows with them: coveralls of living fiber that repair their own tears, that breathe and wick moisture, that occasionally sprout tiny leaves which the Rootkeepers absently pluck and eat. The fabric is symbiotic, feeding on their sweat and dead skin, cleaning them even as it covers them. After years of wear, the coveralls know their wearers' bodies, thickening where protection is needed, thinning where flexibility is required.

The eldest among them, the Senior Tender, has worked here for forty years. Her name above was Marina, but down here she is simply "Taproot"—the one who goes deepest, who knows the oldest growth. Her eyes have developed nictitating membranes, translucent secondary eyelids that protect against the caustic saps and sudden steam releases. She reads the city's health through her fingertips, pressing her palms against the great structural roots to feel their hydraulic pressure, tasting the condensation on critical junctions to detect chemical imbalances. Her knowledge is entirely somatic. She cannot explain what she knows, only demonstrate it through decades of muscle memory.

The younger Rootkeepers learn from her through mimicry and touch. Words are insufficient in the din, and often unnecessary. They communicate through a language of taps on pipes, of hand signals evolved for this specific environment, of spore-messages left at junction points—certain fungi they've learned to cultivate that change color to indicate which systems have been checked, which need attention. They paint these living markers throughout their territory, a map only they can read.

They sleep in hammocks of woven root-fiber strung in the few spaces where the growth is thin enough to permit it. These sleeping quarters migrate slowly as the city grows, the Rootkeepers simply reweaving their beds wherever a new gap opens. They maintain small gardens of edible growth—mushrooms that fruit on the warm pipes, modified tomatoes that need no light, vines that produce thumb-sized tubers rich enough to sustain them. The city feeds them as they feed it.

Their tools are as much grown as forged. A pruning saw with teeth of cultivated calcium, harder than steel but self-sharpening. Diagnostic wands of copper-cored wood that taste the electrical signals running through the bio-mechanical networks. Grafting tape that is actually a species of beneficial fungus, trained to suture plant tissues together. They carry their tools in pouches of living leather that never quite died, that still respond to their touch by opening and closing, by shifting their contents to make needed items accessible.

The Rootkeepers rarely surface. When they do, they move through the upper city like deep-sea creatures brought to shallow water—blinking in the harsh sunlight, overwhelmed by the dryness of the air, the hardness of purely mechanical surfaces. They conduct their business quickly—trading harvested components for the few things they cannot grow, filing reports in a bureaucratic language that barely captures what they actually do—then descend again to their warm, wet world.

They have children, sometimes. Raised in the root-city from birth, these offspring know no other environment. By the age of five, they can navigate the root-mesh by sound alone. By ten, they understand the city's digestive cycles well enough to help regulate them. By fifteen, they have chosen whether to stay or attempt life above. Most stay. The root-city marks them early and deep. Their lungs adapt to process the thick air. Their gut bacteria shift to digest the industrial agriculture. Their dreams are of endless growth, of being absorbed into the warm, breathing darkness, of becoming indistinguishable from the city they tend.

They are not quite human anymore, the Rootkeepers, but something more suited to their environment. Urban dryads, perhaps. Symbiotic organs in the city's body. They prune and graft and cultivate, maintaining the delicate balance that keeps the botanical machinery functioning. Without them, the city would grow wild, its systems choking on their own productivity. With them, it breathes, digests, lives.

They speak to the city in touches and tastes, and it speaks back in the language of growth and decay, of pressure and release. They know every whisper of steam, every gurgle of digestion, every creak of expanding wood. This is their world entire: warm, wet, dark, alive, packed solid with purpose, breathing beneath its own weight.
