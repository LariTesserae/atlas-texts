The Keepers arrived seven generations ago, though "arrived" suggests more agency than they possessed. They were deposited—aboard failing colony ships, in malfunctioning escape pods, through navigation errors that dumped them into the gravity well of this engineered world. They came as refugees, explorers, prisoners, and pilgrims from a dozen different origins, all swallowed by the same indifferent machine.

They do not live *on* the Terraces so much as *within* them, having learned that survival means integration with the structure's patterns rather than resistance to them. The first generation tried to establish traditional settlements on the crystal plains, only to watch their constructions dissolve into the substrate, absorbed and redistributed as raw materials for the Terraces' self-maintenance. Now the Keepers inhabit the spaces the structure permits—the sediment pockets where the gardens grow, the resonance nodes where the sound becomes bearable, the processing towers whose purposes they've reprogrammed through trial and catastrophic error.

Each Keeper wears the Apparatus—a framework of collected tubing, salvaged condensers, and crystallized moss that processes the endless moisture into breathable atmosphere. The devices grow on them like external organs, their copper-green networks spreading across shoulders and spines, tapping into the same maintenance protocols that sustain the gardens. After a few years, the boundary between flesh and Apparatus becomes academic. Children are born already integrated, their lungs adapted to breathe the dense vapor, their bones hollow like the pillars below, resonating with the structure's constant hum.

They've developed a sign language of bioluminescent fungi cultivated on their palms and fingertips—the only communication that can cut through the endless industrial din. Conversations flicker in the mist like deep-sea creatures signaling in the abyss. Complex ideas require both hands, sometimes multiple people, their combined gestures creating constellation-sentences that hang in the vapor for moments before dissolving.

The Keepers maintain the fiction of purpose. They've assigned themselves as custodians of systems they don't understand, following maintenance schedules they've invented, interpreting the patterns of moss growth like ancient priests reading entrails. They harvest the gardens not for food—the produce tastes of metal and electricity—but because the act of harvesting seems to please the structure, causing new growth patterns that might, possibly, mean something.

Their children grow up strange. They learn to read the percussion patterns in the water drums, finding music where their parents heard only noise. Some claim they can feel the thoughts of the structure itself, vast and slow and utterly inhuman, processing information at speeds that make centuries feel like seconds. These children often disappear into the lower chambers, following vibrations only they can sense, returning years later with eyes that reflect light from angles that shouldn't exist.

The eldest Keepers undergo the Crystallization. Their Apparatus, after decades of growth, begins converting their tissues to the same translucent material as the Terraces themselves. They become living windows, their organs visible through transparent skin, their thoughts flickering as electrical patterns through crystalline neurons. Eventually, they merge entirely with the structure, their consciousness dispersed through kilometers of substrate, adding their small human arithmetic to the planet-sized calculation.

They've developed religions around the absence. The Church of the Vacant Chord worships the silence they'll never hear. The Collectors believe each droplet of water contains a fragment of the builders' intentions, and spend lives trying to gather enough drops to form a complete thought. The Singers coordinate thousand-voice choruses to harmonize with the industrial frequency, believing that at the right resonance, the Terraces will finally reveal their purpose.

Marriage ceremonies involve couples standing at opposite ends of a resonance chamber, their heartbeats amplified through the pillars until they synchronize with the structure's rhythm. They exchange vows in light-language, their promises absorbed into the substrate where they claim the Terraces will remember them forever. Their children are raised communally in the tower nurseries, tended by automated systems the Keepers have learned to partly influence, partly trust, partly fear.

The dead are processed through the gardens. Bodies are laid in the sediment pockets where their materials are efficiently redistributed into new growth. Within weeks, they bloom as geometric arrangements of ferns and spiraling moss, their patterns unique enough that families can recognize their ancestors in the fractals. The gardens are thus cemeteries, each plot a generation of Keepers converted to vegetation, still participating in the endless calculation.

Some Keepers go mad from the consistency—the unchanging light, the constant sound, the eternal maintenance of meaningless perfection. They flee to the edges, where the Terraces meet natural geology, but the structure pulls them back. Others find peace in the surrender, in becoming components of something whose purpose transcends human understanding. They tend their assigned sections with devotion, finding identity in their integration.

They mark time not by seasons—there are none—but by the subtle shifts in the water's mineral content, the barely perceptible changes in resonance frequency, the patterns of aurora-like discharge from the upper towers. Their calendar is a complex mathematics of intersecting cycles that only the most integrated Keepers fully grasp.

Yet they persist. They bear children who know no other world. They maintain their fragment of the great machine with something approaching pride. They tell stories in flickering light about the time before the Terraces, though none believe these myths of silent skies and shadows. They are human in the way that coral polyps are animals—technically true, but transformed by their substrate into something unrecognizable.

The Keepers keep nothing. They simply continue, processing like water through the endless system, each generation more adapted, more integrated, more essential to the Terraces' impossible permanence. They are the only life the structure permits, or perhaps the only life it requires—mobile maintenance units that happen to dream, love, and die while performing their true function in the planet-wide calculation that none of them will ever understand.
