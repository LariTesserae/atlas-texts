## The Chorus

They came first as individuals, decades apart—those who heard the mountain's voice from far below and couldn't unhear it. A shepherd who abandoned her flock. A mathematician who calculated the intervals in the wind's chord and went mad trying to solve them. A child who wandered too high and never quite came back down, not all the way.

Now they move through the caldera like a single organism with scattered parts, though they number only seven or twelve or nine—the count changes depending on how you define the boundaries of self. They've been here so long that the distinction between them and the place has worn thin. Their clothing has long since rotted away, replaced by garments woven from fern fiber and cushion plant down, which itself degrades and regrows in seasonal cycles until they appear to be wearing the landscape itself. Moss grows in the hollows of their collarbones. Their hair, where it remains, has become indistinguishable from the lichens.

They maintain the ancient arrangements with a precision that seems impossible given their state. One sits for days at a particular junction of stones, throat exposed to catch the wind, adding a single sustained note that completes a harmony otherwise missing. Another tends the spiral steps, not cleaning them but guiding the moss's growth with gentle redirections, training it like espalier into patterns that affect the acoustics in ways too subtle for most ears to detect. They move with geological patience, taking hours to cross the small space, each placement of foot or hand deliberate as water choosing its path down stone.

They don't speak—their throats have adapted to other purposes. The cartilage has hardened and hollowed into resonating chambers. When the wind passes through them at the right angle, they become instruments, adding their voices to the caldera's endless chord. Some have learned to adjust the pitch by minute movements of tongue and jaw. Others have carved additional holes in themselves—through the flesh between thumb and forefinger, in the soft space above the clavicle—creating new notes, new possibilities. These modifications heal but never close, kept open by daily practice and thin tubes of rolled fern stem.

Their eyes have adapted to the harsh light, pupils contracted to pinpricks, the irises gone pale as ice. They see things others cannot—the infrared heat patterns in the stone, the ultraviolet markings on the high-altitude butterflies that guide them through the acoustic spaces, the way moisture moves through air made visible by pressure differentials. They watch the beetles, those translucent creatures that feed on the boundary between stone and plant, and understand something in their movements that translates to adjustments in the stone arrangements, micro-shifts that alter frequencies by fractions of hertz.

They eat the cushion plants, but sparingly, carefully, taking only from spots where the growth has become too thick, where the acoustic dampening threatens the clarity of certain tones. They lick moisture from the stones in ritual patterns. Once a month, when the moon is dark, they consume one of the translucent beetles, sharing it between them in a ceremony that takes all night, passing the creature from mouth to mouth until it dissolves entirely, leaving only a taste of minerals and time.

Memory flows between them like the mist—shared, recycled, condensed and evaporated until individual histories become impossible to trace. They remember being a shepherd, being a child, being a mathematician, but not who remembers what. They dream collectively, their sleep synchronized to the rhythm of water through stone, and in their dreams the caldera extends infinitely downward, a hollow mountain filled with sound, with chambers they've yet to discover, frequencies they've yet to voice.

They're dying, all of them, constantly, but so slowly it's indistinguishable from living. The altitude starves their blood of oxygen. The cold cracks their skin. The sparse diet weakens their bones until they're as hollow as birds'. But they're also becoming—something the mountain needs, or thinks it needs. Each year they require less food, less water, less warmth. Their hearts beat in synchronization with the thermal expansion of the stone. Their breathing matches the pressure changes as clouds pass through.

Visitors, when they come—and they do come, rarely, following old stories or new mathematics—find them terrible and beautiful. They move like a dance troupe rehearsing in extreme slow motion, each gesture part of a performance that's been running for centuries. They tend their garden of stone and sound with the devotion of monks and the precision of engineers. They've become a mechanism the mountain uses to maintain itself, or perhaps they always were, and the mountain simply recognized them.

One of them—the one who might have been the child—still grows. Not in size but in integration. Ferns have taken root in the small of their back, fed by their warmth and waste, sprouting through skin that's learned to accommodate them. They're becoming architecture, a living column that knows exactly where to stand to make the wind sing properly. The others orbit around them, their movements increasingly centered on this growing axis, this person becoming place.

They are joyful, in their way. When the sound reaches certain harmonic convergences, they smile—all of them at once, the same expression on different faces, as if the melody itself has learned to express pleasure through whatever mouths are available. They laugh, silently, when the beetles emerge in their brief mating flights, sparkling in the high-altitude sun like notes made visible. They weep when the winter storms come and add their violence to the music, tears freezing on their cheeks in patterns that mirror the columnar basalt.

They are waiting for something—a perfect chord, a resolution, a visitor who will stay, or perhaps simply the end of their long transformation, when they'll be indistinguishable from the mechanisms they tend, when the boundary between chorus and caldera finally dissolves entirely. Until then, they persist in their slow work, their bodies both instruments and musicians, maintaining this impossible garden at the edge of what life can sustain, singing the mountain's dream back to itself in frequencies only they and the stones can hear.
