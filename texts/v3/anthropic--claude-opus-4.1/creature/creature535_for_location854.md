The Cultivators move through the terraced depths with a patience that borders on geological. Their forms have stretched across millennia of adaptation—bodies elongated into ribbons of translucent flesh that undulate rather than swim, their movement more akin to the slow unfurling of silk scarves in honey. Each individual spans perhaps thirty meters when fully extended, though they spend most of their existence contracted into dense knots nestled in the corners where terrace meets terrace.

They have no eyes. Instead, their skin has become a sensory membrane of extraordinary sensitivity, reading the chemical gradients in the water like sheet music. Rows of primitive chemoreceptors run along their ventral surfaces—thousands of small pits that taste the substrate's slow decay, the vegetation's metabolic whispers, the mineral signatures of different terrace levels. They navigate by flavor, their world a three-dimensional map of chemical intensities.

Their evolution from the original builders is written in their anatomy. You can still find, in their core structure, the vestigial framework of a bipedal form—a calcified spine that no longer flexes, hip bones fused into a rigid plate that serves as an anchor point for their ribbon-flesh. Their forelimbs have become cultivation appendages, splitting into hundreds of delicate tendrils that work the vegetation with infinite care. They prune and tend the kelp-analogues through touch alone, their finger-fronds reading the health of each plant through minute pressure differences, encouraging growth here, trimming excess there.

They communicate through chemical releases—complex pheromone sentences that diffuse through the viscous water in slow-motion conversations. A single exchange might take days to complete, the molecular messages traveling from terrace to terrace in lazy spirals. Their language has no words for haste. The grammar itself assumes patience—past, present, and future tenses blend into a single grammatical form that might best be translated as "the eternal occurring."

The Cultivators remember everything, but not in the way of active memory. Their history is encoded in their metabolisms, in the way they process certain minerals, in inherited behavioral patterns so ingrained they've become autonomic. They maintain the terraces not through conscious decision but through a kind of cellular imperative. Their ancestors' agricultural project has become their biology—they literally cannot exist without tending the vegetation, and the vegetation has evolved to require their touch.

They reproduce rarely, perhaps once per century, and the process is architectural as much as biological. An individual will begin to construct a nursery terrace—a small depression carved with painful slowness into the existing structure, using specialized secretions that soften the ancient coral-concrete. This construction takes decades. Only when the space is complete does the Cultivator undergo fission, splitting into two smaller individuals who will take another century to grow to full size.

Their diet consists entirely of the vegetation they tend, but the relationship is more complex than simple consumption. They graze selectively, taking only specific fronds at specific stages of growth, and their digestion enriches the water with precisely the nutrients the plants require. They are farmers who have become part of the farm, shepherds who have joined the flock.

In the deepest reaches, where the terraces plunge into the original planetary crust, there are Cultivators who have grown enormous—kilometer-long ancients who move so slowly they seem like geological features. These deep dwellers tend vast gardens in the abyssal zones, where the vegetation grows strange and the water carries trace memories of the planet's pre-construction chemistry. They may be the same individuals who built the original terraces, their lifespans extended beyond reason by the strange properties of the deep water and their own hibernation-like metabolic states.

The younger Cultivators (merely centuries old) sometimes cluster in the mid-level terraces, their bodies interweaving in complex social braids that can persist for decades. These gatherings serve multiple functions—reproduction, information exchange, collective cultivation of particularly demanding garden plots. But mostly they seem to be a form of communion, a temporary surrender of individuality that echoes their species' ancient collective labor.

They fear nothing because fear requires a relationship with time that they no longer possess. Predation doesn't exist here—everything is cultivation, symbiosis, or slow decay. The greatest danger is forgetting—losing track of which terraces need tending, which water layers require adjustment, which sections of vegetation are approaching their centennial flowering. So they have developed elaborate ritual cycles, behavioral patterns that ensure every terrace is visited, every garden maintained, even if the conscious reason for these actions has long since dissolved into instinct.

Some Cultivators have begun to mineralize, their oldest portions transitioning from flesh to something more like limestone. These partial fossils continue their work, dragging their petrifying sections through the gardens, still tending, still maintaining, even as they slowly become part of the substrate they've spent millennia shaping. It's not death exactly—more like a final metamorphosis, a return to the architectural state their ancestors imposed on the world.

In the eternal dark of the terraced depths, they continue their work—pruning, tending, shaping, maintaining. They are the living memory of their species' great project, their bodies and behaviors the only record of why these terraces were built. They are farmers who have outlived the concept of harvest, gardeners in a garden that has become the entire world, architects whose bodies have become the blueprint for a construction that will never be complete because completion would mean the end of purpose, and purpose, encoded in every fiber of their ribbon flesh, is all they have left of what they once were.
