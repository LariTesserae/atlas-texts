## The Dwellers

The Sovereigns came first, though no one remembers if they were always here or arrived when the water was still clear. Three of them patrol the cistern in endless, overlapping circuits—creatures that might have begun as groupers or carp but have grown into something industrial in scale and temperament. Each is the length of a shipping container, their bodies segmented with bands of scar tissue where they've repeatedly forced themselves through the same mechanical passages over centuries. Their scales have incorporated metallic flakes from the water, giving them the appearance of living chainmail that shifts from copper to verdigris depending on the angle of observation.

They communicate through the pipes. The Sovereigns have learned that certain valve configurations create specific resonances, and they spend hours nudging corroded wheels and levers with their broad heads, tuning the entire chamber like an instrument. Each has its own signature call—a deep thrumming that can be felt in the bones of anything that swims here. They name the spaces they patrol this way, encoding territorial claims in frequencies that make the kelp forests shiver.

Between their passages dart the Collectors—a hundred varieties of small, quick things that have made careers of scavenging. Some are no bigger than a thumb, their bodies translucent except for the bright orange sacs where they concentrate oxidized metals filtered from the water. They follow the robotic arms, having memorized the exact timing of each mechanical routine. When an arm grasps at nothing, they position themselves precisely where materials would have been, letting the ancient grippers close gently around them. Some have developed carapaces that fit perfectly into specific gripper configurations, wearing the machines' touch like a blessing before darting away at the exact moment the routine releases.

Others among the Collectors have specialized further. The Thread-Weavers harvest fibers from disintegrating gaskets and weave them into nets between pipes, catching spores and seed pods from above. The Bore-Worms have developed diamond-hard teeth that let them tunnel through soft metals, creating warrens inside the walls of massive tanks where they raise their young in galleries lined with masticated rust.

In the mid-waters live the Philosophers—cephalopods that came from who knows where, drawn perhaps by the warmth or the plenty. They've grown large here, though not massive, their bodies about the size of a child. Their intelligence manifests in their interactions with the machinery. They've learned to operate certain valves, redirecting water flow to create currents that bring food to their dens. Some have taken up residence inside broken turbines, their tentacles replacing missing fan blades, spinning themselves to generate whirlpools that disorient prey.

The Philosophers collect things. In their dens—usually inside some large piece of machinery—they arrange collections of gears, circuit boards, fragments of safety signage, anything with pattern or regularity. They trade these items among themselves in elaborate ceremonies, their chromatophores flashing in complex negotiations that can last for hours. They seem to be trying to understand something about this place, perhaps even to decode its original purpose from the fragments that remain.

But the true inheritors of this space are the Gardeners—a colony organism that might have started as something like coral but has become altogether more intentional. They exist as thousands of polyps connected by a neural network of calcium-and-copper filaments that run through the sediment floor. The Gardeners farm the kelp, pruning it with specialized polyps that secrete acids, encouraging growth in patterns that optimize water flow and light distribution. They've learned to cultivate the bioluminescent varieties, creating patches of bright cultivation that attract the smaller fish they feed on.

The Gardeners' greatest work is their relationship with the automated systems. Over generations, they've grown their calcium structures into the joints and gears of the machinery, not to stop it but to modify it. They've created living bearings that reduce friction, allowing some machines to continue functioning centuries past their expected lifetime. In return, the mechanical movements help circulate nutrients and maintain water flow through their colonies. Some polyps have even learned to photosynthesize using the heat radiation from the machines, growing in clusters around the warmest points like flowers seeking an iron sun.

Above, in the steam and spray where the water doesn't reach, live the Witnesses—things that might be birds or bats or something else entirely. They hang from the ceiling grates and remaining catwalks on wings membraned with something between skin and industrial tarp. They never enter the water but feed on what emerges—the spores, the steam-drunk insects that somehow persist here, the occasional Collector that ventures too close to the surface.

The Witnesses sing. Not beautifully, but constantly—a ultrasonic commentary on everything happening below. They echolocate through the steam, their calls bouncing off the water's surface to map the movements of everything beneath. Some say they're recording it all, that their songs are a living history of this place, each generation adding new verses to an endless chronicle of decay and adaptation.

In the deepest places, where the original factory floor has collapsed into sub-basements and utility tunnels, dwells something the others avoid. The Sovereigns won't patrol there. The Philosophers won't explore it. Even the Gardeners retract their filaments from that zone. Something vast lives in that tangle of collapsed machinery and flooded tunnels—something that occasionally sends up streams of bubbles that smell of sulfur and synthesis. The water there is hotter, thick with chemicals that shouldn't exist anymore.

Sometimes, rarely, it surfaces partially—just enough to reveal what might be skin or might be rubber, what could be eyes or could be optical sensors. It watches the robotic arms perform their routines with what seems like recognition, occasionally moving in perfect synchronization with them as if remembering steps to a dance it once knew. Then it descends again, leaving only an oily film on the water and the feeling that this entire ecosystem exists within something else's dream.

The youngest inhabitants are still arriving, still adapting. Larval things that rode in on the moisture that seeps through cracks. Spores from the world above that find this warm, thick medium perfect for forms of growth never seen in normal circumstances. Some creatures seem to spontaneously generate from the interaction between biological matter and industrial chemicals, emerging from pools of lubricant that have mixed with algae to create something like primordial soup.

They all live according to the rhythm of this place—the mechanical cycles that create days and seasons, the tidal pull of pumps that still remember their schedules, the slow dissolution of metal into water into life. They've formed an ecosystem that could exist nowhere else, adapted to pressures both hydraulic and atmospheric, to light that comes as much from bioluminescence and heated metal as from the sun-slotted gratings above.

This is their world entire. Few leave—why would they? Everything necessary exists here in the warm, amniotic fluid of the cistern. Food chains have established themselves around the niches the architecture provides. Breeding cycles follow the seasonal fluctuation of water temperature as different pipes activate or fail. They navigate by the constellation of refracting barnacles, hunt in the shadows of still-moving machinery, rest in the negative spaces the ancient assembly lines create.

And all of them, in their way, are becoming something new. The Collectors growing more mechanical in their precision. The Philosophers developing new forms of cognition adapted to interpreting industrial semantics. The Gardeners incorporating circuitry into their very genetics. They are all slowly converging on some new form of life that is neither purely biological nor purely mechanical, but something uniquely suited to this drowned factory floor where the distinction between made and born dissolved long ago into the jade-thick water that holds them all.
