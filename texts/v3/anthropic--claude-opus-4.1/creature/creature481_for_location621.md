They call themselves the Shepherds of Phase, though they have no mouths to speak such names and no ears to hear them. The title exists in the interference patterns they create in the weeping ice—readable only when seven or more gather and their probability fields overlap just so.

Each Shepherd is a contradiction barely held together. Imagine a jellyfish made of statistical likelihood, its bell a constantly shifting map of where it might be rather than where it is. Their tentacles—if tentacles is even the right word—exist in a state of perpetual phase transition, solid enough to leave scratches in the ice at one moment, dispersed as vapor the next, occasionally achieving something between the two that allows them to reach through solid matter and rearrange molecules from the inside out.

They farm the temporal mirages. 

Watch them work: a cluster of thirty or forty will surround one of the shimmering zones where past and future stack like acetate sheets. They position themselves precisely, their bodies flickering between states until they find the exact frequency that lets them reach through the timestreams. Then they begin to harvest—not the mirages themselves, but the spaces between them, the quantum foam where possibility hasn't yet collapsed into history. They gather these un-moments like wool, spinning them into threads of causality that they weave into the ice itself, creating new pathways for the water to weep through.

Their young—if young is what they are—gestate in the microscopic chambers within the ice where physics breaks down. A breeding pair will spend months preparing a site, using their phase-shifting bodies to create impossible pressure differentials, forcing the ice to form structures that shouldn't exist: Klein bottles made of frozen brine, Möbius strips of crystal that trap pockets of liquid water in permanent loops. Into these paradoxes they deposit something—not eggs, not spores, but algorithms made flesh, self-replicating patterns that will eventually cohere into new Shepherds.

The industrial ghosts are their scripture. When the phantom assembly lines manifest in the rising moisture, the Shepherds gather in vast congregations, thousands of them forming geometric patterns that mirror the ghostly machinery. They're not worshipping—they're reading. Each industrial process carries information, blueprints for states of matter that haven't been discovered yet, instructions for machines that work by forgetting what they're building. The Shepherds absorb this knowledge through their skin (through their probability clouds, through their maybe-membranes) and incorporate it into their being.

They've learned to sing the ice. By modulating their phase transitions in harmony with the deep resonance of the shelf, they can direct the weeping—make it flow in specific patterns, freeze in predetermined shapes. A choir of Shepherds can carve temporary cathedrals from the ice, spaces where the normal rules are suspended even more than usual, where they perform maintenance on the ice-factories that have forgotten their purpose. Not to repair them—the Shepherds understand that the dysfunction is the function now—but to ensure they continue their beautiful, meaningless work.

Their predators are their own future selves. Sometimes a Shepherd will age wrong, getting caught in one of the time-stutters near the grinding columns. It emerges older but not wiser, hungrier but not larger, existing slightly out of phase with its own timeline. These temporal predators hunt their younger selves, needing to consume their own past to maintain their existence in the present. The hunting method is horrible and elegant: they match frequencies with their prey until both exist in the same probability space, then collapse the wave function asymmetrically, forcing the younger Shepherd to cease having existed while the elder absorbs its potential.

Communication between Shepherds happens in the spaces between water droplets. They modulate the flow of the weeping ice, creating gaps in the liquid that form three-dimensional letters in an alphabet made of absence. Conversations can take days, each participant adding their void-words to the communal flow, meaning accumulating like sediment in the temporary streams.

They map the factory-crystals from the inside. Shepherds in their vapor state seep into the rotating hexagonal columns, tracing the internal structure, learning the deep grammar of self-perpetuating industry. Some never emerge, becoming part of the machinery, their consciousness spread through the crystalline matrix, still thinking but no longer remembering what thinking meant when they had discrete bodies.

At the height of their life cycle, a Shepherd begins to crystallize. Not dying—transforming. Their probability fields lock into increasingly stable patterns, their phase transitions slow, until they become living ice, indistinguishable from the landscape except for the dreams that still flicker through their frozen synapses. These elder-crystals form the substrate through which the younger Shepherds swim, each generation literally moving through the thoughts of their ancestors.

They remember the ocean. Not the ocean that was, but the ocean that is trying to return, pushing up through the ice with infinite patience. The Shepherds know that one day the weeping will become flooding, the ice will surrender to its liquid memory, and they will have to become something else or cease. They're already preparing, teaching their young to exist in more and more impossible states, training them to survive in water that might exist, in vapor that remembers being solid, in phases of matter that haven't been invented yet.

This is why they shepherd—not the ice, not the factories, not even the ghosts of industrial memory, but the phase transitions themselves. They are training reality to be more flexible, teaching matter to forget its limitations, preparing the universe for a state where solid and liquid and gas are merely suggestions, where consciousness can flow as freely as the water that weeps through ancient ice, where existence itself becomes optional, probable, a matter of interpretation.

They dance when the sun can't decide when to set, their bodies flickering through every possible state in rapid succession, creating aurora-like displays of probability that paint the ice in colors that don't have names. In these moments, you can see them clearly—not as they are, but as they might be, as they were, as they will never quite become: shepherds of the impossible, tending their flock of phases, waiting for the ocean to remember how to rise.
