The Hydrologists came first, though they would say the water called them. They move through the city's vertical rivers on stilts of calcified bamboo, their long poles finding purchase in the mineral ridges that line each flowing street. Their bodies have adapted—webbed toes, skin that repels moisture yet never fully dries, eyes that see clearly through the perpetual mist. They speak in drops and streams, a language that mimics water's own vocabulary. Each family tends to a neighborhood's flow, reading the mineral deposits like scripture, knowing which buildings will merge in their grandchildren's time, which channels will run dry next century.

But the Hydrologists are only the city's conscious inhabitants. 

The true residents are the Accumulated—colonies of microorganisms that form living films across the wet walls, organizing themselves into patterns that mirror the city's own architecture. They think, if thinking is the word, at geological speed. A single thought might take a human lifetime to complete. They experience the city as a fast-flowing river of sensation, the Hydrologists as brief sparks that flicker through their awareness. Their memory is written in biofilms that preserve chemical records of every century's rain, every shift in mineral content, every variation in flow.

In the deepest districts, where water has carved caverns beneath the fused buildings, live the Listeners—blind from birth, their entire bodies evolved into sensing organs. They map the city through sound and vibration, reading the health of structures by the tone of water moving through them. Their children learn to speak by imitating the plink of drops, the gurgle of streams. They know a hundred words for echo. Their priests stand for days in the acoustic focal points where the city's water-sounds converge, interpreting the messages they believe the city sends through its liquid circulation.

The Miners of Memory work in the accumulated deposits, those places where centuries of mineral-rich water have built up layers like tree rings. They read the city's history in these strata—thin bands marking dry years, thick cloudy layers indicating times of flood, trace elements revealing when the plateau itself shifted. They carefully extract samples without disturbing the flow, preserving them in containers of the city's own water. Their archive, housed in a building that has become a single massive stalactite, contains millions of such samples, a liquid library of the city's autobiography.

Strangest are the Architects of Absence—those who have learned to guide the city's self-construction by removing rather than adding. They carefully chip away at mineral deposits to redirect water flow, knowing that their alterations will cause new structures to form elsewhere over the coming decades. They work to plans that span generations, each Architect training their successor to continue patterns that won't be complete for centuries. Their tools are simple—chisels made from harder minerals found in the city's depths, brushes of metal wire to clean channels. They believe they are not building but uncovering, revealing structures that were always meant to be.

The Cultivators tend the sparse life that clings to the moisture. They've bred the mosses to grow in spirals that channel water more efficiently, trained the ferns to filter impurities from the flow. Their greatest achievement is the Breathing Gardens—spaces where they've encouraged fungi to form net-like structures that clean the air of excess moisture, preventing the formation of clouds in interior spaces. They speak to their plants through water, adjusting mineral content to communicate needs, warnings, encouragement.

High in the towers live the Weather Readers, who have learned to predict the city's internal climate. They know when mist will form in which district, when the convection currents will shift, when the rare internal rainstorms will occur. Their knowledge is crucial—a unexpected downpour in the wrong place can cause floors to flood, destroying centuries of careful cultivation. They communicate through a system of water-drops released from tower to tower, each pattern carrying messages about pressure, humidity, temperature.

The Paper Makers work with the one thing the city lacks—anything that can stay dry. They've developed a craft using the oils from certain beetles, creating water-repelling sheets on which records can be kept. These papers are stored in the few truly dry spaces, chambers sealed with mineral deposits so dense that even the perpetual moisture cannot penetrate. Each sheet is precious beyond measure, used only for the most vital records. Most information is instead stored in the patterns of water flow itself, in channels carved to remember, in the arrangement of drops.

All of these inhabitants share the city with the Transients—those creatures and people who pass through briefly, unable or unwilling to adapt to the constant wet. Some stay for days, some for years, but eventually the moisture drives them away, or changes them so thoroughly they become one of the permanent residents. The city accepts them all, incorporates their presence into its slow patterns, and continues its patient self-construction around them.

And perhaps most mysteriously, there are the Dreamers of Stone—those who have lived so long in the city that they have begun to mineralize. Water flows through them now as it flows through the buildings. They stand perfectly still for months at a time in the main thoroughfares, accumulating deposits, becoming pillars that support new construction. They claim to share consciousness with the city itself, to dream its long, slow dreams of erosion and growth. Whether they are still truly alive or have become something else entirely, no one can say. The water flows through them, and they seem content.

This is a city that makes its inhabitants as much as they make it—a place where the boundary between the living and the geological blurs, where consciousness might exist at speeds too slow or too vast for ordinary human perception, where adaptation means becoming something not quite human anymore. The water shapes everything, connects everything, remembers everything. Those who live here don't merely inhabit the city; they become part of its vast, patient, ever-flowing system.
