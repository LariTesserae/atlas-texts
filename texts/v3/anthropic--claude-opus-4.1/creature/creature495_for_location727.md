**The Smiths of the Drowning Forge**

Three of them work here, though never all at once. They move in careful rotation—two below while one recovers above, breathing deep of the thin air that still exists up there, on the platform they've built around the breathing pipes. They are siblings, perhaps, or grew up close enough to be. Their bodies have begun to adapt: barrel chests that hold air longer than they should, eyes that have learned to ignore the constant shimmer of underwater light, hands scarred into leather by the work.

The eldest is a woman who speaks only in hand gestures now—not from any injury, but from economy. Sound travels strangely here, and words waste breath. She knows every rivet in the dome by its particular oxidation, can tell from the pitch of the breathing pipes when a storm approaches overhead. Her hair, once long, is now shorn close—it dried too slowly in this humid cave, invited sickness. She works the bellows with a rhythm that matches her heartbeat, slow and steady, conserving everything. When she sleeps, she dreams of drowning, and wakes relieved to find only the same thick air she's learned to sip like water.

The middle child—gender becomes irrelevant in the workshop, where everyone wears the same oiled leather aprons, the same close-cropped hair—maintains the seals. They move constantly along the walls, checking each joint with fingers more sensitive than any instrument. They know the language of metals under stress: the particular whine that precedes a crack, the temperature change that signals corrosion accelerating beyond the acceptable. Their notebook, wrapped in waxed cloth, contains a detailed map of every weakness in their shell. They sleep poorly, wake at every new sound, and have developed a habit of humming—a single low note that harmonizes with the drone of the pipes, making the unbearable bearable.

The youngest still remembers the valley as it was: the workshop on solid ground, the forge open to sky, the ring of mountains that held back nothing more threatening than weather. Sometimes, working metal, they forget where they are—until the light shifts with a wave overhead and reality crashes back. They are the innovator, the one who designed the airlock, who figured out how to adapt the bellows to work with the breathing pipes, who insists they can improve the distillation system if given another month. Their optimism is both necessary and terrible. The others depend on it and fear it in equal measure.

They speak of the water rising as one might speak of a death in the family—obliquely, in half-sentences that trail off. It came fast enough that they had time to see it coming, slow enough that they could choose: abandon the forge their great-grandmother built, or descend with it. The decision was never really a decision. The forge is who they are, its fire the continuation of a flame first lit four generations ago. To let it die would be to become nothing.

So they sealed their world in copper and learned to breathe underwater air. They forge tools for the fishing boats that still sail overhead—anchors and hooks and spear-points that they send up in sealed barrels through a system of pulleys. The fishermen lower payment in the same barrels: dried meat, roots that still remember soil, sometimes (rarely, preciously) a letter from the scattered mountain people who found new homes above the waterline.

Each of them has developed a different relationship with their tomb. The eldest treats it as a temple—every rivet sacred, every beam a prayer against the crushing deep. The middle child sees it as a living thing that must be tended, fed with maintenance, soothed with careful attention. The youngest still believes it temporary, that someday the waters will recede or they will devise a way to raise the forge, to birth it again into air.

But their bodies tell a different truth. Their skin has taken on a permanent damp sheen that never fully dries. Their lungs have learned to extract oxygen from the humid air with terrible efficiency. When they surface to tend the breathing pipes, the real air now makes them dizzy, too rich, too thin, too empty of the salt and metal taste they've come to need. They are becoming something new—not quite human anymore, not quite of the sea, but creatures of the margin, the impossible space between.

They no longer dream of the mountains. Their dreams are all of bronze and copper, of perfect seals and impossible alloys, of fires that burn underwater without need for air. Sometimes they dream they are the forge itself—a heart of fire wrapped in metal skin, breathing through ceramic lungs, alive only as long as the hammer falls and the bellows pump.

The youngest keeps a calendar, marking days with drops of flux on a copper sheet. But the others have stopped counting. Time means nothing when the light is always filtered, when the temperature never changes except from forge-heat, when the only seasons are variations in the color of the water overhead. They work, they maintain, they sleep in shifts, and gradually forget they ever lived any other way.
