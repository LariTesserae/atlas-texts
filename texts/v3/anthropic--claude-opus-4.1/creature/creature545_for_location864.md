The Keening Shepherds arrived in the night season, when the moths cluster tightest and their whistling drops to its lowest ebb—merely deafening rather than truly unbearable. They came in groups of three, always three, walking with the particular gait of those whose inner ears have been deliberately damaged. They sway slightly, constantly recalibrating their balance, but this motion has become ritualized over generations into something approaching grace.

Their bones are denser than lowlanders', evolved or adapted through centuries at altitude. Their barrel chests house lungs of unusual configuration—not just enlarged but internally compartmentalized, allowing them to breathe in rotating sections, never fully exhaling. This constant circulation prevents the altitude sickness that would fell others, but gives their voices a peculiar quality, as if multiple people are speaking in imperfect unison.

They wear the moths. Not as clothing exactly, but as living sheaths that cover their bodies in layers of translucent wings. The moths cling to specially woven underlayers of grass-fiber, their bodies oriented to create overlapping scales. When the Shepherds move, they shimmer like heat mirages, half-visible beings that seem to exist partially in this world and partially elsewhere. The moths feed on their blood through tiny punctures, a symbiosis that keeps the insects close and gives the Shepherds a measure of control over the colonial sound. By flexing specific muscle groups, they can cause sections of their moth-coating to adjust wing positions, modulating the whistle into something approaching communication.

Their faces are the most disturbing aspect. Born with normal features, each Shepherd undergoes a gradual transformation. The constant squinting causes the skin around their eyes to thicken and fold into permanent ridges. But more unnervingly, they develop additional sensory organs—small pits along their jawlines that detect vibration through bone conduction, allowing them to "hear" through frequencies that bypass their damaged ears. These pits appear as dark spots, giving the impression of multiple sets of eyes arranged in descending rows.

The flat-bodied grazers are not wild but managed, though "domesticated" would be too strong a word. The Shepherds know each animal by the pattern of scars on its ears—self-inflicted marks where the creatures have tried to gnaw away their own hearing. The Shepherds guide them between grazing grounds using long poles that they strike against the limestone, creating vibrations the animals can feel through their feet. At night, the herds cluster in the acoustic shadows of the cairns, and the Shepherds arrange themselves in specific formations that create interference patterns in the moth-song, generating pockets of relative quiet where the animals can rest.

They build nothing permanent. Their only shelters are temporary arrangements of the flat-bodied animals themselves, which they coax into clustering together for warmth. The Shepherds sleep pressed against the creatures' warm bellies, their moth-coatings spreading to encompass both human and animal in a living blanket that whistles softer than the ambient sound, creating a white noise that almost passes for silence.

Their children—when they have them—are raised communally in the deepest of the limestone basins, where water muffles some of the sound. The infants are born with a mutation that has become fixed in their population: a thin membrane that covers their ear canals, which naturally ruptures around their third year. Those three years of relative quiet are the only peace a Shepherd ever knows. Some say this is why they always travel in threes—one for each year of silence they can remember.

They feed on more than the obvious. Yes, they drink the blood of their grazers in careful amounts, and yes, they gnaw the flesh from moths that have died (their wings making a crackling sound between teeth). But they have also learned to digest the limestone itself, grinding it into powder and mixing it with water to create a mineral-rich paste. Their teeth are accordingly worn, ground down to flat surfaces by adolescence. The elderly often have no teeth at all, subsisting entirely on the limestone slurry and blood.

The Shepherds' relationship with the edge is complex. They are drawn to it, spending hours sitting with their legs dangling over the impossible drop, their moth-coatings streaming out behind them in the updrafts like living wings. They never jump—suicide holds no meaning in their culture—but they do sometimes simply step off, allowing the moths to carry them down in a controlled fall that can last for hours, depositing them on lower plateaus where they gather specific minerals or plants before beginning the long climb back up. Some never return from these journeys; whether by choice or accident is unknown.

Their language, such as it is, consists entirely of manipulations of the moth-song. They have names for wind patterns, for times of day based on the quality of the whistling, for emotional states that can only be expressed through specific harmonic arrangements of wing-noise. They conduct arguments through competitive sound-shaping, each person trying to create patterns that override or subsume the others. Their singing—if it can be called that—involves subtle modifications of their breathing that cause their chest-moths to flutter in rhythm, creating haunting melodies that weave through the eternal keen.

They mark time not by days or seasons but by the lifecycle of the moths. A single moth lives for forty days; a "year" to the Shepherds is the time it takes for their coating to completely die and be replaced—roughly four hundred days. They know their ages by the number of coatings they've worn. The eldest among them claim to remember twenty or even thirty coatings, though at such ages the distinction between memory and dream becomes meaningless.

Their art consists of arrangements. They position the cairns to create specific acoustic effects, spend decades training moth colonies to roost in patterns that generate desired harmonics. They guide the growth of the wire-grass through careful tending, creating spirals within spirals that, when the wind hits just right, add subtle percussion to the endless whistle. Each Shepherd group maintains its own section of the plateau, and over generations these areas develop distinct acoustic signatures—the only form of territory or property their culture recognizes.

Death, when it comes, is handled with disturbing practicality. The dying individual walks to the edge and allows the moths to disperse, revealing their naked body for the first time since childhood. The other Shepherds watch as their companion simply sits, exposed to the murderous sun and cold, until they stop moving. Then they push the body over the edge with their long poles, watching it disappear into the clouds below. They believe nothing of afterlives or spirits; death is simply the moment when one stops contributing to the management of the sound.

Yet for all their alienness, there are moments of startling humanity. They laugh—a horrible sound that involves rapid contractions of their compartmentalized lungs, causing their moth-coating to pulse and shriek. They play games with the grazing animals' young, teaching them to recognize different vibration patterns through the stone. They create small offerings of arranged stones and carefully positioned moth-wings at the spots where lightning has struck, not out of superstition but from an aesthetic appreciation for the patterns of fused limestone.

They dream collectively. When a group of three sleeps, pressed against their animals in the acoustic shadows, their breathing synchronizes. The moths coating all three begin to move in unison, creating a shared sound-space where their damaged minds meet. In these dreams, they hear impossible things: silence, speech, the sound of water falling rather than whistling wind. They wake from these dreams weeping a thick, mineral-rich substance—their tears concentrated from their limestone-heavy diet.

The Keening Shepherds do not see themselves as unfortunate or cursed. To them, the lowlands are places of terrifying silence, where sound dies instead of living, where the air is thick as water and the sun sets instead of circling eternally at the horizon. They pity travelers who stumble onto their plateau, offering them moth-coatings that the visitors invariably refuse. They cannot understand why anyone would choose deafness over adaptation, why anyone would flee from what they consider the pure state of existence—bright, loud, eternal, and perfectly, impossibly alive.

They are not the plateau's only inhabitants, but they are its only citizens. Other things live in the cracks and shadows—pale spiders that weave webs from processed moth-silk, creating nets that sing in frequencies only the Shepherds can manipulate; colonies of something like ants but soft-bodied, flowing like living mercury through the limestone channels, feeding on minerals and moth corpses; and in the deepest basins, things that might be fish or might be larvae, eternally juvenile in the cold water, their bodies transparent as glass.

But it is the Shepherds who give the plateau its meaning, who transform its hostile indifference into something almost like home. They stand at the edge of the world, wrapped in living sound, their scarred faces turned toward the burning sky, conducting an endless symphony that no lowlander could bear to hear, let alone understand. They are evolution's answer to an impossible question, humanity's furthest reach into realms that should not support human life.

And they are happy, in their way. Happy as only those can be who have never known they were supposed to want anything different.
