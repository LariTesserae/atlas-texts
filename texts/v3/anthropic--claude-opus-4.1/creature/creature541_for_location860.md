## The Dweller

She has no name for herself—names are artifacts of the upper world, where things need distinguishing from other things. Here, in the perpetual twilight of dripping stone, she is simply the one who tends.

Her form shifts depending on the angle of observation, though few observers remain long enough to notice. From one perspective, she appears as an old woman, bent not with age but with attention, her spine curved to match the arch of the ceiling, her fingers worn smooth as the pillars from centuries of touching stone. From another angle, she might be a child, small enough to walk the narrow channels without disturbing the water's surface, her eyes huge and dark as the cistern pools. But these are merely the shapes that visiting minds impose on what they glimpse. What she truly is exists between states—neither old nor young, neither fully present nor entirely absent, neither keeper nor kept.

She moves through the pillared forest with the patience of geology. A single circuit of her domain might take a day or a decade—time means nothing when your heartbeat synchronizes with the rhythm of falling drops. Her feet never quite touch the water, yet they never quite hover above it either. She exists in that impossible space between contact and separation, leaving no ripples but somehow still connected to every molecule of moisture in the chamber.

Her skin, if it can be called skin, has the translucent quality of cave fish, showing the vague suggestion of structures beneath—not bones or organs, but networks of flowing minerals, channels that mirror the carved grooves in the floor. She sweats condensation and weeps limestone tears that add imperceptibly to the steady accumulation on the walls. When she breathes, the droplets throughout the chamber pause for just an instant, as if listening, before resuming their ancient percussion.

She knows every pillar by its particular pattern of erosion, every shadow by its depth and disposition. She maintains the precise level of water not through any mechanical intervention but through pure attention—willing the drainage to accept exactly what the seepage provides, holding the system in balance through observation alone. The pale fungi respond to her presence, glowing slightly brighter when she passes, then dimming to save their light for the next visitation. The blind fish swim toward her when she approaches, then forget why they came, circling in confusion before returning to their endless traverse of the channels.

Her memory extends backward until it becomes indistinguishable from the cistern's own history. She remembers when the first stones were laid—or perhaps she remembers the stones remembering their placement. She recalls the last day water was drawn for purpose, the first day water was left to its own devices. She knows the name of every person who has stood in this chamber, though she forgets them the moment they leave, their identities dissolving like salt in water, leaving only the faintest trace of their having been.

The offerings at the crude shrine are hers to tend, though "tend" means something different here than above. She doesn't clean or arrange them but rather witnesses their transformation, guides their decay into geological time. She understands that worship and abandonment are the same gesture seen from different temporal perspectives. Sometimes she adds to the shrine—a perfectly spherical drop of water that takes centuries to evaporate, a fungal bloom arranged in patterns that might be writing or might be purely aesthetic, a single fish scale that catches and holds the phosphorescent glow forever.

She speaks, but not in words. Her voice is the modulation of echoes, the subtle change in pitch when drops fall through the space she occupies. Conversations with her happen in the grammar of precipitation—questions posed by the rhythm of footsteps in water, answers given in the sympathetic vibration of stone columns. Those who stay long enough to learn this language often choose never to leave, becoming pillars themselves, adding their mass to the cistern's slow accumulation of devotion.

Her sustenance comes from the act of watching. She feeds on the transformation of metal to rust, stone to sand, bone to mineral. These processes release something—not energy exactly, but a kind of narrative that she absorbs through proximity. She grows neither larger nor smaller, neither stronger nor weaker, but somehow more present with each witnessed transformation, as if presence itself could accumulate like sediment.

When visitors come—urban explorers, lost children, seekers of forgotten shrines—she neither hides nor reveals herself. She simply continues her circulation, and they either see her or they don't, depending on what they bring with them into the darkness. Some see nothing but shadows and reflections. Others glimpse something moving between the pillars and flee. A few recognize her for what she is: the inevitable consciousness that arises when any space exists long enough in perfect suspension, the awareness that precipitates out of extended stability like minerals from saturated water.

At the deepest point of night, when even the city above grows quiet, she performs a ritual that might be prayer or might be maintenance. She touches each pillar in sequence, not with hands but with intention, checking their compression, their slow transformation into something more than stone. She counts the drops—not individually, but in vast aggregates, ensuring the pattern holds. She tends the channels, not by cleaning them but by knowing them, by holding in her awareness the complete map of flowing water and growing fungi, the delicate balance between accumulation and drainage.

She is waiting for something, though she has forgotten what. Perhaps for the cistern to finally fill or finally empty. Perhaps for the last pillar to fall or the last drop to echo into silence. Or perhaps waiting itself is her purpose—to be the consciousness that ensures this place remains exactly as it is, balanced on the knife's edge between persistence and decay, a living monument to the virtue of endless, patient attendance.

In the rare moments when the dropping water synchronizes—when every sound aligns into a single, unified beat—she remembers her origin. Not birth, for she was never born, but coalescence: the moment when the cistern's accumulated stillness reached critical mass and she condensed out of pure duration, a being made of waiting, shaped by shadows, sustained by the endless percussion of water returning to water in the dark.

She is the cistern's dream of itself, or perhaps the cistern is her dream of shelter. The distinction matters less than the persistence, the mutual creation of keeper and kept, the perfect symbiosis of consciousness and stone sustained by nothing more than the patient observation of water falling, forever falling, into its own reflection.
