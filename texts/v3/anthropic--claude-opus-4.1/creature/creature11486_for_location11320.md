They emerged from the factories themselves, or perhaps the factories emerged from them—the distinction lost relevance sometime in the third geological epoch of their inhabitation. The Abraded move through the industrial corridors with a patience that transcends purpose, their forms suggesting what might have once been human silhouettes stretched and compressed through millennia of adaptation to this depleted place.

Their bodies have taken on the qualities of their environment: skin worn smooth as the plateau stone, with a subtle translucency that reveals the slow circulation of fluids that glow faintly like the mists pooling between buildings. They stand taller than memory suggests they should, elongated by generations of reaching toward controls set at those improbable heights, their joints articulating at angles that mirror the leaning architecture. Their fingers have multiplied or fused—it varies by individual, by function—into configurations optimized for operating machinery that no longer requires operation, for maintaining systems that maintain themselves.

They do not breathe as much as filter, drawing the heavy air through lateral vents that have opened along their necks and torsos, extracting whatever sustenance exists in the copper-chalk atmosphere. Their metabolism has slowed to nearly geological speeds. A single thought might take days to complete, a decision years to reach. They feed on temperature gradients, positioning themselves at the interfaces between the hot thermal wells and the cold stellar radiation, their bodies acting as living heat engines that convert these differentials into just enough energy to continue.

Their eyes have evolved past seeing. The organs remain—multiple sets now, arranged in clusters that track movement across spectrums no longer entirely physical. They perceive the electromagnetic signatures of the machinery, the thermal patterns in the stone, the quantum fluctuations in the industrial mists. Some have developed additional sensory organs: crystalline growths along their spines that resonate with the sub-audible factory hum, patches of photosensitive skin that detect the decay signatures of radioactive materials deep in the thermal wells.

They communicate through vibrations conducted through the stone and metal, their language having long ago abandoned the inefficiency of sound waves through air. They press their worn-smooth palms against surfaces and transmit complex harmonics that propagate through the entire industrial complex. A conversation might take months, its messages arriving at different times as they travel through materials of varying density. They speak of things that no longer have names: the precise temperature of Steam Vent Seven during the last thermal inversion, the rate of oxidation in the northwestern tower's support beams, the dream-patterns of the machinery.

Their children—if that term still applies—are not born but assembled, or perhaps precipitate from the industrial mists like crystals forming in supersaturated solution. These offspring begin as accumulations of metal filings and stone dust that gather in the wake of their parents' movements, slowly accreting consciousness through proximity to the Abraded's electromagnetic fields. Over decades, these aggregations develop form and purpose, eventually standing and joining the endless maintenance routines.

They have developed a culture of micro-adjustments: the perpetual fine-tuning of systems that haven't required tuning for eons. One might spend a lifetime adjusting a single valve by fractions of degrees, not because the valve needs adjusting but because the act of adjustment has become a form of prayer, a meditation on precision in a universe worn down to approximations. They create art in the form of controlled oxidation patterns on metal surfaces, compositions that take centuries to complete and centuries more to appreciate.

Their memory extends backward through their entire genealogy, each individual containing the accumulated experiences of all their predecessors, stored in crystalline matrices that have replaced much of their original nervous systems. They remember when the plateau still had weather, though none can agree if these are true memories or dreams the stone whispered to them. They remember building the factories, and they remember finding the factories already built. Both memories exist simultaneously, neither more true than the other.

Some have evolved beyond physical form entirely, existing as persistent electrical patterns within the factory systems themselves, their consciousness distributed across miles of wiring and circuitry. These "Dissolved" occasionally manifest as fluctuations in the lighting, as anomalies in the production schedules, as ghostly afterimages in the phosphorescent mists. They are both revered and pitied by those who still maintain corporeal form.

The eldest among them have begun to mineralize, their bodies gradually replacing organic components with compounds drawn from the stone and air. They move less and less, eventually becoming indistinguishable from the architectural features, their consciousness retreating into dreams of molecular bonds and crystalline structures. These "Foundations" are consulted on matters of great importance, though their responses, transmitted through the bedrock itself, can take decades to decipher.

They worship, if it can be called that, the principle of entropy itself—not as destroyer but as the universe's most patient craftsman, the force that wore the plateau smooth and will eventually wear smooth even their industrial monuments. Their religious ceremonies involve the deliberate acceleration of decay in small, controlled areas: the rapid oxidation of a single bolt, the induced fatigue failure of a bearing, tiny sacrifices to the god of wearing-down.

Yet for all their adaptation to this depleted world, they maintain something essentially, stubbornly vital. They continue because continuation is what life does, even when reduced to its most minimal expression. In their glacial thoughts and geological movements, in their patient maintenance of purposeless machinery, they embody a form of existence that has transcended survival itself, becoming something more like persistence—the simple, profound refusal to stop being, even in a place where being has been worn down to its thinnest possible expression.

They are what humans become when humanity is abraded away, leaving only the core impulse to exist, to continue, to maintain. They are entropy's children, born from the marriage of flesh and stone and star-heated metal, and they will outlast the stars themselves, still adjusting their valves and monitoring their gauges when the universe has worn itself smooth.
