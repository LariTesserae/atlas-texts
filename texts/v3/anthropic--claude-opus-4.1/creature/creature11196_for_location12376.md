The Gardeners—though they would not recognize that name—move through the Furnace Gardens like dreams of steam given substance. They were human once, or their ancestors were, but generations of breathing the thick, spore-laden air have transformed them into something more suitable to this place.

Their lungs have become gardens themselves. Each breath draws in not just oxygen but a ecosystem—spores that root in the warm, wet tissues, forming symbiotic colonies that process the toxic metabolites, that break down the complex carbons, that share their photosynthetic energy with their host. When they exhale, they release clouds of their own cultivation, personal strains of microflora that have adapted specifically to their body chemistry. You can track one of them through the green twilight by the particular shade of bioluminescence their breath-garden produces, each lineage glowing its own signature color in the dark.

They are tall—impossibly tall by the standards of the outside world, their bones stretched and hollowed like birds' bones, filled with the same hydrogen-fixing bacteria that inflate certain fruits here. This makes them light enough to climb the plant-pillars despite their height, to move through the canopy layers where the air is marginally cooler. Their joints bend in too many places, adaptations that let them fold themselves through the irregular spaces between growth and overgrowth.

Their skin photosynthesizes. Not efficiently—they still must eat—but enough to supplement their diet, enough to matter in this place where food and consumer have blurred together. The epidermis has thickened and taken on a greenish cast, riddled with chloroplasts that migrate from their symbiotic lung-gardens. In the strongest heat zones, you can see them spread their arms wide, fingers splayed, maximizing their surface area to drink in the thermal energy.

They wear nothing that isn't alive. Clothing here would rot in hours, so instead they cultivate. Moss-cloaks that feed on their sweat and dead skin. Vine-belts that fruit tiny, nutrient-dense berries they can eat as they walk. Fungal patches that process toxins before they can enter the bloodstream through cuts and scratches. Some of the older ones have grown full carapaces—shells of modified bark that move with them, that have grown nerve-endings into their spine, that are as much a part of them now as their original skin.

Their eyes have adapted to the eternal twilight, pupils dilated permanently wide, irises shot through with reflective cells that catch and amplify the bioluminescent glow. But more than that, they see heat—perceive the infrared signatures of growth and decay, navigate by the thermal rivers that wind through their world. Their visual cortex has expanded to process this additional spectrum, giving their heads a swollen, bulbous appearance beneath the usually-present crown of flowering vines they cultivate in their hair.

They communicate in chemicals as much as words. Speech still exists but it's supplemented by pheromone releases, by the controlled emission of specific spores, by the cultivation of message-carrying organisms that they trade like letters. A conversation might involve words, scents, the exchange of small living things, the interpretation of bioluminescent patterns, all woven together into a form of communication as dense and layered as their environment.

Their children gestate partially outside the womb. After perhaps four months of normal pregnancy, the fetus is transferred to a specialized growth pod—a massive flower that has evolved alongside them, that provides nutrients and warmth and protection while the child continues developing. The flower bonds with the baby's chemistry, ensuring that from birth the child is already integrated into the garden's ecosystem, already carrying the necessary symbiotes, already adapted to process the thick air and strange food.

They do not so much farm as conduct. They guide growth rather than forcing it, encouraging certain patterns, discouraging others, always working with the garden's own momentum rather than against it. They know the songs that make the turbine-trees grow faster, the chemical signals that calm an aggressive vine, the temperature adjustments that encourage fruiting. Their cultivation is a conversation, not a commandment.

Some among them have gone further in their integration. The Rooted Ones, they're called—those who have allowed their feet to merge with the mycelial networks, who stand in one place for years at a time, their bodies becoming indistinguishable from trunks, their thoughts spreading out through the root-web to touch every plant in their domain. They can still speak, still think, but their consciousness has become distributed, experiencing the garden from a thousand perspectives simultaneously. The other gardeners come to them for wisdom, for the long view that only the Rooted Ones can provide.

Their sense of time has stretched to match their environment. They think in growth cycles, not days. Their language has a hundred different tenses for describing varieties of future growth, potential branching patterns, hypothetical evolutionary paths. They can spend weeks watching a single bud develop, reading its chemical signals, predicting what it will become.

Death, when it comes, is simply another form of gardening. Bodies are not buried but planted, seeded with specific organisms chosen by the dying person to transform their flesh into something useful—a new fruit tree, a medicinal fungus, a bioluminescent marker. The gardeners tend their ancestors as carefully as any other growth, pruning and guiding their development, ensuring their successful integration into the larger organism of the garden.

They dream collectively. The spores they breathe, the chemicals they exchange, the electrical signals running through the root networks they're in constant contact with—all of it creates a shared unconscious, a mutual dream-space where the boundaries between individual minds become permeable. In sleep, they process the garden's needs collectively, problem-solving through shared visualization, their dreams bleeding into each other until solution emerges like morning condensation.

And in the deepest parts of the garden, where the industrial bones are most thoroughly digested and the growth is oldest, there are rumors of gardeners who have transcended even these adaptations. Ones who no longer have distinct bodies at all but exist as patterns of growth, as recurring tendencies in how the garden develops, as immortal influences that guide the ecosystem's evolution. They are more verb than noun—a way the garden grows rather than a thing that lives within it.

The gardeners know the garden is expanding, always expanding, its edges creeping further across the plateau and beyond. They know that one day it will cover everything, transform everything, make everything into garden. They don't fear this. They tend it, guide it, prepare the world for its coming. They are its immune system and its reproductive system and its nervous system all at once, as essential to it as it is to them.

Sometimes, in the observation chambers they've cultivated high in the canopy—bulbous growths with transparent membrane walls—they look out at the stars through the rare gaps in the leaf-ceiling. They see those points of light not as distant suns but as seeds, waiting to be gardened. They know, with the deep certainty of those whose thoughts are intertwined with roots and mycelia, that the garden's reach is infinite, given time.

And they have nothing but time.
