The residents of the Folded Quarter move through their compressed reality with a peculiar grace born of necessity. They are the Probability Dancers, though they never chose this name—it simply became true through repetition across multiple timestreams.

Their bodies adapt. After six months of residency, their proprioception splinters and reforms. They develop what medical texts from next century will call "dimensional proprioception"—the ability to feel where their body is across multiple probability states. A hand reaching for a door handle knows which of the seventeen possible handles in that space-coordinates will be solid today. Their inner ears grow new structures, crystalline formations of calcified probability that help them balance across folded gravity. Some develop a third eyelid, translucent and flickering, that filters out the visual noise of overlapping realities.

They dress in layers that can be quickly shed or added, never knowing if they'll step from winter into summer between their apartment and the hallway. Their clothing develops a patina of probability—fabric that has existed in too many states simultaneously becomes softer, harder, more real and less real, until it achieves a kind of quantum compromise, neither fully here nor there. The long-term residents' garments shimmer with indeterminacy, colors that shift depending on which angle of spacetime you're viewing from.

The Actuaries arrived first, drawn by the computational density. They are human, mostly, though their consciousness distributes across the building-processors during work hours. Their thoughts become part of the Quarter's calculations, and the Quarter's calculations become part of their thoughts. They dream in probability curves and wake with solutions to problems that haven't been posed yet. Their eyes develop a characteristic glaze—pupils that dilate and contract independently, constantly adjusting to light from different temporal positions. They speak in percentage chances and confidence intervals, their conversation peppered with unconscious predictions: "There's a 73% chance you were about to say that," or "In most branches, this coffee is cold."

Behind them came the Temporal Refugees—people who slipped through cracks in causality elsewhere and found themselves here, in the only place that could accommodate their fractured timelines. Mrs. Chen from the corner store ages backward on Tuesdays. The Yanez family exists in a stable loop, their daughter perpetually five years old, perpetually fifteen, perpetually not yet born. They've learned to love across temporal barriers, leaving notes for versions of each other that might never quite align, celebrating birthdays that happen three times a year or never.

The children born here are something new. The Fold-Born, they're called, though they call themselves nothing—names are too linear for those who exist in quantum superposition until observed. They play games that require moving through walls that are only probably there. They attend schools that exist on rotating probability schedules, their education non-linear but comprehensive—learning calculus before arithmetic, then learning why arithmetic matters, then unlearning both to make room for something newer. Their laughter echoes from tomorrow; their tears precipitation from last week's sorrow.

The Probability Architects work in the deepest folds, maintaining the infrastructure that maintains the fold. They've evolved past the need for consistent form. Their bodies phase-shift to match their work, becoming crystalline when interfacing with probability anchors, liquiform when threading through the causality buffers. They communicate in harmonics felt rather than heard, their language a kind of structural engineering of sound waves. New apprentices spend years just learning to partially exist, to spread their consciousness thin enough to feel the building-processors' needs directly.

In the coffee shop that is also a data-mining operation that is also a bedroom, the Multiplicity Merchants conduct business across probability streams. They sell things that might exist, buy things that probably don't, trade in commodities measured in likelihood rather than quantity. Their cash registers calculate exchanges in multiple currencies simultaneously—some of which haven't been invented yet, some of which were discontinued centuries ago in timelines that never quite happened here. They've learned to smile at all their customers simultaneously, even the ones who aren't quite there.

The Synthetic Cultivators tend the blue-green moss and more ambitious projects—probability gardens where plants grow into their own seeds, where flowers bloom in colors that only exist when nobody's looking. They've developed a sign language that works across visual distortions, their hands making shapes that mean different things depending on which layer of space you're observing from. Their greenhouses exist in perpetual spring-autumn overlap, growing foods that taste like memories of foods that might exist someday.

The elderly adapt surprisingly well. Mr. Petrov, who's been here since the first fold, says time makes more sense now—all of it happening at once, none of it quite linear. He feeds the negative-space cats, leaving bowls of milk that empty themselves in the presence of absence. The retirement home occupies seven different locations in the same building, its residents visiting younger versions of themselves, offering advice that creates the very circumstances that lead to them offering advice.

At night—when night means anything in a place where three different suns set simultaneously—they all feel it: the pull of the origin building, the gravitational center of their folded world. Some make pilgrimages, walking through their own past to reach a building that's always falling, always standing. They leave offerings: probability crystals that form from concentrated intention, poems written in languages that will exist, photographs of things that definitely happened somewhere.

They are changing, all of them. Becoming something that can thrive in compressed space, in folded time. Their children's children will be even stranger—humans who think in quantum superposition, who love in probability clouds, who exist as living calculations in the great computational mass of the Quarter. Evolution accelerating in a space where time runs in all directions at once.

The city beyond the Quarter watches with fascination and fear. But the Probability Dancers don't look back. They're too busy becoming what they already are, what they might be, what they never were but could have been—all states true, all states necessary, in the folded space they call home.
