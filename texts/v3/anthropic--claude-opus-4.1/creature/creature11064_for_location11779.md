The moths have neighbors in this breathing darkness—things that came after the first collapse but before the moth dominion solidified into its current empire of wings.

Deepest in the galleries live the thread-weavers, creatures that might once have been spiders before the limestone changed them. Their bodies are translucent as cave fish, showing dark knots of organs through pearl-pale skin stretched over too many joints. They've lost their eyes entirely, the sockets sealed over with smooth chitin that reflects sound. Eight legs, yes, but also sensing-hairs grown so long they trail like whiskers through the air, reading the moth-wind patterns, mapping the cave's breath through vibration alone.

The thread-weavers don't hunt the moths—not anymore. Evolution taught them a different economy. They position themselves beneath the great silk hammocks the moths have built, patients waiting for the rain of scales and broken wings, the constant detritus of a million bodies in perpetual motion. Their webs are not for catching but for gathering, vast funnels of silk so fine it's invisible until moisture beads along its length. These collecting webs channel the moth-dust into careful caches, protein stores the thread-weavers guard with territorial fury. 

They click when they move, their joints popping with each careful step. The sound serves as echolocation in the darker galleries where even the moths fly less frequently. Sometimes you can hear them calling to each other in percussion patterns, territory markers tapped out in the arithmetic of their footfalls.

In the middle depths, where the occasional light shaft creates gradients between pure darkness and wan illumination, the root-seekers make their homes. These are things that might be mammals—they have fur, though it grows in strange directions, following the airflow patterns of the cave rather than any biological logic. Their bodies are elongated, ferret-like but larger, with foreclaws evolved into living dowsing rods, bone and keratin shaped for detecting moisture through limestone.

The root-seekers remember sunlight in their genes. They follow the ghost-traces of roots that penetrate from above, roots that die in darkness but leave chemical trails in the stone. With infinite patience, the root-seekers excavate tiny galleries along these paths, following them up toward the surface, sometimes spending years on a single tunnel that may only reach a few feet before the trail goes cold. Their galleries honeycomb the cave walls, abandoned excavations that whistle when the moth-wind blows through them, playing the limestone like a vast organ.

They feed on the pale vegetation, yes, but also on something else—minerals they lick directly from the stone with tongues evolved rough as files. Their saliva is mildly acidic, enough to release calcium and other nutrients from the limestone itself. Where they've fed heavily, the cave walls are sculpted into organic whorls and hollows, looking almost decorative, almost intentional.

The root-seekers live in small family groups, never more than three or four, their territories marked by scent but also by sound—they sing, or something like singing, a vibration produced not in the throat but in specialized chambers in their elongated skulls. The songs carry through limestone better than through air, messages that travel through the stone itself to others of their kind miles away. At dawn and dusk—though they cannot see the sun, they somehow know—the caves resonate with their chorus, a sound felt more than heard, making the moth-masses ripple in sympathetic frequency.

Near the surface, in those chambers where the light shafts are strongest, live things that have no proper name. They might be plants that learned to move, or animals that learned to photosynthesize. Their bodies are translucent green, the color coming from symbiotic algae that live in their skin. During the brief hours when sunlight angles directly down through the sinkholes, they unfurl like flowers, spreading wide membrane wings that are neither leaf nor flesh but something between.

These light-drinkers spend most of their time dormant, clustered around the light shafts like barnacles, their bodies contracted into hard, seed-like pods that can survive weeks without illumination. But when the light comes, they transform—inflating with gathered moisture, their membranes stretching until they're thin as soap bubbles, every cell positioned to capture photons. They don't eat in any conventional sense; the algae in their skin convert light to sugar, and they share this bounty with their host in exchange for protection and mobility.

When the light fades, some of them detach and drift deeper into the caves, carried on the moth-winds, their bodies gradually consuming their stored energy as they search for new light shafts to colonize. Many die in the darkness, their empty membranes joining the organic rain that feeds the deeper ecosystem. But some find new perches, new angles of sun, and the cycle continues.

Between all these creatures exists a careful balance. The thread-weavers' abandoned web-silk becomes scaffolding for the lichens. The root-seekers' excavations create new spaces for moths to breed. The light-drinkers' carcasses enrich the thin soil where the pale vegetation grows. Even the moth-mass itself has become habitat—smaller creatures live their entire lives in the space between wings, parasites evolved so specifically for this niche that they would die instantly in any other environment.

And in the very deepest places, where even the moths rarely venture, where the darkness is absolute and the silence breaks only for the distant percussion of thread-weaver speech, there are things that might have been human once. Or might still be human, depending on your definition.

They've been here long enough that their eyes have clouded to milk-white, useful only for detecting the difference between absolute darkness and the faintest suggestion of less-than-absolute darkness. Their skin has thinned to parchment, showing blue veins like underground rivers. They don't speak—their vocal cords have atrophied—but they sing in harmonics that match the root-seekers' stone-songs, a communication that bypasses language entirely.

These deep-dwellers know every passage, every chamber, every breathing rhythm of the caves. They move through the darkness with perfect confidence, their fingers reading the limestone like braille, finding the handholds that nature carved and the ones that tools—or fingers—wore into the stone over patient centuries. They tend gardens of the pale vegetation, cultivation so slow it's almost geological, encouraging certain growths, discouraging others, maintaining patches that might be decorative or might serve purposes comprehensible only to minds adapted to this underworld.

They feed on the moths, but ritually, carefully, taking only what the population can spare. They've learned to read the moth-mass like weather, understanding from the rhythm of wings when the population is healthy, when it's stressed, when it needs to be left alone to recover. In return—though the moths cannot consciously reciprocate—the deep-dwellers maintain the cave system itself, clearing blocked passages that would disrupt airflow, removing diseased sections of lichen that could spread and destabilize the food chain.

Sometimes they climb toward the light shafts, not to escape but to remember. They sit just outside the circles of illumination, letting their clouded eyes register the ancient sensation of brightness, their skin remember warmth. They never enter the light fully—the pain would be unbearable now—but they remember it, or remember remembering it, a chain of memory stretching back to when they first chose the darkness, or when the darkness chose them.
