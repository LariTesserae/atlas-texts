The inhabitants move through their city like antibodies through a bloodstream, though the comparison fails to capture their intentionality. They are roughly human in silhouette—bipedal, bilateral symmetry, the right number of limbs in approximately the right places—but the resemblance ends at distance. Up close, their skin appears quilted, composed of hundreds of hexagonal patches that shift between states: sometimes hard as beetle shell, sometimes soft as rose petals, sometimes permeable as gauze. Each patch responds independently to chemical gradients in the air, creating patterns that ripple across their bodies as they walk—communication, identification, and temperature regulation all at once.

They have no eyes as such. Where eyes might be, clusters of photosensitive nodules form and dissolve, their arrangements shifting based on the spectrum they need to perceive. In the city's eternal twilight, they usually maintain configurations sensitive to infrared and the faint cyan glow of the vertical farms. When they need to examine something closely, the nodules congregate, forming temporary compound lenses that can resolve details down to the molecular. When they rest, the nodules disperse across their skin, leaving their faces smooth as eggs.

Their breathing syncs with the city's rhythm—not through conscious effort but through years of symbiotic adaptation. Modified spiracles line their throats, filtering the spore-thick air, extracting not just oxygen but information: who has passed this way, what the building is digesting, whether the morning's rain carried messages from upstream. They exhale contributions to the conversation—chemical markers that indicate their intentions, their destinations, their metabolic state. Privacy, as humans understood it, became impossible generations ago; secrecy survives only in the grammar of chemical combination, messages hidden in the syntax of pheromone release.

Children are rare and precious, gestated not in bodies but in the nursery towers where the city's intelligence can monitor their development. They emerge as small, translucent versions of their parents, their patch-skin still learning its configurations. For their first years, they are more liquid than solid, able to flatten themselves against walls to absorb nutrients directly, or stretch between surfaces like living bridges. The city recognizes them through their undefined chemical signatures and adjusts itself around them—doorways dilate wider, floors become more porous, air pressure gentles. They learn language not through sound but through taste, licking the bacterial films that coat every surface, absorbing the chemical histories written there.

The adults work, though "work" poorly describes their activities. They are gardeners of abstract gardens, tending to processes rather than plants. One might spend days adjusting the pH in a single building's digestive chambers, not through tools but through their own chemical secretions, sweating precise solutions that cascade through the architecture's metabolism. Others navigate the xylem transport tubes, their bodies temporarily liquefying to merge with the flow, guiding nutrients and messages to where they're needed. The most experienced can interface directly with the city's bacterial consciousness, their patch-skin forming temporary neural bridges, thinking thoughts that require architectural scale to complete.

They eat rarely but elaborately. Food is information as much as nutrition—engineered fruits that release memories when digested, fungal preparations that alter perception for days, distillations of the river's molecular messages that bring news from settlements they'll never visit. Meals are communal, not from social necessity but from biological requirement: certain nutrients only become accessible when multiple digestive systems work in concert, their combined enzyme production unlocking complex molecular structures. They sit in circles, their abdomens translucent during digestion, watching the rainbow shimmer of chemicals breaking down, sharing the experience through synchronized pheromone release.

Their relationships form through chemical compatibility rather than choice, though the distinction blurs when consciousness itself is largely molecular. Attraction registers as harmonious pheromone interference patterns, love as a gradual synchronization of metabolic cycles. Partnerships might last days or decades, might involve two individuals or twelve, might shift between configurations as naturally as their patch-skin changes texture. They touch constantly but not affectionately—every contact is information transfer, chemical samples exchanged through permeable skin, updates on health and mood and the thousand small maintenances their bodies require.

The elderly don't age so much as concentrate, their bodies becoming denser with accumulated adaptations. Their patch-skin grows more sophisticated, capable of chemical synthesis the young can't achieve. Some eventually root, their lower bodies fusing with buildings, becoming architectural advisors whose consciousness spans both biological and structural systems. Others sublime into the bacterial films, their consciousness fragmenting into millions of calculations performed across the city's surface. Death, when it comes, is less ending than translation—the body consumed by the city's digestive chambers, its molecules redistributed, its chemical memories archived in the spore-speech that drifts between buildings.

They dream collectively, their sleeping bodies releasing synchronized pheromones that create shared experiences. In these dreams, they are the city—feeling the river's flow through foundation roots, tasting sunlight through photosynthetic membranes, thinking the slow thoughts of buildings growing toward resources. Sometimes they dream of the before-times, genetic memories of when their ancestors lived in cities of stone and metal, but these dreams feel thin, impossible, like trying to remember being water before learning to be ice.

They have no names that would translate to sound. Identity is a chemical signature, unique as a fingerprint but constantly evolving—modified by diet, by partnership, by the buildings they inhabit, by the work they perform. To know someone is to recognize the particular way they disturb the air, the specific cascade of molecular reactions their presence initiates. Introductions involve careful chemical exposure, each individual releasing identity markers in controlled sequences, building familiarity through repetition rather than declaration.

Their rituals center on the city's maintenance, though the boundary between ritual and necessity has long since dissolved. When the photosynthetic membranes require replacement, they gather in hundreds, their bodies secreting the proteins needed for new growth, their collective exhalations guiding the membranes into place. When buildings reproduce, budding new structures from their bases, the inhabitants dance—if movement coordinated by pheromone trails can be called dancing—their patterns guiding the growth, ensuring the new building integrates properly with existing infrastructure.

They make art, though no museum could display it. Compositions of scent that unfold over days, requiring viewers to follow trails through the city to experience the full sequence. Bacterial paintings that evolve, their colors shifting as engineered microorganisms compete and cooperate. Sculptures of living tissue that respond to observers, growing new forms based on the chemical signatures of whoever approaches. Their music is felt rather than heard—pressure waves too low for human ears, vibrating through the city's structures, creating harmonics in the respiratory pores that translate to emotional experiences for those attuned to receive them.

Language itself has evolved beyond words. They communicate in chemical cascades that convey not just meaning but context, emotion, and sensory experience simultaneously. A single pheromone release might carry the equivalent of paragraphs—layered information that unfolds in the receiver's consciousness over minutes or hours. Misunderstandings are rare but profound when they occur, usually resulting from metabolic incompatibilities that prevent proper chemical processing. They have no written language as such, but the bacterial films remember everything, storing conversations in molecular arrangements that can be accessed by those who know the chemical keys.

The young adults sometimes scale the tallest towers at the delta's edge, climbing until they reach the membrane canopy. There, where the city's influence thins, they can sometimes smell the outside world—the wild marshes, the unmodified river, the air that carries no messages. Some find it terrifying, that emptiness. Others find it exhilarating. A few leave, their patch-skin hardening into permanent armor, their spiracles closing to all but the most basic atmospheric exchange. The city doesn't stop them, doesn't judge. Those who leave are mourned through chemical markers that linger for months, their absence a negative space in the pheromone landscape that gradually fills with new patterns, new inhabitants, new configurations of life.

At night—or what passes for night in the eternal twilight—they gather on the breathing walls, their bodies glowing faintly with bioluminescent bacteria that colonize their skin. They share the day's experiences through touch and scent, their patch-skin forming temporary connections that allow direct chemical transfer. Children practice their configurations, their skin cycling through textures and permeabilities. Adults perform the small maintenances their symbiotic relationships require—feeding the bacteria that aid their digestion, grooming the fungal networks that enhance their chemical sensitivity, adjusting the pH of their secretions to match the city's current needs.

They have no concept of ownership beyond the molecular—the chemicals they produce, the patterns they contribute to the city's consciousness, the small sphere of air they disturb with their presence. Everything else is shared, from the buildings that reshape themselves around whoever needs them to the gardens whose produce belongs to whoever's digestive chemistry can best process it. Theft is impossible when every surface tastes the chemical signature of whoever touches it. Violence is rare and self-limiting—aggressive pheromones trigger immediate architectural response, walls secreting calming compounds, floors becoming adhesive to prevent movement, the city itself intervening to maintain its careful chemical balance.

Some specialize in reading the river's messages, their bodies adapted to process the molecular communications that flow between settlements. They spend days partially submerged at the delta's edge, their patch-skin forming gill-like structures, their consciousness drifting between their own thoughts and the vast, slow conversation of the watershed. They return with news that takes hours to fully convey—chemical stories of upstream cities growing in new directions, of wild zones where the original ecosystem persists, of something vast and patient stirring in the deep delta waters, something that might be another city or might be what cities dream of becoming.

The most honored among them are the memory keepers, individuals whose bodies have been modified to store and process the city's chemical history. Their patch-skin is more complex, capable of synthesizing molecules that haven't existed for decades, recreating the exact chemical signature of long-dead inhabitants or forgotten events. They are living libraries, their metabolism devoted to maintaining archives that exist nowhere else. When they die—a process that can take months as their bodies slowly release their stored information—the entire city grieves, flooding the air with pheromones of loss that can be detected miles downstream.

They reproduce rarely and deliberately, gathering in groups to combine genetic material in careful patterns, their bodies releasing clouds of modified gametes that the nursery towers collect and nurture. Parenthood is communal—everyone who contributed genetic material shares responsibility, though the city itself handles most of the early development. They visit their offspring in the towers, pressing against the translucent growing chambers, their chemical signatures teaching the developing consciousness what it means to be alive in this place, to be both individual and component, to breathe with ten million lungs and think with a bacterial brain that spans a city.

This is who they are: no longer human, not yet something else, suspended in a biological present that has no need for the past's definitions or the future's promises. They are what happens when evolution accelerates, when consciousness discovers it doesn't require neurons, when cities learn to tend themselves and shape their tenders in return. They are citizens in the truest sense—not just inhabitants but components, their bodies and minds integrated so thoroughly with their environment that separation would mean not exile but dissolution.

They live. They breathe. They grow. And in growing, they become more themselves—whatever that means in a place where identity is chemical, where thought is bacterial, where the boundary between self and city dissolves a little more with each breath, each day, each gentle rain that falls on the breathing walls of their architectural body.
