The people of the plateau move through their days with the unhurried precision of those who have learned to read water's moods. They are not tall—the constant bending to tend channels and gardens has shaped them compact and flexible, with strong hands that know the texture of wet stone and the grain of water-swollen wood. Their skin carries the plateau's patina: a permanent dewiness, as if they've just emerged from bathing, and a faint greenish cast where spores and algae have made peace with human flesh.

They dress in layers that can be shed or added as the mist thickens and thins. The outer garments are woven from something that sheds water—perhaps the fibers of a plant that grows lower on the mountain, beaten and treated with oils until it gleams like seal skin. Beneath, they wear softer things that wick moisture away, garments that dry quickly in the brief windows of sunshine. Everyone goes barefoot or in simple sandals that grip wet stone. Leather would rot here; instead they braid their footwear from the same water-resistant fibers, replacing them seasonally like the city replaces its wooden bones.

Children learn early to move like herons, testing each step, reading the shine on stone that means slickness. They play in the channels, floating leaf boats between buildings, their games teaching them the flow patterns they'll need to know as adults. By five, they can tell by sound alone whether a cistern needs clearing, whether a channel runs true. They grow up amphibious, equally comfortable in the shallow pools and on the raised walkways, their bodies adapting to the constant humidity until dry air feels foreign and harsh to their lungs.

The work here is water work, garden work, maintenance work. Each family tends specific channels, responsible for keeping them clear of the algae that would otherwise choke everything in green slime. Young adults spend their mornings walking the stone grooves with long-handled tools, scraping and guiding, adjusting the small wooden gates that direct flow to different cisterns. The sound of scraping stone is as common as birdsong would be elsewhere—except there is no birdsong here, only the human sounds of cultivation and care.

The elders' joints swell with the moisture, but they've developed a medicine from the fungi that fruit in the shadowed places, a tea that tastes of earth and storms but eases the ache. They sit in the covered spaces during the worst of the daily rains, weaving baskets from the reeds that grow in the bog pockets, their fingers still nimble despite their swelling. They remember when each building was raised, who fitted which beam, whose children helped carry the foundation stones.

Food comes from water and what water feeds. The taro roots grow fat in the managed bogs, their leaves broad as umbrellas. The bamboo shoots are harvested young and tender. Mushrooms fruit in carefully tended logs, and the tree ferns' fiddleheads are gathered before they unfurl. They've learned to ferment in this wet place—vegetables pickled in stone jars, sealed with wax and weighted down in the cool pools beneath buildings. The fermentation adds sourness to combat the green sweetness that pervades everything.

Cooking happens in covered pavilions with raised hearths, the smoke directed up through clever chimneys that draw even in the still, humid air. The fuel is precious—wood dried slowly in the few truly sheltered spaces, or charcoal brought up from below. Every family maintains their fire carefully, banking coals, sharing embers with neighbors whose fires have died. The smell of smoke is comfort here, proof against the constant damp.

Their social fabric weaves like water finding its path—fluid but purposeful. Disputes are settled by the eldest of each district, but real authority lies with the water-keepers, those who understand the full network of channels and can predict where a blockage above will flood a garden below. These people, neither purely male nor female in their roles, move between districts with their apprentices, teaching the songs that help remember which gate to open in the dry season, which channel to clear before the afternoon storms.

Marriage happens when two people decide to tend the same gardens. The ceremony involves digging a new channel together, connecting two family plots, water from both sides meeting in the middle. Children belong to the whole district, raised by whoever has dry hands at the moment, learning from anyone willing to teach. The plateau makes orphans of no one—water touches every threshold equally.

They mark time by the growth of moss, by the replacement of beams, by the generations of tree ferns that rise and fall in the courtyards. Their oldest stories tell of the mountain's last anger, how the first settlers waited at the edge of the heat until the stone cooled enough to walk on, how they found the first springs seeping through the young rock and knew this would be home. They have no gods exactly, but they leave offerings at the largest springs—flowers that dissolve in the mineral water, becoming part of the mountain's slow circulation.

At night, they gather in the largest covered spaces, sharing fermented drinks that fizz slightly, made from the wild gingers and sweetened with tree fern sap. They play music on instruments that resist the damp—stone chimes that ring in pentatonic scales, drums with heads of that water-shedding fiber stretched over ceramic frames, voices harmonizing in songs that echo off the wet stone. The music has a liquid quality, notes sliding into each other, rhythms that follow the pattern of drops falling from eaves.

Their art is ephemeral by necessity. They arrange stones in the shallow pools, creating patterns that last until the next storm redistributes them. They train vines into living sculptures that grow and change with the seasons. They carve soft wood knowing it will rot, be replaced, the pattern continuing but never identical. Nothing they make is meant to last forever; everything is practice for the next iteration.

Death comes gently here, usually. Bodies are laid in the highest pools, where the mineral water preserves them long enough for the proper observances. Then they're carried to the volcanic caves lower on the mountain, where the earth is still warm, and placed in niches carved from the stone. The mountain takes them back, digests them in its slow geological metabolism. The living visit these caves during the dry season, when the paths are passable, leaving offerings of fresh water from the plateau springs, completing a cycle.

The youngest generation has started building higher, adding fourth stories to some buildings, reaching above the mist line. They've developed new techniques for waterproofing, learned from traders who sometimes climb the long path from the lowlands. Some elders worry this ambition disturbs the balance, but others remember their own parents saying the same when they first cut channels through solid stone instead of following natural seeps.

They dream of water, all of them, but not drowning dreams. They dream of swimming through air thick as pools, of breathing moisture like fish breathe rivers, of their children's children tending gardens they planted in their sleep. They wake to the sound of rain on their living roofs and know exactly which sedge needs thinning, which succulent has grown too heavy for the beams beneath.

In illness, they seek the dry spaces—the few caves where the volcanic rock sealed itself completely, where the air grows thick and still but loses its moisture. They rest there until their lungs clear, then return to the wet world that claims them. The truly sick are carried down the mountain to healers who understand dry medicine, but most return eventually, unable to sleep without the sound of water finding its way through stone.

This is how they are: a people made half of water, half of waiting. They move through their city like its blood, following channels worn by use and necessity, pooling in the common spaces, cycling through the same patient maintenance that keeps the boundary between human and wild precisely where it needs to be. They are as permanent and impermanent as the gardens they tend, rooted in volcanic stone but shaped by water's will, growing always toward a light filtered green through leaves and mist.
