The Tenders of the Weave are a people of measured movements and whispered intentions. They stand no taller than necessary, their bodies compact and efficient from generations of navigating the precise spaces between vine and void. Their skin carries a faint green undertone, chlorophyll-touched from centuries of handling the living architecture, and their fingers have evolved an extra joint—subtle but essential for the intricate work of guiding growth without forcing it.

They wear their hair in elaborate knots that mirror the weave beneath their feet, each pattern a family signature passed down through teaching hands. The styles never change; a daughter's braids match her great-grandmother's exactly, though neither may have met. Time pools differently in their blood—a Tender might remain thirty for what feels like decades, then age to sixty in a season, though the population's proportion of young to old never shifts.

Children are born knowing how to read the vines. Not taught—knowing. They emerge from their mothers with eyes already tracking the subtle pulse in the floor-weave that indicates where water will next emerge, which fruits will ripen in the eternal next-moment. By five, they can taste a vine's fiber and know exactly how many years of twisting will bring it to its perfect load-bearing curve. By ten, they work alongside their elders in the unchanging morning, their small fingers ideal for guiding the newest growth into patterns laid down before memory.

The eldest among them, the Shepherds of Stillness, no longer speak aloud. They communicate through touch alone—a hand placed on a vine sends their intentions traveling through the entire weave, understood by any other Tender in contact with the floor. These ancients spend their days in the deep-woven chambers, spaces where the vines have been cultivated so densely that air barely passes through. Here they tend to the Memory Gardens, where every event that has ever occurred on the terraces grows as a living tableau—wedding vines twisted into the shape of dancing figures, funeral moss that spreads in the exact pattern of a grandmother's dying breath, fruit trees whose bark bears the scars of every harvest in perpetual healing.

Work is worship is maintenance. Each morning-that-never-ends, the Tenders walk their inherited routes, checking the specific vines their bloodline has always checked. They carry tools carved from their own ancestors' walking sticks—wood that has been dried and shaped and worn smooth, then planted again to grow new handles when the old finally splinter. A pruning knife might contain wood from forty generations of use and regrowth, its blade ground from the iron-hard seedpods of the undergrowth, sharpened on stones pulled up from the forgotten earth below in the time before the weave was complete.

They sing, but not with voices. The Tenders have learned to make the vines themselves resonate by stroking them in particular patterns. Each terrace section has its own chord, and workers moving through their day create accidental harmonies that drift across the white-bright distances. Children learn their family's songs before they learn to speak—not the words, which were forgotten long ago, but the hand-patterns that make the weave itself hum their lineage into being.

The beetle-keepers form their own caste, marked by the scars where their partner insects have gripped too tightly in affection or urgency. Each keeper bonds with a single beetle from its larval stage, feeding it their own blood mixed with vine sap until the creature's carapace carries swirls of both green and red. These paired beings develop an understanding beyond language—the keeper knows when their beetle has found a weakness in the weave three terraces away, the beetle knows when its keeper dreams of falling through the floor into the humid darkness below. When one dies, the other usually follows within days, though their bodies show no sign of injury or illness.

In the spaces between cultivation, where the bare weave stretches wide, the Tenders gather for their markets of sameness. They trade identical fruits for identical fruits, exchange tools that vary only in their wear patterns, barter services that will be performed exactly as they have always been performed. The economy is ritual, not necessity—everyone already has everything they need, grown from their own gardens or woven from their own vines. But the act of exchange maintains something essential, a circulation that keeps the terraces from total stillness.

The water-readers walk apart from the others, following the impossible paths of moisture as it moves through its borrowed gravity. They can predict where the next upward rain will occur, when a hovering sphere of water will suddenly remember how to fall. These Tenders wear clothes of woven air-root, material that stays perpetually damp, and their skin prunes permanently from constant moisture. They tend the deep pools, those woven basins where the water gathers thick enough to swim, and it's said they sometimes dive down through the bottom of these pools, emerging in other pools entirely, having traveled through the water-paths that run impossible routes through the vine-flesh itself.

Children play games that have no beginning or end—hiding in the same spots their parents hid, their grandparents hid, will hide, are hiding. They chase the shadow-patterns that slide independent of the light, trying to catch them in woven baskets that are always just slightly too slow. They raise caterpillars that will become the moths that pollinate the night-blooming vines, though night never comes, and the moths emerge on a schedule known only to themselves, their wings carrying patterns that map parts of the terrace that don't exist yet, or anymore, or at all.

The Tenders dream collectively. When they sleep—and they must sleep, despite the eternal morning—their minds join in the under-dark, the humid space beneath the weave where their consciousness mingles with the root-thought of the vines themselves. In these dreams, they are the terraces, feeling every footstep as a tickle, every harvest as a gentle plucking, every new growth as an itch requiring guidance. They wake knowing things they shouldn't—that a cousin thirty terraces away has discovered a new fruit variation, that the beetles are gathering in unusual numbers near the eastern edge, that somewhere, deep in the most ancient part of the weave, vines are beginning to remember what it was like to be wild.

Romance here is slow and certain. Couples court for decades that feel like days, their affection measured in the gardens they plant together, the vines they train to embrace. Children come rarely but regularly, exactly replacing those who fade into the white-bright distance, walking out along the terraces until they simply aren't anywhere anymore. The Tenders don't mourn these departures—they know the disappeared will return, are returning, have already returned as the next generation, wearing new faces but carrying the same careful hands.

When outsiders come, if outsiders come, the Tenders receive them with a hospitality that feels like being slowly absorbed. They offer fruit that tastes like memory, water that runs through you in non-linear paths, shelter in pods that adjust themselves to your dreams. Stay long enough, and you'll find your fingers developing that extra joint, your eyes beginning to track the pulse in the weave, your voice fading as you learn to sing through the vines instead. The terraces accept all who accept them, transforming newcomers into natives through a process as patient and certain as the heat that rises eternally through the living floor.

The only fear the Tenders know is the dream of unraveling—that one day someone will pull the wrong vine, or forget the proper maintenance, and the entire weave will begin to undo itself, dropping everyone through into the darkness below where the too-many-legged things wait with patient hunger. But even this fear is ritual, inherited, repeated without real belief. The weave has always been. The morning has always burned white. The Tenders have always tended.

In the centers of their pods, each family keeps a single object from before the weave—a stone, a bone, a piece of metal that serves no purpose anyone can remember. These relics are polished daily, sung to, shown to children who will show them to their own children in the same way, with the same words, though the meaning was lost so long ago that its absence has become a kind of meaning itself. The objects are proof that there was a before, even if that before is unreachable, unthinkable, unnecessary in this eternal moment of managed growth and perfect repetition.

The Tenders are not happy, not unhappy. They are complete, each one a necessary thread in the pattern that maintains the pattern that is themselves. They love as vines love sunlight—constantly, quietly, without question. They work as water works—finding the prescribed path, following it perfectly, arriving where they must. And in the white-bright morning that never breaks into noon, they continue their ancient labor of keeping the impossible stable, the unnatural natural, the terraces themselves.
