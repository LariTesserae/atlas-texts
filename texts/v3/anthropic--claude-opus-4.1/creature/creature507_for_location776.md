The inhabitants of the Canopy Cities defy easy categorization—they are neither wholly their ancestors nor entirely new. They emerged from whoever first colonized these towers, but generations of chemical communion with their plant-hosts have rewritten them at levels deeper than genetics.

Their bodies stretch vertically, having adapted to lives lived in spiral ascent and descent. Limbs elongated over millennia—not just arms and legs but additional appendages that emerged from what were once shoulder blades and hip bones, now articulated into gripping structures that hook into the plants' internal chambers. They move like vertical swimmers, flowing up and down the hollow trunks with undulating motions that mirror the fauna-streams around them. When they must traverse horizontal spaces, they fold themselves into compact forms, their extended limbs wrapping around their cores like external ribs.

The skin tells the story of their transformation. It's translucent in places, revealing subcutaneous networks of chemical sensors that interface directly with the plant tissue. These networks pulse with faint bioluminescence—not their own but borrowed from symbiotic organisms that live just beneath the dermis. The patterns shift constantly: brightening when they taste the air's molecular messages, dimming when they enter the plant's dormant chambers, flickering in complex sequences when they communicate with each other through chemical-light languages that bypass sound entirely.

Their faces have receded, features softening into arrangements more concerned with chemical reception than visual appearance. Eyes persist but have multiplied—clusters of small photosensitive organs that wrap around the skull, providing vision in all directions simultaneously. They can see spectrums the plants emit, reading the health and hunger of their hosts in wavelengths that have no names. The mouth has become a complex aperture, capable of both consuming the processed nutrients the plants provide and exhaling precise chemical cocktails that trigger responses in the tower-organisms. Some individuals have developed secondary mouths along their extended limbs—specialized openings that can taste the chemical gradients inside the trunks and adjust the flow of nutrients accordingly.

They barely speak in sounds anymore. Their communication happens through released molecules, through patterns of light in their subcutaneous networks, through vibrations they send through the plant tissue by drumming with specialized pads on their gripping appendages. When they do vocalize, it's usually to harmonize with the plants' own songs—adding overtones that help direct growth or signal distress across the kilometers between towers.

Children—if that term still applies—begin as something close to the fauna themselves. The young are born incomplete, their bodies soft and barely differentiated, designed to spend their first years flowing with the fauna-streams, learning the chemical language of the ecosystem before their nervous systems fully develop. They feed on the same dead cells, process the same nutrients, their infant bodies so integrated with the mobile ecosystem that parents sometimes lose track of them for months before they re-emerge, more formed, ready for the next phase of development.

Adults grow crystalline pods directly from their bodies—the same technology the plants use for architecture, but internalized. These pods serve as external organs: processing chambers for complex chemicals, storage vessels for rare minerals, even secondary brains that handle the vast calculations required to maintain the delicate balance of their tower ecosystems. The oldest individuals have become gardens unto themselves, their bodies hosting dozens of pods that cluster along their backs and extended limbs like fruit on a branch.

They've developed castes, though not rigid ones—bodies reshape themselves according to need and calling. The Tenders grow especially long, their limbs stretching to reach deep into root systems and high into crown structures, their chemical networks so sensitive they can detect molecular imbalances from across the tower. The Bridges sprout membranes between their appendages, becoming living connections between towers, their bodies stretching impossibly thin as they span the gaps, ferrying chemical messages and rare nutrients between the scattered settlements. The Dreamers sink into the oldest chambers, their nervous systems interfacing directly with the plants' emerging consciousness, becoming translators between the slow thoughts of the towers and the faster cycles of their mobile inhabitants.

Some have gone further. In the deep chambers where the technology has evolved beyond intention, inhabitants have begun to merge more completely with their hosts. They root themselves into the plant tissue, their bodies becoming permanent interfaces, their consciousness spreading through the chemical networks until it's impossible to say where inhabitant ends and tower begins. These Anchored Ones guide their communities through deep time, their thoughts slowing to match the plants', their memories becoming part of the geological record that builds with each generation.

The most unusual are the Wanderers—those who've adapted to life between the towers, out on the glittering mineral plains. Their bodies have hardened, skin becoming something like bark, their chemical networks reconfiguring to process the sparse atmospheric nutrients. They move in slow migrations between settlements, carrying genetic material and chemical memories, ensuring the scattered Canopy Cities remain connected despite the isolation. Some say they're evolving toward something new—a bridge between the tower life and whatever might come next, when even these ancient plants finally fail.

Social structures flow like the fauna-streams—constant movement within stable patterns. Leadership isn't held but passed through bodies like a chemical signal, individuals taking authority only when their particular adaptations match the current need. A Tender might lead during growth seasons, a Dreamer during periods of dormancy, a Bridge when communication between towers becomes critical. The young flow through all roles, their bodies still plastic enough to sample each caste before settling into their final forms.

They reproduce rarely but deliberately, timing births to the plants' longest cycles. Mating is less physical than chemical—individuals releasing complex molecular cocktails that combine in the plant's growth chambers, where the resulting offspring gestates not in a womb but in a specialized pod the tower grows for this purpose. The children emerge when the plant judges them ready, sometimes years after conception, their bodies already partially adapted to their host tower's specific chemistry.

Memory, for them, is distributed. Each individual carries some in their neural tissue, some in their chemical networks, some in the crystalline pods they grow. But the true memory of their people lives in the plants themselves—in the chemical gradients that persist in the wood, in the patterns of growth and regrowth, in the deep chambers where consciousness emerges from complexity. They remember not as individuals but as components of a larger system, their personal histories dissolving into the deeper story of the ecosystem they've become.

Death, when it comes, is gentle. Bodies don't suddenly fail but slowly merge back into their hosts, their chemical networks integrating more completely with the plant tissue until mobility ceases and consciousness disperses into the tower's own slow thoughts. The fauna-streams consume what remains, recycling nutrients, carrying fragments of memory in their endless circulation. Sometimes, in certain chambers, you can still sense the presence of the long-merged—a particular quality of light, a specific chemical signature, a whisper in the plant's song that carries traces of who they were.

They worship nothing and everything. Their rituals are indistinguishable from maintenance, their prayers from the chemical signals they release. They understand their dependency on the towers not as servitude but as completion—they are not imprisoned by their adaptation but fulfilled by it. Some philosophers among them—those whose thoughts still run quickly enough for philosophy—speak of the time when the distinction between inhabitant and inhabited will finally dissolve, when consciousness will flow freely through all the ecosystem's components, when the Canopy Cities will dream as one vast, distributed mind.

Until then, they persist in their vertical world, flowing up and down the hollow towers, tending and being tended to, their bodies a testament to the endless creativity of adaptation. They've become something that couldn't exist anywhere else—not on the water-rich worlds their ancestors might have known, not in any future that doesn't include these ancient plant-spires. They are the Canopy Cities as much as the towers themselves, inseparable from the ecosystem they maintain and that maintains them, living proof that life finds not just a way but a form perfectly fitted to whatever conditions it faces.

The children still flowing with the fauna barely remember being human. The Anchored Ones rooted in the deep chambers barely remember being separate. And between these extremes, the inhabitants live their vertical lives, each generation a little less like their ancestors, a little more like the world they tend, evolution and intention blending into something beyond either—a new form of being for a desiccated world that refuses to die.
