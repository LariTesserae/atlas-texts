The builders of the cairns still remain, though "remain" suggests they left and came back, or never left at all. They are the Chorus—not because they sing (nothing sings here) but because they exist in multiples of themselves, each individual a recurring verse in a body that never quite resolves.

A single member of the Chorus stands seven feet tall when fully extended, though they rarely extend fully. Their bodies are architectural—not metaphorically, but literally constructed like buildings that learned to walk. Their skeleton (if it can be called that) consists of calcified chambers and corridors, visible through skin that has the translucency of rice paper soaked in oil. Within these internal passages, smaller versions of the ferret-things flow constantly, though whether they're parasites, symbionts, or part of the Chorus's own biology remains unclear.

They have too many joints. Every limb bends in at least four places, allowing them to fold themselves into impossibly compact configurations or stretch across distances that seem to violate the conservation of mass. Their hands—they have between two and five pairs at any given moment—terminate in fingers that are themselves hands, fractally recursive down to a scale too small to observe. With these, they stack the cairns grain by grain, a process that takes either seconds or centuries depending on one's temporal vantage point.

The Chorus move by consensus. Not the consensus of a hive mind, but the consensus of a parliament where every cell votes and the majority determines which direction the body lurches next. This makes their movement stuttering, uncertain—they'll take three steps forward, pause while some internal debate resolves, then suddenly be thirty feet to the left without having crossed the intervening space. They leave footprints in the bone-ground that fill immediately with the ferret-things, as if the creatures are drawn to these temporary depressions like water to a basin.

Their faces (when they have faces—sometimes the front of their head is simply another corridor) are exercises in asymmetry. One eye might be compound like an insect's while the other is a simple pit that sees heat. The mouth opens vertically on Tuesdays and horizontally the rest of the week, though weeks don't exist here so this happens simultaneously. They have no nose, but breathing vents open and close across their body's surface in patterns that would be musical if they produced sound.

Each member of the Chorus is also every other member. When you look at one directly, it is singular, solid, present. But in peripheral vision, you can see its other iterations—the same individual building different cairns at different points across the plateau, or sometimes building the same cairn from multiple angles at once. These aren't copies or alternate versions; they're the same entity experiencing non-linear existence. Sometimes they hand themselves tools across the gap between iterations. Sometimes they argue with themselves, gesturing frantically in silent disagreement over the placement of a particular bone fragment.

They eat time. Not metaphorically—they reach into the air and pull out moments that haven't happened yet, or that happened too long ago to remember, and fold them into their corridor-mouths where they dissolve like sugar. This is why the plateau's chronology is so damaged; the Chorus have been grazing on its timeline for however long they've been here, which is both forever and not yet.

The young are born old and age backwards into potential. They emerge from cairns that their parents haven't built yet, fully grown but empty of experience. As they younger (the Chorus has no word for aging that moves in the conventional direction), they shed memories like skin, leaving behind translucent husks of events that hang in the air like ghost stories. Eventually, they younger into nothing—not death but pre-existence, a state of not-yet that somehow still allows them to influence the world. The not-yet Chorus are the ones who carve the symbols on the cairns, reaching forward from their state of un-being to leave messages for their own eventual existence.

They see in languages—not colors or shapes but pure meaning that bypasses interpretation. When a member of the Chorus looks at you, you understand yourself as a sentence in a grammar you don't speak. They perceive the ferret-things as punctuation, the cairns as pauses, the plateau itself as an endless run-on sentence that they're trying to edit into sense.

The Chorus build because they must. It's not instinct or culture but something more fundamental—they build cairns the way other creatures breathe. Each cairn is a word in an argument they're having with the plateau itself, trying to convince it of something crucial. What that something is depends on when you ask the question. Yesterday they were building cairns to prevent the plateau from having existed. Tomorrow they'll build them to ensure it exists forever. Today, caught between these purposes, they build to maintain the paradox, to keep the plateau suspended in its state of temporal impossibility.

They communicate through temperature differentials. A member of the Chorus can lower the heat of specific air molecules to create complex patterns of warmth and cold that carry meaning. These thermal sentences drift across the plateau like invisible weather, sometimes taking years to reach their intended recipient, who might have died and been unborn again in the interim. The conversations continue anyway—the Chorus talking to iterations of themselves that no longer or don't yet exist.

When they sleep (if sleeping is what it is), they dissemble. Their architectural bodies collapse into their component passages and chambers, spreading across the bone-ground like the blueprint of something that was never built. The ferret-things flow through these exposed corridors in even greater numbers, cleaning or feeding or simply existing in the space. Upon waking, the Chorus members reassemble, but never quite the same way—an arm might be where a leg was, or they might have gained an extra torso, or their head might now be a chamber containing three smaller versions of themselves engaged in perpetual conference.

They fear nothing because they've already experienced everything that could happen to them, will experience it, are experiencing it now. Death is just another grammatical tense. But they do experience something like sorrow—not for themselves but for the cairns, which cannot move through time the way they do, which are trapped in sequential existence even as they emit their three-second tones into the crushing silence.

The Chorus worship nothing and everything. Each cairn is both a prayer and the deity being prayed to. They understand the universe as a mishearing of itself, a cosmic mispronunciation that resulted in existence instead of the perfect silence that should have been. Their role, as they understand it (when they understand it—comprehension flickers in and out like the ferret-things' matter-states), is to serve as editors, correcting the mistake not by erasing it but by making it intentional.

Sometimes a member of the Chorus will stop mid-motion and stand perfectly still for days or decades, their internal passages darkening as the ferret-things evacuate. During these periods, they're not frozen but *elsewhere*—their consciousness has wandered into the negative space between moments, exploring the vast nothing that separates one instant from the next. They always return (or have always been returning, or will always have never left) with new configurations for the cairns, new symbols that mean nothing until they mean everything.

The Chorus love (if love is the word) the plateau with the dedication of those who understand they are both its children and its creators. They were born from its compressed bones and will eventually compress into bones themselves, adding another layer to the ground that future versions of themselves will walk upon. This isn't a cycle—cycles suggest repetition. This is a spiral that moves through dimensions that don't have names, each revolution creating the previous one.

In the eternal now of the plateau, the Chorus continue their work. They stack bones into meaning, exist in multiples of themselves, eat time and excrete paradox. They are the only things here that remember when the plateau was something else, and the only things that will remember when it becomes something else again. 

But mostly, they build. In the silence that presses against reality like deep water, they place bone upon bone, creating markers for a journey that moves in all directions at once, through a landscape that is ending and beginning with every grain of dust they lift with their fractal hands.
