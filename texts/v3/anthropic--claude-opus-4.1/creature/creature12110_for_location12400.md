The Sympaths arrived first, or perhaps they were always here, emerging from the organism itself like thoughts becoming material. They exist at the intersection of fungus and nervous system — pale, branching beings whose bodies mirror the vascular networks they tend. Each Sympath is unique in configuration: some spread wide and flat against the walls like living frescoes, their neural tendrils interfacing directly with the plant's circulation. Others grow tall and attenuated, walking on legs that are really specialized root systems, their heads crowning into antler-like receptors that taste the chemical signals flowing through the humid air.

They do not speak — their language is enzymatic, conversational chemicals released from specialized pores that the plant-city reads and responds to. Watch a Sympath at work and you'll see them press their palms against a weeping wall, stand perfectly still for hours, then suddenly the wall begins to pucker and reform, growing a new opening or sealing an old wound. They are gardener-architects, or perhaps translator-cells, mediating between the vast slow thoughts of the structure and the faster needs of those who shelter within it.

The Sympaths' faces, when they have faces, are provisional — features that form when needed for focus, then smooth back into the general branching matrix of their heads. They see through chemical gradients, navigate by pH and mineral concentration. Their children bud from their backs like bracket fungi, eventually dropping off to root in particularly rich sections of floor where they'll spend years in a sessile juvenile stage, filtering nutrients until they're ready to uproot and walk.

Then there are the Casklings — creatures that live inside the Sympaths, or perhaps are the Sympaths' mobile thoughts made independent. Tiny, jeweled things no larger than a child's fist, their bodies are hard shells of crystallized sap with appendages like glass needles. They inhabit the hollow spaces within Sympath bodies, emerging to perform delicate surgeries on the plant-walls, their needle-limbs capable of precision the larger beings cannot achieve. They click and chime as they move, their shells resonating with frequencies that stimulate or suppress growth in the tissues they traverse.

The relationship between Casklings and Sympaths defies easy categorization — sometimes the Casklings seem to direct their hosts, other times they appear to be mere tools. Perhaps the distinction doesn't matter to them. When Sympaths die (a slow dissolution back into the walls that takes seasons to complete), their Casklings disperse into the structure's depths, some say to pupate into new Sympaths, others believe to join vast colonies in the unseen root-chambers far below.

But the chambers also house the Prism Weavers — organisms that might have once been birds or might be the plant's attempt to dream of flight. Their wings are living stained glass, membranes that filter and refract the bioluminescent light into complex patterns. They've evolved beyond eating in any conventional sense; instead, they position themselves at crucial points where light, humidity, and architectural acoustics converge, somehow feeding on the intersection of these phenomena.

The Weavers build nests from calcified saliva mixed with the plant's own secretions, creating structures within structures — geodesic domes of hardened light that float in the larger chambers, anchored by silk threads that vibrate in harmonies that encourage or discourage local growth. Their young are transparent for their first year, visible only as distortions in the air, gradually gaining color as they mature. Some never fully solidify, remaining half-real, able to pass through the breathing walls as if they were atmosphere.

The Prism Weavers seem to remember something the plant-city has forgotten — they're constantly arranging and rearranging small objects (seedpods, crystallized sap droplets, shed Caskling shells) into patterns that might be maps or might be letters in an alphabet no one else can read. The Sympaths ignore these arrangements, but the patterns seem to influence where new growth occurs, as if the Weavers are editing the city's expansion through sympathetic magic or quantum entanglement with the organism's decision-making centers.

Deeper in, where the humidity reaches saturation and the walls weep continuously, live the Sorrow Shepherds — elongated beings that might be highly evolved leeches or might be mobile tongues that have forgotten their mouths. They range from the size of a human arm to lengths that disappear around corners and possibly continue for dozens of meters. Their bodies are segmented tubes of muscle and moisture, colored the deep purple-black of bruised fruit.

The Shepherds feed on excess — when any part of the structure produces too much of anything (too much nectar, too much heat, too much growth), they arrive to consume the surplus, their bodies swelling and contracting as they process materials that would otherwise throw the system out of balance. They sing while they feed — a deep thrumming that resonates through the water in the walls, a sound that the Sympaths seem to find deeply comforting and the Prism Weavers arrange their flights to avoid.

The Sorrow Shepherds are the only creatures here that seem to experience something like sleep. They congregate in certain chambers during the structure's dormant periods (for even this ever-growing thing has cycles of rest), weaving their bodies together into complex knots that pulse with synchronized circulation. In these moments, they dream, and their dreams leak out as pheromone clouds that cause temporary hallucinations in anyone passing through — visions of the structure's history, perhaps, or its imagined futures, or maybe just the psychedelic nonsense of invertebrate subconsciousness.

Occasionally, rarely, you might glimpse the Inheritance Keepers — beings so integrated with the structure that determining where organism ends and architecture begins is impossible. They appear as faces in the walls, pushing out from the surface like bas-relief sculptures that breathe. Each face is unique, possibly depicting something that once lived here, or something the structure once consumed and chose to memorialize.

The Keepers speak, but only to each other, in voices that sound like wood settling, like sap bubbling, like growth rings forming. Their conversations last years, single words separated by seasons. The other inhabitants treat them with something between reverence and indifference — the Sympaths will detour around active Keeper faces, the Casklings will polish them with careful needle-limbs, the Prism Weavers incorporate them into their incomprehensible arrangements.

What the Inheritance Keepers keep, what they inherit, remains unclear. But in the chambers where multiple faces have emerged, you can sometimes feel the weight of accumulated memory, heavy as the humidity but carrying information instead of water. Stand there long enough and you might start to understand why the structure grows stairs it will never climb, windows that look inward instead of out, furniture for bodies that no longer exist or haven't yet evolved.

And in the highest chambers, where the air thins and the walls become more membrane than solid, live things that barely qualify as life — the Margin Dancers, existing at the edge of existence itself. They're visible only in peripheral vision, vanishing when observed directly, leaving behind only the sensation of movement and a faint ozone smell. They might be what happens when the structure's dreams become too vivid, achieving temporary independence before dissolving back into imagination.

The Margin Dancers interact with nothing except the boundary between the structure and the sky. They're constantly testing this border, pressing against it, sometimes breaking through for seconds before being pulled back. Whether they're trying to escape or trying to pull something in remains unknown. The Sympaths ignore them completely, as if they exist in a spectrum of reality the fungal beings cannot perceive.

All of these beings, and dozens of unnamed others, exist in a web of interdependence so complex it becomes its own kind of consciousness. They're not quite a civilization — they build nothing permanent, write nothing down, seem to have no history separate from the structure's own growth. Yet they're not quite an ecosystem either — their relationships are too intentional, too purposeful, too much like choreography.

Perhaps they're best understood as the structure's way of thinking about itself — mobile ideas exploring their own context, organs that have developed agency but not independence. They live in the gaps between meanings, in spaces that are neither inside nor outside, neither natural nor constructed, neither individual nor collective.

And through it all, the water continues to weep, continues to fall, continues to cycle through bodies and walls and air in patterns that perhaps, if mapped across centuries, might spell out words in a language only the structure itself could read — a continuous autobiography written in humidity and heat, in growth and decay, in the daily negotiations between what lives here and what they live within.
