In this vertical maze of industry and chlorophyll, the inhabitants have evolved beyond simple humanity. They call themselves the Cambrians, though whether they chose this name or the wood whispered it to them, none can recall.

Their bodies have stretched and thinned over generations, limbs elongating to match the reach between handholds. Their fingers and toes have developed a gecko-like adherence, skin secreting a resin that hardens and releases at will. When they sleep—hanging in their hammock-cocoons strung between walls—their bodies unconsciously grip their moorings, muscles locked in a sympathetic rhythm with the breathing wood around them.

The Cambrians' eyes have grown large and multifaceted, not like insects but like clustered berries, each lens attuned to different spectrums of the fungal phosphorescence. They see in gradients of decay and growth, reading the health of wood like others read weather. Their pupils dilate and contract independently, one tracking the slow crawl of sap-time while another watches the quick-tick of machine-time.

But it's their children who disturb visitors most. Born with fontanelles that never quite close, their skulls remain partially soft throughout life, allowing their brains to pulse visibly beneath translucent skin when they think too hard. The young ones instinctively know which walls will exist tomorrow and which will dissolve into cambium. They play games that involve predicting the city's growth, chalking elaborate equations on bark that solve themselves as the tree expands.

The eldest Cambrians have begun to photosynthesize. Green-black veins web beneath their papery skin, and they spend their dying years pressed against windows, feeding on what little filtered light reaches the city. They speak less and less, communicating instead through chemical releases that make nearby listeners taste colors. When they finally die, they don't decay—they germinate. Their bodies become small groves that their families tend, harvesting thoughts that grow as luminescent fruit.

The factory workers comprise a separate caste, their bodies adapted to interface with the breathing machines. Ports in their wrists connect directly to steam engines via umbilical tubes that exchange some substance neither blood nor oil. They work in shifts timed to their heartbeats—exactly 10,000 beats per shift, no more, no less. The machines have trained them to this rhythm, or perhaps they've trained the machines. During work, their consciousness splits: half remains in their skull, half disperses through the engine's chambers, experiencing combustion as emotion, pressure as memory.

The surveyors have developed a different adaptation entirely. Their inner ears have multiplied into complex gyroscopic organs that maintain perfect orientation regardless of local gravity. They walk on walls and ceilings with nauseating ease, their bodies constantly micro-adjusting to perpendicular realities. Years of mapping the unmappable has given them a prophetic quality—they dream in architectural blueprints that sometimes manifest as actual buildings. The city council has learned to monitor their sleep, transcribing their midnight mutterings into building codes.

The merchants who trade between districts have become partially liquid. Their bones have cartilaginized, allowing them to compress through spaces that shouldn't accommodate human passage. They flow through the city's forgotten ducts and maintenance tubes, their bodies elongating to twenty feet or compressing to the size of children as needed. Their organs rearrange during transit, heart sliding past lungs, liver inverting around stomach. They claim it doesn't hurt anymore, though their faces suggest otherwise.

A priesthood tends the root-ceiling, though they worship nothing so simple as gods. They've developed throats that can produce subsonic harmonics, voices that resonate at the exact frequency of cellulose growth. Their hymns are really growth instructions, sung to encourage certain root patterns that they insist spell out messages in a script only they can read. Their hair has become colonial, each strand a separate organism that feeds on air moisture and extends their sensory range throughout the cavern. When they gather in groups, their hair interweaves into temporary neural networks, allowing them to think collective thoughts too large for single skulls.

The children born near the factory districts occasionally emerge with brass fixtures instead of fingernails, small pressure gauges embedded in their palms, pupils that dilate in perfect circles like aperture blades. The medical workers don't consider this pathology anymore—it's simply another negotiation between flesh and industry, another way the city incorporates its inhabitants into its expanding definition of life.

In the deepest reaches where gravity fails entirely, a subspecies has emerged that no longer maintains fixed form. They exist as colonial organisms, their bodies able to separate into component parts that drift independently through the null spaces, reconvening only for reproduction or complex thought. Their children are born as clouds of cells that take years to decide on a shape, if they ever do at all.

All of them, regardless of adaptation, share one common trait: they've developed a secondary nervous system made of fungal threads that run parallel to their neurons. This mycelial network connects them to the city itself, allowing them to sense the great slow thoughts of the wood-mind above, to feel the dreams of machines, to taste the mathematics of growth. They are no longer simply inhabitants but components, organs in something vast and breathing that might be city, might be forest, might be organism, might be equation—categories that lost their distinction the moment the first root pushed through stone and decided to think.
