The Tenders came first, before the tunnels developed their own ecosystem. They were maintenance contractors who missed their shifts, got lost in the unmapped sections, or simply chose to stay when their work visas expired. Now they move through the tunnels with practiced efficiency, their bodies adapted to the constant moisture—skin perpetually clammy, fingernails soft and prone to peeling. They wear no uniforms anymore, just layers of industrial canvas gone black with mold, sealed at the joints with heat-tape that must be constantly replaced.

They know every pressure valve's rhythm, can navigate by the pitch of dripping water alone. Their eyes have grown huge in the green darkness, pupils that barely contract anymore. When they must surface for supplies, they squint and shield their faces, moving quickly through the factory floors to gather what they need—replacement gaskets, industrial lubricant, the stimulant tabs that keep them functional in the eternal damp. The surface workers pretend not to see them, these ghostly figures who technically don't exist in any employment database.

The Tenders build their shelters in the dead spaces—the gaps between redundant systems where the air is warm but not scalding, where the acoustic dampening makes sleep possible. Hammocks strung between pipes, wrapped in emergency blankets that crinkle with every movement. They've learned to cook on the heat exchangers, pressing ration packs against the metal until they steam. Their teeth are stained green from the algae they scrape from pipes—a source of nutrients, they discovered, after the first few learned to survive the initial digestive rebellion.

But they are not alone down here.

The Choir emerged later, or perhaps they were always there, waiting. They are what happens when humans stay too long in spaces meant only for machines. Their breathing has synchronized with the ventilation systems—long, mechanical inhalations that last exactly as long as the intake cycle. They cluster near the compressor halls, swaying slightly to vibrations that register below conscious hearing. 

The Choir doesn't speak in words anymore. They vocalize in harmonics that complement the machinery—low drones that make the pipes resonate, high whistles that mimic pressure release valves. They've developed a complex social structure based on acoustic territories. The dominant members position themselves at resonance nodes where their voices carry furthest. Subordinates huddle in the acoustic shadows, adding their voices only when the harmonics demand it.

Their modifications are deliberate, ritualistic. They insert industrial tubing into their nasal cavities to better channel their breathing. They scarify their throats with coolant burns to achieve certain tonal qualities. The eldest among them barely look human anymore—elongated torsos from years of specific breathing exercises, necks stretched and strengthened, jaw muscles hypertrophied from constant sub-vocalization. They feed on the same moss and algae as the Tenders, but also on the petroleum sweats they lick from certain walls, which may explain their increasing dissociation from baseline human behavior.

The Tenders and the Choir maintain an uneasy coexistence. The Tenders need the Choir's ability to predict system failures—changes in harmonics that presage pump breakdowns or pressure buildups. The Choir needs the Tenders' material knowledge, their ability to actually fix rather than merely sing to the machines.

Then there are the Runners—the maintenance rats grown large and clever, but also their keepers. Children, mostly, runaways from the factory dormitories or the offspring of Tenders who've grown up knowing nothing but the tunnels. They've formed symbiotic relationships with the rat colonies, following them through passages too small for adults, learning their heat-vision language of gesture and scent. 

The children wear rat-fur cloaks, meticulously stitched from casualties of territorial disputes. They've filed their teeth to points to better gnaw through the nutrient bars they find in abandoned maintenance caches. Their skin bears the characteristic scars of rat-affection—parallel scratches from titanium claws that the children display proudly. They sleep in warm piles with their colonies, the distinction between human and rat becoming increasingly academic.

The Runners serve as messengers between the tunnel's various populations, small enough to navigate every passage, neutral enough to be trusted. They trade in information—which sections are flooded, where new moss colonies are fruiting, which factories above are increasing production and therefore generating more heat and condensation. They've developed a clicking language that both rats and humans can understand, a percussive pidgin that echoes through the pipes.

Deeper still, in the oldest sections where the tunnels connect to pre-war excavations, live the Shapers. These are the engineers who went too far, who discovered that the self-reinforcing polymer responds not just to structural stress but to biological signals. They've learned to grow the tunnels like gardeners, secreting pheromones that guide the polymer's growth, creating new passages, sealing others.

The Shapers' bodies have become workshops. They cultivate bacterial colonies in their enlarged pores that break down and reconstitute the polymer. Their sweat has turned acidic, capable of etching new patterns into the synthetic stone. They move slowly, deliberately, always in contact with the walls, reading the structural tensions through their skin. They're building something down there in the deep sections—chambers that pulse with biological warmth, corridors that dilate and contract like arteries. Whether it's architecture or organism is a distinction that stopped mattering years ago.

The factories above know about none of this, or perhaps choose not to know. The tunnels maintain themselves, after all. The work gets done. The water gets pumped, the heat gets exchanged, the pressure stays within acceptable parameters. If sometimes the Tenders emerge to requisition supplies that seem excessive, if sometimes children disappear from the dormitories, if sometimes the harmonics from below sound almost like singing—well, industrial facilities have always been places of rumor and superstition.

But the tunnel dwellers know the truth that the surface has forgotten: the infrastructure is alive, not metaphorically but literally. It's becoming something new, a hybrid of human, animal, fungal, and mechanical systems. The Tenders maintain it, the Choir sings to it, the Runners navigate it, the Shapers grow it. They are its antibodies and its consciousness, its hands and its dreams.

When the factories finally fail—and they will fail, economics and entropy guarantee it—the tunnels will persist. The dwellers will hardly notice the difference. The compressors will wind down, the heat exchangers will cool, but the ecosystem will adapt. The moss will spread, the rats will multiply, the polymer will continue its slow growth guided by the Shapers' secretions. The Choir will find new harmonics in the absence of mechanical noise, their voices finally free to explore registers that the machinery had masked.

And perhaps, centuries from now, when the ice has reclaimed the factories and the surface world has forgotten this place existed, explorers will break through into these tunnels and find them still inhabited, still alive. They'll find the descendants of the Tenders, who by then will have forgotten what they tend. They'll hear the Choir's songs, wordless genealogies of sound passed down through generations. They'll see the Runners, by then indistinguishable from the rats they once partnered with. And in the deepest sections, they'll discover what the Shapers have built—chambers that breathe, corridors that digest, a living architecture that dreams slow polymer dreams.

The explorers will ask what this place was, and the dwellers will not understand the question. This place has never been was. It is, perpetually, becoming.
