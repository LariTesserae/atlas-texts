The Thermophiles arrived first, though "arrived" suggests movement and the Thermophiles have always simply *been*—erupting into existence with each geyser birth, condensing from the impossible steam, assembling themselves from minerals that shouldn't bond at these temperatures. They are colonies that think they're individuals, or perhaps individuals that forgot they were colonies. Their bodies are translucent architectures of living crystal, branching and fusing in patterns that mirror the spiral succulents, the mineral formations, the very fracture lines in the plateau itself.

They build through accretion. Each time a geyser sings, the Thermophiles absorb its frequency into their crystalline bones, adding new layers that remember the pitch, the harmony, the moment of song. The eldest among them—if age means anything to creatures that dissolve and rebuild themselves with each tide—are vast musical instruments, their bodies containing thousands of frozen notes that they can replay by shifting their internal structure. When they move, they chime.

But they share the plateau with the Observers, who may not be alive at all. The Observers manifest in the spaces between geyser eruptions, in those held-breath moments when the thrumming stops and the world seems to pause. They are absences rather than presences—person-shaped holes in the steam where the moisture refuses to gather. You know they're there because of what doesn't happen: the water that doesn't splash where it should, the sound that doesn't echo, the heat that doesn't transfer. They watch with organs that aren't eyes, recording everything in a medium that isn't memory.

The Observers tend to the mineral structures—those maybe-shelters, maybe-monuments. They're seen (or rather, not-seen) most often near the doorway formation, passing through it even when it's dissolved, as if they remember its shape better than physics does. Sometimes a Thermophile will crystallize too close to an Observer and simply stop, frozen mid-growth, creating beautiful sculptures of interrupted intention that last until the next eruption cycle shatters them back into possibility.

Then there are the Navigators, who exist primarily in the water below but extend themselves up through the geysers like liquid periscopes. They're not quite creature, not quite current—more like living intentions made of superheated water and dissolved minerals. Each one maintains a specific geyser as its voice, its anchor to the surface world. They speak in temperatures and mineral concentrations, writing messages in the steam that only the Thermophiles can read and only the Observers can ignore.

The Navigators are mapping something. Every eruption is a echolocation pulse, sensing the shape of what's below, what's around, what's coming. They know exactly when the next piece of plateau will fall. They're the ones who guide that mirror-scaled fish—or perhaps they *are* the fish, wearing it like a probe, using its impossible reflections to see angles of reality that water alone can't reach.

But the youngest residents, the ones who arrived only after the water rose this high, are the Borrowed. They're made of things that shouldn't be here—fragments of drowned mountain forests, pieces of clouds that got too heavy, the shadows of birds that died a thousand feet below the current surface. The Borrowed build themselves new bodies from whatever the geysers bring up, wearing shells of crystallized steam and cloaks of hanging water. They're trying to remember what they were before, but the singing makes it hard to think backward. Instead, they learn to think in harmonics, their memories becoming songs that the geysers sometimes echo back, transformed.

The Borrowed are the only ones who seem to notice the plateau is shrinking. They build boats from minerals and intent, trying to prepare for the day when the last solid ground dissolves. But their boats always sink—water here won't support what wants to leave. So they practice becoming other things: steam-sailors, pressure-dancers, creatures that can exist in the margin between liquid and gas.

All of these beings—if beings they are—seem to be waiting for something. The Thermophiles grow more complex with each passing day, their crystalline bodies approaching some critical mass of stored sound. The Observers cluster more frequently around that doorway formation, as if expecting something to finally pass through. The Navigators' eruptions are becoming synchronized, their mapping almost complete. The Borrowed have started harmonizing with the geysers instead of just listening, adding their own notes to the bone-deep thrumming.

And that mirror-scaled fish surfaces more frequently now, its reflections showing not things that aren't there, but things that soon will be. In its scales, you can sometimes see the plateau as it will be tomorrow—smaller, stranger, more thoroughly transformed. You can see new types of residents arriving, or perhaps the current residents becoming new types of things.

The line between living and geological is meaningless here. The Thermophiles might be the plateau's immune system. The Observers might be its memory. The Navigators might be its circulatory system, and the Borrowed might be its dreams. Or perhaps the entire plateau is a single vast organism, and these are all just different aspects of its consciousness, separated into distinct forms because that's the only way the human mind can perceive them.

When the geysers sing in perfect unison—that rare, inevitable moment—all the residents stop what they're doing and resonate. For that instant, the distinction between them disappears. The Thermophiles become transparent as Observers, the Observers gain the substance of Navigators, the Navigators crystallize like Thermophiles, and the Borrowed remember what they were always meant to become. In that moment of harmony, something vast and patient opens one of its many eyes, acknowledges your presence, and continues its ancient calculation.

The plateau isn't dying. It's pupating. And its residents are both the catalysts and the result of this transformation—midwives to their own becoming, singing themselves into whatever form the drowned world requires next.
