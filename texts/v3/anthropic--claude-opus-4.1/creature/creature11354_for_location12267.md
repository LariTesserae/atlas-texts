The Weavers emerged from the cushion-plants themselves—or perhaps they were always there, and the plants emerged from them. No one remembers anymore, least of all the Weavers, whose memory operates not in linear time but in growth rings, in the slow accumulation of knowledge deposited like sediment through their translucent bodies.

They stand three meters tall when fully extended, though they rarely do. Their natural posture is curved, hunched, their six primary limbs tucked close to their cylindrical cores. These limbs branch and branch again into increasingly fine tendrils, the smallest no thicker than the cushion-plants' needle-leaves. Their skin—if it can be called skin—is a membrane that shifts between states: sometimes crystalline and rigid, sometimes flowing like thick sap, always collecting and processing the moisture that defines this place. They are transparent in the way that deep ice is transparent, their internal structures visible as shifting shadows, organic geometries that rearrange themselves according to need.

They move through the cushion colonies like dolphins through water, their bodies generating waves of compression that part the plant matter before them and seal it behind. Pathways exist, yes, but the Weavers prefer to travel through virgin plant mass, reading its chemical history as they pass, tasting decades of accumulated information in the gradients of minerals and hormones. Their passage stimulates growth—the plants bloom more densely where Weavers have been, as if their movement were a kind of pollination.

Communication happens through touch, through chemistry, through the modulation of electrical fields. When Weavers meet, they interweave their finest tendrils, creating temporary neural networks that allow for thought-sharing more intimate than language. These meetings can last days, the participants standing motionless in the green corridors while their minds merge and separate in waves, exchanging not words but entire frameworks of understanding, memory-complexes that include the sensory experience of centuries.

They cultivate the plateau. Not as farmers cultivate fields, but as musicians cultivate sound. Each Weaver tends to thousands of cushion colonies, adjusting their chemistry with careful injections of enzymes, guiding their growth with selective pruning that happens at the cellular level. They read the plateau's needs in the color-shifts that pass across its surface, in the subtle changes of pressure and humidity, in the electromagnetic songs that pulse through the root networks. When a colony grows too dense, they thin it from within. When mineral deficiencies threaten, they guide root systems to new veins, sometimes spending years slowly coaxing roots through tons of rock toward a distant deposit of needed nutrients.

Their own biology is more process than structure. They photosynthesize when the rare sunlight breaks through, but they also practice a kind of chemosynthesis, breaking down mineral compounds in their specialized internal chambers. They have no central nervous system but rather a distributed intelligence that runs through their entire body, every tendril capable of independent thought while contributing to a greater consciousness. They reproduce by budding, but rarely—new Weavers emerge only when the plateau itself has grown enough to need additional tenders, perhaps once in a human century.

The Weavers' architecture is indistinguishable from natural growth. They shape dwelling spaces within the cushion colonies, chambers that grow around them like pearls around irritation. These spaces connect through tubes and passages that could be natural or cultivated—the distinction has no meaning here. Inside, the walls glow with the same bioluminescent threading that pulses through the plant tissue, but brighter, more directed. The Weavers can modulate this light through touch, creating three-dimensional patterns that serve as both art and data storage, encoding information in the quantum states of biological photons.

They perceive time through the plateau itself. The growth rate of different colonies serves as their calendar. Chemical signatures laid down during past events become their historical record. They know that the plateau has been attacked—burned, frozen, infected—many times in its history, and they read these catastrophes in the scar tissue of ancient colonies, in the genetic mutations that persist in certain bloodlines of cushion-plants, in the deep chemical memories that surface during the strongest storms.

Their technology is entirely biological, entirely integrated. They breed specialized organisms within their own bodies—living tools that can separate specific molecules from the moisture-laden air, or generate precise electrical currents, or produce chemicals unknown elsewhere in nature. These symbiotic creatures live their entire lives inside the Weavers, evolving across generations to better serve their purposes. Some Weavers carry ecosystems inside themselves more complex than entire forests elsewhere.

Music, for them, is the manipulation of growth rates. By subtly adjusting the chemistry of vast sections of plateau, they can cause waves of accelerated or decelerated growth to pass across the landscape in patterns that take years to complete. These compositions are visible only from great height or great temporal distance—art at a geological scale, meaning encoded in the shape of the landscape itself.

When Weavers die—a process that takes decades—they root themselves in a chosen spot and begin the transformation. Their bodies become indistinguishable from the cushion colonies, their consciousness dissolving into the chemical communication network of the plateau. They don't exactly cease to exist but become distributed, their thoughts flowing with the water through the plant mass, their memories crystallizing in mineral deposits that will be read by future Weavers, their individuality sacrificed to the vast, slow, eternal thought that is the plateau itself thinking about itself.

The younger Weavers—those only centuries old—sometimes venture to the plateau's edges, where the waterfalls dissolve into rainbow mist. They extend their finest tendrils into these spaces where matter changes state, reading information from the lower world in the chemistry of ascending vapors, sending messages downward in carefully composed molecular packages that will travel with the falling water. This is their only contact with the lands below, these chemical letters that take seasons to compose and seasons more to descend through the hydrological cycle to their recipients.

But most Weavers never approach the edges. They live their millennia-long lives in the deep interior, where the green presses so thick that direction loses meaning, where up and down are distinguished only by the subtle pull of gravity on their internal fluids. Here they tend their gardens that are not gardens, pursue their science that is not science, create their art that is not art—all these human categories dissolving like mist in the reality of their existence, which is simply to be the conscious aspect of the plateau, the part that knows itself, tends itself, dreams the slow, chlorophyll dreams that will shape the next million years of growth.
