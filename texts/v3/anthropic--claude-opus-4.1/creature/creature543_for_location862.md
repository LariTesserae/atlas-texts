The colony has no name for itself. Names require boundaries, and they have never known where one ends and another begins.

Each thumb-sized segment of their mass carries within its translucent flesh a crystal seed that forms, grows, and dissolves in perfect rhythm with its neighbors — not synchronized by intent but by the mathematics of heat exchange. When young, these seeds are sharp as glass shards, generating excessive warmth that forces the juvenile to the colony's writhing surface where the heat can dissipate. As they age, the crystals round and slow, their bearers sinking deeper into the mass where the temperature stays constant, cradled by millions of their kind.

They have no eyes — have never had eyes — but possess something far more intimate. Each creature tastes the thermal signature of its neighbors through patches of specialized tissue that read heat like braille, creating a kind of chemical-thermal language that ripples through the colony in waves. A disturbance at one end transmits through ten million bodies in cascading patterns of temperature and pheromone, the message transforming as it travels, becoming rumor, becoming myth, becoming forgotten before it reaches the far chambers.

Their soft bodies compress and extend, able to stretch to three times their resting length or contract into armored balls no bigger than marbles. This flexibility allows them to pack impossibly tight — a single cubic meter might hold thousands, their bodies deforming to fill every gap, every space between spaces. Where they press together longest, their flesh begins to merge at the edges, creating temporary multi-bodied organisms that think with borrowed thoughts until some disturbance breaks them apart again.

They feed through absorption. The sulfurous streams that rise from the cracks below contain complex chemicals that their crystalline organs break down and reconstitute. But this is only baseline survival. True nourishment comes from consuming their dead — not in violence but in careful ritual. When one begins to fade, its crystal clouding and dimming, the others sense it through minute temperature changes. They surround the dying one, pressing close not to kill but to comfort, their heat maintaining its metabolism until the very end. Only when the crystal finally shatters do they begin the consumption, absorbing every molecule with perfect efficiency, the departed becoming part of a hundred others, its minerals strengthening their own crystalline cores.

Memory, for them, is chemical. Certain experiences trigger pheromone releases that bind to their secretions, coating the tube-walls in layers of molecular history. A creature passing over these traces absorbs fragments of ancient fears, obsolete hungers, the chemical ghost of migrations from chambers that no longer exist. The oldest sections of the Gallery are thick with these memory-secretions, creating zones where the colony moves in patterns established millions of years ago, following behavioral scripts from ancestors they never knew they had.

They reproduce by division, but not cleanly. When conditions are right — a perfect temperature, adequate food, the mysterious alignment of factors they themselves don't understand — a creature's crystal begins to resonate at a particular frequency. This attracts others who have reached the same state. They cluster, sometimes dozens at once, their bodies fusing into a temporary superorganism that pulses with shared heat. Inside this mass, the crystals shatter and reform, each fragment becoming the seed of a new being. The parents don't survive the process — they literally dissolve into their offspring, their consciousness (if such a word applies) dispersing among the newborn.

The metallic node terrifies them at a level deeper than instinct. When they encounter it, their crystals begin to vibrate in discordant frequencies, creating a painful feedback loop that spreads through physical contact. They've built walls of their own dead around it, secretion-sealed barriers that must be constantly maintained as the node's emanations slowly dissolve their organic compounds. Some scholars of another world might recognize the node as a beacon, still transmitting its location to receivers that turned to dust eons ago. But to the colony, it is simply the-thing-that-must-not-be-touched, a concept encoded so deeply in their chemical memory that newborns flee from it without knowing why.

They dream, after a fashion. During the deepest heat of their cycles, when the temperature in the densest clusters approaches levels that would melt metal, their consciousness fragments and recombines in ways that generate patterns without purpose — crystalline fractals that exist purely in the medium of shared warmth, collaborative hallucinations that ripple through thousands of bodies at once. In these moments, they might be said to imagine, creating thermal sculptures in their collective sensory space, impossible geometries of heat and pressure that dissipate when the cycle breaks.

The waves of movement that pulse through the colony follow rules that would require supercomputers to model. They are hunting, but not for food — for optimal temperature gradients, for the perfect density of bodies, for chemical signatures that indicate the health of the whole. A section that grows too hot will trigger a cascade of movement away from it; a section too cool draws them in spirals. These movements, viewed from an impossible distance, would reveal patterns of incredible complexity — mandalas of flesh that form and reform, following mathematical principles that govern everything from galaxy formation to the growth of frost on windows.

Sometimes, very rarely, a creature becomes lost. Separated from the mass, it begins to cool, its crystal dimming, its chemical songs finding no answer. These lost ones undergo a terrible transformation. Their bodies harden, their flesh becoming something like stone, their crystal restructuring into a form that can survive alone. They become something else — still alive but no longer part of the colony, wandering the empty spaces between the tubes, their transformed bodies able to digest the Gallery's structural material itself. The colony fears these changed ones, fleeing when they sense their approach, for they carry with them the chemical signature of true loneliness, a concept otherwise unknown in their merged existence.

The empty chambers that spontaneously appear are not random. They are breathing spaces — the colony's collective response to overcrowding, a behavioral release valve encoded in their genetics. When the density reaches critical mass, a chemical cascade triggers mass evacuation, creating temporary voids that allow heat to dissipate and chemical buildups to disperse. These spaces are sacred in their way — no creature will enter one until the colony collectively decides it's time, a decision made through ten million tiny chemical votes cast in pheromone and temperature.

Their secretions have long since modified the original Gallery structure. What began as simple calcified tubes has become something far more complex — a living architecture that responds to their needs. They've built channels for the sulfur streams, creating distribution networks that ensure no section starves. They've reinforced weak points with layers of hardened mucus that becomes stronger than the original bone-like material. They've even begun to digest and repurpose sections of the ancient structure, incorporating its minerals into their own biology, becoming in a very real sense one with their environment.

The soft clicking of their crystalline metabolisms creates more than white noise. Careful listening reveals patterns — rhythms that speed and slow with the colony's movements, harmonies that emerge when different sections synchronize, discordances that predict the spontaneous chamber clearings. It is their only voice, this clicking, and it speaks of processes so alien to individual consciousness that no single mind could comprehend it. It is the sound of a million creatures thinking as one, deciding as one, existing as one, while somehow maintaining just enough separation to remain a they rather than an it.

In the deepest parts of the Gallery, where the heat rises so thick it distorts space itself, the oldest of them cluster. These ancients have lived through hundreds of divisions, their crystals containing fragments from thousands of ancestors. They move slowly, deliberately, their bodies so packed with mineral deposits they've become semi-rigid. They are the colony's memory-keepers, their chemical signatures so complex that younger creatures can spend days just processing a single pheromone strand. When they finally die — a process that can take years — their bodies don't dissolve like the others. Instead, they crystallize completely, becoming part of the Gallery itself, their final thoughts encoded in mineral matrices that will leak slowly into the colony's consciousness for millions of years to come.

This is their civilization — if such a word can apply to beings that know no individuality, no technology beyond their own biology, no art beyond the patterns of their movement. They build nothing that isn't themselves, write nothing that isn't encoded in chemistry, worship nothing that isn't heat and density and the perfect clicking synchronization of a million crystalline hearts.

Yet they are ancient beyond measure, successful beyond any metric we might apply. They have survived changes that erased entire worlds, adapted to conditions that would destroy any other form of life, created a society so perfectly stable it needs no governance, no laws, no conflict resolution because conflict itself is impossible when every disagreement is immediately averaged across ten million minds.

They do not know they are in a place called the Breathing Gallery. They do not know there is an outside to their world of tubes and heat and darkness. They know only the warmth of the mass, the taste of sulfur and memory, the endless clicking symphony of their shared metabolism, and the absolute certainty that they are never, ever alone.
