The people of the salt flats move through their days wrapped in membrane—thin polymer suits that breathe but never let the moisture in, their faces often hidden behind goggles that fog perpetually at the edges. They are a population of technicians and processors, chemists and maintenance crews, their bodies adapted over generations to the constant damp. Their skin carries a permanent sheen, not of sweat but of the mineral film that coats everything here. Children born in the settlement develop a resistance to the corrosive air early, their lungs learning to filter the metallic taste from each breath.

They work in shifts that follow the thermocline—the daily rise and fall of heat from below. Morning crews descend into the extraction facilities before dawn, when the ground is coolest and the machinery least likely to overheat. By noon, only the monitor stations remain staffed, their operators sealed in climate-controlled pods, watching screens that track flow rates and mineral concentrations. The afternoon brings the maintenance shifts, workers moving through the steam and heat to clear the salt accumulation from pipes, to replace corroded valves, to shore up foundations that the wet earth constantly tries to reclaim.

But these are not the only inhabitants. Between the human workers move the scavenger crews—not quite employees, not quite citizens, but essential to the city's function. They follow the amphipod migrations with fine mesh nets, harvesting the crustaceans by the ton for processing into feed and fertilizer. They know the patterns of the pools, where the mineral concentrations are richest, where the biofilms grow thickest. Some families have worked the same sections of flat for generations, passing down knowledge of which steam vents produce the purest condensation, which abandoned structures harbor the largest breeding colonies.

The bird-followers form another caste entirely. They track the gray flamingo flocks, collecting their guano from rooftop rookeries—a phosphate-rich resource that the refineries prize. These workers move like birds themselves, light-footed on the catwalks, their movements slow and deliberate to avoid startling the colonies. They wear feathered cloaks, not for warmth but for camouflage, and speak in whistles and clicks that mimic the birds' own calls. Over time, they begin to smell like their charges—musty, saline, vaguely fishy.

In the depths of the processing facilities live the pipeline dwellers—maintenance specialists who rarely surface, who know the city from below. They navigate the networks of drainage and steam tunnels where the industrial amphipods have built entire ecosystems. These workers develop an uncanny ability to predict system failures by the behavior of the creatures—when the amphipods abandon a section of pipe, rupture is imminent. They communicate in a dialect thick with technical terms and creature names, barely comprehensible to surface dwellers.

The children of the flats grow up amphibious. They play in the shallow pools despite their parents' warnings, their games involving jumping between the solid crusts and the wet spots, learning through experience which surfaces will hold. They keep brine shrimp in jars, race amphipods along the walkways, build elaborate traps for the flies that they never quite manage to catch. By adolescence, they can read the landscape—know by the color of a pool what minerals dominate it, predict by the flight pattern of birds where the next upwelling will occur.

There are the old-timers who remember when the extraction was less mechanized, when crews would rake the evaporation ponds by hand, when the mineral harvest followed seasonal rhythms rather than industrial schedules. They sit in the covered markets—the only truly social spaces in the settlement—mending nets and maintaining equipment with movements so practiced they barely need to look. Their hands are maps of scars from salt cuts and chemical burns, their eyes clouded by decades of glare off the white flats.

The market vendors adapt their wares to the wet. Everything is sealed, preserved, pickled, or dried. Fresh produce, when it arrives from beyond the flats, is a luxury that spoils within hours in the humid air. The local economy runs on preserved foods and synthetic nutrients, supplemented by what can be harvested from the environment—brine shrimp paste, amphipod meal, eggs collected from the bird colonies. The vendors know each customer's tolerance for the local flavors, which grow more intense the longer one lives here.

At night, when the industrial lights create pools of yellow in the gray, the settlement takes on a different character. The swing shift workers gather in the few bars that exist—sealed rooms with aggressive dehumidification that makes the air feel startlingly dry, almost painful to breathe after a day in the moisture. They drink distilled alcohol cut with mineral water, the only thing that tastes right after years of the local brine seeping into everything. They don't talk much about the work or the wet or the isolation. Instead, they play cards with plastic-coated decks, watch sports broadcasts from distant, dry cities, and pretend for a few hours that they live somewhere else.

But there are those who embrace the wet fully—the mystics and the modified. They claim the mineral water has properties beyond the industrial, that long exposure changes more than just lung capacity and skin texture. Some intentionally cultivate the biofilms on their skin, believing it provides protection or enhancement. Others drink unprocessed brine in careful doses, developing tolerances that would poison a newcomer. They speak of communication with the amphipod swarms, of understanding the patterns in the bird migrations, of feeling the pulse of the geothermal systems like a heartbeat.

The modified ones go further. They implant filters in their nasal passages, install moisture recyclers in their suits, some even attempting gill-like modifications to process oxygen from the humid air more efficiently. They move through the settlement like harbingers of what humanity might become if it stays here long enough—neither fully human nor fully creature, but something adapted to the in-between space of land and water, industry and nature.

The city's administrators occupy climate-controlled towers at the settlement's center, rarely venturing onto the flats themselves. They manage by spreadsheet and satellite image, their understanding of the place abstract and numerical. But even they cannot escape the wet entirely. It seeps into their sealed offices through microscopic gaps, corrodes their electronics, forces them to constantly replace and upgrade. They issue regulations about exposure limits and safety protocols that the ground crews largely ignore, knowing that survival here requires a more intimate negotiation with the environment.

There are the shift families—one partner working days, the other nights, their paths crossing only in the gray hours of dawn and dusk. Their children are raised by the settlement as much as by parents, learning from whoever is available, developing bonds with the other shift children who keep the same strange hours. These families develop their own rhythms, their own rituals for connection despite the industrial schedule that pulls them apart.

The medics see the real cost of the wet. They treat the chronic respiratory conditions, the skin infections that bloom in the constant moisture, the joint problems from years of working on unstable surfaces. They've developed local remedies from the environment itself—amphipod oil for burns, crystallized brine for certain infections, even applications of the biofilm for wound healing. They walk a line between official medical practice and the accumulated wisdom of generations who've survived here.

And there are always the newcomers, arriving for the work, for the extraction bonuses, for the promise of good pay in exchange for a few years of discomfort. They stand out immediately—their movements too quick, their breathing too shallow, their constant surprise at how the moisture infiltrates everything. Most leave within a year. Those who stay undergo the change, the slow adaptation, until one day they realize they can no longer imagine living in a truly dry place.

The creatures, too, have their hierarchies and inhabitants. The amphipods exist in dozens of species, each occupying a specific niche—some feeding on the algae in the pools, others on the industrial runoff, still others predating on their smaller cousins. The brine flies have evolved into multiple forms—day flies and night flies, those that cluster on the warm pipes and those that prefer the cool shadows under walkways. Even the gray flamingos have subdivided into distinct populations—the roof-nesters and the ground-nesters, the deep-waders and the shallow-feeders.

In the abandoned sectors, where the extraction companies pulled out years ago, feral ecosystems establish themselves. The machinery becomes reef-like structures for the creatures. Amphipods build massive colonies in old storage tanks. Birds nest in conveyor housings. The brine shrimp bloom in undisturbed pools that slowly expand, undermining foundations, reclaiming the industrial space inch by inch.

Some humans, too, go feral in these zones—dropouts from the industrial system who survive by harvesting the abandoned equipment for scrap, by fishing the richer pools, by knowing the landscape in ways the companies never mapped. They build shelters from salvaged materials, waterproofed with industrial sealants and biofilm. They trade with the settlement at the margins, bringing rare minerals from unexploited pools, unusual specimens that the science crews pay well for, information about which abandoned sectors are truly abandoned and which still harbor valuable resources.

The entire population—human, bird, crustacean—exists in a constant state of negotiation with the wet. Nothing here is permanent; everything is always in the process of either emerging from or dissolving back into the brine. The inhabitants, all of them, have learned to live not on the land or in the water, but in the margin between them, in the space where solid becomes liquid and liquid crystallizes back to solid, where the industrial and natural merge into something altogether different—a hybrid ecology that belongs fully to neither realm but creates its own logic, its own cycles, its own strange persistence in the mineral haze.
