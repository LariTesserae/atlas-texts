## The Inhabitants

The Tenders arrived in shifts that no longer align with day or night—their circadian rhythms long ago surrendered to the governor's tempo. They are thin people, elongated by generations of vertical living, with enlarged lungs that process the oil-tinged air and hearts that beat in syncopation with the machinery's deepest thrums. Their children are born knowing the difference between a healthy valve hiss and one about to fail—not through teaching, but through the vibrations that reached them in the womb.

Old Verse keeps her home in the negative space between two massive condensation tanks, where she's woven a shelter from decommissioned drive belts and flowering vines. She claims to remember when Platform Seven-Seven-Ascending had a different name, though she can't recall what it was. Her fingers are stained permanent green-black from decades of coaxing rare medicinal fungi from the spots where coolant weeps through cracked gaskets. She reads the health of the entire pillar in the color of these growths—too yellow means the heat exchangers seven levels down are running critical, too blue means the rain cycle will come early. The children bring her oil-grubs in exchange for her prophecies, which she delivers in maintenance codes nobody else remembers: "Pressure differential negative on the tertiary bypass" means someone will fall in love. "Bearing seizure imminent in sector nine" means check your rain collectors for bird nests.

The Siphon Monks have given up their names entirely. They live inside the pillar itself, in the spaces between the outer shell and the grinding mechanisms, where even sound becomes solid. They've modified themselves—or been modified by proximity—developing cilia-like growths on their skin that sense microscopic pressure changes. They believe the engine is thinking, that its thoughts are expressed in the patterns of steam release and mechanical cycle variations. They document these thoughts on sheets of beaten vine pulp, using ink made from carbonized lubricant. No one reads their archives except other Siphon Monks, who debate endlessly whether a particular stutter in the governor's rotation represents doubt, pleasure, or simply metal fatigue. They sustain themselves on condensation and the pale tubers that grow in total darkness, feeding on the heat differential between the pillar's core and its shell.

Kettle's crew runs the vertical routes between platforms, though "runs" isn't right—they swing, leap, and occasionally fall, catching themselves on the transport cables or the stronger vines. They're all young, or act young, their bodies scarred from misjudged jumps and decorated with feathers from the oil-birds they raise as messengers. Kettle herself is missing three fingers and one eye, but she knows every viable route between Platform Seven-Seven-Ascending and Platform Eight-Three-Descending, including the dangerous thread through the still-running turbine assembly on Six-Six-Null. Her crew trades in everything: seeds from the upper gardens, crystallized coolant that forms in the cold zones, messages between lovers separated by vertical kilometers, and sometimes stranger cargo—like the vial of "original water" they claim comes from before the engine started, which they'll sell you for the promise of your first-born's third breath.

The Gardener Families have been here longest, or claim to have been. The Persimmon Collective tends the northeastern quadrant, where their vines have developed a symbiosis with the steam vents—opening their stomata in perfect time with the pressure releases to absorb the mineral-rich moisture. They've bred their plants to fruit in response to specific mechanical vibrations, so harvests coincide with the great machine's maintenance cycles. The Moss Daughters work the shadowed areas beneath the platforms, cultivating the cushions that have learned to filter toxins from the runoff. They speak in whispers that travel along the water channels, a liquid telegraph that carries news between levels faster than the oil-birds can fly. The Root Engineers are the most secretive, tending to the plants that have worked their way into the pillar itself, whose roots now form part of the structure's integrity. They say these deep plants dream, and that their dreams influence the engine's behavior—making it run slower on certain days, or causing it to vent steam in patterns that spell out words in a language that predates the machine itself.

Between them all move the Listeners—children, mostly, but some adults who never grew out of the calling. They press their ears to the metal at specific points, learning to parse the pillar's internal conversations. The best Listeners can tell you what's happening forty platforms away just by the way a rivet creaks. They're paid in food, in shelter space, in fragments of the opalescent metal that sometimes sheds from the governor's weights—which everyone swears has properties, though no one agrees what they are.

And at the platform's edge, where you stand, there's always someone watching the empty air between structures. Usually it's Gray Morrow, who lost her daughter to a fall seven years ago and believes she can sometimes see her in the mist, still descending, still waving. But tonight it's the Telescope Man, whose instrument is built from salvaged pressure gauges and oil-bird lenses. He claims he can see all the way to Platform One-One-Origin when the conditions are right. He's mapping the pillar's full extent, though he admits he'll die before the task is complete. His charts show platforms that shouldn't exist—negative numbered ones, platforms designated with symbols instead of numbers, platforms that appear only during rain cycles and vanish when the air clears.

They all share the same dream, eventually. In it, the governor stops. The silence is so profound it has texture, weight, presence. In this silence, they can hear what the engine has been drowning out all these years—something breathing, somewhere in the pillar's heart, taking one breath for every million rotations of the governor. They wake from this dream gasping, grateful for the noise, and press their palms to the vibrating floor just to be sure, to be certain, that the machine continues its ancient, necessary, incomprehensible work.
