The people of the terraced city move through their stone world with an economy of motion learned in childhood and perfected through decades. They are not many—perhaps four hundred souls—and their bodies have adapted to the constant climbing, their calves thick and hard, their breathing deep and measured even on the steepest stairs. They dress in undyed wool and linen, colors ranging from cream to grey to brown, their clothing loose enough to allow for the endless stepping up and down, tight enough not to catch on the narrow passages' rough walls.

Their hands are their most distinctive feature—broad and powerful from working stone, yet capable of delicate precision. Every adult can dress limestone with a hammer and chisel, can feel imperfections in a block's surface that eyes cannot detect. Children learn to carry water jars up the terraces before they can properly speak, their small fingers finding the same handholds in the rock that their grandparents used.

They speak rarely and softly. Not from any cultural prohibition, but because the stone has taught them that words, like water, should not be wasted. When they must communicate across terraces, they use a system of calls—not quite song, not quite shout—pitched to carry through the specific acoustics of their vertical world. Each family has its own call, learned by ear and passed down unchanged.

The old ones no longer climb. When joints stiffen and breath shortens, they settle into the middle terraces where the cisterns are fullest and the distance to the plaza fountain shortest. They become the keepers of seeds, the menders of nets, the ones who remember which years the rains failed and how the city survived. They sit in doorways where morning sun warms limestone walls, their eyes following the shadows' daily progression with a precision that makes them living calendars.

Children exist in small clusters—never more than a dozen born in any year, sometimes none at all. They learn the city's paths like verses of an epic poem, each alley and stair and secret passage memorized through games of chase and hide. By ten, they can navigate blindfolded. By fifteen, they can carry twice their weight in water jars up three terraces without spilling a drop. Their play is quiet—stones clicking in complex games, wooden tops spinning on the plaza's worn surface, elaborate pantomimes performed in courtyards.

The adults work in profound coordination, though rarely in groups. Stone-cutting happens in isolation, each craftsman in their workshop, but the rhythm of hammers creates an irregular heartbeat that echoes through the terraces. Water carriers move in the early morning, their footsteps creating a temporary river of sound that flows up and down the stairs. Garden tenders work in silence, their movements among the plants as deliberate as ritual.

There is a keeper of the fountain—always one, never more, selected not by vote or heredity but by recognition of a certain quality of attention. This person cleans the spiral basin daily with a brush made from mountain grass, ensures the flow remains constant, knows by sound alone when limestone deposits threaten to alter the water's whisper. They sleep in a small chamber carved behind the fountain, dreams filled with the sound of water over stone.

Marriages happen rarely and are celebrated with restraint—a gathering at the plaza, dried fruit and mountain herbs burned in a bronze bowl that predates the city's memory, the couple's hands washed in fountain water mixed with white clay from the valley below. Death receives similar acknowledgment—the body wrapped in undyed cloth, carried to caves in the eastern cliff face where generations of bones rest in natural mummification, the dry air and limestone slowly transforming flesh to something mineralogical.

They track time not by calendar but by wear—when the third step of the southern stair needs redressing, when the cisterns require their decade cleaning, when wooden shutters have hardened enough to require replacement. Their holidays align with practical necessities: the festival of cistern cleaning, the gathering of wild herbs, the checking of roof drains before the rain season.

Trade happens twice yearly when valley dwellers climb the mountain paths carrying what cannot be produced in stone—iron for tools, salt, certain medicines. In exchange, the city provides dressed limestone blocks, carved with such precision that temples five valleys away seek them for their holiest spaces. The traders sleep one night in designated houses on the lowest terrace and leave before noon, as if the city's stillness makes them nervous.

The inhabitants practice a craft that might be called meditation if it were deliberate, but it emerges naturally from their environment. They can sit motionless for hours watching shadows move across stone, their attention both absolute and absent. Their peripheral vision is exceptional—movement three terraces away catches their eye instantly, a survival trait from generations of watching for rockfalls.

At night, they gather in small groups—never more than six—in houses chosen for their acoustic properties. Here they share the day's observations: the angle of a raven's flight, new growth on the olive trees, the changing color of lichen on the northern walls. These gatherings are not social in any conventional sense but rather a collective monitoring of their world's minute variations.

Their dreams are architectural. They dream of stairs that don't exist but should, of water flowing through channels not yet carved, of stones fitting together in patterns that improve on the already perfect. Children sometimes wake describing rooms they've never seen, only to discover them years later behind a door they'd never noticed, exactly as dreamed.

They age like their city—slowly, reaching a kind of equilibrium with time. Hair greys but doesn't whiten. Skin weathers but doesn't deeply wrinkle. They die suddenly when they die, usually at night, as if choosing not to burden others with lengthy decline. Bodies are often found seated, overlooking some familiar view, appearing merely contemplative until stillness reveals itself as permanent.

This is a people who have become geological—patient, erosion-resistant, shaped by their environment into something between human and stone. They persist not through struggle but through a kind of mineral acceptance, their lives flowing through the channels carved by previous generations, wearing them imperceptibly deeper, one footstep, one rainfall, one breath at a time.
