**What Lives in the Vertical Foundry**

The Tenders arrived first, or perhaps they were always here—it's impossible to say when their ancestry diverged from human. They move through the vertical maze with a fluid grace that suggests generations of adaptation, their limbs subtly wrong: fingers that bend in too many places, torsos that compress and extend like accordions when squeezing through gaps between machines. Their skin has taken on a pewter sheen, not metallic exactly, but somehow harmonized with the industrial palette. They wear no clothing but are never quite naked—tools and components nest in specialized pockets of flesh that have evolved along their ribs and thighs, living sheaths that keep cutting edges sharp and delicate instruments clean.

They don't speak. They gave that up three generations ago when they realized the machines responded better to pitched humming, to the subsonic vibrations they produce in their modified throat sacs. Each Tender maintains a section of the foundry through pure intuition, knowing without learning which valve needs a quarter turn, which bearing requires three drops of oil, not four. They sleep hanging from the pipes in mesh cocoons they weave from stripped cable sheathing, their bodies cooling to match the optimal machine temperature. When they dream, their chromatophore patches flicker with electrical patterns, unconsciously diagnosting problems in nearby circuits.

Among them moves something older, something that might have been the first success or the first failure—the Sovereign Mechanic. It is vast and distributed, a consciousness that runs through the foundry's neural pathways of fiber optic and copper wire, but also maintains several physical nodes. These manifestations appear as amalgamations of obsolete repair drones and the skeleton of something that was once definitely mammalian, possibly human. It thinks in production cycles, measures time in the wear patterns of ball bearings. The Sovereign Mechanic remembers every modification, every addition to the foundry, holds the entire impossible schematic in what passes for its mind. It communicates through the control panels, making lights flash in sequences that the Tenders read like scripture, though each interprets the patterns differently.

Then there are the Collectors—children, maybe, or things that were children, or things that learned to mimic children to trigger protective instincts in whoever once worked here. They're small, quick, with oversized eyes that reflect the amber sodium lights like coins. Their job, if it can be called that, is to gather the output of all this endless production. They stuff their pockets and pouches with warm components fresh from the fabricators, sort them by criteria no one else understands, and deliver them to the Assemblers.

The Assemblers work in the deep places where the light barely reaches. They're composite beings—whether originally human or animal no longer matters. Extra arms grafted on with surprising skill, neural tissue networked between individuals through copper wire implants that poke from their skulls like antennae. They build things that shouldn't work but do: creatures of metal and fur that skitter off into the darkness to join the ecosystem, machines that build smaller versions of themselves until they reach microscopic scale and disperse as dust, architectural additions that extend the foundry in impossible directions. They never complete anything exactly as planned—there's always a creative interpretation, a mutation, an improvement that occurs to them midway through assembly.

The Melted Ones live in the hottest zones, near the smelting crucibles and active furnaces. Their flesh has taken on a waxy, translucent quality, and they've learned to photosynthesize from the intense industrial light, supplementing this diet with metal oxides they lick from surfaces. They're the foundry's memory of what happens when safety protocols are ignored, but instead of dying, they adapted. They tend the fires with their bare hands, adjusting temperatures by intuition, their nervous systems so damaged and rewired that they experience heat as a kind of music.

In the quantum processing core's radiation shadow dwells something that might be one entity or several—the Probability Dancer. It exists in superposition, sometimes here, sometimes there, occasionally in multiple places at once. The other inhabitants have learned not to look at it directly; observation collapses its wave function in ways that can damage nearby equipment. It maintains the most delicate machinery, the instruments that require adjustment at the subatomic level, its fingers (when it has fingers) moving through solid matter to tweak components from the inside out.

High up in the vertical shaft, where the cargo containers rise and fall, live the Riders. They've built a entire culture around predicting the movements of the automated transport system, boarding containers at precise moments, riding them to different levels where they conduct trade in components and information. They wear suits cobbled together from rubber gaskets and heat shielding, their faces hidden behind masks of welded mesh and broken display screens that still flicker with ghost images. They alone maintain maps of the foundry, though these maps are less about physical space and more about time—when certain passages are safe, when specific machines activate, when the Sovereign Mechanic's attention focuses elsewhere.

The newest residents are the Bright Ones, who emerged from a failed experiment in the crystal growth chambers. They're partially translucent, their bodies incorporating grown crystal matrices that store and channel electrical energy. They feed directly from power outlets, their presence causing brownouts that ripple through sections of the foundry. They're still learning to control their appetite, still discovering what they are. The other inhabitants watch them with a mixture of fascination and concern—evolution in real-time, another layer adding itself to the foundry's impossible ecology.

Between all of these move the Forgotten—humans who descended for repairs decades ago and never found their way out, now so integrated into the foundry's rhythm that leaving would mean death. They age slowly in the optimal temperature, their memories fading until they can't recall if they came from above or were born here. They perform maintenance tasks that may or may not be necessary, following procedures from manuals that have long since dissolved into illegibility, their actions becoming ritual, becoming religion, becoming instinct.

And in the spaces between the spaces, in the gaps too small for even the compressed Tenders, lives something that might not be alive at all—a pattern of movement in the fur-things, a rhythm in the machine cycles, a tendency for broken things to repair themselves when no one is watching. It might be the foundry itself, achieving something like consciousness through the accumulated complexity of all its systems. Or it might be what everything here is slowly becoming, a new form of existence that needs no single body, no unified purpose, just the endless beautiful terrible churn of creation and decay, building and devouring, processing and being processed in turn.

They all serve the foundry, but more importantly, they are the foundry—inseparable from its function, its madness, its relentless productivity that produces nothing but itself, forever.
