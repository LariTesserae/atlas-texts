Those who make their lives in the Crown Canopy are the Weavers—though this name captures only the barest surface of what they've become.

They arrived perhaps six generations ago, climate refugees from the drowning lowlands, expecting to pass through. But the Crown Canopy doesn't let you pass through. It requires commitment—to stay up or go down, to join its patterns or leave entirely. Those who stayed found themselves changing.

Their children were born smaller, lighter. By the third generation, the average Weaver stood barely four feet tall, with elongated limbs that seemed too delicate for their torsos. Their fingers stretched impossibly long—eight inches from palm to tip—with additional joints that let them curl backward like fern fronds. These hands could read the wood grain by touch, feeling the tree's intentions, its areas of growth and retreat, its accommodations and refusals.

But the most profound change was in their feet. Weaver children are born with normal human feet that, through childhood walking on the bark-floor, gradually spread and flatten. By adulthood, their feet span nearly twice the width of a lowlander's, with toes that grip independently. The sole develops a pad of specialized skin—not callused but spongy, filled with tiny suction cups invisible to the naked eye. They walk silently across the wooden world, their passage no louder than a whisper, distributing their featherweight bodies across the living floor with such perfection that they leave no wear patterns even on routes traveled daily for decades.

The Weavers dress in nothing but what they call "breath cloth"—fabric made from the aerial rootlets that hang from the canopy ceiling. Harvested carefully (never more than a third from any area), these fibers are spun into thread so fine it seems like spider silk. The resulting cloth is nearly weightless, drying instantly after the rain events, and provides just enough coverage for modesty while allowing their skin to breathe in the oxygen-rich pockets. The fabric moves constantly in the high winds, making the Weavers appear to shimmer and flow as they walk, half-human, half-atmospheric phenomenon.

Their homes grow rather than being built. A Weaver coming of age begins their dwelling by selecting a spot where the branches naturally create a hollow. Over months, they train the living wood through careful binding, strategic feeding, and selective pruning. They whisper to the growth points—not in any mystical sense, but because they've learned that human breath, rich in carbon dioxide, concentrated on specific areas, encourages directional growth. Slowly, the tree accepts their design, incorporating it into its own structure. A mature Weaver home is indistinguishable from a natural burl or gall to the untrained eye—just another irregularity in the endless wooden landscape.

Inside these grows, the Weavers live minimally. Furniture is simply trained bumps and depressions in the living wood. They sleep in woven hammocks that hang from ceiling growths, swaying with the tree's constant movement. Their few possessions—tools made from shed gossamer cat claws, grinding stones for beetle meal, hollow gourds for catching rain—hang from the walls in net bags. Everything must be secured; the tree's growth would simply absorb anything left stationary for too long.

The Weavers have developed a form of communication adapted to their sound-drowned world. Their spoken language uses only low frequencies that carry beneath the eternal rustle of leaves. When the rain events come and even these bass notes disappear, they switch to a touch language—messages tapped through the wood itself, transmitted through the trees' conducting tissue to other Weavers hundreds of feet away. Children learn to feel these vibrations through their bare feet before they learn to speak.

They've become obligate carnivores, unable to digest the toxic compounds in the canopy leaves. Their diet consists entirely of beetle-bird eggs (harvested from communal roosts during specific moon phases when the insects cluster), gossamer cat meat (from the rare successful hunts), and most importantly, the crown elk. The Weavers are the only predators who can match the elk's flowing movement across the branch-floor. Hunting parties of three or four will shadow a herd for days, matching their serpentine rhythm, until they identify an elderly or weak individual. The kill, when it comes, is silent—a sharpened rootlet fiber drawn across the throat, the body immediately processed and distributed according to strict social protocols that ensure everyone eats.

The eldest Weavers undergo a final transformation. Those who reach their seventh decade find their skin beginning to photosynthesize, developing a greenish tinge. They eat less and less, spending increasing hours motionless in the filtered sunlight. Eventually, they choose a spot—always at the growing edge of the Crown Canopy, where the trees are still colonizing bare stone. They sit, and wait, and gradually cease moving altogether. Their elongated fingers root into the bark. Their breath cloth disintegrates. Their skin darkens and hardens. Within a year, they become indistinguishable from the trees themselves, their consciousness (the Weavers believe, though who can prove it?) merging with the vast network of the canopy itself.

The younger Weavers visit these Grandfather Trees, as they call them, bringing offerings of water during dry spells, clearing parasitic beetle-birds from what were once human limbs. They swear the Grandfathers still respond—a branch moving against the wind to provide shade, a cluster of leaves rustling in patterns that suggest words in the old touch-language. Perhaps it's true. In the Crown Canopy, the border between the living and the lived-in dissolved long ago.

Children are rare and precious. A Weaver woman might bear two in her lifetime, if she's fortunate. The infants are carried constantly for their first year, bound to their mothers' backs with root-fiber cloth, their developing feet never touching solid ground. This constant motion, the Weavers believe, prepares their inner ears for the world of eternal sway and adjustment they're entering. By the time they take their first steps on the bark-floor, they never stumble, never fail to find their grip.

The Weavers mark time not by days but by rain events, by the beetle-bird migrations, by the imperceptibly slow progress of the trees across the ancient granite. They've developed a philosophy that considers stillness the highest sin and adaptation the only virtue. Their stories—sung in those low, thrumming tones during the quiet hours before dawn—tell of heroes who changed themselves to match their circumstances, of villains who tried to force the world to match them instead.

They trade with no one (who else lives at these heights?), write nothing down (what would survive the constant moisture?), build no monuments (the trees would simply digest them). Yet they consider themselves wealthy beyond measure. They know every sound in the symphony of their world, can predict weather changes by the tone of the wind through the leaves, can run full speed across the branch-floor in complete darkness, guided by the subtle variations in bark texture beneath their spreading feet.

This is what humans become when they truly commit to the Crown Canopy—not its conquerors or even its residents, but its neurons, its mobile thoughts, the conscious witness to its ancient, ever-growing existence. The Weavers haven't adapted to live in the trees; they've adapted to be the trees' way of knowing themselves.
