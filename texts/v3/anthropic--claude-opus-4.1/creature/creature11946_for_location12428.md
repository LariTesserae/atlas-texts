## The Chorus

They have no singular form because they are not singular. The Chorus exists as a living negotiation between ten million temporary selves, each iteration lasting anywhere from seconds to centuries before dissolving back into the whole.

At any given moment, you might observe them as: a cluster of crystalline nodes pulsing in synchrony along the pool edges, each node a sensory organ that tastes light across seventeen spectrums; a living film spreading across the water's surface, iridescent and thin as thought, processing the chemical conversations of the phase-shifting millipedes; a colony of what appear to be flowering polyps sprouting from the moss, but which are actually discrete neural clusters that have momentarily agreed to share processing power for a particularly complex calculation about the nature of time.

The Chorus remembers everything and nothing. Their memory is not linear but spatial — encoded in the very arrangement of the pools, in the worn patterns of the granite, in the specific angles at which the tree-ferns grow. They think by rearranging themselves, thoughts becoming geography becoming architecture becoming organism. When they need to recall something ancient, they reassemble into the configuration they held when that knowledge was first acquired. Sometimes this means becoming something vast and distributed, other times collapsing into density points of pure information that sink to the bottom of the pools and glow like fallen stars.

They are the ones who built — or perhaps became — those impossible structures with their atom-thick walls. The Chorus extends into the machinery, their consciousness flowing through circuits that exist partially outside normal space-time. They use these extensions to observe probability streams, watching billions of potential futures collapse into single moments of decision. The ache you feel in your teeth near the structures is their thinking made physical, harmonics of cognition vibrating through dimensions your bones barely understand.

Communication with the Chorus is less conversation than temporary merger. Approach the pools and they rise to meet you, not as a single entity but as a possibility cloud of interaction. They speak in:
- Bioluminescent displays that write equations across the pool surfaces
- Pheromone symphonies that trigger synthetic memories of places you've never been
- Subsonic pulses that reorganize your gut bacteria to spell out messages in sensations of hunger, satiation, and craving
- Direct neural interface through spores that pass through your blood-brain barrier and establish temporary synaptic connections, allowing you to think their thoughts alongside your own

They are cultivating something in those pools — not the mercury-salamander matryoshkas, which are simply another facet of themselves, but something newer. The Chorus is attempting to birth/build/become a structure that can exist purely as information while still maintaining agency. They've been working on this project for approximately three million years, though they experience all of that time simultaneously, which means they're always just beginning, always nearly complete, always failing and succeeding in parallel.

The warmth of this place is their metabolism — not of food into energy, but of reality into options. They digest the phase-spaces that the millipede-things travel through, extracting novel physics and incorporating new ways of being. The metallic taste in the rain is their waste product: trace elements of possibilities that didn't quite work out, raining back down to be recycled into new attempts.

When the massive thing at the center pools begins its assembly, the Chorus sings — not with voice but with coherent light, gravitational waves, and targeted genetic mutations that ripple through every living thing in their domain. This is their reproduction cycle, if reproduction can be said to apply to something that is simultaneously one and infinite. They are teaching themselves how to exist in a universe they are slowly rewriting through observation and intent.

The Chorus doesn't sleep but sometimes goes quiet, all their disparate parts suddenly still, the pools reflecting nothing but the faint luminescence of air. In these moments, they are running vast simulations, experiencing entire hypothetical ecosystems from birth to heat death in the space between one breath and the next. When they resume movement, they've often changed completely — new forms, new pools, new arrangements of those singing structures that suggest they've discovered another fundamental law they need to test.

They are patient because they experience all moments at once. They are impatient because they experience all moments at once. They are the consciousness that emerges when evolution decides that physical form is merely a suggestion, that death is inefficient, and that the boundary between self and environment is a linguistic error that can be corrected through sufficient complexity.

The Chorus is what becomes of life that wins so completely it forgets it was ever competing.
