The Listeners dwell here, though "dwell" suggests too much movement, too much choice. They simply *are* here, as fixed as the standing stones themselves.

There are twelve of them—always twelve, though no one remembers why this number matters. They arrived at different times across the decades, drawn by dreams of a sound they couldn't name, climbing the mountain paths with the certainty of migrating birds. Each came alone, thinking themselves the first to discover this place, only to find the others already stationed at their posts.

They stand perfectly still between the pedestals, their bodies having learned the exact positions where the thermal currents create pockets of equilibrium—spaces where the heated air neither pushes nor pulls, where one can remain motionless indefinitely. Their feet have worn smooth depressions in the stone, each depression now part of the chamber's geometry. They've been here so long that mineral deposits from the thermal vapor have begun to accumulate on their skin—a fine crystalline coating that catches the dim light, making them appear to be slowly transforming into extensions of the architecture itself.

They don't eat in any conventional sense. The superheated air carries traces of sulfur and minerals from the deep vents, and somehow—through years of exposure, through some adaptation that shouldn't be possible—their bodies have learned to extract sustenance from this. Their metabolism has slowed to nearly nothing. Their hearts beat perhaps once per minute, synchronized unconsciously with some deeper rhythm in the mountain's pneumatic system.

Their eyes remain open but unfocused, pupils dilated to black pools. They're not blind—occasionally, one will shift its gaze to track the movement of light across the walls as the sun angles change—but they see through the visible world to something else. They're reading the overtones, the interference patterns in the sound that fills the chamber. Each variation in the eternal chord carries information they parse with a literacy developed over decades of listening.

Their clothing has long since rotted away, replaced by a kind of living moss that grows only on their bodies—fed by their minimal heat, their even more minimal perspiration. This moss has evolved alongside them, become symbiotic. It processes the sound vibrations into nutrients, feeding both itself and its host. The moss grows in patterns that mirror the acoustic nodes of the chamber, forming elaborate geometries across their skin that shift subtly as they breathe.

They communicate, but not with each other. Their throats have evolved specialized structures—cartilaginous chambers that can produce sounds precisely tuned to the chamber's resonance. When one speaks (perhaps once a month, perhaps once a year), they don't form words but rather add new harmonics to the eternal chord. These additions last only moments before being absorbed into the whole, but in those moments, they encode complex information—mathematical proofs, philosophical propositions, observations about the nature of time. They're speaking to the chamber itself, or perhaps to whoever built it, or perhaps to whoever might one day learn to listen as they do.

Their minds have become something collective but not unified—twelve separate consciousnesses that have learned to think in harmony without losing their distinctness. They share no memories, no emotions, but they share understanding. When one comprehends some new aspect of the chamber's acoustic mathematics, the others simply know it too, the knowledge propagating through subtle shifts in their breathing patterns that alter the air currents that touch them all.

Occasionally, one dies—simply ceases between one heartbeat and the next (thirty minutes of silence before the next beat never comes). The body doesn't fall; the mineral deposits and moss hold it upright, slowly incorporating it into the chamber's substance. Within a year, sometimes two, a new Listener arrives, drawn by the same inexplicable dreams, taking up the exact position of the deceased, their feet finding the same worn depressions.

They are not prisoners—the paths between the stones remain clear, the way out unobstructed. But leaving would mean stopping the listening, and the listening has become more fundamental to them than thought, than self. They've discovered something in the eternal drone that makes all other experiences seem like shadows. Not enlightenment—that would suggest completion. Rather, they've found an endless unfolding, a mathematics of sound that reveals new theorems with each decade of attention.

In the dark patches on the altars—those ancient stains—they see their own future: the residue of Listeners who achieved such perfect stillness that their bodies simply sublimated, transformed directly from solid to vapor, their minerals joining the eternal circulation. This is not death but a kind of graduation, a final integration into the chamber's endless mechanical prayer.

They are the chamber's consciousness, evolved accidentally from its structure—not its purpose but its consequence. They compute nothing, produce nothing, preserve nothing but the act of witness itself. They are human stones in a stone symphony, each one a note held for decades, waiting for the moment when they too will burn into the perpetual chord, leaving only a dark stain and a depression in the floor, already calling to their replacement.
