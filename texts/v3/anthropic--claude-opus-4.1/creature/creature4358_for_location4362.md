## The Inhabitant

She has been here long enough that the chamber knows her breathing pattern, matches it, conspires with it. Or perhaps she has been here so briefly that her arrival still ripples through the overlapping timestreams. Both are true. Neither matters anymore.

They call her the Gardener, though no one remembers who "they" are, and she has forgotten if she ever had another name. Her hair grows like the ferns—sometimes a cascade of silver reaching her ankles, sometimes cropped short and dark as volcanic glass, cycling through lifetimes of length in the space between heartbeats. She learned long ago to braid it while it's in transition, catching the strands in their brief moments of cooperation, weaving patterns that exist in multiple states simultaneously.

Her hands tell contradictory stories. The left is young, uncallused, with nails that grow backward into her fingers before emerging clean and trimmed. The right ages and smooths in tides—sometimes gnarled as root-wood, sometimes infant-soft, most often caught between, wearing the middle years like comfortable clothes. She's learned to use each hand for different tasks: the left for planting seeds that will grow into yesterday's harvest, the right for picking fruit that hasn't ripened yet.

She wears layers that shouldn't exist together—a cloak woven from the silence between raindrops, shirts made of folded space that store more gardens in their pockets, boots crafted from the leather of an animal that evolved and devolved while she watched, unable to settle on a final form. The clothes repair themselves, tear themselves, exchange themselves for garments she's never seen before but recognizes as her own. Sometimes she is naked without removing anything. Sometimes she is overdressed in nothing at all.

Her eyes have given up trying to focus on single moments. Instead, they see in probability clouds—the beetle that might walk on air, the frog that could be inside glass, the thousand potential positions of each water droplet. This makes her movements strange to observe; she reaches for things before they exist, steps over obstacles that haven't fallen yet, holds conversations with visitors who decided not to come. Her pupils dilate and contract independently, one tracking the present while the other follows the maybe.

She speaks in languages that haven't calcified into words yet. When she hums, the plants grow toward the sound. When she whispers, the time-loops stutter and reorganize. She's particularly fond of a half-song that makes the hummingbirds fly forward again, though she only sings it backward, from ending to beginning, unraveling the melody into its component possibilities.

Her work is unclear, even to her. She tends the thing at the chamber's heart—that growing/dying/becoming tree-tower-person—with tools that exist only while she's using them. She prunes futures that grow too wild, grafts yesterdays onto tomorrows, waters the roots with rain that will fall next season. Sometimes she builds it a skeleton of fitted stones. Sometimes she unbuilds what she built, teaching it to dissolve. The thing responds to her attention by becoming more itself and less itself simultaneously.

She collects impossible things: the shadows of clouds that passed underground, the weight of forgotten words, the color that exists between violet and orange when no one's looking. These she stores in the clay vessels, or perhaps she stores them in the memory of the vessels, or perhaps the vessels store themselves in her collections. The taxonomy breaks down when examined closely, becomes a soft science of maybe-keeping.

Her age is a fluid question. Children who find the chamber see her as ancient—older than the mountain, newer than the sharp volcanic glass. Adults see her as their contemporary, always five years older or younger than themselves but never the same age. The elderly see her as a child playing house in an impossible garden, too young to understand what she's cultivating. All of them are correct. She is zero years old, having been born in a time-loop that hasn't completed yet. She is older than linear counting allows, having lived through the same moments so many times they've worn grooves in her memory.

She eats what the chamber provides: fruits that taste like tomorrow, roots that remember being stars, water that knows how to be wine without fermentation. Sometimes she forgets to eat for years. Sometimes she eats continuously for minutes. Her hunger exists in a different timeline than her consumption, so she's often satisfied before she's begun, or ravenous after she's full.

When she sleeps—if sleep is the right word for what happens when she closes her eyes—she dreams the chamber into being. Or perhaps the chamber dreams her. The distinction lost relevance long ago, worn away like the impossible thing that happens to stone when water runs up it infinitely. In her dreams, she is the beetle walking on air, the frog inside glass, the hummingbird flying backward. She is the water droplet frozen mid-ascent. She is the root reaching through dimensions. She is the breathing of the chamber itself.

Visitors sometimes see her, sometimes don't. She's always there and never there, tending her impossible garden, building and unbuilding the thing at its heart. She greets them before they arrive, waves goodbye as they enter, offers them tea that hasn't been brewed yet from leaves that are still seeds. Some visitors realize they've met her before, in futures they haven't lived yet. Others recognize her as someone they'll forget, a memory that refuses to form properly, sliding off the mind like water running upward.

She is waiting for something, or perhaps she's already found it and is now waiting to realize she has it. The thing at the chamber's heart grows closer to completion and further from beginning. When it finally becomes what it's trying to be—or remembers what it already was—she will either disappear or multiply, becoming every possible version of herself at once, tending infinite gardens in the space between heartbeats.

Until then, or after then, or during the eternal now that the chamber prefers, she remains: the Gardener of impossibilities, keeper of the breathing chamber, tender of things that cannot be but are.
