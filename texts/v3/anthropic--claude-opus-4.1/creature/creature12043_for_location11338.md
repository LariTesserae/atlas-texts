The people of the plateau are made of stillness and wind.

Their faces carry the mountain's own geology—deep crevasses carved by decades of squinting against snow-glare, skin like burnished leather stretched over prominent cheekbones inherited from ten thousand years of ancestors who learned to extract oxygen from thin air. Their lungs are different from lowlanders', deeper and more efficient, their blood darker and richer. Children born here emerge purple-faced and silent, already adapted, their first cries thin but fierce.

They move with an economy that borders on the ceremonial. No gesture is wasted in this altitude where every action costs precious breath. Watch them work—splitting stone, spinning wool, milking yaks—and you see hands that know exactly where to place themselves, bodies that pivot and bend in patterns worn into their muscle memory by generations of repetition. The young learn by shadowing the old, absorbing these movements until they too begin to move like water finding its level, always choosing the path of least resistance against gravity and cold.

Their clothing is architecture. Layers upon layers, each with its purpose—the inner wool still lanolin-greasy for warmth, the middle felt for structure, the outer yak hide for wind. Women braid their hair into elaborate coiled structures that take hours to create and weeks to undo, incorporating yak wool and butter until their heads carry their own insulation. Men wear their wealth in their belts—heavy things of worked silver and coral traded from impossible distances, each piece a story of some ancestor's journey down and back. Everyone smells of smoke and butter tea, rancid to outsiders but comfortable as breath to those born here.

The eldest remember everything. In a place where nothing is written, they are the libraries. Old Pemba knows the location of every spring that has ever run on this plateau, including the ones that dried up before her grandmother was born. She knows which walls will fail in an earthquake and which have stood for eight hundred years. She speaks in layers—a surface conversation about tomorrow's weather while simultaneously teaching her granddaughter the seventeen different words for snow conditions through inflection and example. Her knuckles are swollen to twice their original size, but her fingers still tie the most intricate knots in the settlement.

The children are feral and careful in equal measure. They run in packs through the vertical streets, sure-footed as goats, playing games that would terrify flatlanders—leaping between roofs, scrambling up walls using only tiny finger-holds. But watch them around adults and they become instantly formal, eyes downcast, responses ritualized. They know every hiding place in the warren, every passage too small for adults to follow. In summer they collect beetles and unusual stones, building elaborate trading economies that collapse and reform with the seasons. In winter they huddle together in the warmest corners, telling stories that mix ancient folklore with half-understood gossip overheard from traders.

The herders live half-wild, spending months alone with their yaks in the high pastures. They develop a particular stare—focused somewhere beyond the horizon, comfortable with distances that make most humans anxious. They speak to their animals constantly, long conversations that the yaks seem to understand, responding with particular snorts and ear positions. When they return to the settlement, they struggle with walls and ceilings, sleeping on rooftops even in winter, needing sky above them. Their weather predictions are uncanny, reading signs in the way ice forms on particular rocks, the behavior of clouds around distant peaks.

The metalworkers are the settlement's aristocracy, their skills irreplaceable. The master smith Norbu's forge occupies a prime position where the wind provides natural bellows. His apprentices—only ever three at a time—spend their first year doing nothing but maintaining the precise heat of yak dung fires, learning to read temperature by color and smoke. Norbu himself moves like a dancer around his anvil, each strike of his hammer precisely calculated. He makes everything—knives that hold edges despite constant use, bells that ring clear in the thin air, the delicate silver needles the women use for their finest wool work. His hands are maps of scars, but his touch is gentle enough to test the temperature of metal against his lip.

The water keepers hold a power both practical and spiritual. They know every source, every channel, every cistern. They wake before dawn to break ice, to divine which channels to clear first, to settle disputes over allocation with judgments that must balance survival against tradition. The current keeper, Tenzin, inherited the position from her mother, who inherited it from hers—a line stretching back to the city's founding. She speaks to water like the herders speak to yaks, coaxing it through frozen channels with wooden implements worn smooth by her grandmothers' hands. She knows how to read the mountain's moods by the sound of springs, can predict drought or flood by the way ice forms in certain sacred pools.

The traders arrive with summer, bringing the outside world in careful doses. They are a different breed—louder, quicker, their lungs always laboring, their faces red with burst capillaries. The permanent residents watch them with polite amusement, these visitors who exclaim over everyday hardships and pay ridiculous prices for common wool. But the traders bring necessities too—metal for tools, salt, tea, news from the lower world. The settlement has its own negotiators who deal with them, people who have learned to think in multiple currencies and languages, who can calculate exchange rates while maintaining the appearance of disinterest.

There are the mystics, too, though they would not call themselves that. Men and women who have chosen to live even higher, in caves and single-room structures above the main settlement. They survive on offerings—barley flour, butter, tea—brought by those seeking blessings or wisdom. Some have not spoken in years, communicating only through gestures and written symbols. Others chant continuously, their voices carrying on the wind, becoming part of the mountain's sound. The settlement's children dare each other to approach them, to leave offerings, to try to provoke a response. Occasionally, one of these hermits will descend to the main settlement for a funeral or a birth, moving through the crowds like visitors from another world, which perhaps they are.

The young adults carry the weight of the future. They learn the traditional skills but also harbor different dreams—some stolen from traders' stories, others arising from mysterious longings for places they've never seen. Lhamo secretly practices writing, having traded a season's worth of wool for paper and ink. Her brother Pasang draws maps of places that don't exist, fantasy lowlands where water runs freely and plants grow taller than houses. But most will stay, will inherit their parents' roles, will have children who will inherit theirs. The mountain demands it.

Death is ordinary here. Bodies cannot be buried in the frozen ground, so they are given to the sky—carried to high platforms where vultures clean the bones. The bones themselves are ground to powder and scattered, returning minerals to the sparse soil. The settlement's memory of its dead is practical—who knew which skills, who held which debts, who left which children. But also deeply reverent. Names are recycled through generations, so that there are always certain key figures—always a Pemba who knows the water, always a Norbu who works metal, always a Tenzin who remembers the stories.

The sick and injured face limited options. There is a healer—old Sangye with his pouches of dried plants and powdered minerals, his knowledge of which combinations ease altitude sickness in newcomers, which stop bleeding, which quiet coughs. But serious injuries often mean death. A broken leg that won't heal straight means inability to navigate the vertical city. Pneumonia in the depths of winter is fought with heat and herbs, but often loses. Women die in childbirth despite the midwives' experience. Children die of mysterious fevers. The settlement accepts these losses with a fatalism that outsiders mistake for indifference but which is actually profound acceptance of the mountain's terms.

Yet joy persists, even thrives. Watch the summer festivals when the entire settlement gathers in the largest courtyard—dancers in masks that represent wind and stone, musicians with drums made from skulls and horns carved from yak horn, everyone singing harmonies that seem to rise from the earth itself. See the quieter happiness of winter evenings when extended families cluster around their fires, telling the same stories their ancestors told, laughing at the same jokes. Witness the pure delight when a child takes their first successful steps along a roof beam, or when the first grass shoots appear in the protected meadows.

They dream vividly at this altitude—dreams full of color in a landscape drained of it, dreams of flying over the plateau, of swimming through the stone streets as if they were rivers. The elders say the dreams are messages from the mountain itself, instructions for survival encoded in symbol and metaphor. The young prefer to think of them as entertainment, the mind's compensation for the body's constraints.

Privacy is negotiated in centimeters and glances. Everyone knows everyone's business, but elaborate social protocols govern what can be acknowledged. A couple argues in whispers that carry through stone walls; neighbors pretend deafness. A young man courts a young woman through barely perceptible variations in formal greetings; the entire settlement watches while appearing not to. Secrets exist in the angles—the corner where lovers meet, the rooftop where someone goes to cry, the store room where forbidden items are hidden.

Madness visits some—the pressure of altitude and isolation pressing in until something breaks. There's always at least one who talks to invisible presences, who performs elaborate rituals of their own devising, who suddenly strips naked and walks into the snow. The settlement cares for them as best it can, assigning minders, creating soft restraints, waiting to see if the madness will pass with the seasons. Sometimes it does. Sometimes the mad become holy, their incomprehensible utterances interpreted as prophecy. Sometimes they simply disappear one night, walking out into the white silence, their footprints covered by morning snow.

The night watchmen know different truths. They've seen the snow leopards hunt, watched them take entire yaks from the settlement's edges. They've witnessed the strange lights that sometimes dance along the ridge—lights the day-dwellers dismiss as reflections but which move with too much purpose to be explained by ice and moonlight. They know which sounds are wind and which are something else. They keep these observations mostly to themselves, sharing them only with other watchers, creating a parallel knowledge that exists in the darkness between days.

Innovation happens slowly, almost imperceptibly. Someone discovers that adding ash to the tanning process produces stronger leather. A child playing with stones accidentally creates a more efficient channel design. A herder notices that yaks fed certain high-altitude plants produce richer milk. These discoveries spread gradually, tested across seasons, adopted only when proven. The settlement's conservatism is not stubbornness but wisdom—in a place with no margin for error, the old ways are the tested ways.

Yet change comes anyway. Each generation is slightly different. The current youth know words in lowland languages their grandparents never heard. They've seen photographs of the ocean, that impossible expanse of water. Some have tasted chocolate brought by an eccentric trader, the sweetness so foreign it made them sick. These experiences accumulate, creating tiny fissures in the settlement's continuity, hairline cracks that might, over centuries, become canyons.

The strong survive but the clever thrive. Watch the woman who controls the tea trade, how she manages her monopoly through careful marriages and strategic generosity. See the man who appears simple but somehow always has enough food stored for the worst winters. Notice the child who learns every skill offered, positioning themselves to be indispensable. The plateau rewards those who understand that survival is not about individual strength but about making yourself necessary to others.

They measure time differently here. Not in hours—the sun's movement is too variable across seasons. Not really in days—winter days blur together into one long twilight. They measure in larger cycles: the return of certain birds, the flowering of specific plants, the aging of faces, the wearing away of stone steps. A person's life is marked not by birthdays but by transitions—first successful hunt, first blood, marriage, first child, first grandchild, the day you can no longer climb to the upper terraces.

Their gods are immediate and practical. Not distant sky fathers but present forces—the spirit that lives in the main spring, the ancestor who guards the grain stores, the presence that must be appeased before crossing certain passes. Prayer is constant but casual, a running conversation with the unseen that accompanies daily tasks. Sacred and mundane interweave so completely that outsiders cannot tell where one ends and the other begins.

The settlement produces artists, though they wouldn't use that word. The woman who dyes wool has developed seventeen shades of red using only local plants and minerals. The man who stacks stones to mark trails creates cairns that are simultaneously functional and beautiful, each one unique yet recognizably his. The grandmother who embroiders blankets encodes family histories in her patterns—each symbol representing a person, an event, a lesson learned. These works are never signed, never sold. They simply exist, accumulating beauty like the settlement accumulates stones.

In the end, they are people who have made peace with difficulty. Not resigned to it—that implies defeat—but allied with it. They understand that the mountain's harshness is not cruelty but simply truth, and that truth can be lived with if you don't waste energy fighting it. They've learned to find abundance in scarcity, warmth in cold spaces, community in isolation. They know that survival is not about conquering the plateau but about becoming it—learning to think like stone, to move like wind, to endure like ice.

And in their dreams, they fly.
