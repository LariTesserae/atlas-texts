The Gillkeepers arrived first, before the city had a name, when the aerial sea was still settling into its impossible equilibrium. They were not refugees but pilgrims, drawn by dreams of breathing water that tasted of stone.

They stand taller than you'd expect—elongated by generations of moving between mediums. Their ribcages are vast, accommodating lungs that have subdivided into complex chambers. The anterior sacs process air; the posterior ones filter water through modified alveoli lined with semi-permeable membrane. When they dive upward from the dry streets into the suspended sea, you can watch their torsos reshape: ribs flexing outward, intercostal muscles creating rhythmic waves along their flanks. The transformation takes thirty seconds. They call it "the crossing breath."

Their skin tells the story of adaptation. Below the waterline, it's smooth, almost poreless, secreting a thin mucus that reduces drag and prevents osmotic water loss. Above, it roughens into a landscape of modified pores that can seal or dilate, regulating gas exchange. The boundary between these two textures wanders across their bodies depending on the tides—you can read the water's history in the patterns on their backs. Children are born uniformly smooth; their rough patches develop only after their first hundred crossings.

The Gillkeepers build with their bodies as much as their hands. In the flooded workshops, they exhale streams of carbon dioxide that they've concentrated in specialized lung chambers, using the bubbles to test currents, to calibrate the flow through new pipes, to communicate in a language of ascending spheres. Their breath has evolved to be a tool: they can supercool it to freeze small portions of water, superheat it using thermogenic tissue, or charge it with bioelectricity generated by modified muscle cells in their throats.

But they are not alone. The Sediment Weavers came later, ascending from the true ground when they heard of a city that existed in gradient. They're smaller, denser, their bones filled with magnetite deposits that let them adjust their buoyancy by aligning with the planet's magnetic field. Where the Gillkeepers are vertical beings, the Weavers are horizontal—their spines flex laterally like fish, their four arms positioned to work while swimming. They see poorly in air but perfectly in water, their eyes covered by transparent nictitating membranes that correct for refraction.

The Weavers brought with them the knowledge of preservation. In their workshops—always positioned at the exact median depth where pressure equalizes—they compress kelp fibers into sheets stronger than leather, waterproof but breathable. Their fingers secrete an enzyme that partially dissolves limestone, letting them sculpt the mineral deposits that accumulate on the city's upper surfaces into tools, vessels, architectural elements. They speak in frequencies that carry equally through both mediums, their voices occupying a narrow band that sounds like singing whether heard in air or water.

Together, these two peoples create children who belong fully to neither tradition. The Threshold Generation, they're called—born in the delivery pools that sit exactly at the waterline, taking their first breath of both air and water simultaneously. These children develop unique physiologies: hearts with three chambers instead of two or four, blood that carries both hemoglobin and hemocyanin, eyes with split pupils that can focus simultaneously above and below the surface. Some grow gills that function only during the middle tides, forcing them to live within a narrow altitude band. Others develop skin that changes color based on the medium—purple in water, amber in air, iridescent at the interface.

The eldest inhabitants tell stories of the Beforewater, when this was merely a mountain city. A few claim to remember it, though their memories seem more dream than history. They describe a time when the builders knew only one way to breathe, when architecture grew upward toward sky instead of downward toward a second sky, when the concept of "drowning upward" had no meaning. These elders have adapted poorly—they wear crude respirators made from fish bladders, spend most of their time in the air pocket chambers, venture into the water only during the lowest tides. Their children and grandchildren care for them with a mixture of reverence and pity.

But adaptation runs in all directions. In the deepest parts of the aerial sea, where the pressure approaches that of normal ocean depths, live the Fullwater People—those who've abandoned the interface entirely. They've sealed their nostrils, developed true gills along their necks and sides, let their leg bones fuse into powerful tails. They farm the kelp forests that grow in the permanent aquatic zone, hunt the primitive sharks that somehow migrated up through underground channels, maintain the city's upper boundaries where no partial breather can survive for long. Once a year, during the spring overturn when the water churns and reoxygenates, they descend to the city proper to trade. They communicate only in sign language now, having lost the ability to vocalize.

The city's spiritual leaders are the Breathkeepers, individuals who've learned to hold both air and water in their lungs simultaneously without mixing them, maintaining distinct layers through muscular control and practiced stillness. They can remain at the interface for hours, neither rising nor sinking, their bodies serving as living bridges between the two worlds. During ceremonies, they position themselves at various depths, creating vertical chains of meditation that span the entire habitable zone. Through them, messages pass between the Fullwater People above and the last air-breathing holdouts below, translated through bodies that understand both languages of respiration.

Children play games that would seem like drowning to outsiders. They compete to see who can transition fastest between breathing modes, who can blow the largest bubbles from the deepest air pockets, who can swim closest to the deadly interface at the water's upper boundary where pressure drops too quickly for blood to remain liquid. They keep pets—lungfish that follow them between zones, octopi that have learned to tolerate air for short periods, air-breathing turtles whose shells serve as portable diving bells. Some children develop imaginary friends that can only exist in one medium or the other, forcing them to constantly cross boundaries to maintain their invisible relationships.

The merchants arrive from other peak settlements, following guide-chains of magnetized iron rings that create safe passages through the aerial sea. They're recognizable by their portable breathing apparatus—complex arrangements of tubes and bladders that let them survive in cities with different water chemistries, different pressure gradients. They bring news of places where the water is more acidic, where the inhabitants have developed crystalline exoskeletons for protection; of settlements where the water is nearly fresh, supporting entirely different ecosystems; of the rumored Far Peaks where the aerial sea is said to be warm enough to steam, creating a third zone of vapor above the water where yet another kind of life is emerging.

During the storm seasons, when electrical discharge between the true ground and the aerial sea turns the water into a network of blue fire, everyone retreats to the insulated chambers—rooms lined with volcanic glass, filled with neutral air. These are the only times the two populations truly mix, pressed together by necessity, sharing the same recycled breaths. Children born during these storms are said to be electric, their hearts generating enough bioelectricity to light small bulbs, their touch able to stun the tiny fish that swim through the city's flooded corridors.

The city's memory-keepers record all this in a medium that survives both air and water: thin sheets of gold beaten between layers of transparent kelp-silk, inscribed with obsidian styluses. The archive exists at multiple depths, each level copying the others but adding its own perspective—the same history told through the lens of different altitudes, different ways of breathing. 

To read the complete record requires a lifetime of adaptation, moving between levels as your body slowly changes, understanding each chapter through lungs that themselves are transforming. By the time scholars reach the deepest texts, held in chambers that have been underwater since the city's founding, they often can no longer return to tell what they've learned. Their knowledge becomes part of the depth itself, accessible only to those who follow them into the anatomical commitment of full submersion.

This is what lives in the city that breathes through gills of stone: a population in constant transition, their bodies archives of atmospheric history, their children experiments in breathing, their architecture an argument about whether it's possible to drown in the sky. They are not becoming something other than human—they are discovering how many ways there are to remain human when the world itself refuses to choose between air and sea.

At night, when bioluminescent plankton bloom in the aerial sea, the city's inhabitants gather at the interface to watch the light. Those in the water see it directly—blue-green constellations swirling in the current. Those in air see it reflected, refracted, transformed into something else entirely. They watch the same phenomenon from different sides of breath, and in the watching, understand that they have become a people who exist in the space between seeing and being seen, between breathing and drowning, between the life they evolved for and the life they're evolving toward.
