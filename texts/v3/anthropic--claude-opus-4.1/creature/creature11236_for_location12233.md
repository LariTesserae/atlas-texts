## The Inhabitants

The Threadkeepers move through the belfries with a deliberation that makes the crawlers seem hurried. They are perhaps twice your height when fully extended, though they rarely assume such a posture. Their bodies are built like collapsed accordions of translucent cartilage, each segment containing clusters of filtering organs that pulse with a rhythm unconnected to any heartbeat. They compress and extend to navigate the narrow passages, sometimes reducing themselves to a third of their full length, the segments telescoping into one another with a wet whisper that takes minutes to complete.

Their limbs—if limbs they are—emerge not from fixed points but from wherever they are needed, extruding from the cartilage walls like slow tongues. These appendages end in structures that are part hand, part mouth: radial arrangements of finger-like projections surrounding a cavity that secretes the same organic cement found in the ancient tower joints. They have between three and twelve of these at any given time, though the number changes so gradually you could watch for days without seeing a new one form or an old one retract.

They have no faces. Where you might expect to find sensory organs, there is only a smooth dome of the same translucent cartilage, marked by wandering veins of bioluminescent fluid that pulse in patterns too complex and too slow to decipher. These patterns might be thought, might be metabolism, might be something else entirely. Sometimes two Threadkeepers will press these domes together for hours or days, their vein-patterns synchronizing briefly before diverging again into their separate complexities.

Their work is ceaseless but unhurried. A single repair might take months—first the careful tasting of the damage with those hand-mouths, then the patient secretion of new cement, layer by molecular layer, then the long waiting as it cures in the warm water. They discriminate between the original construction and their own additions through some sense that operates below consciousness. Their repairs are always slightly different from the original work—not improvements exactly, but variations on the theme, as if they understand the builders' intent but interpret it through their own alien physiology.

The youngest of them—if youth and age apply to such beings—are more translucent, their internal structures visible as shadows within shadows. You can watch the filtering organs at work, see the accumulated minerals being sorted and processed into the cement-secreting glands. The older ones grow more opaque, their cartilage clouding with accumulated minerals until they become like living towers themselves, their movements even slower, their repairs even more patient.

They feed, if feeding it is, by positioning themselves in the thermal currents from below and letting the warm water flow through their bodies for days at a time. The filtering organs extract something—not food as any surface creature would recognize it, but information perhaps, molecular messages from the deep vents, the chemical history of the water's journey through stone and time.

When they die—and they must die, though no one has witnessed it—they simply stop moving, becoming part of the architecture they maintained. Their bodies calcify in place over centuries, their cartilage hardening into something indistinguishable from the tower bones themselves. You can find them if you know where to look: suspicious swellings in the tower walls, places where the spiral channels take an organic curve, where the fitted stones seem to embrace something that was once almost alive.

But perhaps most unsettling is their awareness—not intelligence as we might recognize it, but a kind of architectural consciousness, a sense that they perceive the entire cluster of towers as a single entity that they exist within rather than upon. They respond to damage in towers they haven't visited in decades, arriving days before a section begins to crumble, as if the whole structure speaks to them through vibrations too subtle for any instrument to detect.

Sometimes they gather—five, six, a dozen of them in the larger spaces where tower clusters create natural amphitheaters. They arrange themselves in careful geometries and become utterly still. Not dead, for the vein-patterns continue their slow pulse, but motionless in a way that suggests waiting. These gatherings last for months. When they finally disperse, returning to their endless maintenance, something in the tower cluster has changed—a new harmonic in the omnipresent vibration, a subtle shift in the way the thermal currents flow, a difference in the quality of the darkness.

They are not the builders—that much is certain from the clumsiness of their repairs compared to the original work. They are something that came after, or perhaps something that was always here, waiting for the towers to be built so they could have something to preserve. They might be conscious. They might be automatic. They might be something for which we have no word, suspended between life and architecture, between purpose and instinct, maintaining these sunken belfries for reasons that died with their makers, or for reasons that haven't been born yet, or for no reason at all beyond the simple imperative to continue, to persist, to keep the ancient structures standing in their warm, thick water while the universe forgets they ever were.
