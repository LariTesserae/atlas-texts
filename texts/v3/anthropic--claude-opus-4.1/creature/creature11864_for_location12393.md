The Tenders move through the district like careful spiders navigating their own web. You recognize them by their oilcloth cloaks—waxed canvas in bottle green or rust brown that sheds the perpetual drip—and by the way they read the pipes like braille, fingers trailing along brass surfaces to feel for vibration irregularities, temperature variations, the subtle calcification that presages a blockage.

They are thin people, made lean by climbing. Their hands are permanently stained with verdigris, nails outlined in black from scraping gaskets. They wear their hair short or tightly bound—anything loose catches in the machinery, winds around valve stems. Their eyes have adapted to the permanent twilight, pupils wide and dark, capable of distinguishing between fifty different qualities of steam by its color and drift pattern. They speak in undertones that somehow carry perfectly beneath the mechanical din, having learned the exact frequency that travels along the pipes.

Children here are born listening. The pregnant workers sit with their backs against the warmest pipes, letting the vibrations conduct through amniotic fluid. Infants sleep in hammocks strung between steam valves, lulled by the chunk-hiss-chunk rhythm. By three, they can distinguish between the sound of a healthy pump and one beginning to fail. By seven, they climb the lower pipes unsupervised, gathering the pale mushrooms that fruit from the junction boxes, collecting condensation in copper cups worn smooth by generations of small hands.

The elderly become listeners—too brittle-boned for the climbing but too valuable to waste. They sit in chairs positioned at acoustic focal points where multiple pipe systems converge, eyes closed, heads tilted, diagnosing problems three buildings away by sound alone. They wear felted wool even in the heat, joints swollen from decades of moisture. Their apprentices bring them samples—a vial of water from the third-tier collectors, a scraping of scale from the primary fermentation tower, a cutting from a vine that's grown too aggressive around the intake valves. The elders taste the water, rub the scale between arthritic fingers, smell the sap of the vine, and prescribe adjustments: *two turns clockwise on the blue-tagged valve, increase the copper sulfate in tank seven, leave the vine but install a deflection plate.*

The workers' formal names are forgettable, bureaucratic things recorded in the district registry. But each carries a use-name earned through their relationship with the machines: *Whistler* for the woman who can diagnose problems by the pitch of steam leaks. *Copper-Touch* for the man whose palms can detect temperature differences of less than a degree. *The Moss* for the ancient tender who hasn't left the third tier in twelve years, whose cloak has grown a living coat of the pale vegetation, who seems to photosynthesize more than eat.

They trade in pressures and flows rather than currency. A family's status is measured by their proximity to the primary pumps—the massive, ancient mechanisms at the district's heart whose tenders pass their positions to their children like heirlooms. These central families have developed physical adaptations over generations: extended lung capacity from breathing the thick air, enhanced night vision, a resistance to the fungal infections that plague newcomers. Their children are born with a slight green tinge to their skin that deepens with age—copper accumulation in the dermis, harmless, marking them as true natives.

Romance happens in the brief spaces between shifts, on the catwalks where lovers meet to share dried fruit and bottles of the distilled water that's the district's only export. They court with gifts of rare mushrooms, with well-oiled wrenches, with sketches of pipe configurations that solve long-standing flow problems. Marriage ceremonies involve the couple's hands being bound with vine while they recite the Oath of Continuous Flow, promising to maintain their systems—both mechanical and personal—in good working order.

The sick are treated with steam and minerals. The local medicine woman operates from a corner where seven different pipe systems converge, using the various temperatures and mineral contents like a pharmacy. Respiratory ailments are treated in the high-sulfur chambers. Joint problems respond to the magnetized water from the iron pipes. The depressed are prescribed time in the bright chambers near the surface, where actual sunlight filters through greenhouse glass.

Death, when it comes, is marked by silence—the stopping of one small part of the great mechanical organism. Bodies are wrapped in the same vines that embrace the pipes and lowered into the biosump, where the district's accumulated botanical mass slowly processes all organic matter into the dark, nutritious water that feeds the plants that feed the people. The only monuments are valve handles—removed from decommissioned systems and hung in clusters from the undersides of catwalks, where they turn slowly in the rising steam, each engraved with a use-name and the years of service.

The Tenders dream in pressure differentials and flow rates. They wake with solutions to problems they hadn't consciously recognized. They read the health of their district in the color of the condensation, the pattern of moss growth, the frequency of the pressure releases. They are simultaneous mechanics and gardeners, wholly adapted to this twilight world where the boundary between machine and organism dissolved so long ago that they no longer perceive a difference.

In the depths of night shifts, when the steam thickens until visibility drops to arm's length, they navigate by touch and sound alone, their movements so practiced they seem choreographed—a dance of maintenance that has continued unbroken for generations. They are the antibodies in this great industrial organism, moving through its vessels, ensuring the endless circulation continues.

And in their cramped apartments between the machines, they raise children who will never see a dry surface, who will think the constant drumming of water on metal is what silence sounds like, who will grow up believing that all air should taste of copper and chlorophyll, that all light should be green-tinted and wet, that the purpose of human life is to tend the great breathing apparatus that processes water from darkness into steam into rain into darkness again, forever and ever, world without end.
