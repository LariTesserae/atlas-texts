The people here move like the water they harvest—finding the path of least resistance, pooling where conditions allow, always flowing toward purpose. They are engineers by birth rather than training, their understanding of fluid dynamics encoded in muscle memory from childhoods spent racing paper boats down the condensation channels, learning to read the stone's moisture gradients before they could read words.

Their bodies tell the story of adaptation across generations. Broad, flat feet for gripping wet limestone. Shortened tendons in their ankles from perpetual navigation of shallow steps. Their lungs process thin air efficiently, but more distinctively, their nasal passages have elongated, lined with specialized membranes that reclaim moisture from every exhalation. They breathe through cloth filters during working hours—not for the dust, but to prevent the precious water vapor from escaping. The oldest families show this most strongly: children born with naturally narrowed nostrils, tears that barely wet the eye.

They wear the moisture-seekers like living jewelry. The creatures cling to specially treated shoulder patches, collar stays, belt loops—trained generations kept as ancestral lines, their shells selectively bred for iridescent patterns that indicate family trades. A metallurgist's bloodline might carry moisture-seekers with shells that gleam blue-black like tempered steel; a water technician's show rippling patterns that mirror the flow of their condensation systems. The creatures feed on their hosts' sweat, keeping the skin dry, preventing the fungal blooms that plague newcomers. Children receive their first breeding pair at seven, when they've proven they can keep them alive through a full seasonal cycle.

The water engineers comprise the city's nervous system—a dispersed consciousness of maintenance and measurement. They know their districts by taste: each terrace's limestone has a slightly different mineral signature, each condensation system leaves its own metallic trace. They work in twelve-hour shifts aligned with the moisture cycles, sleeping when the stone exhales, waking when it drinks. Their apprenticeships last decades. A master engineer can place their palm on warm limestone and feel the humidity gathering beneath the surface, can predict within minutes when the stone will begin to weep.

In the factories, the processor crews work in choreographed clusters, their movements synchronized to the rhythm of the moisture-seekers' migrations. They've developed a signed language that functions in the perpetual machine noise—complex hand positions that communicate not just words but water volumes, pressure readings, chemical compositions. Their children learn this language alongside speech, tiny fingers practicing the gesture for "saturation" before they can tie their shoes.

The residents dream collectively of drowning, though none have seen enough water to truly drown in. It's a racial memory, perhaps, from wherever their ancestors lived before the terraces. They wake gasping, checking their moisture-seeker companions by habit—the creatures grow agitated when their hosts' stress hormones spike, chittering warnings that wake whole apartment blocks in cascading waves of insectoid alarm.

Social hierarchies crystallize around water access. The highest-status families control spillway-adjacent properties, where the constant mist creates micro-climates of abundance. They grow small gardens of the fungal-adjacent organisms in their apartments, prestigious living sculptures that require precise moisture control. Their children's moisture-seekers are enormous, palm-sized specimens that move with languid confidence. The poor cluster in the dry zones between condensation plants, their moisture-seekers stunted and desperate, barely larger than fingernails.

They eat proteins mostly—processed from the endless carpet of dead moisture-seekers, formed into blocks and flavored with mineral salts extracted from the condensation. Traditional cuisine involves careful rehydration, an art form where master chefs can make a single cup of water serve a twelve-course meal through precise recycling and recapture. Wealthy families import ice from the highest terraces, serving it at celebrations where guests watch it sublime directly from solid to vapor in the thin air, never quite reaching liquid form.

Their music mimics the spillways—long, sustained drones punctuated by rhythmic clicking that matches the moisture-seekers' patterns. Orchestras tune to the frequency of water droplets on limestone. Their instruments use directed streams of vapor, shaped by resonating chambers, producing sounds that carry for kilometers in the moist air. Children learn rhythm by matching the industrial release cycles, their playground songs timed to the scheduled floods.

They mark time differently here. A "cycle" spans from dry stone to full saturation—roughly fourteen hours, though it varies by season and terrace. They age not in years but in accumulations, counting the mineral deposits that build on their tools, their doorframes, their bones. An elder's joints click like moisture-seekers from decades of precipitated calcium. They consider this beautiful.

Death comes quietly. Bodies are processed through the industrial systems, their water reclaimed first, their minerals returned to the collection channels. The mourning period lasts one full cycle, during which the deceased's moisture-seekers are released at the spillway's edge, carrying engineered pheromones that draw wild populations to gather in temporary memorial clouds. The city's children know to be quiet when they see these gatherings, these living monuments that disperse with the morning condensation.

Romance follows water's logic—slow percolation through social strata, sudden phase changes when conditions align. Courtship involves gifting rare moisture-seeker breeds, proving one's ability to maintain delicate humidity requirements. Marriage ceremonies take place at dawn, when the ground fog is thickest, the couple exchanging vows while visibility drops to nothing, their words echoing off stone they cannot see. They trust the terraces to witness what human eyes cannot.

The young dream of descending—following the spillways all the way down to wherever the water ultimately gathers. Expeditions have tried, but the lower terraces become increasingly hostile, the moisture-seekers growing strange and aggressive, the fungal-adjacent organisms forming defensive colonies that release neurotoxic spores. The elders say there's nothing down there but accumulated runoff and the bones of cities that forgot how to harvest. The young don't believe them. They train in secret, breeding moisture-seekers for size and venom, studying the old survey maps that stop three terraces below the lowest inhabited zone.

In the quiet hours between shifts, when the factories reduce to maintenance power and the moisture-seekers settle into temporary stillness, the city's inhabitants stand at their windows and listen to the stone breathing. They feel the vast, slow pulse of water through rock and metal, through flesh and shell. They are part of this cycle, neither beginning nor end, but process—like water itself, taking the shape of their constraints, always seeking, always flowing, always becoming something else while remaining essentially themselves.
