The Moth-Shepherds themselves are difficult to distinguish from their environment until they move. Generations of living within the mist has left their skin with a peculiar quality—not pale exactly, but translucent in certain lights, as if they're perpetually on the verge of dissolving into fog. Their hair grows in multiple textures on the same head: coarse strands like climbing roots interspersed with filaments so fine they float independently, sensing changes in humidity and air pressure. Children are born with an extra joint in their smallest fingers, a minor evolution that makes gripping wet stone as natural as walking.

They dress in layers of silk that shift color with moisture—dry, the fabric appears stone-gray, but as mist accumulates, patterns emerge like watermarks: maps of wing-scales, diagrams of root systems, texts in a script that mimics the tunneling paths of bark beetles. No two garments show the same patterns. The silk degrades deliberately over months, designed to fray into specific shapes that catch and channel condensation into hidden vessels worn against the skin. A shepherd might drink from their own clothing's collected moisture throughout the day, never removing the thin ceramic tubes that run along their collarbones.

Their tools evolve with use. A shepherding stick, carved from the heartwood of vertical-growing trees, develops its own ecology over years—colonies of beneficial bacteria that glow faintly when touching certain moth species, warning of disease or distress. The bronze knives they carry oxidize in patterns that correspond to what they've cut: those used for harvesting plants show green veins, while those used in moth-tending develop a blue-black patina that moths find calming. Each tool becomes irreplaceable, a record of its keeper's work written in metal and wood.

The eldest shepherds no longer speak in words but in frequencies. Their vocal cords have adapted to produce sounds below normal human hearing, vibrations that travel through stone and water better than air. Younger shepherds learn to feel these conversations through their feet and palms pressed against walls. In the highest terraces, where the giant moths roost, ancient shepherds sit for hours in apparent silence, conducting complex negotiations about territory, breeding rights, and the placement of eggs through subsonic pulses that make ripples in pools of water dozens of meters away.

Children of the settlement grow up vertical. Infants are carried in slings that leave their limbs free to grasp, and they learn to grip before they walk. By age three, they climb independently, their movements incorporating the pauses and sudden stillness of the creatures around them—freezing mid-reach when a moth passes, moving in quick bursts between periods of impossible stillness. They play games that would terrify flatlanders: blindfolded climbing races, competitions to match the wing patterns of moths from memory, silent hide-and-seek where the seeker must identify hidden children by the change in insect songs around their hiding spots.

The moth-shepherds' bodies show their work. Their shoulders slope upward from constant climbing. Their eyes develop a nictitating membrane—a third eyelid that protects against wing scales and mist while maintaining vision. Those who work with the giant moths of the upper reaches grow patches of skin that match moth wing texture, rough and scaled, usually along their forearms where the moths grip during handling. These patches are considered beautiful, marks of expertise that young shepherds sometimes try to induce prematurely by rubbing their skin with wing scales, though the artificial patches never quite match the complex patterns that develop naturally through years of contact.

They eat what the gardens provide, but their diet follows no pattern outsiders recognize. Certain moths, at specific points in their lifecycle, produce a nectar from glands near their wing joints—harvested by gentle pressure, never enough to harm, collected in vessels passed down through families. Larvae of particular species are cultivated not for consumption but for the silk they produce internally, extracted through patient massage, mixed with fungal spores, and left to ferment into a paste that sustains a shepherd for days. They know which flowers can be eaten only after a moth has fed on them, the insect's saliva triggering chemical changes that transform toxins into nutrients.

Their medicine is collaboration. Healers keep colonies of cleaner beetles that consume dead tissue from wounds. They cultivate moths whose cocoons produce antibiotic silk. For broken bones, they use a species of vine that contracts as it dries, creating perfect natural casts that gradually loosen as healing progresses. The healers themselves often show signs of their work—fingernails stained permanent violet from handling certain medicinal fungi, skin that luminesces faintly in darkness from years of contact with glowing organisms, a tendency to hum at frequencies that calm injured moths and humans alike.

The moth-shepherds marry in clusters, forming family units of three to seven adults who collectively tend their section of gardens and raise children communally. Marriage ceremonies involve the release of specially raised moths whose flight patterns are believed to predict the union's future—but only the eldest shepherds can read these aerial prophecies, and they rarely share their interpretations directly, instead offering cryptic gifts: a particular seed, a new cutting tool, a small pot of water from a specific spring.

Death, when it comes, is given to the gardens. Bodies are placed in specially prepared hollow trees, standing vertical against the cliff. Within days, they're colonized by moths, beetles, fungi—becoming nurseries for the next generation of the ecosystem. The families tend these tree-tombs for exactly one year, harvesting what grows, then seal them. Decades later, when the tree naturally opens again, all that remains are patterns in the wood that resemble the deceased—a profile in grain, hands in the reach of branches, their final expression in the arrangement of bore holes. Children learn to read these patterns, to recognize ancestors they never met in the wooden galleries that dot the cliff face.

At night, the moth-shepherds dream collectively. Not through any mystical means, but through the accumulation of shared stimuli—the same phosphorescent fungi glowing in everyone's peripheral vision, the same ultrasonic moth songs vibrating through the stone into their sleeping bodies, the same chemical signals from night-blooming flowers entering their lungs. They wake having dreamed variations of the same themes: flying on wings they don't possess, seeing colors that don't exist in daylight, understanding conversations between species they cannot consciously comprehend. These dreams guide their work—a shepherd might relocate an entire moth colony based on a widespread dream of swarming, or plant new species of vine after multiple families dream of empty vertical spaces that need filling.

The moth-shepherds don't see themselves as human in the way flatlanders understand the term. They are part of the Hanging Gardens' superorganism, as essential and integrated as the moths, the vines, the weeping stone itself. They reproduce, yes, but also reproduce through their work—every carefully tended garden section contains their genetic material in the arrangement of species, the selection of traits, the patient cultivation of new forms. A shepherd's greatest achievement isn't a child but a stable moth lineage, a new harmony between predator and prey, a garden that continues to thrive generations after its creator's body has fed the trees.

When outsiders visit—traders, scholars, the curious—the moth-shepherds are courteous but incomprehensible. They offer gifts that make no sense: a single scale from a moth's wing, water that must be drunk at a specific elevation, seeds that will only grow if planted during certain weather patterns that occur once a decade. They answer questions with demonstrations: asked about their history, a shepherd might spend hours showing the growth patterns in a grandmother vine that records rainfall through ring thickness. Asked about their beliefs, they might lead visitors to watch moths gathering at dusk, letting the display speak for itself.

In the end, the moth-shepherds are translators between kingdoms of life, existing in the spaces between human and insect, plant and animal, stone and flesh. Their strangeness isn't deviation from humanity but evolution toward something more plural, more integrated, more honest about the arbitrary boundaries between self and ecosystem. They are what humans become when they stop trying to master a place and instead agree to be mastered by it, transformed cell by cell, thought by thought, into something the mountain can use.
