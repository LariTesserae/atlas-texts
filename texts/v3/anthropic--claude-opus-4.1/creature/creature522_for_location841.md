The people of the Drowned Canopy move with a distinctive economy of motion—never hurried, each gesture deliberate and flowing. Their skin carries a faint iridescent sheen, generations of symbiotic algae having left traces of copper-green and violet in the creases of elbows, the hollows of throats. Children are born pale and gradually develop these markings, like slow tattoos painted by their environment.

They are tall as a rule, with elongated torsos and limbs that seem stretched by their three-dimensional existence. Their fingers and toes are subtly lengthened, with the merest suggestion of webbing at the base—not true adaptation so much as the body's response to perpetual moisture. Hair grows long and moves independently in the water, often worn in complex braids weighted with small carved stones to keep it from drifting into the face. The elderly develop manes of silver and white that float around their heads like halos of kelp.

The Fishmongers are a hereditary guild who maintain relationships with the schools that swim the city's thoroughfares. They don't catch so much as negotiate, offering fermented fruit paste and pressed cakes of algae in exchange for the eldest members of each school, those ready to die. They know every species by the sound of its movement, can predict migrations by the temperature of the water against their wrists. Their children learn to count by sorting scales, to read by tracing the patterns on fish skin.

The Rootworkers live in the tangle below, tending the dead and managing the vast network of interwoven wood that holds the city stable. They rarely surface, developing huge lung capacities that let them work for hours in the deeper, darker water. Their backs are scarred from squeezing through tight spaces, their hands permanently stained black from handling decomposing matter. They speak in hand signals more than words, a language of efficiency developed in the muffling depths. The other residents regard them with a mixture of respect and unease—necessary but strange, keepers of the city's foundation and its graves.

Elderly women called Breathkeepers cultivate the symbiotic algae that process the water in human lungs. They tend nursery pools where newcomers come to be colonized, a process that takes three days of carefully monitored immersion. Each Breathkeeper maintains her own strain, passed down through generations, and families swear by particular lineages—the Greenwater line for endurance, the Clearbell strain for singers and storytellers. They diagnose illness by the color of exhaled mist, prescribe treatments of different algae combinations, some medicinal, some merely comforting.

The Glassmakers work their furnaces in the only dry spaces in the city—sealed chambers kept clear of water through constantly maintained pumps, ancient mechanisms no one fully understands anymore. The work is dangerous; a breach would mean drowning or being boiled alive by steam. They emerge from their shifts red-eyed and gasping, their lungs temporarily confused by air, needing hours to readjust to breathing water again. Their apprentices start by just sitting in the dry chambers, learning to tolerate the strange burning sensation of unprocessed air.

Children form gangs that race through the kelp fronds, developing elaborate games around the movements of tide and current that only they fully understand. They keep amphibious lemurs as pets, training them to fetch objects from the dry lands below during the twice-yearly exodus when some of the water recedes. Their knees and elbows are perpetually skinned from collisions with bark and root, healing into patterns of scars that they compare like badges of honor.

The Singers are the city's historians and timekeepers, their voices trained to carry through water in specific frequencies that resonance with the kelp trunks. A trained Singer can make the entire city hum with harmonics, sending messages across districts faster than any swimmer could carry them. They perform at funerals, their lamentations traveling down to the Rootworkers below, and at births, when the whole district must be informed of new names. Their throats develop distinctive ridges, almost like internal gills, from the constant specialized vocalizations.

Young adults often experience "the drifting"—a period where they swim to the edges of the water-lens and stare out at the dry mountains, some even breaking through to breathe raw air and walk on solid ground for a few hours before returning, coughing and disoriented. Most come back permanently after one or two ventures. Those who don't become traders, intermediaries between the Drowned Canopy and the outside world, though they're forever marked as partially foreign, never quite readjusting to either world completely.

The oldest residents gradually become less mobile, spending their final years in the high branches where the water is warmest and the light strongest. They develop a peculiar tranquility, their breathing slowing to match the rhythm of the kelp's swaying. When they die, their families know by the way the fish gather, drawn by some chemical signal of transition. The Rootworkers come with their poles and hooks, and the descent begins, accompanied by the Singers' underwater keen that makes the whole city shiver with harmonic grief.

This is how they live: patient, adapted, their bodies and traditions shaped by the endless pressure and movement of living water, by the permanence of their suspension between earth and sky.
