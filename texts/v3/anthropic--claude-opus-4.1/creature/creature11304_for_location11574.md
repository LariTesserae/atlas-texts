The people of the canal quarter move like careful spiders through their vertical world, though they have long since ceased to think of their movements as careful — this is simply how bodies navigate when every step might be over water.

They are a small people, made smaller by generations of ducking through low doorways and crouching in compressed spaces. Their bones show it: curved spines that accommodate perpetual hunching, elongated arms from reaching across channels, feet that splay wide and grip like hands. The children born here can hang by their toes from the underside of walkways while they work, their ankle joints loose in ways that would seem broken to an outsider.

Their skin carries the quarter's palette — the grey-green undertone of people who rarely see direct sunlight, mottled with patches of chronic dampness that never quite becomes infection but never quite heals. They oil themselves with fish fat rendered down with the crushed shells of the wall-creatures, creating a barrier against the constant moisture. It gives them a faint iridescence in certain lights, like the bronze they work.

The fungi farmers tend their vertical gardens with an attention that borders on religious. Each family maintains its own strains, varieties cultivated over generations to fruit in the particular conditions of their specific walls — this corner that gets two hours of reflected light, that surface that weeps mineral-rich water from the stone behind it. They know the growth patterns like heartbeats, can tell from the color of a sprouting node whether the water has shifted its mineral content, whether the building has settled another fingerbreadth into the substrate. The curved bronze knives they use are hereditary, their handles worn to fit specific hands, passed from parent to child with the knowledge of exactly how to slice to encourage regrowth, which spots will fruit again and which must be left to spore.

The bronze workers have developed a craft that exists nowhere else. In workshops that are more like caves — spaces between buildings that have been roofed over and sealed with pitch — they add their strange alloys under conditions of permanent twilight. They've learned that certain metals must be worked in the presence of the quarter's water, quenched in the channels rather than in buckets, to achieve the oil-slick finish that resists corrosion. Their hammers ring in rhythms that work around the water's sound, creating a percussion that the wall-creatures have learned to incorporate into their tapping conversations. The youngest apprentices spend their first years just learning to keep the forge coals dry, an art involving elaborate shields of beaten metal and systems of reflected heat that would baffle an outside smith.

The fishers are perhaps the strangest adapted. They work by touch rather than sight in the murky channels, their fingers reading the water's movement to know where the schools move. They've developed a sensitivity to vibration that lets them feel a fish's heartbeat through the water, can distinguish between species by the rhythm of their swimming. Their nets are not thrown but built — semi-permanent structures of bronze wire and scraped gut that they adjust daily based on the water's mood. Some have begun to show webbing between their fingers, though whether this is evolution or just the swelling that comes from constant immersion is debated.

There are the message-runners, children mostly, who navigate the vertical maze with memorized precision, never looking at their feet, reading the soft spots in the wooden walkways by the give beneath their weight. They carry words between levels, between quarters, their voices pitched to cut through the constant dripping. They know every shortcut, every board that will hold weight and every one that won't, every window that can be passed through and every household that permits it.

The wall-creature keepers have emerged as an unexpected priesthood. They interpret the tapping that fills the night hours, claiming to read patterns in it that predict the water's rise, the next building's collapse, the quality of the coming fungi harvest. They feed the creatures specific molds to influence their messages, creating what they insist is a conversation but might just be conditioning. They wear cloaks of the creatures' shed shells, a rattling armor that marks them as intermediaries between the human and invertebrate residents.

At night, the quarter transforms. Those who must work in darkness — the drain cleaners, the foundation reinforcers, the ones who harvest the night-fruiting fungi — emerge with bronze lanterns that burn fish oil with a peculiar blue flame that penetrates the mist better than yellow light would. They move in practiced groups, one to work, one to hold light, one to watch for the signs of imminent collapse that are clearest in darkness when the buildings' sounds aren't masked by daily activity.

The oldest residents have begun to change in ways that suggest the quarter itself is remaking them. Their eyes have enlarged, pupils dilated permanently to gather more light. Their children are born this way now, owl-eyed babies who cry at sudden brightness. These elders sit in the highest rooms, the only dry spaces, and remember when there was solid ground here, or claim to. They speak of the time before the quarter pressed in on itself, though their stories change with each telling, as fluid as the channels below.

There is one figure who lives at the cracked foundation, the keeper of the strange clear pool. They are so old their gender has become irrelevant, their body a monument to adaptation — skin like leather from the oil treatments, eyes clouded but somehow still seeing, fingers that can read the clear water's refractions like text. They lower bronze vessels on chains into the pool, pulling up water that they distribute in tiny vials for purposes never explained. Some say it's medicine, some say it's poison, some say it's the same thing. The keeper never speaks, communicating only in the tapping language of the wall-creatures, which they produce by clicking their remaining teeth.

The children born here now are different from their parents in subtle, accumulating ways. They can hold their breath for impossible lengths, their hearts slowing to near-stillness when they dive into the channels. Their bones are less dense, more cartilaginous, allowing them to compress through spaces that would trap an adult. They learn the wall-creature's tapping before they learn speech, and some never quite transition fully to human language, speaking in a hybrid of words and clicks that requires both voice and teeth to understand.

This is a people becoming something new, shaped by their compressed, vertical, dripping world into forms that would seem alien to their own ancestors. They are still human, but only just, and less so with each generation that grows up never knowing dry land, never seeing the horizon, never experiencing silence without the sound of water finding its way through stone.
