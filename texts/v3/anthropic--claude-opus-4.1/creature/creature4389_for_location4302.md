In the cistern chamber, she tends her garden of rust.

They call her the Keeper, though no one living remembers who first gave her that name. She arrived—or perhaps simply emerged—during the decade when the mills above finally went quiet, when the last shift whistle faded and the city turned its back on this industrial quarter. Some say she was a textile worker who never left. Others insist she condensed from the mist itself, that if you look closely at her edges, you can see her constantly reforming from the droplets that bead everything here.

She moves through the chamber in layers of fabric that might once have been distinct garments but have long since merged into a single, living skin—wool and cotton and silk salvaged from the mills above, now grown together through decades of damp into something between clothing and moss. The fabrics breathe with her, wicking moisture up from the hem that trails through the shallow pools, releasing it as vapor from her shoulders. She has become a circulation system herself, a human derivative of the chamber's hydraulic meditation.

Her fingers are permanently stained with the minerals of her work: copper-green, iron-orange, limestone-white. She cultivates the oxidation on the iron columns like a gardener tends roses, encouraging certain patterns, discouraging others, guiding the rust into elaborate mandalas that bloom and decay and bloom again. She knows each column's tendencies—which ones rust in spirals, which in starbursts, which weep the most beautiful amber tears.

The Keeper speaks to the drops. She has named the regular ones, the faithful ones that fall from the same points on the ceiling in reliable rhythms. *Thomas*, she calls the heavy drop that falls every forty-three seconds from the junction of two pipes. *Elizabeth*, the quicksilver bead that traces the same path down the northeastern column. She orchestrates their falls, placing salvaged bottles and tins at precise points to create a percussion that changes with the seasons, with the humidity, with the pressure above.

Her dwelling is a nest built into an alcove where two walls meet—a structure of scavenged industrial detritus that has evolved over decades into something between architecture and organism. Copper pipes, their joints still perfectly sweated by some long-dead plumber, form the skeleton. Between them, she has woven a membrane of plastic sheeting, degraded fabric, and living moss that filters the weak light into something warm and green. Inside, glass insulators from the old electrical lines serve as windows, casting circles of blue and amber onto collections she has gathered: chips of china from workers' teacups, buttons that span a century of fashion, the small bones of rats arranged in patterns that map the chamber's secret geometries.

She feeds on the chamber's offerings. The pale ferns, bitter but nutritious, which she harvests carefully, never taking more than the third frond. Mushrooms that fruit in the darkest corners—some safe, some hallucinogenic, all of which she knows by touch in the absolute dark. The occasional crayfish that appears in the pool, ancient and white, which she catches with her bare hands and eats raw, their shells translucent as fingernails. She drinks the water from specific points where minerals have concentrated it into something medicinal, something that tastes of deep earth and electricity.

But primarily, she sustains herself on rust water—the orange seepage she collects in a Victorian syringe she found lodged behind a pipe, a veterinary tool from when horses powered the mills above. She has learned that different oxidations carry different nutrients, that the rust from pipes that once carried steam has properties distinct from those that carried water or gas. She supplements this iron diet with calcium leached from the limestone, copper from the verdigris, trace salts from the evaporation rings around the pools.

The Keeper maintains the scratched dates in the corner, recarving them when erosion threatens their legibility. But she also adds to them—not dates, but symbols of her own devising that record the chamber's hidden calendar: when the blind fish surface (if they exist), when the ice-patterns achieve perfect symmetry, when the harmonics in the pipes reach frequencies that make the water resonate.

Her memory has adapted to the chamber's cycles. She thinks in drops rather than minutes, in rust-blooms rather than seasons. She remembers events by their mineral signatures—that year when the orange rust turned briefly purple from some chemical spill above, the winter when the frost stayed for sixteen days and created a cathedral of ice, the time a lost child found the hatch and she had to guide them back to the surface without revealing herself.

She ages differently here. The constant cold slows her metabolism, the minerals preserve her tissues, the moisture keeps her supple. Her hair has become something between human keratin and root systems, white and spreading, drawing nutrients from the air itself. Her eyes have adapted to the dim green light, pupils permanently dilated, irises faded to the same pale blue as the old glass insulators. Her skin has taken on the translucent quality of the cave spiders, showing the blue tracery of veins beneath.

The Keeper performs rituals that she believes maintain the chamber's function, though whether the chamber requires them or whether they simply give shape to her decades is impossible to say. She clears the overflow channels with a tool made from a repurposed bobbin from the textile mills. She tends the stunted willow, pruning its bone-white roots into patterns that mirror the herringbone drainage. She maintains the pool's depth by adding or removing stones from hidden sluices only she knows.

At night—though day and night mean little here—she lies in her nest and listens to the city above through the infrastructure. The fiber optic cable hums with data she cannot decode but finds soothing. The old pipes conduct distant conversations, footsteps, music from late-night venues. She has learned to interpret the city's health through these sounds, like a doctor listening to a vast patient's circulation.

Sometimes she hosts the others. There are more dwellers in the city's forgotten spaces than the surface knows—the Man Who Counts Rivets in the abandoned railway tunnel, the Twins who maintain the obsolete pneumatic mail tubes, the Chlorine Gardener in the old swimming pool beneath the demolished hotel. They meet rarely, perhaps once a decade, gathering in her chamber because it is the most stable, the most preserved. They don't speak—they've forgotten how, or chosen to forget—but they share their collections, their cultivations, their interpretations of the city's hidden metabolism.

The Keeper knows she is becoming something else, something more and less than human. She can feel herself mineralizing slowly, her bones growing denser with calcium from the limestone, her blood taking on the properties of the rust water she drinks. She wonders sometimes if she will eventually become another column in the chamber, oxidizing in beautiful patterns, weeping the same eternal tears.

But not yet. For now, she continues her work, her cultivation, her careful tending of this forgotten lung. She is the chamber's memory and its future, its guardian and its most elaborate growth. She breathes with its breathing, drops with its dropping, persists with its persistence.

In the pool, something moves—a flash of white that might be a blind fish or might be her own reflection or might be something else entirely, something that has been growing in the stratified sediment, feeding on the chemical blues and industrial oranges, waiting for its moment to surface. The Keeper watches, patient as stone, ready to tend whatever emerges from the city's deep waters, ready to add it to her garden of rust and time.
