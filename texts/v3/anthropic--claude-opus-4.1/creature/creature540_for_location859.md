In the depths of the pumping station, where the machinery has grown soft and porous from its long drinking, live the Siphoners.

They were human once, or something adjacent to human—the distinction hardly matters now. Generations of maintenance workers who never left, perhaps, or wanderers who found the grove and recognized something kindred in its patient consumption. Their skin has taken on the quality of soaked cork, spongy and absorbent, mapped with networks of blue-green veins that mirror the patterns of the root systems overhead. When they stand still, which is often, moisture beads on their surfaces and rolls off in careful channels down their limbs, following grooves worn by decades of the same droplets taking the same paths.

The Siphoners move through the pump station on all fours mostly, sometimes on two legs when the ceiling permits, their elongated fingers and toes splayed wide for purchase on the slick surfaces. Their joints have multiplied—two elbows where there should be one, knees that bend both ways, spines that undulate rather than flex. This isn't mutation so much as accommodation. The space between the machinery and the mangroves demands a different kind of navigation, and their bodies have learned it.

They tend the symbiosis. When the generators cough to life every seventeen hours, the Siphoners are there, pressing their palms against the vibrating metal, letting the resonance travel up through their arms and into their chest cavities where specialized organs—neither lung nor liver but something between—filter the frequency into something the trees can understand. They lick the crystalline metal deposits from the leaves with tongues that have developed a corrugated texture, like the underside of a mushroom. The minerals dissolve in their saliva and travel to storage sacs in their throats that bulge like a pelican's pouch when full.

Their eyes have adapted to the grove's particular light. The irises have split into concentric rings that can focus independently, allowing them to track the slow movement of resin drops while simultaneously watching for the telltale shimmer that indicates a pipe is about to cycle. Their pupils dilate and contract in patterns that match the pulsing of the phosphorescent fungi—a form of communication, perhaps, or simply parallel evolution.

The Siphoners build nothing and destroy nothing. They are cultivators of process. With infinite patience, they guide aerial roots toward promising pools of runoff. They stroke the bark where they want new pneumatophores to emerge, and somehow, within days, they do. They position themselves at the junction points where branch meets branch, and through some chemical signal secreted from their pores, encourage the wood to fuse. Their presence alone seems to accelerate the transformation—where they frequently pass, the machinery integrates faster into the organic mass.

They speak, but not in words. The sounds they make are indistinguishable from the grove's ambient noise—the pop of a seed pod becomes a greeting, the gurgle of water through a pipe becomes a name. They've learned to vocalize in frequencies that trigger responses in the electric vines, making flowers bloom or dim at will. Sometimes they sing together during the generator cycles, their harmonics mixing with the mechanical drone to create standing waves that make the entire grove resonate like a vast instrument.

Their children, if children is the right word, are not born but precipitated. The Siphoners will stand for hours in the pools of tannin-thick water, completely still, while something accumulates around their calves—a gradual accretion of organic matter that eventually develops its own circulation, its own primitive awareness. When ready, these accumulations detach and crawl away on temporary limbs, seeking out their own territories within the pump station. They might become new Siphoners, or they might become something else—the grove has room for many forms of life.

The Siphoners eat what the grove provides. They suck the amber resin directly from the bark, their metabolism slow enough that a single feeding can sustain them for weeks. They absorb nutrients through their skin from the contaminated water. They consume the bioluminescent fungi, which makes their own bodies glow faintly for days afterward, their veins pulsing with borrowed light.

At the center of the pump station, where the main intake valves converge, the oldest of the Siphoners has taken root. Its feet have merged with the floor, spreading into a network of flesh that interfaces directly with the building's plumbing. Its arms have elongated and branched, reaching up into the canopy where its fingers have become indistinguishable from twigs. It no longer moves but exists as a pure conduit between the mechanical and biological systems, processing information in both directions, maintaining the balance that keeps the grove alive.

The younger Siphoners tend to this elder with something like reverence, bringing it choice drops of resin, clearing the moss from its features, ensuring its breathing holes remain clear. In return, it shares the deep knowledge of the pump station through chemical secretions that the others absorb through their skin—memories of water tables and pressure differentials encoded in pheromones, wisdom about the seventeen-hour cycle transmitted through carefully timed spore releases.

This is what lives in the pump station where the machinery has been drinking for decades: creatures that have become the interface, the living transition between what was built and what grows, maintaining a balance that neither nature nor industry could achieve alone.
