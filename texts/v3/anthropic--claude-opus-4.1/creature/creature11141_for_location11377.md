# The Inhabitants

The **Threadkeepers** move through the scaffolds with a purpose older than memory. Their bodies suggest moth and mantis and something unfinished—six primary limbs that each terminate in clusters of smaller appendages, like hands made of hands. Their flesh has the texture of wet paper, gray-white and translucent enough that you can see the slow pulse of fluids through their tissues. They have no true skeleton; instead, their bodies maintain shape through hydraulic pressure, swelling and contracting as needed.

Their heads are mostly mouth—a complex arrangement of grinding plates and secretion glands that produce the various substances needed to maintain the great ropes. When they work, they embrace the vines with all their limbs, their belly-segments splitting open to reveal specialized organs that read the chemical memory of the knots. They taste the rope's history, adjusting their secretions accordingly—this enzyme to prevent fraying, that hormone to encourage controlled growth, another to suppress the vine's desire to flower and die.

They live in colonies of twelve to thirty, each colony maintaining a specific section of scaffold according to contracts written in pheromone and passed down through generations. The young are born knowing their territory's needs. They pupate inside knot-chambers, their bodies taking shape around the specific problems they will spend their lives solving.

Between the basket-gardens drift the **Sievewings**—creatures built like living filters. Their wings are not for flying but for straining. Four translucent membranes, each the size of a small building, stretch from a central body no larger than a person. The membranes are riddled with pores that adjust their size according to what needs catching—moisture, pollen, the chemical messages that serve as the scaffold's only communication system.

The Sievewings are hollow. Their bodies are essentially tubes with wings, processing what they filter in specialized chambers. They feed on aerial plankton—the microscopic life that blooms in the humid spaces—but their true purpose is messenger. They absorb the chemical signals released by one basket-garden, process them through organs that can store meaning for weeks, then release them at their destination. A single Sievewing might carry news of structural weakness, warnings of storm formation, or the complex negotiations between gardens over water rights.

Their young are born as parasites on the Threadkeepers, tiny winged mites that feed on excess secretions. Only when they've absorbed enough chemical knowledge do they metamorphose into their filter forms, their wings unfolding like wet flowers in the warm air.

In the basket-gardens themselves dwell the **Cultivators**—the descendants, perhaps, of the original builders, though changed beyond recognition. They walk upright on legs that bend in too many places, their bodies covered in what might be fur or might be mobile moss—a symbiotic covering that processes waste and provides camouflage. Their faces are dominated by compound eyes adapted to the eternal green twilight, and their hands have evolved secondary thumbs that oppose from both sides, perfect for the endless weaving their existence requires.

The Cultivators speak in bioelectric pulses transmitted through the moisture in the air—sparks of meaning that jump from water droplet to water droplet. Their language is partially encoded in the gardens themselves; sentences begun by one generation might be completed by their great-grandchildren, the grammar growing through the living walls.

They practice a form of agriculture indistinguishable from architecture. Each family group tends specific structural plants, training them through generations to serve architectural needs. A grandmother might begin bending a support beam that her granddaughter will finally anchor. They carry bronze tools passed down through bloodlines—tools that have been used so long they've worn grooves that fit specific hands, tools that must be fed regular drops of blood to prevent them from sprouting.

The Cultivators dream collectively through the root networks that form their floors. At night, the bioelectric activity of their sleeping minds flows through the moisture into the plants, and the gardens themselves experience something like consciousness—a slow, vegetative awareness that helps coordinate growth and respond to structural stress.

In the pooling waters of the basket-lakes swim the **Condensation Fish**—creatures of impossible delicacy. Their bodies are barely more solid than the water they inhabit, held together by surface tension and minute electrical charges. They have no proper bones, only cartilaginous suggestions of structure that bend and flow with their movement. Their skin is permeable; they feed by allowing nutrients to drift through their bodies, filtering what they need.

These fish navigate by reading the chemical gradients in the water—each basket-lake has its own signature, determined by the plants that surround it and the creatures that drink from it. The fish remember every chemical landscape they've encountered, building mental maps of the scaffold's water system that span territories no single solid creature could traverse.

When the rains come heavy and the basket-lakes overflow, the fish ride the waterfalls down the vine-ropes, their bodies stretching and compressing to survive the journey. They can travel this way between levels, carrying with them genetic material and chemical messages from distant gardens, facilitating a slow exchange of information that helps the scaffolds maintain their impossible coherence.

Higher still, in the cold zones where ice forms on the ropes at night, live the **Frost Shepherds**. These are colonial organisms—what appears to be a single creature is actually hundreds of smaller beings clinging together for warmth and coordination. They look like mobile clouds of dark fur interrupted by crystalline patches where their collective body heat has formed ice armor from the condensation.

The Frost Shepherds tend to the high pools where the purest water collects. They've developed a complex chemistry that allows them to survive partial freezing—sections of the colony can ice over while others maintain enough warmth to keep the whole alive. They sing in ultrasonic frequencies that shatter excess ice formation on the structural vines, preventing the weight from breaking critical connections.

Their relationship with the Threadkeepers is mysterious but essential. Once per season, delegations of Frost Shepherds descend to the warmer levels, their bodies steaming in the humid air. They meet with Threadkeeper colonies at predetermined knots, exchanging substances—the Threadkeepers providing enzymes that help process the high altitude algae, the Frost Shepherds offering crystalline secretions that the Threadkeepers incorporate into their preservation fluids.

Throughout all levels move the **Memory Wasps**—thumbnail-sized insects with bodies of living amber. They build their nests in the bronze tools, laying eggs in the patina. Their young feed on metal oxides and emerge with shells that carry the chemical signature of the tools' history. An experienced Cultivator can read a Memory Wasp's shell and know which tool it emerged from, when that tool was last used, what it was used for.

The wasps serve as living records. They're attracted to significant events—a major retying of knots, the weaving of a new chamber, the death of an ancient Threadkeeper. They absorb chemical traces of these moments into their shells, which darken with age and memory. The oldest wasps are nearly black, their bodies heavy with accumulated history. When they die, Cultivators collect them, dissolving their shells in special solutions that release the memories as hallucinogenic vapors—the only way to access the deep history of the scaffolds.

There are things that live in the empty spaces between gardens that no one properly sees—shadows that might be creatures or might be tricks of the light filtering through moisture. The Cultivators call them the Forgotten Workers and leave offerings at the edges of their gardens—tools weighted with stones to prevent them drifting away, vessels of water that somehow empty even in the still air. Sometimes knots appear in places no one could reach, tied in patterns that solve structural problems no living Threadkeeper could have diagnosed.

The youngest Cultivators sometimes report seeing figures in the mist that look almost like the statues their ancestors carved—bipedal forms with the right number of joints, hands that could hold proper tools. But the moisture distorts everything here, and the young are known for their overactive imaginations. Still, sometimes a bronze tool will disappear from where it hung and reappear seasons later, its blade freshly sharpened, its handle rewrapped with gut-string no one recognizes.

Life here is recursive, each species containing or tending something that contains or tends it in return. The Threadkeepers maintain ropes that maintain gardens that maintain Cultivators who maintain the conditions that allow Threadkeepers to survive. Even the parasites are essential—mites that clean the Sievewings' pores, fungi that process the Frost Shepherds' waste into nutrients the high-altitude plants require, bacteria in the condensation fish that help purify the water they swim through.

The scaffold's inhabitants have evolved past the concept of individual survival. Each creature is a component in a living machine too complex for any single intelligence to grasp. They exist in states of mutual dependency so absolute that removing any species would cause cascading collapses throughout the system. Perhaps this is what the builders intended—not to create a structure that would last forever, but to become part of a structure that would outlast the very concept of builders and built.

In the depths of night, when the condensation is heaviest and the bioluminescent algae in the pools glow their brightest, you can sometimes hear the scaffolds singing—a harmony made from the ultrasonic calls of the Frost Shepherds, the bioelectric crackling of Cultivator conversation, the subsonic rumbles of vine-ropes adjusting to weight, the whisper of Sievewing membranes filtering the thick air. It's not music, exactly, but it's not not music either. It's the sound of a living system so complex it has developed something like consciousness, something like purpose, something like dream.
