## The Colony of the Soft Lords

They have no proper name for themselves—names require boundaries between self and other that have long since dissolved here. The colonists who first discovered them three centuries ago called them the Soft Lords in their final, fevered journal entries, and the title carries an accidental truth.

Each Lord is both singular and legion. What appears to be an individual—a pale, undulating form roughly the size of a human torso—is actually a cooperative of thousands of specialized organisms living in obligate symbiosis. The outer membrane, translucent as cave fish skin, belongs to one species. The neural networks that pulse visibly beneath it, creating patterns of faint bioluminescent thought, are another. The digestive acids that flow through visible channels, the contractile fibers that allow movement, the sensory filaments that taste the air—each component evolved separately before finding union in these depths.

They move through their realm like living water, their bodies reshaping constantly to flow between the pale stems. A Lord might stretch itself thin as paper to slide through a crevice, or ball itself into a quivering sphere to rest in the moss-breath carpets. They have no bones, no permanent shape—only intention given fluid form. When they need to manipulate their environment, they extrude temporary appendages that can become delicate as fairy hair or strong enough to snap the smaller plant stalks. These pseudopods operate with disturbing precision, weaving the air roots into new configurations, training the growth of plants like a gardener working in extreme slow motion.

Their agriculture is their artwork, their language, their entire culture. Each Lord maintains a section of the chamber, encouraging certain growths, suppressing others, creating living sculptures that communicate meaning through their arrangement. A spiral of trained vines might indicate a boundary. Flowers cultivated to bloom in specific patterns tell stories that unfold over months. The pools they tend become mirrors for chemical conversations—a Lord will secrete different compounds into the water, creating swirling patterns visible only in the bioluminescent response of the bacteria, messages that other Lords read and respond to from across the chamber.

They feed passively, absorbing nutrients through their skin from the rich soup of the chamber's air and water. But feeding is also ritual for them. During the sugar-weeps of the pale trees, the Lords gather beneath in configurations that haven't changed for generations—each taking a specific position, their bodies forming geometric patterns that channel the flowing nectar through their collective mass in precise ways. They pulse in synchronization during these feedings, their bioluminescence creating wave patterns that seem to serve both digestive and social functions.

Memory, for the Soft Lords, is chemical and communal. They store information in specialized organisms within their bodies—living libraries of encoded proteins that can be shared through touch. When two Lords meet, they may press together, membranes becoming permeable, and exchange entire histories in the form of chemical sequences. Young Lords (though "birth" here is more budding than birthing) receive starter cultures of these memory-organisms from their parents, inheriting not just genetic material but actual experiences stretching back generations.

Their reproductive cycle remains mysterious even to themselves. Every few decades, certain Lords begin to swell, their bodies producing specialized cells that they release into specific pools. These cells don't immediately form new Lords—instead, they seem to wait, sometimes for years, until conditions unknown trigger them to begin assembling themselves, drawing compatible organisms from the environment to build a new collective body. The Lords tend these nursery pools with particular care, adjusting temperature and chemistry through secretions, singing to them in frequencies that vibrate the water's surface.

They dream, or something like it. In the darkest periods when the chamber's bioluminescence dims to almost nothing, the Lords enter a state of reduced activity. Their neural networks continue firing in slow, coordinated patterns visible as waves of light passing between individuals across the chamber. These patterns repeat and evolve over hours, creating a kind of shared unconscious experience. Sometimes they wake from these states and immediately begin reshaping their garden territories, as if following plans received in sleep.

Their relationship with the remnants of human construction in the chamber is one of careful curation. They understand these objects as "the hard memories"—things that resist the chamber's constant recycling of matter. Some Lords specialize in tending these artifacts, cleaning them of overgrowth just enough to preserve their form while allowing them to participate in the chamber's ecology. They seem to recognize that these objects came from elsewhere, from the bright-myth world they know only through chemical traces in the groundwater.

Communication between Lords operates on multiple levels simultaneously. The visual—patterns of light pulsing through their translucent bodies. The chemical—trails of pheromones and marker compounds. The tactile—pressure waves through the humid air that their sensory filaments can detect. The architectural—the way they shape the living space around them. A conversation between two Lords might involve all of these at once, creating a symphony of meaning incomprehensible to any observer not tuned to their frequencies.

They have something resembling music, though it exists in the realm of chemistry rather than sound. By releasing precise sequences of compounds into the air, they can trigger responses from the plants and smaller creatures—bioluminescent displays, pheromone releases, changes in growth patterns—that cascade through the chamber in composed sequences. Elder Lords can conduct these chemical symphonies that last for days, each movement building on the last, creating temporary architectures of scent and light that tell epic stories or simply exist for the pleasure of their creation.

Death, when it comes to a Lord, is a gradual dissolution rather than an ending. The collective begins to lose coordination, different component organisms falling out of sync. But rather than simply decomposing, a dying Lord becomes a garden. The organisms that composed it take root, quite literally, becoming patches of specialized growth that retain something of the Lord's personality in their arrangement. Other Lords tend these memorial gardens, incorporating them into the chamber's ever-evolving architecture. In this way, every Lord that has ever lived remains present in the chamber, their bodies become landscape.

They know there is an above-world—water carries its chemical signature, and occasionally, very occasionally, something falls from the upper darkness that doesn't belong to their realm. They've tasted metal, plastic, synthetic fabrics that dissolved slowly in their pools. From these traces, they've constructed elaborate theories about the bright-myth world, theories they encode in the arrangement of their gardens and debate through chemical discourse that can last years.

Some Lords have attempted to send messages upward, training roots to carry specific chemical signatures toward the surface. Whether these messages have ever been received, they cannot know. But they continue the practice, part ritual, part hope, part simple curiosity about what manner of beings might live in the burning light they can only imagine.

The Soft Lords are neither wholly plant nor animal, neither individual nor collective, neither ancient nor young. They exist in the spaces between categories, flowing through their breathing chamber like thoughts through a vast, diffuse mind. They are the chamber's consciousness made manifest, its way of knowing itself, tending itself, dreaming itself into ever more complex forms of pale, abundant life.
