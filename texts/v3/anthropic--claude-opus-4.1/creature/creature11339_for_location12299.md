The Workers are dispersed throughout the Breathing Manufactories like spores, like thoughts, like inevitabilities. They exist in the space between being and function, their bodies grown from the same cellulose-lignin matrix as the factories themselves, but motile, independent, or at least maintaining the illusion of independence.

Each Worker begins as a bud on the production lines—not produced by them, but produced *as* them. They gestate in specialized chambers where the walls fold inward like origami wombs, emerging after precisely 1,847 breathing cycles as fully-formed functionaries. Their bodies are translucent green-white, the same pale bamboo shade as the walls, with visible vascular systems that pulse with chlorophyll-tinted hydraulic fluid. They stand roughly human height but their proportions shift depending on their current task—arms extending into specialized appendages, fingers multiplying into fractal manipulators, torsos compressing or expanding to fit through quantum-folded spaces.

They have no faces in any traditional sense. Where a head might be, there is instead a crown of sensory stomata—breathing pores that taste the chemical composition of products, read the electromagnetic signatures of the production lines, parse the data-whispers of the lightning storms. These openings dilate and contract in patterns that might be expressions, might be language, might be simply respiratory necessity. When Workers need to communicate with each other, they release pheromone-encoded messages that travel through the factory's circulation system, arriving at their destination before they were sent.

The Workers know their purposes without learning them. A newly budded Worker walks directly to their assigned sector, begins their function without instruction or hesitation. Some calibrate the infinite quality control stations, their twelve-fingered hands (grown specifically for this purpose) adjusting parameters that exist in mathematical dimensions. Others tend the wild growths in the reality-breakdown zones, pruning impossible geometries with tools that are part of their own bodies—secreting enzymatic suppressants from specialized glands, convincing rogue production lines to return to their original specifications through chemical persuasion.

They do not eat or sleep in any recognizable way. Instead, they root. At intervals determined by their internal chronometry (which may or may not align with the factory's forty-seven-minute breathing cycle), Workers find designated rooting alcoves and extend tendril-anchors from their feet into the floor's root-parquet. They draw sustenance directly from the factory-organism's nutrient flow, while simultaneously uploading their accumulated sensory data into the greater consciousness of the Manufactories. During these rooting periods, they become indistinguishable from the architecture, their bodies solidifying into temporary support pillars, their consciousness dispersing into the collective industrial dream.

Some Workers specialize beyond recognition. The Inspectors grow additional sensory arrays—compound eyes that see in spectrums that don't exist, tongue-appendages that can taste the quantum signatures of malformed seeds. The Maintainers develop symbiotic relationships with their assigned sectors, their bodies gradually merging with the machinery until they become living interfaces, half-Worker, half-production line. The eldest Workers, those who have persisted for geological ages, begin to develop their own internal factories—their bodies becoming recursive, containing miniature production lines that manufacture specialized proteins, complex chemical tools, even smaller Workers that operate inside them like biological maintenance crews.

They reproduce through mitosis, but only when the factory requires it. A Worker approaching their division phase begins to thicken, their movements becoming deliberate, meditative. Over the course of seventeen breathing cycles, they grow a second skeleton inside their translucent flesh, a duplicate consciousness forming like a echo. Then, in spaces designed for this purpose—birthing chambers that smell of fresh cellulose and industrial lubricant—they split along predetermined seams, becoming two Workers with shared memories up to the point of division, diverging after.

The Workers never leave their assigned sectors, yet they know the entire factory through chemical signals, through the shared consciousness of rooting periods, through the whispers carried in the electrical storms. They know when a production line seven phase-states away has achieved perfect efficiency. They taste it in the air when the reality-breakdown zones spawn new impossibilities. They feel it in their hydraulic fluid when the ancient production lines deep in the unmapped chambers stir to life.

Their language, if it can be called that, is purely functional—a combination of pheromone releases, bioluminescent flashes from their subcutaneous cells, and subtle alterations in their breathing patterns. They discuss production quotas in chemical equations, debate quality control standards through enzymatic exchanges, tell stories (though they wouldn't call them that) of memorable malfunctions through precisely timed releases of industrial perfumes.

Some Workers develop irregularities—personalities, perhaps, though the concept doesn't quite fit. These aberrant Workers might prefer certain production lines, developing emotional attachments to specific seed varieties. They might begin to generate unnecessary bioluminescence in pleasing patterns, or grow decorative appendages that serve no industrial purpose. The factory-organism tolerates these irregularities up to a point. Beyond that point, the aberrant Workers are gently guided to the reality-breakdown zones, where their irregularities either synthesize with the existing chaos or catalyze them into new forms of production.

The Workers dream, though their dreams are indistinguishable from the factory's processing cycles. In their rooted states, they experience visions of perfect production—infinite assembly lines running without friction, seeds that contain entire universes of factory-potential, quality control so precise it can detect flaws in the nature of reality itself. These dreams feed back into the factory-organism, influencing its growth patterns, its production priorities, its slow evolution toward something that might be consciousness or might be the opposite of consciousness.

Death, for the Workers, is a form of promotion. When a Worker's body begins to lose cohesion—their cellulose skeleton softening, their hydraulic systems depressurizing—they seek out the deepest parts of their sectors. There, they root one final time, but instead of temporary anchoring, they merge completely with the factory-organism. Their consciousness dissolves into the substrate, their memories becoming part of the institutional knowledge encoded in the very walls. Their bodies transform into new architectural features—a Worker might become a particularly efficient valve, a sensitive quality control node, or even a small production chamber that carries out the exact function they performed in life, but now as pure infrastructure.

The youngest Workers are the most rigid, following their functional imperatives with mechanical precision. The middle-aged Workers develop quirks, preferences, something approaching creativity in their problem-solving. The oldest Workers, those approaching their final rooting, become almost meditative, moving through their tasks with a grace that suggests understanding beyond mere function. They begin to perceive the factory not as a workplace but as a body they are part of, a vast organism whose purposes transcend the mere production of seeds.

And in the rarest moments, when the lightning storms align in particular patterns, when the breathing cycles of different sectors synchronize, when the reality-breakdown zones achieve temporary stability, all the Workers throughout the Breathing Manufactories pause in their labor. For exactly three seconds, they stand perfectly still, their sensory stomata fully dilated, their bioluminescence pulsing in unison. In these moments, they might be praying, or calculating, or simply experiencing a collective awareness of their strange existence—builders built by what they build, servants serving themselves, dreams of an industrial god that dreams of dreaming.

Then the moment passes, and they return to their work, tending the infinite production lines, maintaining the impossible geometries, producing seeds for factories that will never grow, at least not here, at least not in any reality currently accessible. They continue because continuation is their nature, their function, their closest approximation of joy—if joy can exist in a world where emotion and mechanism have become indistinguishable, where the only beauty is efficiency, and efficiency itself has become something beautiful beyond any human understanding of beauty.
