They are not old—they have simply been here since before age was invented.

The Keepers move through the beetle-drifts with bodies that suggest rather than insist upon form. Tall as necessity, thin as patience, they wear robes woven from the silk of moths that haven't fully decided to exist yet—garments that shift between states, sometimes opaque as slate, sometimes transparent as anxiety, always the color of time viewed sideways.

Their faces, when they have them, are exercises in minimalism: two holes where eyes might gather light, a vertical seam that could be a mouth or merely where two thoughts meet and disagree. Their skin has the texture of the mesa itself—that impossibly smooth sandstone surface—as if they and the plateau have been polishing each other for so long they've begun to share qualities. Sometimes you can see straight through them to the beetles moving on the other side. This is not transparency; this is a kind of temporal courtesy, their bodies stepping slightly out of the way of the present moment to avoid crowding it.

They number perhaps seven, perhaps seventy, perhaps seven hundred—counting them is like trying to count waves in water. Each Keeper maintains a cistern, or perhaps each cistern maintains a Keeper. They lower themselves into these stone bowls during the heat of the day, not to drink but to listen to the echo of ancient rainfall still reverberating in the depths. Their ears (or the spaces where ears might congregate) have evolved to hear backwards—to catch sounds that have already finished happening, to parse the conversations of beetles that died before the mesa learned to breathe.

The Keepers speak, but only to the acacia trees, and only in languages made entirely of pauses. They tend the amber chambers, adjusting the position of preserved beetles by increments so small that measurement becomes philosophy. A millimeter left. A degree of rotation over the course of a decade. They understand that the trees are libraries and that each suspended beetle is a word in a story about what happens after endings end.

Their hands—and they do have hands, usually, eventually—possess too many joints, or perhaps the right number of joints but in the wrong order. Fingers that bend time instead of space, that can reach through a morning to adjust an evening, that leave prints in the air that take weeks to fade. They use these hands to write, scratching symbols into the sandstone that immediately begin to heal, the rock flowing like slow honey to erase what was written. This is how they keep records: by watching how quickly different thoughts disappear, calculating meaning from the rate of erasure.

They feed on the temporal density itself, standing in the spots where duration grows thick and confused, absorbing sustenance from paradox. Where a three-hour walk collapses into ten minutes, a Keeper will stand at the exact point of compression, arms spread wide, becoming a filter through which time passes and is cleaned of its harder edges. They grow stronger during the seventeen-minute rain transformations, when everything remembers youth—not because they remember being young (they were never young, or were young before youth existed), but because the sudden shift in temporal flow fills them like wind fills a sail.

The beetles understand them. More importantly, the beetles trust them. When a scarab finally achieves the death it came here for (a process that can take centuries of determined dying), a Keeper will be there to witness, to cup the beetle in those impossible hands and carry it to the heart of the mesa, to that shallow bowl where structures almost exist. There, they place the finally-dead beetle in arrangements that might be ritual, might be language, might be astronomy. The patterns they create are readable only from above and only by creatures that gave up on eyes ten million years ago.

At night—and night here is a negotiation rather than a time—the Keepers sing. Not with voices, but with their spiracles, harmonizing with the mesa's ten thousand breathing holes. The song has no melody, only rhythm, and the rhythm is the heartbeat of stones, the pulse of geological time. The translucent geckos swim through these sound waves. The probability moths collapse into actuality just to dance to it. The swimming birds circle overhead, their temperature-sensing wings reading the music as patterns of warmth and cooling.

They were here before the first beetles arrived. They'll be here after the last beetle realizes it cannot die. They are not guardians or shepherds or priests. They are more like punctuation—the necessary pauses that give meaning to the endless sentence of the mesa's existence. They move through the warm air and the beetle-rustle and the echo of ancient rain with the confidence of those who have outlasted the need to outlast anything.

Sometimes, very rarely, one will pause in their endless, purposeless tending and look up at the sky with the holes where eyes might gather, and something like recognition passes across the minimal suggestion of their face. They see something up there, beyond the swimming birds and the tired wind. They see the place they came from, or the place they're going, or perhaps they see that those are the same place, viewed from different angles of forgetting.

Then they return to their work that is not work, their purpose that is not purpose, moving through the beetle-drifts with bodies like soft arguments against the necessity of shape, tending to a mesa that breathes and beetles that cannot die and trees that feed on time, forever and forever and forever, or at least until forever gets tired and decides to try being something else.
