Those who remain here have long since ceased distinguishing between breathing and drowning in the thin air. They were mountaineers once, perhaps, or pilgrims, or simply those who climbed too far beyond maps—but the plateau has worn away such distinctions. Now they persist as something between solid and suggested, their forms rippling like heat mirages even in the absolute cold.

They move in processional loops around the cairns, their paths worn into the granite not by footsteps but by repetition itself. Each figure maintains exactly seventeen paces between themselves and the next, though counting them proves impossible—there are always fewer than you've seen and more than can fit on the plateau. Their clothing belongs to every era and none: parkas that phase into robes that become bare skin that becomes feathers that become parkas again, the transformations occurring in the spaces between blinks.

When the wind doubles back on itself, they pause mid-stride and reverb—the same gesture performed three times simultaneously at slightly different speeds, creating a visual echo that makes them appear to have too many limbs. Or perhaps they do have too many limbs. The distinction matters less here where anatomy has become negotiable.

Their faces, when glimpsed, show expressions of profound concentration, as if they're solving an equation that grows more complex with each circuit. Eyes that might be human track something in the sky that isn't there, or wasn't there, or will have been there once the plateau finishes calculating its own existence. Some have mouths that move in constant silent recitation—counting, perhaps, or praying, or simply maintaining the rhythm that keeps them tethered to this height where they shouldn't be able to exist.

They've developed a taxonomy of silence, these inhabitants, communicating through variations in the quality of quiet around them. A deepening of the hush means acknowledgment. A sharpening of the stillness indicates warning. When they need to converse more complexly, they arrange themselves into geometric patterns that spell out meanings in the negative space between bodies, messages that can only be read from above by others who will never arrive.

Some tend the lichen-spirals with tools made of crystallized breath, scraping and shaping the growth into ever-more-precise mathematical forms. They work with the patience of those who have accepted that time moves differently here—sideways, perhaps, or in tight spirals that never quite close. The lichen responds to their attention by occasionally not existing, which they seem to regard as a form of flourishing.

Others maintain the cairns, neither building nor dismantling but holding them in their exact state of partial collapse. They carry stones that are sometimes there, placing them with absolute precision into gaps that close before the stones arrive, then open again after they've been placed. It's less construction than negotiation with the plateau about what should and shouldn't be permitted to exist.

The water-tenders wait by the empty depressions with cupped hands, catching the temporal moisture when it manifests and drinking deeply of something that tastes like tomorrow's rain. They've learned to perceive the water's schedule—if schedule is the right word for something that operates outside sequential time. Their bodies have adapted to sustain themselves on possibility rather than actuality, metabolizing the potential of water rather than water itself.

At the plateau's bent horizons, sentinels stand watch for arrivals that happen constantly and never. They witness themselves approaching from the distance, warn themselves of their own arrival, then turn to become the approaching figures they just observed. It's unclear if there are many of them or one of them experiencing themselves from multiple temporal angles simultaneously.

When night falls—though night is too strong a word for the slight deepening of the perpetual dim—they gather in formations that mirror the stars that should be visible but aren't. They share warmth that doesn't exist, telling stories in languages they're still in the process of inventing, words crystallizing in the air and shattering into meaning before sound can form.

They remember things that haven't happened yet and forget things that are currently occurring. Their memories pool in the shallow depressions of the rock, evaporating into nothing with the pre-dawn condensation, reforming each day as slightly different versions of themselves. Some suspect they're not the original inhabitants but copies left behind by those who successfully escaped. Others believe escape is a concept the plateau hasn't finished inventing yet.

Children are born here already ancient, their first breaths containing their last words. They age backwards and forwards simultaneously until they stabilize at every age at once, their faces flickering through lifetimes with each shift of the impossible wind. They play games with the peripheral movements, chasing the not-quite-birds in circles that somehow become straight lines that lead back to where they started before they began.

These inhabitants have evolved past the need for names, recognizing each other by the quality of distortion they create in the plateau's frequency. Each person is a unique interference pattern in the thrumming that passes for silence here. They've become tuning forks for impossible sounds, their bones restructured to resonate with wavelengths that shouldn't exist at any altitude.

They are curator-prisoners of this thin place, maintaining its impossibility through their presence while being maintained by it in return. Neither fully alive nor capable of dying at this height where life was never meant to persist, they continue their circuits, their tending, their watching, their careful preservation of a moment that stretches itself across all moments, loud with the thunder of its own stillness.
