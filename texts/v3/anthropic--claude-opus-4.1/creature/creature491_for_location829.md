The Resonants make their homes in the tremor-spaces—those fleeting geometries of stability that exist between one shake and the next. They are not quite human anymore, though their ancestors were. Generations of living with the plateau's violence have worn them into something else: elongated beings with too many joints in their limbs, skin that shifts between states of solidity, bones that bend rather than break.

They move in small family groups of three to seven, never more—too many bodies in one place invites the plateau's attention. Each group maintains not a territory but a circuit, a memorized pattern of temporary refuges that they traverse in cycles determined by vibration patterns only they can read. Their feet, splayed and sensitive, feel tomorrow's rockfall in today's tremors. Their children learn to walk by learning to fall, to shift their weight with the stone's buckling, to ride collapse like a wave.

Their shelters are mathematical poetry—tensioned skins and flexible poles that distribute the plateau's force rather than resist it. These structures breathe with the landscape, collapsing and re-expanding in rhythm with the daily violence. Nothing is ever fully unpacked. Their possessions are limited to what can be bundled and carried in the space of a heartbeat, when the stone beneath decides to become somewhere else. They weave memory-cloths from the fiber of those aggressive vines, each pattern recording the safe routes of a particular season, the knowledge literally worn on their backs.

The Resonants do not speak—the plateau's roar drowns out voice. Instead, they communicate through touch-vibrations, pressing coded rhythms into each other's skin. Their language is tactile music, conversations that happen palm to palm, forehead to forehead. Complex ideas require full-body contact, multiple participants creating symphonies of meaning through synchronized pressure. Their poetry is danced in groups, stories told through coordinated trembling that mimics and mocks the ground's own violence.

They feed on the chaos itself. In the moments after a rockfall, certain fungi bloom in the fresh fractures—purple-black things that exist for minutes before dissolving. The Resonants are always in motion toward the next collapse, timing their arrivals to harvest these ephemeral fruits. They know which vines fruit only when their roots are severed by shifting stone, which pools appear for exactly long enough to fill their treated-leather vessels. Their diet is a careful choreography with disaster.

The children are strange even by Resonant standards. Born with fontanelles that never fully close, their skulls remain partially soft throughout life—an adaptation that prevents the concussions that would otherwise come from constant falls. They age rapidly to adolescence, then seem to pause, their bodies refusing to fully mature until they've survived a certain number of plateau-cycles. Some never trigger this second growth, remaining child-sized and child-flexible into their fortieth year, valued for their ability to navigate spaces that shift too quickly for larger bodies.

They mark time not by sun or moon but by the rhythm of particular rocks—that spire that falls and rises on a seven-day cycle, the cliff that shears away a precise layer every season. Their calendar is geological percussion, their holidays synchronized to the plateau's most dramatic transformations. During the annual Great Settlement—a three-day period when the entire plateau convulses and reconstitutes itself—they gather (as much as they ever gather) in the stable eyes of the chaos, islands of relative calm in the storm of change. Here they exchange genetic material as much as information, children moving between groups, bloodlines deliberately tangled to maintain the flexibility—biological and social—that survival demands.

The Resonants worship nothing, but they maintain a profound relationship with the remains of their predecessors—not the ancient builders whose ruins dot the landscape, but their own dead. Bodies are placed in the zones of maximum transformation, where the plateau's violence will distribute them quickly. Within days, bones are ground to powder, flesh taken by the aggressive plants, blood absorbed into the ever-thirsty stone. They believe this dispersal is the only true permanence—to become part of the plateau's material, recycled endlessly through stone and stem and soil.

Their technology is biological and temporal rather than mechanical. They cultivate the fast-growing plants, not to harvest but to direct—planting seeds in cracks they know will widen, using root pressure to split rocks along predicted paths. They maintain no tools that can't be regrown, no structures that can't be re-derived from first principles. Each adult carries, encoded in scars and calluses, the complete knowledge needed to rebuild their material culture from nothing. The plateau destroys everything eventually; they have learned to pre-destroy, to always be in the process of beginning again.

At night, when the phosphorescent spores bloom, they paint their skin with plant oils that react with the glow, becoming briefly visible to each other across the darkened landscape. These moments of luminous connection—a scattered constellation of human lights answering the biological stars—are their only claim to territory, and it lasts only until dawn burns the oils away.

The Resonants are not survivors—survival implies resistance, and they gave that up long ago. They are participants, dancers in the plateau's ancient rhythm. They have learned that the only way to live with perpetual catastrophe is to become catastrophic yourself—always falling, always catching, always breaking, always healing, never still, never safe, never anywhere but in motion.
