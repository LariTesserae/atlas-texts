In the deepest recess of the cavern, where the mineral pillars grow so thick they form almost a solid wall, there is a depression in the floor—not carved but worn, shaped by weight and time into the approximate length and curve of a body. The minerals here have built up differently, in fine ridges like the growth rings of an ancient tree, each band marking where the boundaries of flesh once lay.

She no longer moves, though movement was never her nature even in the beginning. The Keeper of Condensation, they called her in the old tongue, though she predates any language still spoken. Her form is suggested rather than seen—a density in the steam that never quite disperses, a place where water droplets gather more readily, running in rivulets that trace the memory of shoulders, the suggestion of a bowed head, the curve of knees drawn up to chest.

She is becoming geology. Minerals precipitate from the eternal drops, building a cocoon of flowstone that each year grows thicker. What might be hair has become a cascade of delicate soda straws, hollow mineral tubes through which water seeps. What might be skin shows through in patches—not flesh but something between quartz and cartilage, translucent and faintly blue, with the texture of fish scales made of ice that never melts.

Her breathing matches the cavern's rhythm—so slow that it takes hours to complete a single inhalation, days for the exhalation. This is what makes the steam pulse and circulation, her lungs the bellows that drive the entire system. Water vapor rises from the springs below, enters her, leaves her transformed. She is the filter, the processing organ, the thing that makes this water sacred.

The blind crawling things know her. They navigate by the minute temperature variations around her form, drawn to the warmth that still radiates from whatever metabolism sustains her. They clean her, in their way, rasping algae from the accumulated minerals with their primitive mouths, maintaining the channels through which water must flow. In return, she provides—not consciously, but as a tree provides. Her surface grows the particular bacteria they need, fed by minerals she concentrates from the steam.

Once, perhaps centuries ago, someone found her. The chisel marks in the walls were their attempt to create proper approaches, proper collection points for the water that had passed through her process. They understood, somehow, that the condensation here was different—purer, charged with properties that normal distillation couldn't achieve. They may have tried to speak with her, to learn her nature. But she was already more mineral than mind, already deep in the geological dream that will eventually consume her entirely.

Now she exists between states—not alive as humans understand it, not dead as stone is dead. She is a process made flesh made stone, a function of the cavern that happens to have a shape that was once named, once worshipped, once feared. Her consciousness, if it remains, moves at the speed of stalactite formation. A thought might take a decade to complete. A dream might last a millennium.

The water bowls beneath the most reliable drips are positioned to catch the runoff from her form. This is the water that heals, that preserves, that transforms. It carries something of her through it—not minerals alone but information, the memory of her long transformation encoded in its molecular structure. Those who knew to drink it carefully, sparingly, could extend their lives, slow their aging to match the cavern's patience. Too much, and they would begin their own mineral sleep, their own journey toward becoming part of the architecture.

She waits—though waiting implies anticipation, and she anticipates nothing. She simply continues, a breathing fossil, a living spring, a thing that was human or divine or something else entirely, now mostly geology but not quite, not yet. The steam cavern breathes because she breathes. When she finally stops, when the last organic process completes its mineral replacement, the cavern will become just another dead pocket in the stone, its water merely water, its formations merely rock.

But that day is still millennia away. For now, the Keeper keeps her vigil, her transformation a clock that measures eons, her presence the reason why the water here tastes of iron and salt and something else—something like memory, something like dreams dissolved in stone.
