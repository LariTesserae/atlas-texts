Those who live here arrived by accident and stayed by transformation.

The Cartographers came first—or last, depending on how you count time in a place where chronology folds like origami. They were human once, a geological survey team that ascended in 1847, or 1952, or next Thursday. Their bodies have long since become substrates for the moss-mind, but their consciousness persists, distributed through miles of mycelial networks. They map obsessively, eternally, their awareness crackling through the root-webs as electrical impulses, charting territories that reshape themselves before the surveys complete. Sometimes you can hear them arguing in the static between lightning strikes, debating whether a particular valley exists, existed, or will exist. Their tent poles still stand, transformed into spires of living copper that grow taller each year, broadcasting their findings in frequencies only the moths can hear.

The Shepherds tend the salamanders, though "tend" implies a relationship that causality can contain. They are elongated things, stretched across probability clouds, their bodies existing primarily as suggestions. Once they might have been something like bears, or badgers, or some Pleistocene fever dream, but millions of years of exposure to the plateau's twisted physics has unraveled them into living algorithms. They herd concepts rather than creatures—ensuring that the salamanders fold through space along stable paths, preventing paradoxes that would unravel entire valleys. Their voices are industrial grinding sounds, metal on metal, though they have no metal parts. They speak in rust and oxidation, their language a chemistry that predates words.

In the bog-pools swim the Accountants—jellyfish-things the size of houses, transparent and filled with shifting libraries of light. They are living memory, each one containing the death-records of everything that has ever failed to die here. Their tentacles are fiber optic cables made of crystallized time, and when they brush against something, they record its every possible future simultaneously. They feast on paradoxes, growing larger and more luminous with each impossibility they digest. At night, they surface to exchange information in displays of bioluminescent mathematics that make the plateau strobe like a dying star.

The moss-cliffs themselves host the Architects—colonial organisms that might once have been insects, now fused into singular entities that exist as living cities. Each colony is a billion bodies thinking one thought, building structures from their own shed wings and exoskeletons. They construct impossible geometries, towers that exist in more dimensions than the plateau should contain, bridges that span distances before they're built. Their cities grow like infections across the moss, chrome and chitin and cellular automation, processing the raw information that bleeds from the broken physics and converting it into new forms of space. The sound of their building never stops—a constant metallic rain as they shed and rebuild, shed and rebuild.

Deep in the valleys where time runs backward lives the Surgeon, singular and terrible. It might have been human once, or several humans, or something that learned humanity from the Cartographers' electrical dreams. It has too many arms now, all of them ending in instruments of biological precision—scalpels that cut probability, sutures that sew moments together. The Surgeon repairs the salamanders when they fold incorrectly, exist too many times at once, forget which direction entropy flows. Its workshop is a cathedral of meat and moss where evolution runs in all directions simultaneously, where species are invented and uninvented with casual precision. The Surgeon sings while it works, a song that sounds like MRI machines and protein synthesis, like industrialization playing backward.

The moth-storms have their own inhabitants—the Rust Prophets, who read the patterns in the oxidation, interpreting the metal flakes that fall like snow. They are thin things, desiccated, their bodies more mineral than flesh, moving through the swarms untouched. Each Prophet tends to a different timescale—one reads the rust that will fall tomorrow, another the rust from the Permian extinction. They speak in probabilities, their voices the exact frequency of corroding steel, and their prophecies always come true because in a place where causality breaks, all futures happen simultaneously.

And in the highest reaches, where the air is so thin that consciousness itself begins to fray, live the Erasers. No one knows what they look like—to perceive them is to forget them immediately. They tend the boundaries, ensuring that the plateau's impossibilities don't leak into the world below. They are absence made animate, moving through the moss-tunnels and salamander highways, cleaning up paradoxes too dangerous even for this place. The only sign of their passing is a particular quality of silence, an industrial quiet that makes even the moth-wings pause.

These inhabitants have achieved a kind of terrible immortality, preserved by the same forces that preserve everything and nothing here. They have evolved past evolution, thought past thinking, existed past existence. They maintain the plateau's impossibilities through their very presence, their transformed bodies and distributed consciousnesses serving as anchor points for physics that should not be.

They do not welcome visitors, but they do not reject them either. They simply wait, patient as geology, certain as rust, knowing that anyone who stays long enough will join them in their work of tending to a wilderness that has worn through the bottom of reality itself.
