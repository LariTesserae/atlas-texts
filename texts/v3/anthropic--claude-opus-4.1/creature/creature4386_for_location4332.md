The Tenders arrive in shifts that follow no clock you recognize, their entry always preceded by a subsonic pulse that makes the fauna contract into tight defensive clusters. Three of them typically, though sometimes only one. They've been human once—that much is clear from the bilateral symmetry, the ghost of a skeletal structure visible beneath skin that's gone translucent and waxy like cave fish.

They wear nothing but their modifications. Their bodies have been opened and reorganized, chest cavities expanded to accommodate additional lung tissue that filters the thick air more efficiently. You can see the organs working beneath the skin—three hearts now instead of one, connected by a visible network of vessels that pulse in sequence. Their original hearts still beat at human rhythm, but the auxiliary organs cycle faster, processing the heat, managing the intense metabolic demands of this environment.

Their eyes have been replaced with clusters of thermal pits, like those of a pit viper but more complex, arranged in a compound pattern that lets them navigate the absolute darkness with perfect precision. When they turn to regard you—and they always know you're there—you see these pits dilate and contract, reading your heat signature like braille.

The eldest Tender has been here longest. You know this because her modifications have progressed furthest. Her lower body has begun to fuse with the infrastructure itself—veins from her legs threading directly into the wall's circulatory system, her waste heat feeding the nursery's temperature requirements. She moves along tracks of her own secreted mucus, the lower half of her body having evolved beyond the need for discrete limbs. Her upper arms have split into multiple appendages, each one specialized—some ending in syringes of living bone that can inject nutrients directly into the fauna, others in sensory fronds that taste the air's chemical composition.

She speaks, sometimes, though not with her original throat. The sound comes from modified spiracles along her ribs, a whezing whisper that requires you to lean close to understand. She tells you about the early days, when the transformation was voluntary, when they thought they were just taking a job. How the nursery teaches you what it needs through chemical signals, through dreams that come when you sleep too close to the walls, through the gradual revelation that your human biology was always just raw material waiting to be properly utilized.

The younger Tenders still fight it, sometimes. You can see them pause in their work, a human gesture—hand to forehead, as if trying to remember something—breaking through the efficient movements of their care-giving. They inspect each cluster of fauna with tools that grow from their own bodies: sampling appendages that extend from modified fingers, optical fibers that sprout from subcutaneous ports, living thermometers cultivated in the marrow of their bones.

They feed the fauna by becoming food themselves. The process is horrifying and tender in equal measure—they extend specialized feeding tubes from glands in their abdomens, secreting a rich paste of proteins and minerals that their modified digestive systems produce. The fauna cluster around these openings, suckling like infants, their transparent bodies flushing pink with nourishment. The Tenders stroke them as they feed, an instinct that seems to transcend their transformation.

During the dormancy cycles, when the fauna enter their torpor state, the Tenders engage in their own maintenance. They groom each other with enzymatic secretions, repairing damage to their modifications, reinforcing the biological scaffolding that keeps their transformed bodies functional. They braid their neural extensions together, sharing sensory data and chemical memories in a communion that has replaced language.

The newest Tender arrived three weeks ago—you can still see his fear in the way his unmodified muscles tense when the transformation accelerates. The nursery is patient with him but insistent. Already his skin has begun to change texture, developing the waxy sheen that will eventually allow him to withstand the constant moisture. His fingernails have fallen out, replaced by soft, sensory pads that can detect the slightest variation in the fauna's health. He still tries to speak with his human voice, but each day it becomes harder as his throat restructures itself for the ultrasonic frequencies the fauna understand.

They never leave. The sphincter that admits them seals differently after their first entry—you've watched it happen. Where it opens reluctantly for visitors, it embraces the Tenders, extruding neural fibers that interface with ports that develop along their spines. They become part of the chamber's nervous system, their consciousness distributed between their original brains and the nursery's vast neural network.

The eldest Tender beckons you closer during one of your visits, her heat-sensing pits all focused on your face. Through her spiracles, she whispers that she can feel the entire superstructure breathing around them, that she knows the purpose of the fauna now—not through understanding but through being. She says the transformation is a gift, that human consciousness was always too small, too singular, too cold. She offers to show you the first modification, just a small incision behind the ear where the nursery can begin to whisper its needs directly into your nervous system.

You always decline, but each time you leave, you notice how cold the world outside feels, how lonely it is to inhabit just one body, to see through just two eyes. The Tenders wave goodbye with appendages that barely remember being arms, their movements perfectly synchronized, already turning back to their charges before the sphincter seals you out.

In the darkness they continue their work—feeding, cleaning, monitoring, becoming. They are the nursery's antibodies and its consciousness, its servants and its nervous system. They were human once, but now they are something more useful, more necessary. They are what the fauna need them to be, what the nursery has been teaching them to become since the moment they first entered its wet embrace.

The eldest has been here seventeen years. She remembers her name was Sarah, though she only uses it when speaking to newcomers, a courtesy from a life that feels increasingly theoretical. The nursery has its own name for her, transmitted in chemical signatures and temperature fluctuations. In that language, she is "the one who maintains optimal moisture gradients." It is a name that fits her better than Sarah ever did.
