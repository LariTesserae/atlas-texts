The Tenders move through the vertical garden like deep-sea creatures navigating trenches. You hear them before you see them — the soft scrape of their modified boots against wet metal, the whisper of their breathing apparatus cycling filtered air. They've adapted to work in near-total darkness, their eyes grown large and light-sensitive from years in the shaft. When they must use illumination, it's red-spectrum only, dim enough that it barely pierces the moisture-heavy air.

They wear suits of their own design — part wetsuit, part mechanical exoskeleton. The material is living rubber, actually, cultivated from special fungi in the lower levels and trained to grow around their bodies. It breathes with them, expanding and contracting, wicking away the constant moisture while maintaining body heat. Copper tubing runs along the outside like external arteries, carrying coolant when they work near the steam vents, heated fluid when they ascend to the ice-prone upper reaches.

Their hands are grotesque with modification. Each finger extends into a different tool — one might terminate in a pH sensor that they dip into the nutrient streams, another in a microscopic camera for examining root systems, a third in a pruning blade that vibrates at ultrasonic frequencies. They've learned to move these hand-tools with uncanny precision in the darkness, guided by sound and the subtle changes in air pressure around the vegetation.

The Tenders rarely speak. When they must communicate, they use a clicking language adapted from the sounds of the machinery itself — mimicking the patterns of pumps and pistons to convey meaning. Three rapid clicks like a pressure valve releasing: danger. A grinding sound like the cable platform: ascend. They've been here so long that some have forgotten human speech entirely, their throats restructured to better produce these mechanical sounds.

They know every plant in the garden by touch, by smell, by the particular sound its leaves make when brushed. Their mental maps of the space are impossibly detailed — they can navigate to any single growing pod among thousands, moving through the darkness with absolute certainty. Some say they've developed new senses entirely, detecting the electromagnetic fields of the growth lights, feeling the chemical signatures plants release into the air.

In the few maintenance alcoves carved into the rock, they've made their homes. These spaces are cramped, humid chambers barely large enough to lie down in, the walls lined with the same growing medium as the plant beds. They sleep among the roots, letting them grow over and around their resting bodies. Some Tenders have been still so long that moss covers their suits, making them indistinguishable from the garden itself until they suddenly move.

They feed on what they grow, but not in any way you'd recognize as eating. They've developed a symbiotic relationship with certain climbing vines that tap directly into ports they've had surgically installed along their spines. The plants provide nutrients while the Tenders' blood, rich with carbon dioxide, feeds back into the root system. It's a closed loop, human and vegetation becoming increasingly difficult to separate.

The youngest among them — if young is even the right word anymore — still remember the world above. They speak in whispers of sunlight, of seasons, of growth patterns that follow nature rather than the programmed cycles of the machines. But these memories fade quickly in the constant dark. Within months, new Tenders stop looking up when the cable platform passes. They stop flinching at the sudden hiss of steam. Their pupils dilate permanently, black pools that reflect nothing.

The older ones have grown strange in other ways. Some have multiple arms now — grafted on and integrated with their nervous systems to better tend the vast walls of growth. Others have extended their bodies vertically, stretching somehow, their spines elongated to reach the higher terraces without ladders. One ancient Tender has become nearly sedentary, positioned at a crucial junction of pipes where they monitor flow rates through skin that's developed chemical sensitivity. They've become part of the garden's sensing apparatus, a living gauge.

They maintain the machines too, but not through any technical knowledge. It's all intuition now — they feel when a pump is about to fail by the change in its vibration, know when nutrient mixtures are off by the way the plants whisper, predict blockages in the irrigation system by the quality of echo in the shaft. They move through the mechanical systems like antibodies through a bloodstream, fixing problems before they fully manifest.

When Tenders die — and they do die, eventually, even with their modifications — the garden consumes them. Their bodies are processed through the same systems that break down plant waste, their nutrients returned to the feeding streams. New Tenders find bones sometimes, wound through with roots, incorporated into the structure of the garden itself. They see these remains as good omens — proof that the cycle continues, that the boundary between tender and tended has fully dissolved.

There are stories of Tenders who've gone too deep, descended below the established growing floors into the reservoir systems at the mountain's heart. They return changed beyond recognition, if they return at all — their bodies more plant than animal, their consciousness distributed somehow through the root networks. They can make the entire garden respond to their will, causing waves of growth or dormancy to ripple up the shaft. The other Tenders fear and revere these deep ones, leaving offerings of specially cultivated fruits at the entrance to the lower tunnels.

The Tenders reproduce now, though not in any human way. They cultivate new workers like they cultivate plants — starting with a cutting from themselves, a piece of modified tissue that they place in the growth medium. These clone-sprouts develop in the garden walls, fed by the same nutrient systems, shaped by the same mechanical forces. When they're ready, they simply step out of their growing pods, fully formed, already knowing the click-language, already adapted to the darkness. They have no childhood, no learning period — they simply begin tending, as if they've always been there.

This is what becomes of humanity in the vertical garden — not replaced by machines, but merged with them, merged with the plants, becoming something new in the roaring darkness. They are the garden's consciousness, its immune system, its method of reproduction. They ensure the cycles continue, that growth never stops, that the shaft remains alive with the sound of water and steam and the endless rustling of leaves in manufactured night.
