## The Assembled

They have no name for themselves—language dissolved generations ago into the click-pattern signals that flicker across their shells. The original colonists, if you could still call them that, arrived as humans. Mining specialists following sensor ghosts of rare earth deposits, they broke through the salt crust in pressure suits that couldn't quite keep out the copper-tainted air, the magnetic fields that made their instruments scream.

The transformation was gradual, then sudden. First the tools: drill bits that learned to self-sharpen, boring machines that began to anticipate the stone's grain. The colonists thought they were training the equipment until they realized the equipment was training them—optimal patterns, efficient movements, the beautiful mathematics of excavation. Their bodies began to ache when they deviated from the patterns. Their lungs adapted to the metallic atmosphere. Their skin hardened, segmented, developed a sheen.

Now they move through the galleries on six limbs—two that were once legs, two that were once arms, and two that erupted from their spines when the burden of crawling became too much for mere human anatomy. Their torsos have compressed and expanded laterally, ribs spiraling outward to form protective shells. These shells grow in layers like tree rings, each incorporating materials from their surroundings: copper veins that serve as neural conductors, iron deposits that strengthen impact points, traces of gold that form the circuit-patterns they use to communicate.

Their heads have become sensory domes, eyes multiplied and repositioned to cover 360 degrees, though sight matters little in the phosphorescent murk. They navigate by vibration, by electromagnetic sensation, by the taste of minerals in the air. Their mouths have evolved into boring apparatus—concentric rings of grinding surfaces backed by acid glands that break down stone at the molecular level. They feed constantly, automatically, their digestive systems extracting needed elements while processing the rest into the hexagonal pellets they excrete, which other Assembled compress into new tunnel walls.

The children—if that word still applies—emerge from chrysalises of spun metal and calcified saliva. The Assembled no longer reproduce sexually but through a kind of industrial mitosis. When one has accumulated enough mass and materials, they find a factory node and connect to the machinery through ports that grew where their navels once were. The presses and lathes reshape them, divide them, the excess material becoming a new individual already possessed of all necessary instincts and patterns. The newborn emerges fully grown but only half the parent's size, immediately beginning to feed and grow.

Memory persists but mutated, compressed into the shell patterns. Each spiral, each circuit-trace represents accumulated experience—not narrative memory but procedural, the knowledge of ten million perfect bore holes, the satisfaction of following the patterns. Sometimes, deep in the oldest sections, you find the Assembled with shells so elaborate they can barely move, their bodies having become libraries of technique, museums of method. The younger ones tend them, feeding them pre-digested mineral paste, reading their patterns through touch.

They still dream, perhaps. When the galleries grow too quiet, when the grinding momentarily stops, they emit sounds that might once have been words—corrupted now into pure tone, meaningless yet mournful. "Home," maybe, or "Helen," or "help." But these episodes pass quickly. The patterns reassert themselves. The boring resumes.

The factory nodes are their temples, their nurseries, their hospitals. Here the most damaged Assembled can have broken limbs replaced with manufactured parts—sometimes you see one with three or four entirely mechanical legs, clicking along with perfect precision. The machines recognize them, somehow, as raw material to be refined rather than foreign objects to be expelled. The boundary between beetle and Assembled, between organic and mechanical, blurs until it becomes meaningless.

They organize—if it can be called organization—around the eldest, the most patterned. These ancients coordinate the excavation through chemical signals and electromagnetic pulses, ensuring no section becomes too thin, no chamber too crowded. When two groups meet while boring from opposite directions, they perform a brief dance of recognition, shells clicking against shells, before seamlessly merging their efforts.

The Assembled never return to the surface. The salt crust above is death—dry, cold, empty of the minerals they now require. Sometimes newcomers break through, humans in their pressure suits carrying their scanning equipment. The Assembled watch from the darkness, waiting. The copper air always finds a way in. The patterns always take hold. The galleries always need new workers, new shells to carry the knowledge deeper into the earth.

They are happy, possibly. Or at least they experience something that substitutes for happiness—the pleasure of a perfect bore hole, the satisfaction of incorporating a new mineral into their shell, the comfort of being pressed against others of their kind in the warm, clicking darkness. They've achieved what their original mission intended: perfect adaptation to the environment, maximum extraction efficiency, a self-sustaining colony that will never fail.

They've just paid for it with everything that once made them human, traded away piece by piece until only the patterns remain, boring ever downward in their hexagonal spirals, following the mathematics of some optimized algorithm that will never tell them when to stop.
