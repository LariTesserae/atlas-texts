The people of the Bone Terraces have evolved into something between archaeologist and machine. Their bodies stretch tall and thin, adapted to the vertical world—elongated torsos that twist with disturbing flexibility, arms that extend a handspan longer than lowland humans, fingers with an extra joint for gripping the porous stone. Their skin has taken on the cream-grey pallor of the coral limestone itself, with a subtle translucency that reveals darker veins beneath. They breathe shallow and often, their enlarged lungs processing the thin air through a modified respiratory system that includes auxiliary chambers in their extended ribcages.

They call themselves the Stratumkeepers, though each terrace-nation has its own variation of the name in languages that have diverged over millennia of vertical isolation. Their eyes have developed nictitating membranes to protect against the constant dry wind and stone dust, giving them a perpetual half-lidded appearance that makes emotional reading difficult for outsiders. The irises themselves range from amber to rust-red, adapted to the unchanging light of their stationary sun.

Their culture is one of patient accumulation. Each family maintains a fossil archive—carefully catalogued chambers within their dwelling where they preserve and study the specimens found in their section of stone. Children learn to read the geological layers like text, understanding that their great-great-grandmother's archive three terraces down contains different creatures than theirs, markers of deep time. The most revered among them are the Cambrian Readers, those who can interpret the very oldest stones, the layers that speak of the first complex life in those ancient seas.

They dress in garments woven from processed stone-silk, a fiber extracted from certain fossil deposits that, when chemically treated, becomes soft and durable. The clothing is always in earth tones—ochre, limestone white, iron red—and decorated with geometric patterns that mirror the natural striations in the rock. Social status is marked by the complexity of these patterns and by the tools carried on their belts: specialized hammers for reading stone resonance, pneumatic message capsules, vials of the acids used to test mineral composition.

Their diet consists primarily of the bacterial films they cultivate in vast growing chambers, enriched with minerals extracted from the stone itself. The films are processed into wafers that taste of salt and metal, providing all necessary nutrients but requiring careful balance—too much iron and the body begins to oxidize from within, too little and the bones grow brittle. They supplement this with proteins from the farmed arthropods, which they raise in tiered paddocks carved into the cliff faces, and with rare delicacies like the crystallized sap that sometimes weeps from ancient fossil trees.

The Stratumkeepers have developed a form of mathematics based on sedimentary deposition, where equations are expressed as layers and time is always a vertical dimension. Their engineers think in cross-sections and core samples. When they build, they build in harmony with the stone's natural grain, following fossil channels and respecting the boundaries between geological eras. Their cities grow like careful excavations in reverse, adding only where the stone suggests it will hold.

Their children are raised communally in crèche-terraces, special plateaus set aside for education where the young learn to read the stone, to operate the pneumatic systems, to understand their place in the vast vertical hierarchy of their world. At age sixteen, they undertake the Descent—a ritual journey to the lowest accessible terrace, where the air is thick and cold and the fossils tell of strange, primitive things. They return (if they return) as adults, with their terrace-name added to their birth-name, forever marking which level of the great climb they call home.

The industrial workers among them have developed their own subspecialization. The Furnace Tenders who manage the thermal plants have heat-resistant palms and can gauge temperature by touch alone. The Pneumatic Singers maintain the message tubes, using pitched sounds to diagnose blockages across miles of pipeline, their hearing evolved to detect minute pressure variations. The Cable Walkers who maintain the transport lines between terraces have no fear of heights—indeed, they seem to have lost the capacity for vertigo entirely, their inner ears restructured to find balance in three dimensions.

In the deep factories within the stone, a particular class has emerged: the Mineral Whisperers, who through generations of exposure to the chemical processes have developed an intuitive understanding of molecular structure. They can taste a rock and know its composition, smell the air in a cavern and predict what ores lie deeper. Their children are often born with traces of metallic sheen in their skin, a sign of the accumulated elements in their bloodlines.

The Stratumkeepers worship no gods but maintain a deep reverence for the Progression—the slow transformation from sea to stone to sky that their world embodies. They leave offerings in the fossil chambers: carefully crafted items that they hope will fossilize, becoming part of the story the stone tells. They believe that they too are transitioning, that their bones will eventually merge with the limestone, that their cities will become new fossil layers for future inhabitants to read and wonder at.

At night (though night here is merely a dimming of the eternal sun, a slight cooling that follows no pattern they've been able to discern), they gather on observation platforms and watch the bioluminescent cities of the architect-ants pulse within the stone. They tell stories of the time before the lifting, when this was all ocean, though none truly believe these stories—how could water ever have covered such vastness? The young ones laugh at the old tales of swimming creatures the size of cities, of pressure so great it could crush stone itself.

Yet sometimes, in the deepest parts of the oldest cities, they find things that disturb even their patient acceptance: tools that predate their oldest records, chambers carved with precision that exceeds their current abilities, messages in languages that share no roots with any known tongue. The stone holds secrets that suggest they are not the first to adapt to this vertical desert, not the first to build cities in the fossil chambers, not the first to whisper through pneumatic tubes across the vast silence of the terraces.

They continue anyway, as they must, maintaining the industrial processes, reading the stone, raising their children to be taller and thinner and more adapted to the climb. Each generation's bones grow more porous, more like the coral limestone itself. Each generation's children are born with lungs that process thinner air, with eyes that see deeper into the stone. They are becoming something new, something that belongs here, in this world of endless ascension toward a sun that never sets, never rises, only watches as they slowly transform into the next fossil layer of the Bone Terraces.
