The inhabitants of the plateau city move with a particular economy of motion, their bodies adapted over generations to the perpetual moisture that would rust ordinary lungs. They are tall—height helps with heat dissipation—but built narrow, with elongated torsos that house modified respiratory systems. Their skin has a peculiar quality, neither fully permeable nor waterproof, but something in between: a surface of microscopic channels that direct the constant condensation into collection points along their spine, where specialized pores can either absorb or expel moisture as needed.

Their eyes have developed nictitating membranes—not for underwater vision, but to constantly clear the condensation that forms on every surface. They blink in slow, deliberate sweeps, the membrane sliding across eyes that have grown large and dark to pierce through the eternal haze of steam. Some have developed a second, translucent eyelid that they can leave closed while working, seeing the world through a permanent film that protects from the scalding vapor.

Children are born with sealed airways that don't open fully until their third year. Until then, they breathe through specialized gill-like slits along their necks that filter oxygen from the humid air while preventing their developing lungs from drowning in moisture. Parents carry them in slings that position them at precise angles—too vertical and condensation pools in their breathing slits, too horizontal and their modified blood, thicker than baseline human, struggles to circulate in the heavy air.

The social structure has evolved around water rights—not scarcity, but management. Families maintain hereditary claims to specific condensation channels, the depth of the grooves worn by their ancestors' daily collections becoming a kind of geological genealogy. They've developed a mathematics based entirely on flow rates and bacterial growth cycles. Their most complex machines are not for industry but for calculating the precise bacterial harvest that maintains ecological balance—take too much and the films die back, take too little and they grow thick enough to clog the channels.

They wear clothes of metallic mesh, woven from threads drawn from the same heat-resistant materials as their buildings. The garments hang loose, allowing air to circulate while channeling runoff away from the body. Status shows in the oxidation patterns of these meshes—the wealthy can afford alloys that patina in brilliant blues and greens, while workers wear the dull gray of cheaper materials that must be replaced as they corrode. Jewelry consists of sealed glass capsules worn at pulse points, filled with bacterial cultures that glow different colors in response to body heat—living mood rings that must be fed daily with drops of the wearer's filtered sweat.

Their language has no words for "dry" that aren't theoretical abstractions. They have seventeen different terms for varieties of dampness, each carrying its own emotional and practical connotations. Conversations are held in low, thrumming tones that carry better through the thick air than high pitches. They've learned to read meaning in the speed of each other's blinks, the rhythm of membrane sweeps becoming a kind of emotional punctuation.

They dream of cold, dry places the way desert dwellers dream of ocean. Their art depicts impossible landscapes of frost and sand, painted with bacterial cultures bred to produce specific pigments when exposed to different temperatures. The paintings are alive, shifting color as the heat of the day cycles, never quite the same twice. Musicians create instruments that use steam pressure and condensation drip rates to generate rhythm and tone—concerts are performed in the early morning when the temperature differential between night-cooled stone and rising thermal heat creates the most complex atmospheric patterns.

The elderly develop a condition where their moisture-regulation system begins to fail. They gradually lose the ability to process the constant humidity, their bodies either desiccating despite the wet air or becoming supersaturated, their tissues swelling. The city maintains hospice towers at the plateau's edge where these elders spend their final years, suspended in hammocks over the misty void, their bodies slowly equalizing with the atmosphere until they simply dissolve into the eternal cycle of condensation and evaporation that defines this impossible place.

They worship through maintenance—the cleaning of channels, the cultivation of bacterial mats, the careful calibration of industrial systems. Their priests are engineers, their prayers are mathematical proofs demonstrating the continued stability of the thermal system that makes their city possible. They fear not death but system collapse—the day the deep heat fails, or the bacterial mats die, or the delicate balance tips too far toward any extreme.

In their most private moments, they stand naked in the steam vents, letting the superheated vapor scour their modified skin, a sensation both painful and necessary, like the memory of what they were before they became what this place required them to be.
