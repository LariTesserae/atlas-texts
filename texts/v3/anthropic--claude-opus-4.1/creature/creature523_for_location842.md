The ten thousand are not quite human anymore, though they remember being so.

The Watershed People, as surface dwellers call them when they dare speak of them at all, have developed heart rates that slow to match the drip-tempo of condensation. Their children are born with nictitating membranes—translucent third eyelids that flick across their vision in the humidity, inherited from grandparents who first developed them as adults, crying mineral tears until their bodies adapted. The membranes give everyone a perpetual look of half-sleep, of submersion.

They organize themselves into Pools—kinship groups that share water rights, sleep shifts, and increasingly, physical characteristics. The Seventh Pool grows hair that naturally mats into felt-like sheets, perfect for water collection. The Nineteenth Pool's children teethe twice, losing their milk teeth for adult teeth, then losing those for rows of harder, mineral-enriched points that can scrape edible algae from stone. The Second Pool has developed throat sacs that inflate slightly when they speak, amplifying their voices in the water tribunals where volume often determines victory.

The Salamander Shepherds form their own caste. These are the ones who gave up trying to keep the amphibians out and instead learned their language—chemical signals released through skin oils, territorial displays using bioluminescent fungi paste, rhythmic tapping that mimics mating calls. They guide the salamander populations, driving them toward neglected cisterns that need cleaning, away from drinking supplies, into sacrifice pools during the terrible dry seasons when protein becomes worth more than doctrine. The Shepherds' skin has grown permeable and faintly toxic; they absorb traces of salamander secretions until they themselves can barely be distinguished from their charges in the dark. Their children are born slightly damp and never fully dry out.

The Listeners squat at the intersection points between chambers, ears pressed to the limestone. They've developed the ability to echolocate through the stone itself, reading vibrations from the surface world above—approaching sandstorms, traveling merchants, the deep tremors that precede rain. Their ear canals have widened and developed tiny, flexible bones that click against each other. They sell their prophecies for water rations, tapping out tomorrow's weather on interested buyers' wrists in a code that uses the same rhythm as the great pump.

Water Wives and Water Husbands marry the cisterns themselves in ceremonies that would horrify surface dwellers. They lower themselves into the deepest accessible pools and stay submerged for days, breathing through reed tubes, letting their skin absorb minerals until they're more limestone than flesh. When they emerge, they can taste theft in the water, identify which family has been skimming more than their share by the precise mineral signature their bodies leave behind. Their fingernails grow thick and ridged, perfect for cleaning the carved channels. They consider themselves wedded to the stone, and their human partnerships, if they have them, are always secondary to their first marriage.

The Pump Prophets maintain the ancient mechanism, but they've become something more than maintenance crew. Six generations of the same six families, they've interbred until their musculature has reformed around the requirements of the pump. Their shoulders sit differently in their sockets. Their grip strength could crush stone. They sleep in piles at the pump's base, their bodies unconsciously maintaining the rhythm even in dreams. When one dies, their replacement is chosen from the children who already show the telltale signs—the broadening back, the unconscious rocking motion, the ability to hear the aquifer's depth by the sound of their own heartbeat.

The children adapt fastest. Those born in the cisterns develop what the elders call "wet lung"—a condition that makes surface air feel like drowning. Their alveoli are so adapted to the humid atmosphere that dry air causes them to bleed. They play games that would kill surface children: holding their breath in the algae pools until they hallucinate prophecies, diving to the bottom of cisterns to retrieve stones that previous generations of children have dropped, each one carved with a wish that will come true only if retrieved by someone who wasn't alive when it was dropped.

The merchants who brave the descent learn to identify residents by smell alone—each Pool has its distinctive mixture of minerals, fungi, and salamander secretions that cling to skin and hair. The Water Tribunal judges wear robes of living moss that grow directly from their skin, fed by their sweat, trimmed into patterns that display their juridical decisions in green and brown. The oldest residents have stones growing in their tear ducts, tiny pearls of calcium that they cry out during the spring rains and string into necklaces for their grandchildren.

But perhaps the strangest adaptation is psychological. The ten thousand have developed a collective sense of water level. When the cisterns are full, the city becomes manic—laughter echoes too loud, children sprint through the passages, love affairs bloom in the dark corners. When levels drop, a torpor spreads like fog. Metabolisms slow. Speech becomes whispered. The salamanders burrow deep, and the human residents follow them down into a kind of walking hibernation, their eyes open but unseeing, their bodies moving through necessary routines while their minds drift in the space between sleep and death.

They dream collectively during these times, ten thousand minds touching in the dark, sharing visions of rain that become more elaborate with each passing dry season. In these dreams, they are water itself, seeping through limestone, gathering in pools, evaporating into mist, falling again as rain. When they wake, they often find they've been unconsciously forming human chains, passing imaginary water from hand to hand in their sleep, practicing for the next crisis, the next ceremonial sharing, the next time the cisterns breathe with ten thousand lungs, all drawing the same precious, recycled air.
