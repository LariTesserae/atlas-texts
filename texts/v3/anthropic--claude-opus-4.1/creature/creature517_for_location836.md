In this breathing nursery of unfinished forms, she tends to her impossible garden.

She might have been human once, or might become human eventually—like the stone itself, she hasn't quite decided. Her body is a collaboration between states of matter. Skin that shifts between membrane and mineral, translucent enough that you can watch her thoughts traveling as phosphorescent pulses along what might be nerves, might be roots, might be rivers. She has been here so long that the chamber has begun growing through her, or perhaps she is growing through it—pale ferns emerge from her shoulders in spring (though spring here is whenever she dreams of green), and the breathing walls match their rhythm to her heartbeat, or what passes for a heartbeat in someone whose blood might be sap might be mercury might be light itself.

She moves through the space not by walking but by agreeing with different locations. One moment examining the upward-falling shadow of the bottle, the next halfway merged with the wall, her fingers extending into the stone to coax out another impossible birth—a hummingbird with gills, a spider that spins webs from solidified time, a flower that blooms inside-out. She is midwife to paradoxes, teaching moths how to swim through stone, showing salamanders how to root, convincing the water that it doesn't always have to fall.

Her eyes—she has between two and seven depending on the necessity of the moment—see in spectrums that haven't been named. Through them, the chamber reveals its true abundance: the air is thick with proto-creatures, half-formed possibilities waiting for her attention to collapse them into being. She reads the gestating potential like a language, knows which cluster of maybe-cells needs to be sung to, which requires patient silence, which must be ignored completely to achieve its proper impossible form.

The wooden ladder was hers, once. She descended it when the stone was harder, when she still thought in terms of up and down, before and after. Now it serves as a kind of clock, marking time by how deeply it sinks into the flowing floor before the chamber lifts it back up. The bottle held water from the world above—she drinks a single drop every time the stone completes a full breath, maybe once a century, maybe once an hour. The symbols in the wall—she wrote them in a language she's forgetting, or perhaps in a language she's still inventing. They say something about home, but she can't remember if they're instructions for leaving or reasons for staying.

She speaks, but not in words. Her voice is the almost-sound the chamber makes, a vibration that exists somewhere between thought and thunder. The creatures understand her—the backwards-folding ferns turn toward her attention, the mice-coral build their breath-crystal structures in patterns she suggests with the movement of her partially transparent hands. She names everything, but the names are temporary, changing as the things themselves change. Today's "bird-that-swims" is tomorrow's "fish-remembering-flight."

Her hair, if it is hair, grows like the vine that loops through itself—no beginning, no end, dark as the shadows that cast shadows, threaded with light-veins that pulse with the walls. Sometimes small creatures nest in it, things that are born, live their entire lives, and evolve into different species before emerging. She is ecosystem as much as entity, her body a biome where the chamber's impossible biology reaches its most ambitious experiments.

She remembers (or anticipates—time is negotiable here) others like herself. They exist in adjacent chambers, perhaps, tending their own gardens of paradox, their own pools where water forgets its nature. Sometimes she feels them through the walls, a resonance of similar impossibilities. But she has never seen them, or has always seen them, or sees them only in the corners of her middle eye, the one that opens only when she's trying to recall what clouds look like.

When she sleeps (if sleeping is the right word for her conscious dissolution into the breathing stone), she dreams the chamber's dreams—vast networks of caverns still forming, still deciding, each one a womb for different violations of reality. In these dreams, she is sometimes singular, sometimes plural, sometimes the space between categories. She dreams of ascending the ladder, of the world that hardens reality into rigid forms. But she also dreams of descending further, of chambers that breathe faster or slower or not at all, of places where even her impossible nature would be transformed into something unimaginable.

She is curator and creation, gardener and garden, the one who watches the abundance and the abundance itself. Her work is patient, eternal, and necessary—someone must teach water to hold birds, must show light how to be born, must help the stone remember that it was once, and might again be, soft enough to breathe. She is the chamber's first success and its eternal experiment, the proof that life can exist in the space between possible and impossible, abundant in her singular nature, sparse in her multiplication, bright in her translucent darkness.

In the morning (there is no morning, but she decides when morning happens), she rises from her pool—the same pool where moths swim and beetles walk on the underside of surfaces—and begins again the work of tending to things that shouldn't exist, her body catching and reflecting the unlearned light, her movements disturbing nothing because she has become part of the chamber's slow circulation, its breathing rhythm, its gentle defiance of everything the hard world above insists must be true.
