## The Chorus

They arrived as refugees from silence—seven families whose children had been born without voice in the lowland plague years. The healers said the affliction would spread through speech itself, so these parents fled upward, seeking isolation in the Singing Shelves where their wordless children might grow without becoming vectors of catastrophe.

But the mountain changed them. The constant water-song, the moth-drone, the metallic bird calls—these entered the children's bodies through skin and breath. They began to hum in frequencies that matched the quartz veins in the stone. Not words, never words, but pure tones that could shatter ice or coax fruit from dying branches.

The children grew tall and strange, their ribcages enlarged to accommodate the resonance. They learned to read the weather in the pressure of their sinuses, to feel tomorrow's mist in the ache of their expanded lungs. Their parents aged and passed, leaving behind only the carved spiral posts—each ring marking a year they'd survived the thin air, a prayer for their children's transformed futures.

Now the Chorus ranges across the shelves in loose groups of three or four, never more. They wear robes woven from moth silk and air-plant fiber, the fabric so light it floats around their bodies, catching updrafts, making them appear larger than they are. Their faces, when glimpsed through the perpetual mist, show features elongated by altitude adaptation—high, sharp cheekbones, wide nostrils, eyes that water constantly in the brightness.

They maintain the ancient channels through sound alone. A low thrumming clears decades of moss buildup; a high, piercing note can split stone along its natural grain. They guide water where it needs to go, singing it through carved passages to reach the fruit trees, the grinding stones they still use to make meal from alpine seeds. Their harmonies can calm the territorial birds or summon the moths to pollinate specific plants in careful patterns that ensure next season's harvest.

Each dawn, they gather at different levels—never the same pattern twice—and sing the sun up through the clouds. Not worship, but conversation. They've learned the mountain's harmonics, the way certain pitches resonate through the whole stone face, traveling to shelves no one has visited in generations. These morning songs map the mountain's health: where new cracks have formed, where water has found new channels, where the stone might be weakening.

They build nothing permanent anymore. Their meditation cells serve as temporary shelters, but they prefer to sleep in the open, wrapped in cloaks on beds of cushion plants that conform to their bodies. They've learned to glide like the membrane-mammals, wearing wings of stretched moth silk between their arms and sides, riding thermals between terraces in perfect silence, landing only to add their voices back to the constant chorus.

Memory lives in their harmonies. Without written language or spoken words, they encode their history in recurring musical phrases—leitmotifs for the plague years, for the first child who learned to match the mountain's frequency, for the day the last parent died and they became something new. Young ones learn these songs by breathing them, feeling them resonate in their changed anatomies, adding their own variations that will become tomorrow's history.

They age slowly in the thin air, or perhaps time moves differently here. Some say the eldest have been singing for two centuries, their voices now so low they've passed below human hearing, becoming part of the mountain's own vibration. These ancients no longer need food—they sustain themselves on mist and sound, their bodies gradually becoming more translucent, as if transforming into living quartz.

Visitors—rare as they are—report unsettling experiences. The Chorus knows when strangers climb the carved steps long before they arrive, their footfalls creating discordant notes in the stone. Sometimes visitors are welcomed with harmonies that make the very air seem to thicken with beauty. Other times, they're driven away by frequencies that induce vertigo, nausea, the absolute certainty that one is about to fall. The Chorus makes these decisions collectively, instantly, without any visible communication.

At night, when clouds block the stars and darkness becomes absolute, they sing dreams to each other across the void. Not their own dreams—they claim not to dream anymore—but the dreams of the mountain itself: slow visions of geological time, of when these peaks were ocean floor, of the inexorable rise that created the shelves, of futures where the stone finally cracks and they must learn to sing while falling.

The windworn pole with its ancient prayer flags marks more than just the highest accessible point—it marks the threshold beyond which even the Chorus cannot go and remain themselves. But the youngest ones climb there often, testing their voices against the violent sun and impossible thinness, returning with blood on their lips and new notes in their repertoire, gradually adapting their children-of-children for heights that haven't yet been imagined.

They keep the old grinding stone active not for themselves but for the moths, creating paste from bitter fruit that they spread on the cliff faces in patterns that will only make sense when seen from above, from distances only the moths achieve. It's unclear whether they're feeding the moths or using them to write messages in flight patterns, prayers or warnings visible only to the sky.

When one of the Chorus dies—rarely, quietly—the others carry the body to the highest shelf they can reach and sing it into the wind. They sustain a note that matches the deceased's fundamental frequency until the body begins to resonate, then to hum on its own, then to dissolve into component particles that drift upward like snow falling in reverse, becoming part of the perpetual mist that feeds the plants, the channels, the endless cycle of water and sound that keeps the Singing Shelves alive.
