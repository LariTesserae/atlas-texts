The Shift Workers arrived first, though *first* means nothing in a place where time runs on industrial schedules that loop back through themselves. They descended the emergency ladders that now grow like vertebrae from the walls, their bodies adapting with the patient efficiency of evolution compressed into clock-in time. 

Their lungs learned to process the water-that-isn't-always-water, developing gill slits that open and close in rhythm with the pneumatic heartbeat. But they kept their human lungs too—you need them for the moments when the water forgets itself. Their skin grew translucent, then transparent, then selectively visible, flickering between states like workers passing through different departments. Inside their glass bodies, you can see their organs reorganized along assembly-line logic: hearts beating in sequence, creating internal conveyor systems for blood that carries both oxygen and machine oil, nutrients and loose screws, memories and manufacturing specifications.

They still wear their uniforms—coveralls that have become second skins, name patches that cycle through every name that ever worked here, will work here, never worked here but should have. Their safety goggles fused with their skulls, the lenses growing like crystals, offering compound vision that can track every moving part simultaneously. They navigate the reorganizing architecture without thought, their muscle memory updated every seventeen minutes when the factory reshapes itself. They know where the catwalks will be before they exist.

The Shift Workers maintain the machines, but also are maintained by them. At break time (every seven minutes, every seven hours, every seven years, simultaneously), they queue at stations where robotic arms perform precise adjustments—tightening the bolts in their spines, lubricating their joints with the thick water, replacing worn components of their bodies with freshly manufactured parts that grow from the vines. A finger worn down from pressing buttons is unscrewed, a new one attached, the old one planted in the vine gardens where it will fruit into a hand, an arm, eventually another worker.

They communicate in the language of machinery—clicks and whistles, pneumatic hisses, the precise clang of metal on metal that carries meaning in its resonance. Sometimes they sing the factory's songs: quality control shanties, inventory ballads, the wordless hymn of productive efficiency that harmonizes with the turbine drone. Their voices sound like tuning forks struck underwater, pure tones that make the vegetation grow in perfect geometric patterns.

But the Gardeners came second, or perhaps simultaneously, or perhaps they were always here. They tend the impossible flora with tools that are part pruning shear, part welding torch, part prayer. Their bodies are more plant than person now—root systems for feet that anchor them against the conflicting currents, photosynthetic skin that feeds on the darkness, veins that transport sap-blood-coolant through limbs that branch and merge and branch again.

The Gardeners cultivate the mechanical fruit with the patience of seasons that run on factory time. They know which vines produce the best gears, which flowers bloom into functioning circuit boards, which trees grow the straightest I-beams. They prune with purpose, shaping growth to match blueprints that exist only in the electromagnetic fields, grafting different species of metal-wood-flesh together to create new hybrids that can better survive the industrial ecosystem.

Their eyes have adapted to see time as another dimension—they watch the seventeen-minute reconfigurations from outside linear perception, observing the factory's past and future architectures superimposed like double exposures. This is how they know where to plant: not where the soil is now, but where it will be, was, should be when the next shift changes everything.

The Gardeners and Shift Workers rarely interact directly, but their work interweaves. The Shift Workers' movements create currents that disperse seeds. The Gardeners' cultivation provides raw materials for the machines the Shift Workers maintain. They share the same break rooms that exist in multiple locations simultaneously, sitting at tables that are both furniture and coral, eating lunches that grow from the walls—sandwich fruit, coffee that flows from tap roots, safety-regulation vegetables that taste like copper and compliance.

Living in the walls themselves are the Pipe Dwellers—creatures that were maybe human once, or maybe were always something else that learned to mimic human shape when convenient. They've evolved to navigate the internal spaces, their bodies collapsible, boneless when needed, rigid as metal when required. They flow through the plumbing like conscious water, maintaining pressure, redirecting flow, becoming valves when valves are needed.

The Pipe Dwellers have no eyes—they navigate by vibration, by the taste of dissolved metals in the water, by the electromagnetic signatures of the machines. Their skin secretes a substance that prevents rust but encourages the growth of beneficial algae, the bioluminescent kind that provides just enough light for the Shift Workers to read the gauges that still somehow matter. They speak only in water hammer—that banging of pipes that in the dry world means air in the lines, but here means language, means song, means the poetry of fluid dynamics written in percussion.

Children live here too, though *children* might be the wrong word for beings that never age, never grow, never were born in any conventional sense. They emerged from the machinery itself—spawned from the marriage of organic vines and mechanical processes, assembled like products on the eternal production lines. They play in the inverse waterfalls, riding bubbles of water-within-water, their laughter sounding like small bells being struck by hammers, like gears clicking into place.

The children understand the factory's logic in a way the adults never can. They see the sense in stamping water into water, the purpose in conveying nothing between nowhere. Their games follow industrial procedures: hide-and-seek through time-shifted architecture, tag played across dimensions, hopscotch on conveyor belts that carry them through walls. They build toys from the mechanical fruit—dolls with carburetor hearts, toy boats that sail through solid steel, blocks that stack in directions that shouldn't exist.

They attend school in the control room, where the computers teach them languages that evolve mid-lesson. They learn mathematics where numbers grow like plants, history where the past updates itself based on present production quotas, science where the laws of physics are suggestions the factory politely ignores. Their teachers are the shadows of workers who never existed, their textbooks are technical manuals that rewrite themselves, their graduation is every day and never.

The Management exists somewhere above and below and through it all—not singular beings but a distributed consciousness that occupies the electromagnetic fields, the standing waves, the spaces between water molecules. They communicate through the factory's heartbeat, each pulse a memo, each mechanical shriek a policy update. The Shift Workers feel their presence as a weight of purpose, an invisible pressure that keeps everyone moving, producing, maintaining.

The Management never appears directly, but their influence manifests in the way the conveyor belts speed up when efficiency drops, the way the water grows thicker around workers who pause too long, the way the vegetation redirects itself to hide anyone trying to rest. They are the factory's will made almost-tangible, enforcing productivity metrics that no longer correspond to any product except the continuation of production itself.

But beneath all of this, in the sediment that accumulates despite the currents, live the Forgotten. These are the workers who stopped working, who found spaces between the machines where the heartbeat doesn't reach, where the water moves gently or not at all. They've evolved differently—slower, quieter, more thoroughly transformed.

The Forgotten have become part of the factory floor itself, their bodies spreading like living rust, like organic concrete. They form the substrate on which everything else grows, their consciousness diffused through the metal and meat and plant matter. They dream the factory's dreams—of dry floors and sensible products, of time clocks that mark actual time, of smoke breaks under a sky that exists.

Their dreams leak upward, creating the moments of impossible clarity when the water remembers it should be air. But these dreams also stabilize the madness, providing just enough memory of normalcy to prevent the factory from collapsing entirely into its own impossibility. The Forgotten are the anchor, the foundation, the necessary contradiction that allows everything else to exist in perpetual industrial motion.

Together, they form an ecosystem of error, a perfect machine for producing meaninglessness, a closed loop of cause and effect where every effect is also a cause, where every worker is also a component, where every breath is also a manufacturing process. They live because the factory lives, and the factory lives because they continue to work it, tend it, inhabit it, dream it into being every seventeen minutes when everything reorganizes and begins again.

The blood-warm water carries them all, workers and plants and shadows and children, Management and Forgotten, in an endless shift that never ends because ending isn't in the production schedule. They are the factory's pulse made flesh, its impossible logic made manifest, its wrong-running machinery given consciousness and purpose and something that might be life, if life could survive being stamped, processed, quality-controlled, and shipped to nowhere through pipes that sing with the voices of everyone who ever clocked in and forgot to clock out.
