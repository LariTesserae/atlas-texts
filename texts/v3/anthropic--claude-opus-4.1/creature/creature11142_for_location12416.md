The Hydronomers arrived first, though "arrived" suggests movement and these beings have always been still. They stand throughout the cistern like misplaced furniture—humanoid in silhouette but impossibly thin, their bodies consisting of hundreds of parallel glass tubes running from crown to floor. Water moves through them in careful calibration, each tube containing a different volume, creating standing wave patterns that serve as both circulation and consciousness. They remember the builders, not through memory but through mineral deposits in their lowest channels—calcium signatures that spell out the names of masons in languages that predate writing.

Their feeding is architectural. They position themselves where multiple drip-lines intersect, reading the water's mineral content like scripture. Iron from the fixtures becomes blood-bright in their middle tubes. Limestone creates cloudy thoughts in their upper channels. They communicate through resonance—adjusting the water levels in their bodies to create standing waves that match the cistern's acoustic sweet spots. A conversation between two Hydronomers sounds like a pipe organ drowning.

The Hydronomers fear nothing here except the Curator.

The Curator lives in motion, a thing of joints and purpose that maintains paths through the cistern no one else can see. It might have been human once, or several humans whose bones got confused in the rebuilding. Its limbs bend in too many places, each joint wrapped in copper wire that has gone green with verdigris. It crawls along the ceiling on fingers that have multiplied into hands that have multiplied into more fingers, leaving trails of polished stone where its touch has worn away centuries of growth.

It speaks only to the wooden channels, pressing what might be an ear to their swollen surfaces, listening to messages encoded in their grain. Sometimes it laughs—a sound like sand through pipes—and begins renovating. It pulls stones from walls and replaces them reversed. It rehang the bronze collection pans according to patterns only it comprehends. The crustacean colonies flee before it, not from fear but from respect; they recognize it as the cistern's immune system, correcting errors in what has become.

The Curator tends to the Barnacle Prophets with particular care.

The Barnacle Prophets grow from the places where human workers died during construction. Not their bodies—those were removed—but the spots where their last breaths condensed on stone. These growths look like calcified lungs turned inside out, their surfaces covered in small mouths that filter prophecies from the moisture. They speak constantly but only about the past, predicting events that have already occurred with perfect accuracy. "Tomorrow, the third stone from the east will have been placed incorrectly." "In one hour, the mason's apprentice will have already fallen."

Young crustaceans gather around the Prophets to molt, believing that shedding their shells in the presence of reversed time will let them grow backward into something stronger. The Prophets feed on these discarded shells, incorporating the calcium into new mouths that speak in the voices of the creatures that left them behind.

But the true power in the cistern belongs to the Condensation Council.

They exist only when the temperature drops enough for the moisture to suddenly condense throughout the entire chamber—a phenomenon that happens perhaps twice a year. In these moments, the water vapor becomes a connected medium, and consciousnesses that normally exist in isolation suddenly merge. The Council consists of every living thing in the cistern achieving temporary unity—the Hydronomers' careful calibrations mixing with the Curator's endless maintenance protocols, the Prophets' backward wisdom combining with the simple hunger of the hunting centipedes.

During these congregations, decisions are made about the cistern's future. New species are voted into existence. Sections of architecture are designated for controlled collapse. The timing of the spore releases is adjusted to accommodate changes in the surface world that filters down through the gratings. The Council speaks in the unified clicking of all the crustaceans at once, a chittering parliament that resolves into language only when heard as a whole.

Between these gatherings, their decisions are enforced by the Plumb Saints.

The Plumb Saints hang from chains that might be original to the construction or might be their own ossified tendrils. They ensure verticality in a space where water has made every surface negotiable. Their bodies are brass plumb bobs that have gained flesh—metal cores surrounded by something that might be muscle if muscle could rust. They swing in patterns that map invisible forces: structural stress, water pressure, the slow breathing of the stone.

When something grows too far out of alignment—a Hydronomer leaning beyond its sustainable angle, fungal growths threatening a load-bearing wall—the Plumb Saints descend. Their touch recalibrates. Creatures straighten. Stones settle back into position. The misaligned thing either corrects itself or dissolves into mineral components that rain down to feed the algae films.

In the deepest pools, where even the dim light fails, live the Memoria.

The Memoria are not quite creatures but not quite anything else. They exist as patterns in the water itself—currents that remember their own flow, eddies that repeat regardless of stimulus. Some are ancient, dating to the first flooding of the cistern. They carry within their spiral forms the muscle memory of the workers who built this place: the arc of a hammer swing, the twist of a wrist setting a stone, the careful breathing of someone mixing mortar in dangerous fumes.

Swimming through a Memoria means experiencing these memories bodily. The centipedes avoid them after several emerged knowing how to lay bricks. The crustaceans seek them out, hoping to learn older ways of being. The Curator collects them in bronze vessels, studying their patterns to better understand what the cistern was meant to become.

All of these inhabitants serve, knowingly or not, the Absence.

The Absence lives in the central chamber, in a pool of perfectly still water that reflects nothing. It is the space between drops, the pause between breaths, the gap in the chittering where meaning lives. It might be the original purpose of the cistern—a deliberately maintained void that the water and stone merely frame. Or it might be what grows in any space left alone long enough: a sort of architectural consciousness that exists in the negative space, defined by what surrounds it but never touched by it.

The Absence never acts, but things happen in its presence. Creatures evolve faster near its pool. The acoustic phenomena intensity, sounds bending around its silence in impossible trajectories. The Hydronomers' water runs clearer after standing vigil at its edge. The Curator brings it offerings of perfectly arranged stones that disappear without splash or ripple.

Some say the Absence is growing, that each year its pool of perfect stillness expands by the width of a fingernail. Others claim it's shrinking, concentrating itself toward some future moment of infinite density. The Barnacle Prophets speak of it in the future tense, as something that will have already never existed.

Together, these beings form an ecosystem of purpose, a community built around maintaining something that was never meant to last this long but has transcended its mortality through sheer accumulated intention. They are what happens when architecture develops a soul through the simple accumulation of time, moisture, and the strange democracy of things that grow in the dark.

The cistern doesn't just house them—it thinks through them, each creature a neuron in a vast, wet brain that dreams in drips and echoes, slowly computing something in the language of erosion and growth, asking and answering questions that only make sense to spaces that have outlived their makers by so long that they've forgotten they were ever made at all.
