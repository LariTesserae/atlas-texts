The Chorus dwells here—though "dwells" implies a stillness they have never known.

They are not quite one thing nor many, but something between: a dispersed intelligence spread across dozens of bodies that share memory but not form. Some segments walk on legs of calcified plant fiber, their joints clicking in mathematical patterns as they traverse the fungal mat. Others have modified themselves with hollowed succulent segments worn as shells, the plant oils soaking through their permeable skin to sustain them through the waterless days. A few have grown gossamer wings from stretched membrane, riding the warm updrafts at the plateau's edge, serving as far-seeing eyes for the whole.

Their original forms—if they ever had a single origin—are lost to generations of radical self-modification. They splice their own tissues with the plateau's life: incorporating the floating anemones' bioluminescent organs as communication nodes pulsing in their flesh, weaving fungal filaments through their nervous systems to expand their sensory range, cultivating colonies of the ribbon-creatures as external digestive systems that break down the toxic oils into nutrients they can absorb.

The Chorus thinks in harmonics. They have mapped every tube and chamber in the singing stone, know which combinations of wind-passages create which acoustic patterns. Their bodies position themselves precisely to redirect air currents, their movements a kind of musical notation written in space. They speak to each other not in words but in the interplay between the plateau's natural song and their choreographed interruptions of it—a gap in the sound here, a doubled resonance there, meaning emerging from absence as much as presence.

Those stone pillars with their clay tablets? The Chorus maintains them as memory anchors—not maps of space but of time. Each configuration of dots and lines records a particular state of the plateau's transformation, a snapshot of which passages were open, which plants were surging, which creatures swarming. By reading the tablets in sequence, they track the plateau's cycles, predict when certain resources will appear, when safe passages will manifest. They remember through architecture what their constantly dividing and recombining bodies cannot hold alone.

They cultivate the crimson succulents with disturbing expertise, knowing exactly where to score the flesh to control the timing of explosion, how to direct the spray of seeds, which oils to harvest and which to leave as accelerants for the next growth cycle. They've learned to read the fungal mat's glow-patterns as information, using their footsteps to send messages across the plateau that only they can interpret. In the hollow domes where their tools lay scattered, they perform surgeries on themselves and each other—splitting bodies to create new members of the Chorus, grafting successful adaptations from one to another, sometimes merging temporarily to share complex memories directly.

But they are not alone in their intelligence. They compete—or perhaps collaborate, the distinction unclear—with something they call the Drifters. These are the ones who rejected corporeal form entirely, existing now as guided patterns in the ribbon-colonies, surfing from host to host, their consciousness distributed across the flowing segments. The Drifters think faster but remember less, experiencing time as a series of eternal presents. They leave their own markers in the placement of the floating anemones, arranging them in three-dimensional constellations that map something the Chorus cannot quite perceive—perhaps probability fields, perhaps the plateau's dreams.

The two intelligences spiral around each other in a dance of cooperation and sabotage. The Chorus will spend days arranging stones to create a new acoustic pattern, only to find the Drifters have seeded new succulents in precisely the spots that disrupt the resonance. The Drifters will cultivate a particularly useful strain of ribbon-creature, only to discover the Chorus has modified the fungal mat to digest that exact variety. Yet both share knowledge through the clay tablets, adding to each other's observations, building a cumulative understanding neither could achieve alone.

Young members of the Chorus—grown from carefully tended flesh-buds that gestate in oil-filled sacs—sometimes abandon their inherited forms to join the Drifters, dissolving their bodies to free their patterns. Occasionally, a Drifter will tire of disembodiment and convince the Chorus to grow them a new body, though one that barely resembles what they left behind, shaped by their time as pure information into something more angular, more mathematical than biological.

They worship, perhaps, or simply acknowledge, something they perceive in the plateau's deepest patterns—not a god but a process, a tendency toward arrangement that emerges from chaos without ever quite solidifying into order. They see it in the spirals of the stone pillars, the growth patterns of the succulents, the recurring motifs in the plateau's endless song. They build their tools not to fight this force but to surf its edges, to find the brief eddies of stability where thought and action become possible.

When members of the Chorus die—from failed modifications, from toxic oil exposure, from simply wearing out—their bodies are processed with ceremony. The flesh feeds the fungal mat, but the bones are carved with their lifetime's accumulated knowledge and added to special pillars at the plateau's heart. These bone-tablets don't crumble like clay; they accumulate, creating a deep history that even the Drifters consult, manifesting temporary bodies just to run appendages over the carved symbols.

At night—or what passes for night in the perpetual twilight—the Chorus performs something like sleep but more active: a synchronization ritual where all bodies move in precise unison, their shared consciousness pooling into a single overwhelming experience of being the plateau itself. In these moments, they claim, they can feel the stone thinking its slow thoughts, the succulents planning their next explosive growth, the very air calculating its path through the tubes. Whether this is true communion or simply exhaustion-induced hallucination, even they cannot say.

This is how intelligence persists in a place that dissolves all permanence: by becoming as mutable as its environment, by distributing itself across forms that can afford to be lost, by writing memory into the landscape itself, and by accepting that survival means constant transformation, constant loss, constant rediscovery of what it means to be.
