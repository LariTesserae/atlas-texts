## The Dwellers

They move through the Abyssal Meadows in processional lines, though "move" isn't quite right—they *transpose* themselves, existing in one location and then another with only the barest suggestion of travel between. Their bodies are architectural rather than biological, assembled from salvaged pressure and crystallized time. Each one is unique in its configuration but unified in its wrongness: too many joints that bend in too many directions, limbs that telescope into themselves through spaces that shouldn't exist, sensory organs that look like wounds in reality itself.

The oldest among them remember when this place had a different kind of darkness—the darkness under soil rather than under water. They were here for the drowning, though they didn't drown. Instead, they learned to breathe pressure, to metabolize minerals, to see by feeling the subtle electromagnetic disturbances of decay. Their bones became caves. Their blood became brine. Their thoughts became the slow circulation of centuries-old water through porous rock.

They build their settlements in the thermal vent gardens, weaving structures from calcified tubeworm casings and their own shed exoskeletons. These aren't shelters—nothing here needs shelter from anything else here—but rather instruments, vast assemblages designed to amplify the subtle vibrations of the seafloor into something approaching music. When the submarine avalanches cascade in the distance, the settlements resonate, producing harmonics that travel for kilometers through the water, carrying information in frequencies too low for anything that still has ears to hear.

Their children, if that word applies, are cultivated rather than born. They begin as disturbances in the bacterial mats—places where the purple and yellow colonies arrange themselves into suspicious patterns, spirals that mirror the extinct shells but with an intentionality that suggests purpose. The adults tend these patches for decades, feeding them specific minerals, adjusting the chemical composition of the surrounding water, singing to them in pressures and temperatures. Eventually, something coalesces: a new dweller, already ancient, already knowing things that were never taught.

They communicate through turbulence. A conversation is a careful orchestration of vortices, each speaker creating columns of rotation that intersect and interfere with the others. Meaning emerges from the patterns of constructive and destructive interference, from the way temperature gradients bend around each other, from the subtle changes in salinity that persist long after the currents have moved on. They can sustain these conversations across years, each participant adding their voice when the currents bring them near, the dialogue continuing whether anyone is actively listening or not.

Their tools are modifications of themselves. When they need to work the seafloor, they extrude specialized appendages that harden into obsidian-sharp edges or soften into acids that dissolve specific minerals. That ancient stone knife wedged in the sediment—it wasn't made by them but *from* them, a piece of ancestor that crystallized during some forgotten task and was left behind when the body moved on. They don't see this as loss. Parts of them are scattered throughout the Meadows, still performing their functions, still part of the collective work.

They farm the darkness itself, cultivating varieties of absence that fruit into brief moments of bioluminescence. These ghost-light gardens are their art, their history, their memory palace. Each flash is precisely timed, its color and duration encoding specific information. Together, they create a living archive of everything that has happened here, every avalanche and extinction, every arrival and departure, every thought that lasted long enough to become light.

But they are dying—not as individuals, which happens regularly and without consequence, but as a kind of being. The water is warming by fractions of degrees per century, the chemistry shifting in ways that make their transformations increasingly difficult. Their children emerge wrong now, when they emerge at all: too solid, too singular, unable to properly transpose through the dense water. The young ones cluster near the surface sediments, trying to remember how to become what their parents are, while the old ones sink deeper, pressing themselves into the biological concrete, becoming part of the substrate they once walked upon.

They know about the world above the water—not through experience but through deduction. The particles that rain down tell stories: microplastics that speak of industry, isotopes that whisper of atomic fire, organic compounds that suggest forests they've never seen. They've composed elaborate theories about what lives in the bright spaces, creatures of air and light that must be as incomprehensible to them as they are to us. Sometimes they send messages upward—carefully orchestrated bubble streams that encode their entire civilization in the physics of rising gas—but they have no way of knowing if anything receives them.

In their mythology, if they have mythology, the surface world is heaven—not in the sense of paradise but in the sense of an afterlife, a place their matter goes when it finally escapes the pressure that holds them together. They see the sixgill sharks as angels, primitive and perfect, carrying fragments of the deep upward in their bellies. When one of them finally dissolves beyond recovery, they consider it ascension, though to what, they cannot imagine.

They are the deep's memory of what it was to be earth, before the water came. They are the future's fossil record, still writing itself. They are the only things that have ever truly lived here, and they are the proof that here, life means something entirely different than what we thought we knew.
