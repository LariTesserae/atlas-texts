The Boilermen drift between shifts like sap between seasons. They wear no uniforms—their clothes gradually adopt the district's palette of rust and chlorophyll until they become indistinguishable from the moss-softened machinery. Their hands are permanently stained green-black at the creases, their fingernails outlined in machine oil that no amount of scrubbing can remove. They smell of iron and photosynthesis.

Most are thin, wiry people with a particular economy of movement, having learned to navigate the narrow root-walkways without thought. They communicate in gestures as often as words—a tap on a warm pipe means the pressure is building nicely, a palm flat against bark indicates the tree is drinking too greedily and needs its valves adjusted. Their children learn to read the district's vital signs before they learn to read letters: the pitch of a healthy boiler's whistle, the color of sap when the trees are thriving, the particular drip-pattern that means a gasket-leaf needs replacing.

They live in the symbiotic apartments carved into the upper reaches, where the walls breathe and occasionally fruit. These dwellings were not built but trained—generations ago, someone began coaxing hollow chambers in the growing wood, installing windows that the bark would eventually seal around, running pipes that the tree would incorporate into its vascular system. Now the apartments regulate their own temperature, drawing cool water up through their walls in summer, sealing their pores against winter cold. The residents pay no rent but provide care—pruning where needed, applying poultices of fertilizer and fungicide, monitoring the health of their living homes.

Among them move the Graft-tenders, a specialized subset who understand the delicate art of merging the mechanical with the botanical. They carry leather satchels of hand-forged brackets and grafting tape, bottles of growth hormone and machine oil, the specialized tools needed to encourage a valve stem to fuse with a branch or to train roots to function as pump impellers. They speak to both the trees and the machines as they work, crooning wordless encouragements, scolding gently when a pipe rejects its living gasket or a root refuses to grip a gear. Their apprenticeships last decades.

The children are amphibious in their play, equally at home scrambling through the canopy catwalks or poling makeshift rafts through the canal system. They keep industrial pets—beetles that feed on rust and excrete a protective coating for metal, mice that nest in the warm spaces between boilers and trim excess growth from the gasket-leaves. They learn young which vines are meant to overtake the machinery (to be harvested for fuel) and which are parasitic (to be burned out before they compromise the structure). Their games involve racing through the pneumatic tube system that carries messages between factory floors, or competing to diagnose mechanical problems by sound alone.

At night, the workers gather on the flat rooftop gardens where food crops grow in the steam-enriched air. They share meals of vegetables that taste faintly of iron, fermented drinks made from the industrial kudzu, fish caught in the canals that have adapted to the warm, mineral-rich water. They tell stories of the district's moods—the summer it grew so vigorously that they had to work in shifts around the clock to keep it from crushing its own machinery, the winter when the trees went dormant and they had to feed the boilers with imported coal, black and alien-smelling.

The oldest among them remember their grandparents' stories of when the district was young, when the balance was precarious and collapses were common. They bear the scars of those early days—missing fingers from when they hadn't yet learned to read the trees' hunger, burn marks from before they understood the rhythm of the steam vents. Now they train the next generation in the patient art of industrial symbiosis, teaching them that the district is neither machine nor organism but something more ancient—a breathing entity that has learned to digest the modern world and transform it into something altogether stranger.

They rarely leave the district. Why would they? Everything they need grows here, is processed here, returns to the soil and water and air here. They mark time not in days but in growth rings, in the slow expansion of the industrial forest, in the gradual adaptation of each generation—both human and tree—to the demands of this hybrid ecosystem. They are not workers in the traditional sense but organs in a larger body, as essential to the district's breathing as the steam vents and root pumps and photosynthetic panels.

When they die, they are composted in the warm depths of the factory-trees, their bodies feeding the same system they tended. Their children take up their tools and their apartments, the cycle continues, and the district breathes on through its iron lungs, patient and eternal.
