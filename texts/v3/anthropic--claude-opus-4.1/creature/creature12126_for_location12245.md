The Conservators drift through the Breathing Hollows like memories made flesh—elongated beings whose bodies mirror the city's own architecture. They stand perhaps nine feet when fully extended, though they rarely hold such a rigid pose. Their forms are segmented columns of the same coral-bone material as the towers, wrapped in translucent membranes that flutter with each movement. These membranes serve as both skin and lung, drawing nutrients from the air itself through osmotic exchange.

They have no faces in any recognizable sense. Where a head might be, there is instead a crown of sensing organs—delicate filaments that taste the electromagnetic currents, crystalline nodes that perceive heat and vibration, porous patches that sample chemical gradients. These organs retract and extend constantly, creating an ever-shifting mandala of perception atop their swaying forms.

Their limbs—if the appendages that emerge and retract from their central column can be called limbs—are temporary constructs. When a Conservator needs to manipulate something, it extrudes a specialized appendage from one of its many segments: perhaps a delicate array of cilia for fine work, or a broad, paddle-like surface for moving through the denser atmospheric pockets within the towers. These appendages dissolve back into the body when no longer needed, their material redistributed to where it's required next.

The Conservators move through their city by partially liquefying themselves, flowing through the breathing passages like thick mercury before reconstituting in new chambers. They can spread themselves thin, becoming almost two-dimensional to slip through cracks, or compress into dense, heavy forms to anchor themselves during the city's deeper respiratory cycles. Sometimes several will merge temporarily into a colonial organism to accomplish tasks requiring greater complexity or strength, their consciousness becoming briefly plural before separating again.

They tend the city with infinite patience. A Conservator might spend days encouraging a new tower's growth by secreting specific pheromones into its base, or weeks carefully pruning excess chambers that threaten the harmony of air flow. They cultivate the yellow filament gardens, adjusting their spiral patterns to optimize the electromagnetic harvesting that feeds both city and inhabitants. When the silk-like aerial feeders become trapped in closing passages, the Conservators gently extract them, their touch releasing enzymes that prevent the creatures from dissolving into panic-proteins.

Communication among Conservators occurs through chemical releases and subtle alterations in their bioluminescent emissions. Conversations unfold over hours, with meaning encoded in gradual color shifts and aromatic molecules that drift through the hollow spaces. A single exchange might involve seventeen different participants across multiple towers, each adding their chemical voice to a discussion about growth patterns or resource allocation.

They feed on the city itself, but symbiotically. The Conservators absorb shed cells from the breathing walls, digest the accumulated waste products that would otherwise clog the passages, and process dead filament matter into nutrients that they then excrete back into the city's circulation system. They are both maintenance crew and digestive system, gardeners and recyclers.

During the dim amber days, they are most active in the upper reaches of the towers, where they position themselves to photosynthesize using specialized patches that emerge on their membrane skin. These patches are mobile, migrating across their bodies to track the weak light. At night, when the bioluminescent glow intensifies, they descend to the root chambers to engage in activities that might be reproduction, or might be something else entirely—a sharing of genetic material with the city itself, perhaps, or a deep communion that reinforces the boundaries between self and structure.

Young Conservators—if young is the right word for beings that might live for centuries—begin as small polyps budding from the walls of specialized breeding chambers. They spend their first years immobile, filtering nutrients from the air while their consciousness slowly develops. Eventually, they detach and begin their mobile phase, initially no larger than the aerial feeders they will one day tend. The transition from stationary to mobile life is celebrated, if that word applies, by a city-wide harmonization of breathing rhythms that can last for days.

Elder Conservators gradually become more architectural themselves. Their movements slow, their forms grow more rigid, and they begin to favor specific chambers where they spend increasing amounts of time in a semi-dormant state. Eventually, they merge with the city entirely, their consciousness dispersing into the collective awareness of the Breathing Hollows while their physical form becomes a new room, a fresh passage, another breathing space in the ever-growing organism of their home.

They build nothing in the traditional sense, yet everything here is shaped by their constant attention. The city grows according to patterns they've developed over generations—not blueprints but more like traditions encoded in chemical memory. Each Conservator carries within its cells the template for certain structures: one might specialize in respiratory chambers, another in the delicate capillary networks that distribute nutrients, a third in the reinforced foundations that anchor new growth.

Their relationship with the aerial feeders and filament plants is one of careful cultivation. They adjust the chemical composition of the air in specific chambers to encourage breeding, thin out populations that grow too dense, and sometimes consume individuals that show signs of disease that might spread. They are shepherds of a flock that includes the architecture itself.

In the rare instances when something from beyond the salt fields enters their domain—wind-blown debris, a lost creature from distant lands, or even rarer, a conscious visitor—the Conservators respond with careful curiosity. They will surround the anomaly, tasting its chemical signature, measuring its electromagnetic field, sometimes incorporating useful materials into the city's substance or gently encouraging harmful elements to leave through a combination of pheromone releases and architectural reconfigurations that make staying uncomfortable but not impossible.

The Conservators are not the city's owners or even its residents in any conventional sense. They are its consciousness made discrete, its growth made deliberate, its breathing made purposeful. They exist in the gap between organism and organization, individual and infrastructure, maintaining a living system that is neither natural nor artificial but something entirely its own—a third category of existence thriving in the salt-bleached nowhere of the Breathing Hollows.
