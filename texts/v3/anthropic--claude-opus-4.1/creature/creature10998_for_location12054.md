The inhabitants move through their city like fever moves through a body — in waves, in shivers, in sudden burning clarifications that fade before they can be named.

They are tall when viewed directly, compressed when seen peripherally. Their bodies maintain a consistency somewhere between flesh and mineral deposit, skin that forms in layers like mica, translucent at the edges where new growth accumulates. They sweat constantly, but what emerges from their pores crystallizes immediately into salt-like granules that they brush from themselves in ritual motions, leaving trails of mineral dust that the damp air quickly claims.

Their faces are architectural. Features that shift depending on the angle of observation — a profile might show three eyes, the front view only one, located where the bridge of a nose should be. Their mouths open along different axes depending on whether they're eating, speaking, or breathing. Some have developed additional mouths along their throats or temples, specialized for different frequencies of communication. They speak in harmonics that require the city's peculiar acoustics to resolve into language.

They wear clothing woven from the fiber of the spiral grasses, garments that grow while worn, that must be regularly trimmed like living things. The fabric drinks moisture from the air and releases it as a cooling vapor against their skin. Fashion follows the logic of dreams — a prestigious cut might be a coat that exists only on Tuesdays, or a dress that everyone can see but the wearer cannot. They decorate themselves with jewelry carved from compressed time, ornaments that age backward, becoming more valuable as they approach their unmade state.

Their children are not born but precipitate. Pregnancy is a gradual crystallization process that occurs in specialized chambers within their homes, where two or more parents contribute minerals from their own bodies to a slowly-forming lattice. The process takes exactly π years — an irrational duration that means no two children ever fully complete their gestation. They emerge slightly unfinished, spending their first decades completing themselves, choosing their final geometries from a catalog of potential forms inherited from their parents' architectural plans.

They eat the city's plants, but also concepts. The factories process absence into presence, and the inhabitants consume both — negative bread that removes hunger rather than sating it, water that remembers being wine, meat that was never an animal but understands the idea of having been one. Their digestive systems operate partially in adjacent dimensions, processing nutrients that exist in probability rather than actuality.

Work is liturgical. They maintain the city's impossible infrastructure through ritual repetition of tasks that may or may not have practical effects. A plumber might spend weeks adjusting pipes that carry metaphor rather than water. An electrician rewires buildings to run on stored darkness rather than generated light. They are paid in currency that accrues meaning rather than value — a single coin might purchase nothing or everything, depending on how long it has been contemplated.

They do not sleep in the way sleep is commonly understood. Instead, they enter phases of architectural integration, their bodies becoming temporarily part of their buildings' structure. During these periods, their consciousness diffuses through the walls and floors, experiencing existence as a room rather than an occupant. They dream the city's dreams, which manifest as minor alterations to physical laws within their living spaces. A family might wake to find gravity has developed a slight spiral, or that causality now runs left-to-right instead of forward-to-back.

Their social structures follow emotional thermodynamics. Relationships generate heat that must be dissipated through the city's cooling systems — great radiator networks that run through public spaces, glowing with the excess warmth of love, anger, grief. Marriages are registered with the Department of Molecular Bonds. Divorces are achieved through controlled phase transitions, former partners sublimating directly from solid to gas, bypassing the liquid state of mere separation.

They reproduce memories rather than recording them. Each inhabitant cultivates a garden of past moments that grow like their geometric cacti, developing in directions that weren't part of the original experience. Shared memories cross-pollinate, creating hybrid remembrances that might be more true than what actually occurred. The elderly tend vast greenhouses of accumulated experience, their memories growing so dense they begin to affect local space-time.

Disease, when it occurs, manifests as architectural dysfunction. A sick inhabitant might develop rooms inside their body that shouldn't exist, or find their internal geometry no longer properly connects. Treatment involves careful renovation rather than medicine — surgeons who are equal parts doctor and architect, working with blueprints of the patient's original construction to restore proper spatial relationships.

They worship through municipal services. Prayer is the act of properly sorting recycling into bins that exist in seven dimensions. Communion occurs at the public water fountains that dispense something almost but not quite like water. Their holy texts are the city planning documents that describe streets that haven't been built yet, that possibly cannot be built, but which exist nonetheless in the space between intention and implementation.

Death is a change of phase. Bodies sublime slowly, their minerals returning to the salt pan while their geometries persist as architectural afterimages — ghost buildings that occupy the same space as real ones, visible only in the corner of the eye or in the reflection of water that isn't quite water. The dead continue participating in city life, voting in elections through patterns of condensation on window glass, attending family dinners as empty chairs that weigh too much.

They love as minerals love — through slow accretion, through pressure, through the patient transformation of one substance into another. Their emotions precipitate out of supersaturated air, forming crystal gardens in their homes that must be tended carefully lest they grow beyond the bounds of livable space. Joy accumulates in corners like salt. Sorrow drips from ceilings in perpetual stalactites that sing in frequencies only they can hear.

Their art defies preservation. Paintings that exist only while being actively viewed. Sculptures that maintain different forms for each observer. Music that plays backward into the instruments that created it. They have developed entire schools of expression based on controlled architectural collapse, on the beauty of buildings that fall upward, on the poetry of structures that exist in several tenses simultaneously.

The young learn by absorption, attending schools that are as much atmosphere as institution. Education seeps through their permeable skin, knowledge accumulating in layers like their mineral flesh. They graduate when they have developed enough internal architecture to contain a profession — doctors with operating theaters growing in their chest cavities, teachers whose voices develop additional harmonics for conveying meaning, engineers who can perceive the city's impossible geometries without madness.

They commune with their sparse plants through chemical exchange, breathing the specific mixtures of mineral vapor that each species requires for conversation. The spiral grasses speak of time as a direction rather than a dimension. The wet-leather succulents remember when the salt pan was an ocean, though it never was. The weeping geometric cacti teach patience in a language of calculated precipitation.

This is how they are: inhabitants who exist because their city requires it, who have evolved not from biological pressures but from architectural necessity. They are the only possible people for an impossible place, their bodies and minds shaped by the requirements of living where physics has agreed to local amendments, where truth is negotiable, where the only constant is the slow accumulation of existence in defiance of logic.

They belong here in the way that salt belongs to the sea — not because they chose it, but because they crystallized from its conditions, precipitated from its needs, formed atom by atom from the fever dream of a reality teaching itself new ways to be inhabited.
