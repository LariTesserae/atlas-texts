The Cultivators wear the city's moisture like a second skin. Their coveralls—waxed canvas in browns and grays—never fully dry, perpetually darkened at the shoulders and cuffs where the condensation soaks through first. They move through their greenhouse halls with practiced efficiency, bodies adapted to the peculiar hunched-but-fluid gait required to navigate between the dense growing racks while ducking beneath the low-hanging pipe networks that feed the hydroponic systems.

Their hands are the most telling feature—permanently stained green-black under the nails from the industrial growing medium, crosshatched with tiny scars from wire training systems, swollen at the joints from the constant damp. They wear no gloves; they need to feel the subtle differences in stem tension, the temperature of leaves, the moisture content of the growing substrate. Their fingertips have developed an almost supernatural sensitivity to plant health, reading chemical signals through touch that others might need instruments to detect.

The Pipe Runners live between the walls. Children mostly, though some never grow out of it—small-bodied adults who've found their niche in the three-foot gaps between buildings where the major steam conduits run. They know every valve, every junction, every place where the metal has worn thin and sings a different note when tapped. They communicate in a percussion language along the pipes themselves, messages hammered in code that travels faster than any pneumatic tube. Their clothing is minimal despite the damp—tight-fitted leather and rubber that won't snag, bodies greased to slide through spaces where pipes nearly touch. They've developed a peculiar grace, able to spider along the hot metal using palms and soles that have built up calluses thick as boot leather.

In the machine halls work the Millwrights—massive humans who've grown to match their environment. Generations of families who've worked the same facilities, their bodies evolved to the work: broad shoulders for bearing loads, thick fingered hands that can grip slick metal, legs like pillars that can stand for entire shifts on vibrating platforms. They breathe the steam-mixed-with-machine-oil air as if it were clean mountain wind. Their guild marks are burned into their forearms—not tattoos but actual brands made with heated brass stamps, the raised scars forming geometric patterns that identify their specialization, their lineage, their terrace level.

The Brass Wardens patrol the catwalks in groups of three, their uniforms a deliberate anachronism—high-collared coats with tarnished buttons, peaked caps that shed water, boots with metal plates that ring against the grated walkways. They carry no weapons except weighted measuring chains worn like bandoliers, tools that double as restraints when needed. Their real power is knowledge: they know every resident by their gait, every machine by its sound, every violation of the city's byzantine industrial codes by instinct. They age into the job more than train for it, senior Wardens growing so attuned to their territories that they can sense a pressure valve failing three terraces away by the change in the steam's whisper.

The Vertical Farmers live lives of calculated migration, following the growing seasons up and down the mountain's terraces. In the lower levels during the cold months, working the root vegetables and fungi in the deep warm halls. Rising with the year to tend the fruit trees espaliered against the factory walls, the berry vines that produce in the middle terraces' greenhouses. At the peak of summer, they work the highest levels where medicinal herbs grow in the thin air. Their children are born into this rhythm, educated in mobile schools that rise and fall with the families, learning botany and hydraulics and the careful chemistry of industrial agriculture.

In the depths, where the water treatment facilities thunder, live the Processors—pale from the lack of even diffused sunlight, their eyes adapted to the sodium-vapor glow of the processing halls. They speak in shouts even when the machinery quiets, their hearing half-gone from the constant roar of the pumps. Their knowledge is chemical and crucial: they can taste the runoff and know which terrace has over-fertilized, smell a contamination before any instrument detects it. They live in sealed quarters built right into the facility walls, never fully escaping the vibration of the great pumps.

The Pneumatic Messengers shoot through the city in human-sized tubes, their bodies trained from youth to handle the acceleration, the sudden pressure changes, the disorientation of being fired through darkness at tremendous speed. They wear padded suits that lock into the capsules, turning them into human packages. Only they know the true map of the city—not the visible one of terraces and factories, but the hidden one of tubes and shunts and switching stations that honeycomb the mountain. Some say they've evolved different inner ears, better equilibrium. They retire young, bones compressed, joints worn out, but wealthy from hazard pay.

The Steam Readers occupy the highest positions, literally and figuratively. They live in observatories built at the peaks of the tallest towers, watching the patterns of vapor that rise from the city. In the way the steam moves, they read the health of the entire system—blockages in the pipes, overheating in the factories, illness in the greenhouses, unrest in the workers' quarters. Their art is part meteorology, part divination, part systems engineering. They wear robes that are always dry, somehow, as if the moisture that permeates everything else cannot touch them. They rarely descend, communicating their readings through speaking tubes that amplify their voices into the wind.

The city's children grow up amphibious, learning to move through water and air with equal ease. They play in the runoff channels, build dams and diversions that the Brass Wardens tolerantly destroy. Their toys are miniature turbines they carve from scraps, set to spinning in the steam vents. They learn to read the city's moods by its sounds—knowing when to hide from a factory's anger, when to follow the promise of a greenhouse harvest, when to run the catwalks in the brief moments between tram passages.

And in every terrace, in every hall, moving between all these specialists, are the Oilers—perhaps the most essential and most ignored. They tend the bearings, the pistons, the endless mechanical joints that keep the city breathing. Each carries their own formula of lubricant, mixed with herbs from the greenhouses, minerals from the mountain, secrets passed down through generations. They move like ghosts through the machinery, present everywhere, noticed nowhere, their work only visible when it fails to happen—when a bearing screams, when a piston seizes, when the great mechanical metabolism stutters and coughs.

These are the lives that have grown to fit this vertical city of steam and steel, humans shaped by their environment as surely as the trained vines that curtain the pipes, adapted to thrive in the eternal dampness, the mechanical breathing, the constant motion of a city that has consumed its mountain and transformed it into something alive in an entirely different way.
