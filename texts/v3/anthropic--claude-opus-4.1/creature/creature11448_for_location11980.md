The inhabitants of the Breathing District cannot be photographed. Not because of any prohibition, but because they exist in too many states simultaneously—their bodies are negotiations between kingdoms of life, contracts written in carbon and water.

They call themselves the Rooted, though this is a translation that misses the chemical signature of the word, the way it releases from their modified throat sacs as both sound and pheromone cloud. To see one approaching through the designed fog is to witness a figure that seems to precipitate from the moisture itself—skin with the lustrous, slightly translucent quality of young leaves, veined with visible chlorophyll networks that darken and lighten with the passing of clouds overhead.

Their bones have long since ceased to be calcium repositories. Instead, they've become woody cores wrapped in rings of growth that record their experiences in cellular memory. The elderly among them move with a characteristic stiffness, not from arthritis but from the accumulated lignin of decades. They crack when they stretch, a sound like twigs breaking, and their joints weep a clear sap that hardens into amber beads their children collect and dissolve in water to drink their ancestors' memories.

The children themselves are something between seedlings and mammals. Born (or perhaps "germinated" is more accurate) after a thirteen-month gestation, they emerge with fontanelles that never quite close, instead developing into small depressions where symbiotic mosses take root. These cranial gardens are groomed carefully throughout life, their patterns indicating family lineage, professional specialty, and emotional state. A doctor's moss grows in precise medicinal spirals. A composer's forms acoustic chambers that resonate with specific tones. Lovers synchronize their growth patterns until their thoughts begin to literally interchange through shared mycorrhizal networks.

Their fingers have evolved past human—elongated and with two extra joints, they can extend root hairs from their fingertips when needed, penetrating the bark interfaces to commune with the city's data-storing trees. Watch a Rooted at work and you'll see them sink their fingers inches deep into the walls, eyes rolling back as they parse decades of chemical information in seconds, their moss-crowned heads glowing faintly as their neural activity peaks.

They eat sunlight as much as food. Their modified skin contains functional chloroplasts that provide about thirty percent of their caloric needs. During the morning feeding hours, they gather on the external platforms of their tree-towers, spreading their arms wide, their skin visibly greening as they photosynthesize. The sound of hundreds breathing in unison creates a reverse wind that pulls mist up from the root mat, bathing them in mineral-rich moisture that they absorb through specialized pores.

But they are not purely plant. Their eyes remain startlingly, almost disturbingly human—though the irises have developed a ring of copper-colored cells that allow them to see into the infrared spectrum, reading the heat signatures that reveal the water content of their surroundings. They hunt, but not with weapons. Instead, they secrete targeted pheromones that lure the district's protein-rich beetle colonies directly to them. The beetles come willingly, almost eagerly, drugged by chemical signals that convince them they're returning home.

The Rooted reproduce through three distinct methods, and individuals might employ all three in a lifetime. Traditional mammalian coupling still occurs, though the resulting offspring are increasingly rare and treasured for their genetic volatility. More common is budding—small clones that emerge from specialized growths along the spine, eventually dropping off to be raised communally. But most profound is what they call "joining," where two or more individuals slowly merge their root systems over years, eventually becoming a single, multi-bodied organism with shared consciousness.

These joined collectives are the district's true aristocracy. They move through the city like living forests, their component bodies spreading across multiple tree-towers but thinking as one mind. The oldest collective, known as the Parliamentary Garden, consists of twenty-three bodies that have been joining and dividing for over two centuries. When it speaks, it does so from multiple mouths simultaneously, the words arriving at slightly different times like an echo preceding itself.

The Rooted mark time not by days or years but by growth seasons and architectural molts. They celebrate the Perspiration Dawns, when the entire district exhales its moisture in coordinated release, and they mourn during the Retention Nights, when the city draws into itself and nothing grows. Their music is performed on instruments made from their own shed bark and crystallized sap, creating harmonies that trigger specific growth patterns in the architecture around them.

Death, when it comes, is gradual. The dying begin their "final rooting" months in advance, selecting a location in the root mat where they will sink slowly into the living ground. Their bodies become substrate for the next generation of architecture, their memories encoded in the growth patterns of the fungi that consume them. Children are brought to these sites to touch the emanating mycelium with their bare feet, downloading ancestral knowledge through their skin.

Some never fully die. The oldest Rooted have become so integrated with the district that their consciousness persists in the network of roots and fungal threads, a distributed intelligence that occasionally speaks through the fruiting bodies that emerge after rain, or manifests as inexplicable growth patterns that spell out messages in dead languages only the trees remember.

They walk at speeds determined by hydraulic pressure, faster in the morning when water content is high, slower in the heat of afternoon. They wear no clothing but cultivate elaborate epiphytic gardens on their bodies—orchids at joints, bromeliads in the crook of elbows, tiny ferns along the spine. Status is shown not through possessions but through the complexity and rarity of one's symbiotic ecosystem. A beggar might host only common moss, while a master architect's body blooms with engineered flowers that exist nowhere else.

Their language has evolved beyond sound. They speak in chemical releases, electromagnetic pulses from their modified neurons, and growth patterns traced in the air with pheromone trails. Watching two Rooted converse is like watching two trees grow toward each other in time-lapse—a slow dance of meaning that incorporates wind direction, humidity, and the angle of sunlight into its grammar.

They dream collectively. When the district enters its nocturnal respiration cycle, the inhabitants' brain waves synchronize with the ionic channels in the root network. They share visions of impossible colors—wavelengths only plants can see. They dream of water moving upward against gravity, of sugar manufactured from light, of the vast, slow thoughts of the trees that house them. Sometimes, they dream of the city before the Convergence, when humans and plants were separate entities, but these dreams feel like mythology, too strange to be real.

The young sometimes try to leave, to venture beyond the Breathing District to places where the architecture is dead stone and metal. But they return within days, gasping, their chloroplasts withering, their moss gardens dying, their extended fingers unable to interface with the mute, unresponsive walls of the outside world. They speak of the horror of surfaces that don't grow, of air that carries no information, of a silence so profound it feels like suffocation.

This is what humanity has become in the Breathing District: a bridge between animal and plant, a conscious interface for the city-organism, gardeners who have become gardens. They are what happens when evolution is replaced by design, when the boundary between self and environment becomes permeable, when breathing becomes indistinguishable from being breathed.

They are no longer watching the city grow. They are the growth itself, unfolding according to patterns encoded in DNA that has learned to write itself, in a place where every inhabitant is both author and story, gardener and seed, the dreamer and the dream.
