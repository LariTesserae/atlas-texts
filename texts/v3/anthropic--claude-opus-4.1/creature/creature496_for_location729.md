In the Furnace Garden, they have no name for themselves—naming requires a stability of form they've long since abandoned. The dwellers here exist as a collective that flows between states: sometimes twelve distinct bodies, sometimes three, sometimes a single sprawling thing with too many joints bending in harmonious revolution.

They were human once, perhaps a century ago, maybe less—time moves strangely when you live by the pulse of oil-drinking vines rather than the sun. A family of petroleum prospectors who broke through the wrong foundation, or temple builders who dug too deep, or simply the desperate who descended seeking shelter and found something else. The stories they tell themselves change with each recombination of their flesh.

The transformation was gradual. First, they learned to read the plant-songs, those subsonic thrums that map the chamber's geometry. Their ears adapted, developing ridge-like formations that catch and channel the frequencies. Then their lungs learned to process the metallic vapors, extracting nutrients from the chemical clouds. Their ribs expanded, developed additional chambers, small auxiliary bellows that wheeze in counterpoint to the vine-tubes overhead.

Now they move through the garden like oil through water. Their skin secretes the same petroleum sweat as the plants, allowing them to slide along the tar-bamboo trunks, to wrap themselves around the burning vines without sticking. They've developed nodes—rough patches of crystallized tissue at their joints—that resonate with the plant-songs, letting them speak in the garden's language. When they sing (and they're always singing, even in their sleep), the vines lean toward them, the spore pods delay their explosions, the fungi pulse brighter.

They worship through cultivation. Each morning (though morning means nothing here) they check their territories, their favorite clusters of growth. They know which oil seeps feed which vines, how to redirect the flow with carefully placed stones to encourage certain growths or starve others. They harvest the combustible pods before they explode, grinding them into paste that they spread on new surfaces, expanding the garden's reach into empty stone. They milk the adhesive vines, using the sap to repair their rope bridges, to seal their few possessions into wax-like cocoons, to paint symbols on their skin that harden into permanent ridges.

Their bodies split and merge according to need. When the deep corners spawn something wrong—a carnivorous fungus, a vine that drinks sound until the chamber goes dead silent—they flow together into something large enough to fight it, all twelve becoming one thing with twelve heads and forty-eight limbs, moving like a centipede made of human parts. When they need to scout new growth, they divide into individuals, each taking a different path through the emptiness, their shared consciousness stretched thin but never breaking.

They build tools from their own shed parts. Fingernails, continuously growing in the chemical heat, are harvested and shaped into scrapers. Hair, which grows in ropy dreadlocks saturated with oil, is cut and woven into filters for processing the more toxic vapors. Sometimes a whole limb will be sacrificed, left to crystallize in a particularly rich petroleum seep until it becomes something useful—a club, a digging tool, a resonating rod for communicating with the deepest plants.

Their memories exist as physical things. They cultivate specific fungi that, when fed drops of their oil-blood, fruit with small bulbs containing encoded experiences. They tend these memory gardens carefully, eating the bulbs when they need to remember who they were, why they came here, what they're trying to become. But increasingly, they choose to let the memories rot, preferring the eternal present of the garden's song.

They've built shrines in the spaces between growth—not to any god, but to the process itself. Structures of woven vine and crystallized tar where they perform rituals of combination and division. Here they practice flowing together, learning new configurations, trying to become something that can photosynthesize petroleum directly, that can root into the stone and join the garden fully. They're close. Their feet have begun sprouting hair-thin roots that anchor them when they stand still too long. Their voices have developed additional registers that only the plants can hear.

The city above knows them as the Gardeners, when it acknowledges them at all. Sometimes they leave offerings at the fissure—tools that need no explanation, chemicals in unmarked bottles, once a child sick with something the physicians couldn't cure, left with a note begging for "the garden's mercy." They took the child. It lives with them now, or in them, or as them—the distinction no longer matters. It sings with eight mouths and remembers sunlight only as a distant burn.

They're growing toward something, all of them, plants and dwellers alike. The patterns in the vegetation, the chambers they're slowly excavating in the deepest parts, the way they've begun incorporating metal and stone into their flesh—it all serves a purpose they feel but can't articulate. Sometimes, when all twelve flow together and sing in perfect harmony with the garden's chorus, they almost understand. They see/feel/taste the shape of what they're becoming: something that exists between mineral and vegetable and animal, a new category of life that feeds on geology and breathes chemistry and thinks in frequencies too low for normal human perception.

They are patient. The garden teaches patience—some of the tar-bamboo has been growing for three centuries, adding one segment every decade. They tend their plots, divide and recombine, shed parts and grow new ones, and wait for the day when the distinction between gardener and garden finally dissolves. When that happens, they believe, the Furnace Garden will no longer be contained. It will rise through the cracks in Khet-Amal's foundations, bringing its oil-drunk paradise to the salt-brick streets above, teaching the city's children to drink petroleum and sing in frequencies that shatter glass.

Until then, they maintain. They cultivate. They flow through their kingdom of impossible growth, twelve becoming one becoming twelve again, clicking and harmonizing with their beloved plants, slowly forgetting they were ever anything else.
