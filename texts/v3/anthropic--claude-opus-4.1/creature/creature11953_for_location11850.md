The Gardeners move through their vertical city like lymph through a vast green body, and you might watch one for hours before realizing it isn't simply another cluster of vines shifting in the thermal currents.

They are utterly hairless, their skin the mottled gray-green of lichen, with a texture that shifts between smooth and minutely scaled depending on the humidity. Each Gardener stands perhaps four feet tall when fully extended, but they rarely hold such a pose. Their bodies compress and elongate as needed—ribs that telescope, a spine of cartilaginous segments that can curve in any direction. When one needs to traverse the hollow grass-pillars, it simply pours itself inside, moving up through the tubes like thick sap.

Their faces, if you can call them faces, are mostly eyes—six of them, arranged in pairs at different heights, each pair adapted to a different light spectrum. The topmost see into ultraviolet, reading the health of plants by their hidden emanations. The middle pair perceive the visible spectrum but with incredible sensitivity to moisture gradients. The bottom pair see heat, letting them track the metabolic fires burning in every stem and leaf. They have no visible nose or ears, but their entire skin is a sensing organ, reading chemical signals from the plants, vibrations through the root networks, changes in air pressure that forecast the weather days in advance.

Instead of a mouth, each Gardener has a ventral slit running from what might be called their throat down to their midsection. This opens to reveal not teeth but hundreds of small, mobile tendrils, each tipped with a different enzyme. They don't eat—not in any sense we'd recognize. Instead, they interface directly with the plants, their tendrils entering specialized pores that the vegetation has evolved just for this purpose. It's not parasitism; it's mutualism so complete that neither could exist without the other. The Gardeners provide something—hormones, perhaps, or some essential mineral their bodies synthesize—and in return, the plants feed them through their skin, nutrients passing directly through that lichen-textured membrane.

Their hands are their most disturbing feature: too many fingers (the number seems to vary between seven and twelve), each finger branching into smaller fingerlets, and those into cilia-like projections. Watching a Gardener work is hypnotic—those fractal hands dancing over plant surfaces, pruning with microscopic precision, adjusting the chemical balance of sap with a touch, coaxing new growth in directions that maintain the city's architecture. They communicate with each other not through sound but through pheromone clouds that they exude from pores along their arms. In the thick, humid air of the plant-city, these chemical messages can hang for hours, creating conversation threads that multiple Gardeners can contribute to over time.

They organize themselves in groups of twelve, each group tending to a specific level of the city. But there's no hierarchy that an outsider could detect—decisions seem to emerge from the collective like fruiting bodies from mycelium. When major work needs to be done (guiding the city's response to a storm, for instance), groups merge into larger collectives, their bodies actually fusing temporarily at the wrists and ankles, creating living chains that can span entire districts of the plant-city, synchronizing the vegetation's response with perfect coordination.

At night, they don't sleep but enter a kind of stasis, pressing themselves against the ice waterfall. The cold slows their metabolism to nearly nothing, and in this state, they can survive for months if necessary. But more importantly, this is when they dream—or whatever their equivalent might be. Their eyes cloud over with a milky film, and beneath their skin, you can see things moving, branching patterns of bioluminescence that mirror the root systems hidden in the ice. They're downloading something, or uploading, or simply communing with whatever intelligence lives in that frozen water.

The young—if they have young, no one has ever seen them reproduce—must exist, but perhaps not in any form we'd recognize as offspring. Sometimes a Gardener will spend days crafting something from plant matter, a small homunculus of woven grass and compressed moss. They'll breathe on it for hours, those enzyme-tipped tendrils working microscopic changes into its surface. Then they'll plant it like a seed in the densest parts of the moss floors. Months later, something that might be a new Gardener will emerge, but it's already ancient, already knowing everything it needs to know, its movements perfectly synchronized with all the others from its first moment of mobility.

They sing, after a fashion—not with voices but with their whole bodies, vibrating at frequencies that resonate through the plant matter. It's below human hearing, but you feel it in your bones when you stand in their city. The plants respond, growing faster or slower, flowering or seeding according to these subsonic instructions. During the spring melt, when water runs heaviest and the ice waterfall groans with the strain, all the Gardeners will gather at its base and vibrate in unison, a frequency that somehow strengthens the ice, prevents catastrophic collapse.

They never leave the vertical city. Why would they? They are the city, as much as the plants are, as much as the ice is. Together, they form a single superorganism, a living tower that breathes and thinks and dreams its slow, cold dreams in the eternal twilight between the peaks.
