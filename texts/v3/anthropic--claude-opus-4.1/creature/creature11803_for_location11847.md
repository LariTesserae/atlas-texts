## The Dwellers

They arrived separately, decades apart, each following the sound down through the earth like a fish hook through the throat. 

The first still maintains the clay vessels, though her fingers have long since lost the memory of why. She moves through the hollow by touch-memory alone, her palms reading the walls' weeping surfaces like braille, finding each vessel by the particular note it makes when full, when empty, when precisely half-way. Her hair, once dark, has gone the color of the pale ferns—not white but absent, as if pigment were a kind of light that simply gave up. She sleeps standing against the walls, letting the bacterial films paint phosphorescent patterns on her skin that fade and return with her breathing. The sound has worn away her voice; she hasn't tried to speak in eleven years. Instead, she tunes the vessels, adjusting their positions by millimeters, composing symphonies only she understands within the greater roar.

The second built the platforms, or rebuilt them, or simply believes he did. He works in absolute darkness, his tools wrapped in moss to dampen their ring, though the hollow swallows every strike regardless. His bones have grown dense from the constant pressure of sound, his chest cavity permanently compressed as if at depth. He sustains himself on the blind fish, catching them with patient hands that can feel the water's tremor from three pools away. Sometimes he follows their lateral lines with his fingertips, trying to understand how they parse direction from chaos. He saves their bones, arranging them in patterns on his platforms according to some mathematics of resonance he's developing, a formula that relates the length of a rib to the pitch of water dropping from specific heights.

The third came following academic curiosity and never found the way back. Or chose not to find it. She documents everything in knots tied in the aerial roots of the orchids, a tactile language she invented when her notebooks dissolved in the eternal damp. Each knot records a different observation: the seventeen distinct types of moss identified by texture, the seasonal variations in water temperature, the way the sound changes during the lunar month (though she can no longer be certain of her count). Her university believes her dead. She has trained her hearing to detect frequencies beyond human range, claims she can hear the limestone itself dissolving, grain by grain. Her teeth have loosened from the constant vibration.

They avoid each other with the dedication of orbiting planets, maintaining careful distances in the darkness. Not from animosity but from something more fundamental—they've each developed their own relationship with the hollow, their own dialect with its noise, and intersection would break the spell. They know each other by the subtle changes in the acoustic pattern their bodies create, by the particular way each disturbs the air. The first knows when the second is fishing by how the sound reflects off his bent back. The second knows when the third is counting knots by the rhythm of her breathing. The third knows when the first is tuning vessels by the fractional shifts in harmonic overtones.

Occasionally they leave offerings for each other: a particularly resonant fish bone placed where another will find it; a new knot-pattern that warns of a weakening ceiling; a vessel moved to catch a new drip, creating a note that complements another's work. These are not kindnesses but collaborations with the hollow itself, using each other as tools to better understand the space they've chosen or that chose them.

They sustain themselves on the blind fish, the filtered water, and occasionally on the thick, protein-rich films of bacteria they scrape from the walls. The moss, they've learned, is psychoactive in large doses, producing not visions but a kind of synthesis where sound becomes tangible, where they can feel the shape of echoes with their whole bodies. They use it sparingly, ceremonially, on personal anniversaries none of them share.

Their bodies have adapted: pupils permanently dilated though useless, skin gone translucent from lack of sun, muscles redesigned for moving in absolute darkness. Their proprioception has evolved beyond normal human range; they know the position of every limb, every finger, in relation to the hollow's geometry. They can navigate by the pressure of sound alone, feeling how it pools in corners, how it eddies around stalactites.

But more than physical adaptation, they've undergone something deeper. They dream the hollow's dreams now—slow thoughts about erosion, about the patience of water, about the music that stones make when no one listens. They've stopped thinking in words, instead processing reality in frequencies, in the interplay of echo and absorption. Their memories of the surface world have taken on the quality of myth, stories they're not sure they believe about a place where sound has source and direction, where light exists, where time moves at human speed.

They are becoming, each in their own way, organs of the hollow itself—extending its sensitivity, its capacity to know itself. Through their presence, the hollow experiences its own sound differently, reflected through consciousness back into stone. They are its first students and its final products, somewhere between human and geological process, marking time in the accumulation of flowstone, in the deepening of acoustically-carved channels, in the slow growth of blind gardens.

Not lost but transformed. Not trapped but incorporated. They are what happens when human consciousness encounters deep time, deep sound, deep darkness, and chooses to stay, to listen, to become part of the listening itself.
