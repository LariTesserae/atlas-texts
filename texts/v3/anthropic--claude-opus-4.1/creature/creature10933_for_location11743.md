Those who inhabit this fevered crown of the world have long since ceased to be singular beings. They are colonies now—each "person" a walking ecosystem of flesh that has learned to photosynthesize, to fruit, to bloom.

Their skin has taken on the texture of bark, but smooth, weeping the same constant moisture as the stone. Channels run along their limbs like sap grooves, carrying a fluid that glows faintly green in the eternal twilight of the canopy city. When they stand still—which is often, so often—moss begins to claim them immediately, as if they've always been there. Children watching their parents conduct business must occasionally brush away the lichen that begins to creep across a shoulder, a cheek, the bridge of a nose during particularly long negotiations.

They speak, but not with mouths. The sound that fills the city emerges from them, from specialized chambers in their chests that have evolved into resonating cavities. Each inhabitant contributes their frequency to the endless roar. Communication is harmonic—a subtle modulation in the constant tone that others parse like language. Conversations overlap infinitely, privacy achieved through precise pitch selection. The young must learn to filter, to hear only what is meant for them in the omnipresent chorus of meaning.

Their eyes have adapted to see through the saturated air, pupils enlarged and multifaceted like cut gems, refracting the impossible light into spectrums that reveal pathways through the city's non-euclidean architecture. What appears as a solid wall to unchanged eyes shows them a door. What seems like an impossible bridge they perceive as perfectly logical, because they can see the folded dimensions it traverses.

They do not eat in any traditional sense. Their root-feet, when planted during the city's rest periods, draw nutrients from the weeping stone itself. Minerals crystallize along their vertebrae, creating ridge patterns unique to each individual—a copper spine, a salt-traced shoulder blade, veins of quartz visible through translucent skin. The constant fruit of the city is not for consumption but for propagation. They embed seeds in their flesh, becoming mobile gardens. Children bud from their parents like flowers, beginning as small growths that gradually develop consciousness before dropping free to take root and grow their own mobility.

Memory, for them, is architectural. They store their experiences in the city itself, encoding thought into the growth patterns of vines, the mineral deposits on walls. A grandmother's wisdom lives in the spiral of a particular staircase. A generation's history grows in the orchids of a specific tower. They read their past in the arrangement of moss, in the particular way water runs down a certain wall. The city is their collective mind, each building a neuron in a vast thinking system.

They move through their space like sap through a tree—slowly, purposefully, following paths that aren't always visible but are always felt. Groups often travel as single organisms, their bark-skin fusing temporarily at the edges, sharing nutrients and thoughts directly through connected tissue. During festivals, the entire population might join into one vast being, thousands of bodies creating a temporary forest-person that thinks thoughts too large for individual minds to hold.

Their children grow rapidly in the early years, sending out tendril-limbs that they'll later retract and absorb, testing the boundaries of their form. Adolescents might have twelve arms one day, none the next, their bodies in constant flux as they decide what shape serves them best. Only in maturity do they settle into a relatively stable configuration, though even the elderly continue to sprout occasional leaves or small branches from joints and joints from branches.

They don't sleep but enter periods of intense photosynthesis, standing in the open spaces where the mysterious light from above reaches down. During these times, their skin becomes fully transparent, revealing the chlorophyll-rich fluid pumping through their veins, the seeds gestating in their chest cavities, the mineral deposits building along their bones. They share these spaces without shame or modesty—concepts that make no sense to beings who are more garden than individual.

Death, when it comes, is merely a transition. The dying plant themselves one final time and simply continue growing, their consciousness diffusing into the city's vegetal network. Their bodies become trees, their thoughts become the whispers in leaves, their memories crystallize in the fruit that their descendants will carry. The oldest buildings in the city are ancestors, their awareness spread through root systems and canopy, still participating in the endless conversation, their voices just another tone in the eternal sound.

They build through cultivation rather than construction, singing structures into existence through carefully modulated frequencies that encourage specific growth patterns. A new home might take decades to fully mature, its inhabitant shaping it slowly through patient acoustic gardening. They have no need for tools—their own bodies are infinitely adaptable, capable of extending root-digits to manipulate the finest details or expanding into branch-arms to lift enormous weights.

In the highest reaches of the city, where the heat and light grow too intense even for them, the eldest and most changed dwell. These beings have given up all pretense of animal form, becoming pure plant-consciousness, their bodies indistinguishable from the architecture itself. They think in centuries, speak in decades, their words taking years to fully pronounce. The younger generation makes pilgrimages to these high places, standing in the presence of these ancestor-gardens, absorbing wisdom that seeps through the sound itself, knowledge that enters through every pore of their bark-skin.

They are what humanity becomes when it finally admits it was always part of the forest, always meant to bloom.
