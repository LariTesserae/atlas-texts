# The Dwellers of the Singing Canopy

## The Chorus Weavers

They move through your peripheral vision first—not quite bipedal, not quite otherwise. The Chorus Weavers stand seven feet when fully extended, but they rarely hold such a pose. Their bodies are built for the eternal vertical, with four primary limbs that end in hands capable of impossible articulation. Each digit splits twice, creating fractals of grip that can find purchase on wet bark or thread through their own woven bridges with equal grace.

Their skin shifts between states—sometimes taut membrane for gliding, sometimes furred for warmth, sometimes something between scales and bark that makes them invisible against the trunk surfaces. They communicate through the hollow trees themselves, pressing specialized throat sacs against the breathing holes to add their voices to the mountain wind's song. A conversation between two Weavers might take place across kilometers, their words becoming part of the eternal symphony, understood only by those who know which harmonics to follow.

They build, constantly, but not as humans build. They weave living vines into geometric patterns that guide growth, creating spiral staircases that won't be complete for three generations. Their cities are vertical territories claimed within the hollow trunks—chambers carved by careful cultivation of boring insects, connected by trained vine bridges that respond to whistled commands. Some families have tended the same trunk-city for a thousand years, their ancestors' bones calcified into the walls, becoming part of the structure.

The Weavers see in thermal spectra and echolocation, their large eyes clouded like moonstones but perceiving the heat traces of climbing colonies, the sonic maps of empty spaces. They know every creature in their territory by its warmth signature, every pathway by its echo. Their children learn to glide before they walk, thrown gently from platforms into the void to discover their membrane-wings, caught by patient parents who remember their own first flights through the singing dark.

## The Symbiont Colonies

But the Weavers are not alone, cannot be alone. Living within their skin, in specialized pockets that line their shoulders and run down their spines, dwell the Luminal Mites—creatures no larger than rice grains that glow with the same pale green as the falling spores. The relationship is ancient beyond memory. The mites process toxins from the foods the Weavers eat, while the Weavers' blood nourishes the mites' bioluminescence.

When a Weaver extends their arms in the darkness, patterns of light trace along their limbs—messages, warnings, poetry in a visual language that predates their vocal communications. Young Weavers receive their first mite colonies at adolescence in ceremonies held in the highest chambers, where the air is thinnest and the mountain cold most bitter. The shock of the cold triggers the mites' first illumination, and the young Weaver's pattern—unique as fingerprints—burns itself into clan memory.

The mites are semi-sentient, responding to their host's emotional states. Fear brings rapid flashing, love creates slow pulses, grief dims them to almost nothing. When a Weaver dies, their mites continue glowing for days, gradually dimming as they consume the last of their host's chemistry, creating a funeral light that the colony honors with silence.

## The Deep Shepherds

Lower, in the root-halls where the hollow trunks meet the actual earth, live the Deep Shepherds. These beings chose, generations ago, to adapt to the eternal darkness of the foundation spaces. Their eyes have sealed over, leaving smooth skin where sight once lived. Instead, they navigate through vibration sensors that cover their elongated bodies—bodies that have stretched over generations to better move through the cramped spaces between roots.

The Shepherds tend to creatures that never see even the dim twilight of the middle heights. Blind millipedes the length of train cars that process fallen organic matter into fertile soil. Colonies of fungi that the Shepherds cultivate in careful gardens, some producing medicines the Weavers trade for, others creating heat through biological processes that warm the root-halls. Most mysteriously, the Shepherds maintain relationships with things that live in the underground rivers—pale, singing fish that navigate by echolocation, which the Shepherds call up through sonic passwords for reasons they share with no one.

Once each year, selected Shepherds make the journey upward, their elongated forms struggling with the vertical climb, to meet with the Weavers at the traditional platforms. They bring offerings from below—crystals that form in root chambers, medicines that require darkness to develop, messages from the deep places written in knots on fungal fiber cords. In return, they carry back gossip from the heights, seeds from the canopy world, and most importantly, new songs learned from the mountain winds.

## The Drift Gardens' Keepers

Not all inhabitants are bound to the trunks. The Aerial Tenders live their entire lives on the floating garden masses, being born, living, and dying without ever touching solid wood. They are small—no larger than housecats—with bodies covered in specialized hairs that trap air, making them naturally buoyant in the humid atmosphere. Their limbs are elongated and delicate, ending in pad-like extremities that distribute their weight across the root networks of the drift gardens.

The Tenders cultivate these floating ecosystems with obsessive care, trimming growth to maintain balance, introducing new species through careful trade with trunk-dwelling peoples, managing the complex relationships between the hundreds of plant species that compose each garden. They sing constantly—not in the deep harmonics of the Weavers, but in ultrasonic ranges that stimulate plant growth and guide the gardens' movements through the air currents.

Their society is matriarchal and botanical. Leadership is determined by the health and diversity of the gardens under one's care. They measure time in growth cycles, wealth in rare seeds, and success in the ability to create new hybrid plants that can survive the floating life. Their children are raised communally, learning to read the health of plants by the sound of sap flowing through stems, to predict weather changes by the way morning condensation beads on leaves.

When a garden becomes too large, threatening to sink, the Tenders perform division ceremonies—carefully separating the root mass while singing harmonic frequencies that help the plants survive the trauma. These divisions are also social events, as young Tenders choose which garden to follow, often forming new communities around the freshly divided ecosystems.

## The Memories

But perhaps most unsettling are the ones the Weavers call the Memories—beings that may have once been Weavers themselves, or Shepherds, or something else entirely, who have undergone the Wooden Dream. These individuals, through meditation or madness or careful chemical intervention, have slowed their metabolisms to nearly nothing and allowed the trees to partially absorb them.

They stand in carved alcoves throughout the trunk heights, their skin bark-rough, their hair become moss, their eyes open but seeing on timescales others cannot fathom. Fungal networks run through their bodies, connecting them to the trillion subtle chemical communications of the forest organism. They breathe perhaps once an hour, their hearts beat once a minute. They are living libraries, processing the chemical history of the forest, understanding patterns that take centuries to complete.

Young Weavers are brought to them for naming ceremonies, as the Memories can taste bloodlines in the chemistry of breath, can name ancestors by the mineral content of tears. The Memories speak rarely—perhaps once in a decade—but when they do, their words become law, become prophecy, become new songs for the mountain wind to play through the hollow towers.

Some say the Memories are becoming the forest, that in another thousand years they will be indistinguishable from the bark that holds them. Others believe they've already become something beyond personhood—nodes in a consciousness so vast and slow that their individual thoughts take seasons to complete. Their breathing harmonizes with the trees' respiration. Their blood moves with the sap. When one finally dies—a process that can take decades to complete—their alcove becomes sacred, and new growths emerge from where they stood, bearing fruit that carries memories in its flesh.

## The Visitors from Above

Only rumors and half-glimpsed shadows speak of the Canopy Dwellers, those who live in the mythical realm above the first branches. The Weavers sometimes find artifacts that fall from above—tools made from materials unknown in the twilight world, crystals that hold light and release it slowly over days, seeds for plants that cannot survive below the canopy but whose fruit, when it rarely grows, grants visions of vast bright spaces.

Sometimes, shapes descend on wings that aren't membranes but true feathered pinions, beings adapted for the thin air and harsh light of the crown world. They trade with the Weavers briefly, exchanging information about weather patterns, about the movements of the stars that the under-dwellers never see. Their language includes concepts for which no words exist in the twilight—colors beyond the bioluminescent spectrum, temperatures that would kill anything adapted to the humid middle heights, distances measured in cloud-leaps rather than trunk-spans.

These visitors speak of ice gardens in the crown, of creatures that breathe both air and vacuum, of viewing platforms where the curve of the world becomes visible. They speak reverently of something called the Summit Singers, beings so adapted to altitude that coming below the canopy would crush them, who maintain the ancient observatory structures built into the highest wooden peaks, who alone know the true extent of the Singing Canopy—whether it covers continents or the entire world.

## The Unchanged

And moving through all these societies, traded like precious commodities or feared like plagues, are the Unchanged—normal animals from the world beyond the Canopy that occasionally find their way in. A simple sparrow becomes an oracle, its ability to survive without modification seen as divine. A mundane mouse that wanders in from the forest edge is housed in elaborate temples, its offspring studied for signs of adaptation, its death mourned by entire communities.

The Unchanged remind all dwellers that there was a before, that evolution is a choice as much as an inevitability, that the Singing Canopy is not the entire world but a magnificent exception to it. Some cultures worship them. Others consume them in ritual feasts, hoping to absorb their connection to the original world. Still others simply observe them with the mixture of wonder and pity reserved for living museums, remnants of a simplicity that the Canopy has grown beyond.

## The Harmony

Together, these peoples create a vertical civilization that moves to rhythms both biological and musical. The Singing Canopy is not just their home but their instrument, their god, their body extended into wood and space. They are all soloists in an eternal composition that began before remembered history and will continue after all current singers have become bark, bone, or starlight.

Each species knows fragments of the others' songs. Together, their calls weave through the hollow spaces, creating harmonies that tell the story of the day—here, a birth announced in ultrasonic celebration; there, a death mourned in subsonic waves that make the great trunks shudder. The mountain wind provides the drone note, constant and changing, while ten thousand species add their voices to a music that is conversation, culture, and communion all at once.

This is who lives in the Singing Canopy—not separate peoples but movements in a living symphony, each essential, each incomplete without the others, all of them gradually becoming something more than the sum of their breathing, singing, growing parts. They are the small gods of an enormous space, the quick thoughts of an ancient mind, the warm blood in wooden veins that reach from the earth's heart to the edge of sky.
