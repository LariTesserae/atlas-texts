## The Cultivator

She arrived here wearing a hazmat suit that has long since been repurposed into a dozen different things—strips of it seal the greenhouse gaps, pieces patch the filtration systems, the helmet serves as a terrarium for something that might once have been moss but now defies classification. The woman who wore it has evolved too, though more slowly than her surroundings.

Her name was Dr. Sarah Chen, though she rarely thinks of herself that way anymore. The grant proposal that brought her here—*Accelerated Speciation in Isolated Volcanic Ecosystems*—feels like fiction written by a stranger. She came to observe. She stayed to participate.

The transformation was gradual. First, the replacement of her damaged equipment with improvised solutions—bamboo pipes to channel steam, volcanic glass lenses to focus light, living filters made from modified root systems. Then her diet shifted as supply runs became impossible; the caldera provides, if you know what to ask for. The bitter roots that grow near sulfur vents, when processed through the right bacterial cultures, become edible. The metallic insects, roasted, taste of copper and salt.

Her skin has taken on a faint luminescence—not enough to see by, but visible in the dark. Spores, she thinks, or perhaps a symbiotic relationship with the phosphorescent bacteria. Her lungs have adapted to the volcanic gases; ordinary air now feels thin, incomplete. She breathes deeper here, each breath a conversation with the mountain.

She speaks to her specimens, and increasingly, they seem to respond. Not in words but in patterns—the way the moss pulses when she approaches, how the metal-winged creatures spiral around her hands, the carnivorous vines that part to let her pass. She's become part of the caldera's rhythm, another instrument in its endless song.

The greenhouse-engine-shrine is her collaboration with the mountain. What started as a research station has become something between laboratory and organ—a living machine that processes the caldera's raw materials into increasingly complex forms. She feeds it fallen insects and volcanic minerals; it produces crystalline structures that might be alive, solutions that glow and remember their shape when poured, seeds that germinate into things she has no names for.

Her notebooks, what remains of them, chart not just the evolution of species but the evolution of evolution itself—how the rules change in this accelerated place, how DNA becomes suggestions rather than instructions, how life finds ways to incorporate the impossible. The later entries are less text than diagrams, chemical formulas that spiral into patterns, observations that read like poetry or fever dreams:

*"The golden beetles are teaching their offspring to sing in harmony with the Tuesday vents."*

*"Successful pollination between the copper orchids and something that might have been a bird. Offspring pending."*

*"The mountain dreams. I've started dreaming its dreams."*

She's not alone, exactly. The caldera is full of life, and some of it is almost companionable. A creature she calls the Librarian—part spider, part architect—builds geometric webs that seem to map the air currents, creating three-dimensional charts she's learning to read. The ferns rustle in patterns that feel like language. Even the bacteria colonies demonstrate what might be intentionality, forming symbols in their mats that change based on temperature, pressure, chemical availability.

Occasionally, she remembers the world beyond the rim—conferences and coffee, her apartment with its dying houseplants (ironic now), colleagues who probably think she's dead. But that world feels fictional now, a story told about someone else. This is real: the weight of humid air, the taste of sulfur, the constant sensation of life inventing itself around and through her.

She's working on something in the greenhouse's heart—a cultivation that combines elements from across the caldera's ecosystem. Not quite plant, not quite animal, not quite mineral. It grows in spirals, following mathematical patterns that seem to predict the steam vents' schedule. She feeds it everything—her blood sometimes, when it seems necessary, fragments of the old monitoring equipment, spores and solutions and crystallized gases. It sings back to her in frequencies that make her teeth ache.

She doesn't know what it will become. Neither does it, probably. That's the beauty of this place—nothing knows what it's becoming until it becomes it. Evolution at the speed of decision, life as jazz improvisation.

At night (though day and night matter less here, under the constant glow of phosphorescence and lava), she climbs to where the old walkway broke off, sits with her feet dangling over the newest lava flow, and watches life colonize the fresh rock. Sometimes she helps it along, dropping spores and seeds, placing beetles where they might thrive. Sometimes she just watches, taking notes in a notation system that's becoming less human and more... indigenous.

She's growing something like wings. Not for flying—the membrane between her fingers and toes helps her navigate the steep walls, catch the updrafts of steam. The mountain is teaching her its body, and she's learning to climb it from the inside.

There are others, she thinks. In other calderas, other impossible gardens, other humans who came to study and stayed to become. Sometimes, when the wind is right and the whole mountain resonates, she swears she can hear them—a network of cultivators, each tending their own evolutionary explosion, connected through the planet's volcanic nervous system.

Or perhaps that's just the gases talking. It's hard to tell, after a while, where thoughts end and the environment begins. That's probably the point. The caldera doesn't just create new life—it dissolves the boundaries between kinds of being, making everything into everything else, forever, at the speed of steam and stone and song.
