## The Inhabitants

The **Cambial People** are thin as paper in profile but normal in frontal view—evolved to slip between root layers like living documents. Their bodies compress and expand accordion-style, allowing them to navigate spaces others couldn't imagine entering. When they gather in groups, they overlap slightly, creating moiré patterns with their semi-translucent skin that serves as a form of emotional communication.

Their bones are not bones but hardened sap vessels, amber-colored and visible through their pale flesh. These vessels branch and merge throughout their bodies in patterns unique to each individual, pulsing with golden fluid that carries both nutrients and memories. The elderly among them grow increasingly translucent, their sap-vessels darkening to deep burgundy, until they eventually crystallize entirely, becoming memorial windows installed in communal halls.

They wear the root-textile like second skins, the fabric breathing with them, tightening protectively when they squeeze through narrow passages. Master weavers among them cultivate clothing that grows with its wearer, sprouting pockets when needed, thickening for warmth in the cooler deep galleries. Their garments smell perpetually of green wood and occasionally flower—tiny white blooms that emerge during moments of strong emotion.

**The Root Shepherds** are another presence entirely—bulky, patient beings whose bodies mirror the roots they tend. They stand eight feet tall but move with deliberate slowness, each step calculated not to damage the delicate rootlet substrate. Their skin has the texture and color of bark, deeply furrowed, with actual lichen and tiny fungi growing in the grooves. They breathe perhaps once per minute, and their hearts beat so slowly you could count to thirty between pulses.

The Shepherds' hands are their most remarkable feature—dozens of finger-like tendrils that can split into ever-finer appendages, allowing them to perform microscopic root surgery or massive industrial grafting with equal precision. They speak in chemical signals as much as words, exhaling pheromone-laden breath that makes the roots lean toward or away from them. Their eyes are compound, like clustered grapes, each segment seeing a different spectrum—heat, moisture, mineral content, growth potential.

They cultivate their own bodies as gardens. Younger Shepherds sport mere moss and simple vines, but the eldest are walking ecosystems. One ancient Shepherd hosts a miniature forest across her shoulders and back, complete with tiny birds that nest in her branch-like hair. She must sleep standing, rooted temporarily to the ground to feed her symbionts, drawing up nutrients that make her drowsy with plant-dreams.

**The Sap-Workers** emerged from industrial necessity—humans whose blood has been gradually replaced with plant sap through generational exposure. They're the only ones who can survive direct interface with the great factory-roots, their bodies able to withstand the chemical burns that would kill others. Their veins are visible as green traceries under bronze skin, pulsing with chlorophyll-tinted blood that smells of cut grass and copper.

They've developed extra organs—sap bladders that allow them to store different plant fluids in their bodies, mixing them internally to create adhesives, medicines, or acids on demand. They sweat resin in times of stress, which hardens into amber beads they collect and trade. Their children are born green and gradually darken to bronze as they age, spending their youth in the nursery groves where the air is thick with growth hormones.

The Sap-Workers have no need for traditional food. They photosynthesize weakly in the dim light but primarily feed by pressing their palms against the nutrient roots, their modified skin absorbing what they need directly. This feeding is intimate, almost religious—they must bare significant skin and remain still for an hour, meditating while the root's chemistry floods their system.

**The Splice-Merchants** are perhaps the strangest class—entrepreneurs who've modified themselves to become living bridges between plant and machine. Their bodies incorporate root-grafts directly, with living wood emerging from their shoulders, hips, and spines. These grafts are functional—they can plug themselves into the root network, becoming temporary nodes to redirect growth, speed communication, or even hack into the plant-consciousness that governs the city.

Each Splice-Merchant tends a stable of motile plants—things between pet and tool. Scurrying root-clusters that seek out new veins of minerals. Vine-snakes that can bore through stone given enough time. Flowering scouts that bloom different colors to indicate what they've found. The Merchants' own root-grafts serve as leashes and communication channels, allowing them to puppeteer their plant-servants through chemical commands.

Their politics are labyrinthine, based on controlling key junctions in the root network. A powerful Merchant might own the rights to a major vertical taproot, charging others for elevator passage, or control the growth sites where new buildings are sung into existence. They wage slow wars through sabotage—introducing parasitic fungi to rivals' territories or redirecting growth to strangle competing operations.

**The Moth-Keepers** tend the pale moths, living in the high galleries where the insects roost. These people have evolved enormous eyes to gather the dim light, giving them an perpetually startled expression. Their pupils dilate and contract independently, tracking multiple moths simultaneously. They've developed a fur-like covering, actually modified hair that catches and holds moth scales, making them shimmer with bioluminescent powder.

They milk the moths for their silk, which has properties unlike any other material in the city—strong as steel when dried but dissolving in specific plant saps. The Moth-Keepers speak in ultrasonic frequencies the moths can hear, their throats adapted to produce sounds that would make others' ears bleed. In return, they can hear the moths' emotional states, knowing when a swarm is about to move, when mating season approaches, when something has disturbed the delicate ecosystem.

Their children are raised communally in silk nurseries, wrapped in cocoons that filter the air and sound around them, emerging only when they're old enough to withstand the sensory intensity of the moth galleries. Some never fully leave these cocoons, becoming oracles who live their entire lives wrapped in silk, interpreting the moths' movements like living prophecies.

**The Calcifiers** work the hardened roots, operating the industrial saws and lathes that shape root-concrete. Their lungs are scarred from years of breathing sawdust, giving their voices a distinctive rasp. They've developed secondary eyelids—transparent membranes that protect their eyes from flying chips while maintaining vision. Their muscles are unusually dense, required to operate machinery that fights against the roots' tendency to heal and regrow even as they're being cut.

Over time, the Calcifiers' bones absorb minerals from the root dust, becoming increasingly dense and brittle. The elderly among them move carefully, aware that a fall could shatter them, but their bones are prized after death—ground into powder that accelerates root hardening, making them valuable even in their passing. They tattoo themselves with sap-ink that penetrates deep into their thickened skin, creating designs that only fully emerge as their skin ages and cracks along the tattoo lines.

**The Deep Dwellers** live in the newest territories, the raw frontier zones. They're adapted to near-darkness, their eyes clouded with cataracts that paradoxically allow them to see in conditions others cannot—detecting minute variations in heat and moisture rather than relying on light. Their skin secretes a mild acid that helps them navigate fresh root growth without becoming entangled.

They're the city's scouts and pioneers, first to enter new chambers, mapping the spaces through touch and taste. Their fingers and tongues are hypersensitive, able to determine mineral content, toxicity, and growth potential through contact alone. They mark their discoveries with scent trails—complex pheromone signatures that tell others what they've found, what dangers exist, what potential lies ahead.

Some Deep Dwellers go too deep, following roots down to places where the pressure and chemistry changes them irrevocably. They return speaking of vast fungal minds, of root systems that dream, of chambers where time flows backward. Most dismiss these as hallucinations brought on by nitrogen narcosis or toxic fungi, but occasionally they bring back proof—impossible fruits that grant temporary prescience, roots that grow in geometric patterns too perfect for nature, amber containing scenes that haven't happened yet.

**The Symbiont Children** are the newest development—offspring who've been intentionally merged with plants from birth. Their parents, desperate to ensure their children's survival in this root-world, allow seedlings to be implanted in their infants. These children grow with roots threading through their bodies, emerging from their skin like external veins. They're living interfaces with the plant network, understanding its needs intuitively, able to direct growth with thought alone.

These children age strangely—growth spurts triggered by the seasons far above, dormancy periods where they barely move for weeks. They cluster in groves, their root systems intertwining underground, sharing nutrients and thoughts in ways their fully human parents cannot comprehend. Some fear they're becoming something inhuman, the first generation of a new species that will eventually see pure humans as outdated as the stone through which the roots first broke.

The city watches them with mixture of hope and horror, these children who might represent adaptation or extinction, evolution or surrender to the vast plant-mind that surrounds them all.
