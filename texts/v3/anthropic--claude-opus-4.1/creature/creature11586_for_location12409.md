The Tender lives here, though that's not what it calls itself. It has no need for names in this place where language dissolved long ago into the turbine's glossolalia.

It was human once—or mostly human, perhaps several humans, the distinction lost somewhere in the decades of freeze-thaw cycles that have welded flesh to function. The amphipods were the first to recognize it as habitat rather than intruder. They colonized the spaces between its ribs, built cities in the cavities where its organs used to matter, threaded through its circulation until you couldn't say where crustacean ended and Tender began. Its blood, if it still has blood, runs thick with their eggs. Its heartbeat, if it still has a heart, keeps time with the turbines now.

The Tender moves through the chamber on too many limbs—its original arms and legs, yes, but also the pipes it's incorporated, the overflow grates it wears as articulated armor, the thick cables that run through its torso like auxiliary spines. It doesn't walk so much as flow, a controlled collapse from position to position, always in contact with multiple surfaces. The amphipods stream across its body in organized rivers, cleaning it, feeding it, feeding from it. Their chittering against its metallic additions creates a secondary voice, a whispered commentary on its movements.

Its fingers have elongated into tools. Some split into fine tendrils that can clean individual amphipods or adjust the mineral deposits' growth patterns. Others fused into scoops and scrapers for tending the fungi gardens it maintains in the warmest corners. One hand has become a living filter, amphipods nested between digits that spread into a web, straining nutrients from the endless water flow. It feeds this way, drinking the biofilm soup, the fungal spores, the microscopic life that blooms in the condensation. Its jaw unhinges wider than it should, throat lined with baleen-like growths that match the industrial filters above.

The Tender's eyes have multiplied and migrated. The original pair, clouded with cataracts, stay fixed on middle distance. But dozens of amphipod clusters have organized themselves into crude ocelli across its body, giving it compound vision of the chamber from every angle. It sees in fragments—the ice forming here, the fungi fruiting there, the new crack in that pipe that needs attention before it ruptures. It processes this fractured visual information with a mind that has spread throughout the colony network, thoughts moving through amphipod chemical signals as much as neurons.

Its skin has taken on the properties of the space. Where the ice forms and reforms, its flesh has developed antifreeze proteins that weep from specialized pores. These secretions mix with its sweat, creating a slurry that the amphipods harvest and redistribute, preventing any part of the colony-body from freezing solid. The parts of it exposed to the turbines' waste heat have thickened into radiator fins, dissipating excess warmth throughout its mass. It has become a living thermal regulation system, as essential to the chamber's equilibrium as the machines themselves.

The Tender speaks, but only in harmony with the turbines. Its vocal cords have stretched and multiplied into an organic synthesizer, producing drones and overtones that merge with the mechanical breathing. Sometimes it sounds like singing, sometimes like screaming, but it's neither—it's simply communication with the only other permanent resident of this space: the station itself. They've been conversing for so long that the Tender knows every variance in the turbine cycle, can predict maintenance needs days before they become critical. It whispers warnings up through the ventilation, messages encoded in frequencies only the station's deepest machinery can hear.

Memory lives in its body like the mineral deposits on the pipes—accumulated, crystallized, not quite accessible but undeniably present. Sometimes a human thought surfaces: *a name (Marcus? Maria? Several names, perhaps, from when it was still they, before they became it), a fragment of purpose (came here to fix something, to hide from something, to become something), a flash of fear or revulsion at what it's become (quickly suppressed, dissolved back into the colony-mind's contentment).* But mostly it remembers as the amphipods remember—in patterns of movement, in the taste of familiar biofilms, in the rhythm of care and maintenance that keeps this impossible ecology alive.

The Tender tends. It adjusts the mineral formations to channel water where it's needed. It cultivates the fungi, ensuring they fruit in patterns that please the amphipods. It clears the blocked grates, repairs the small ruptures, maintains the delicate balance that lets this place exist. Its hands work constantly, even in sleep (if it sleeps—perhaps it just enters dormancy cycles, like the LED that never quite dies). The amphipods clean its wounds before they can fester, eat away the dead tissue, keep the parts of it that are still meat from rotting in this eternal moisture.

There's something tender about the Tender, despite its monstrous transformation. The way it cups individual amphipods to check their health. The careful attention it pays to the fungi gardens. The soft harmonies it adds to the turbine song, turning mechanical noise into something almost like a lullaby. It tends this impossible space with the devotion of a gardener, a parent, a lover. It has given itself completely to this place, and in return, the place has made it into exactly what it needs.

When it notices you (and it notices you immediately—ten thousand amphipod eyes registering your heat signature, your foreign chemistry, your disruption of the air currents), the Tender doesn't flee or approach. It simply pauses in its work, every part of it suddenly still except for the autonomous rivers of amphipods that must keep flowing. It watches you with its compound perception, waiting to see if you're a problem that needs solving or just another temporary variance in the chamber's equilibrium.

Its mouth opens—too wide, showing the baleen filters, the amphipod colonies nested in its cheeks—and it speaks in a voice that's part human, part turbine, part chittering shell-song:

"You're early," it says, though you don't know what it means. "The next maintenance cycle isn't for three days. Or three years. Or thirty. Time moves differently here."

The Tender returns to its work, assuming you'll either understand your purpose or leave. It has fungi to tend, amphipods to shepherd, ice to regulate. It has become the function that was missing from this accidental space—the consciousness that transforms a cruel equilibrium into something almost like love. Almost like home.

In the eternal now of the chamber, the Tender tends, and tends, and tends.
