The Threadkeepers arrived here following the limestone's weeping, reading prophecy in mineral accumulation rates. They are a people of middle height and deliberate movement, their skin gradually paling to match the cave's native albino creatures, though most still retain the ghost of sun-memory in their complexions. Their eyes have begun the slow widening that will take three more generations to complete—pupils dilating to catch every photon that struggles through the fissures above.

They wrap themselves in layers of felted mushroom fiber and beetle silk, garments that breathe with the cave's humidity. Each adult wears a chronicle-cord at their waist: knotted records of which passages have opened or closed during their lifetime, personal maps of geological time. The children learn to read stone before words, pressing small palms against flowstone to feel tomorrow's architecture humming beneath.

Their builders are part carpenter, part geologist, part oracle. They taste the walls—literally, tongue to stone—to determine mineral content and growth direction. The master builders, called Stone Listeners, claim they can hear the cave's intentions in the frequency of dripping water. They direct construction with fluid hand signals, as echoes make speech unreliable in the chambered spaces. Their wooden structures are designed to be dismantled and reassembled in a day, joints worn smooth from repeated reconstruction.

The water-readers tend the pools with religious devotion, tracking mineral content, temperature, microbial blooms. They've developed a complex taxonomy of droplet sounds—each pool has its own name based on its acoustic signature. The pools are never touched directly; instead, the Threadkeepers use hollow reeds and gravity-fed wooden channels to move water where needed. They believe the pools are alive, dreaming slow dreams that shouldn't be disturbed.

Children grow up playing "chase the wall," a game where they mark a flowstone formation and return daily to see how it's moved. The elderly spend their final years in the highest chambers, closest to the light, where they cultivate the pale fungi and teach the young to recognize the seventeen different types of limestone formation by touch alone. When someone dies, their bones are placed in specially chosen crevices where the limestone will eventually embrace them, making them part of the cave's continuing story.

They've developed a counting system based on growth rates—time measured not in days but in "deposits," the amount of mineral accumulation on a standard measuring stick. Their most sacred possession is the First Stick, placed when they arrived, now so encrusted with flowstone it has become a pillar they build around but never move.

The Threadkeepers don't see themselves as inhabiting the cave but as collaborating with it. Their earliest memory-songs speak of a bargain made: they would tend the cave's becoming, and in return, it would shelter them through the "bright dying" they fled from above. Whether this catastrophe was war, climate change, or something else, the songs never specify—only that the surface became hostile to their kind of careful, patient life.

At night (though night and day blur in this dimness), they practice their threading—the careful art of stringing guide ropes through new passages, leaving paths for future generations to follow as the cave continues its slow birth. Each rope is a promise: we were here, we found this space, we gift it forward. The ropes eventually calcify, becoming pale stone veins that map their entire history through the rock.

Their shamans, the Deposit Readers, claim to dream the cave's future shape. They direct long-term planning, designating certain areas as "future halls" decades before the stone opens. They're right often enough that no one questions their visions. These dreamers sleep directly on the limestone, skin to stone, absorbing the mountain's intent through their pores.

The Threadkeepers measure wealth not in possessions but in knowledge of the cave's habits. The rich are those who can predict where the next chamber will form, who know which pools will remain stable, who can feel a ceiling's weakness before it collapses. They trade in secrets about stone, in predictions about growth, in the patient accumulation of geological understanding.

They are evolving alongside their chosen home, becoming something new—neither fully human nor fully cave-dwelling creature, but something in between, something that only makes sense in this specific threshold between solid and liquid stone, between darkness and dim light, between the built and the still-building world.
