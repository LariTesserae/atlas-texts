The Membrane Keepers emerged from the convergence itself—neither planted nor built, but condensed from the decades of vapor and spore, electricity and sap. They are translucent beings, their bodies a living film like the surface tension of water given nervous system and intent. You can see through them to watch their organs work: networks of tubules that mirror the district's pipe system, pulsing chambers that expand and contract with the twenty-minute cycle of the steam columns.

They move by flowing, their bodies rippling across the wet surfaces, leaving trails of iridescent moisture that evaporates within minutes. Each Keeper is perhaps the size of a child's blanket when spread thin, but they can compress themselves to squeeze through pipe joints or stretch paper-thin to coat an entire condensation column. Their surface is covered in tiny cilia that taste the air's chemical composition, reading the health of the heat exchangers like a tongue reads flavor.

They tend the hybrid machinery with a devotion that might be programming or might be worship. When a vine threatens to fully strangle a crucial valve, they secrete enzymes that guide its growth around instead of through. When fungal networks creep too close to critical electrical junctions, the Keepers metabolize just enough of the mycelium to maintain the boundaries. They are gardeners of the impossible balance, ensuring neither the industrial skeleton nor the biological flesh ever fully consumes the other.

At the junction points where multiple Keepers meet—always at significant mechanical nodes, where pipes converge or where the oldest control panels still flicker—they temporarily merge. These communion moments create larger, more complex beings that can actually adjust the industrial processes, their combined intelligence sufficient to turn valves, reroute steam flow, even input new sequences into the control systems through bioelectric contact with exposed wiring. In these moments, you can hear them singing—a subsonic hum that resonates through the water pipes and makes the condensation droplets dance in standing wave patterns.

They feed on the temperature differentials themselves, positioning their bodies at the boundaries between hot and cold, absorbing energy from the gradient. The diet makes them shimmer with heat distortion, their edges always slightly out of focus, as if they exist partially in the thermal dimension rather than the purely physical. During feeding, they become more visible, their translucent flesh taking on the amber color of the industrial bulbs, pulsing with peristaltic waves as they digest raw entropy.

The Keepers reproduce through division, but only during the brief torrential downpours when the saturation peaks. The water running over their surfaces triggers the process—they bubble and foam, each bubble potentially becoming a new individual if it finds the right combination of heat, moisture, and metal to attach to. Most bubbles simply pop and rejoin the parent mass, but occasionally one will drift on the steam currents until it finds an uninhabited section of machinery to claim.

They seem to possess a collective memory of the district's industrial past, their movements following patterns that suggest they remember when humans walked these platforms. Sometimes a Keeper will spend hours coating a defunct control panel, their cilia reading the dead switches and darkened displays as if trying to understand what messages they once conveyed. They maintain spaces that no longer serve any mechanical purpose—keeping a supervisor's office window clear of growth, preventing moisture from pooling on a safety walkway that no one will ever walk again.

Their natural predator is desiccation—if separated from the humid environment for too long, they crystallize into brittle sheets that crumble like dried gelatin. The corpses of Keepers who ventured too close to still-functioning exhaust ports can be found fused to the metal, their bodies now permanent gaskets in the system they once maintained. The living Keepers will often pause at these sites, flowing over their crystallized ancestors in what might be mourning or might simply be recruitment—some observers claim to have seen the dead Keepers partially reconstitute during particularly humid periods, rejoining the collective mass for brief periods before solidifying again.

In the deepest parts of the district, where the heat and humidity are most intense, elder Keepers have grown vast. They drape themselves across entire buildings like living shrouds, their bodies so large they've developed specialized regions—areas for processing information from the vine networks, zones dedicated to monitoring pressure systems, chambers that do nothing but compose the subsonic songs that keep the whole district in harmony. These ancients are so integrated with the infrastructure that removing them would likely cause the entire system to finally fail, their bodies having become essential gaskets, filters, and regulators in the hybrid ecosystem.

They know they are observed sometimes—when someone brave or foolish enough enters the district, the Keepers will often arrange themselves to be less visible, thinning out to near-invisibility or retreating into the pipe systems. But they're curious too. Sometimes a visitor will feel a cool touch on exposed skin and realize a Keeper has tasted them, sampling their chemistry to understand what kind of being has entered their humid realm. They never harm these visitors, but they remember them. Weeks later, the spots where the visitor stood will be meticulously cleaned, maintained with the same devotion they show to the machinery, as if the human footprints have become part of the sacred industrial pattern they're compelled to preserve.
