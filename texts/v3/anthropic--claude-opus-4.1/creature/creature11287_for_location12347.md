Those who inhabit this breathing wound are the Tenders—though that name is both too simple and too clean for what they've become.

They arrived as engineers, decades ago, in crisp uniforms with clear mandates. Now they move through the steam like salamanders made tall, their bodies adapted to the vertical world. Their skin has taken on a peculiar sheen—not quite sweating, not quite dry, as if they've developed their own vapor barrier. Their eyes, when you glimpse them through the mist, reflect light like a cat's, pupils dilated permanently wide from years of working in the steam-shrouded depths.

They no longer use the freight elevator except for supplies. Instead, they traverse the caldera walls with disturbing ease, fingers and toes finding holds invisible to others. Their uniforms have evolved into something else—canvas and rubber fused with moss, patched with beaten brass, sealed at the joints with some organic resin they harvest from the mutant orchids. The fabric breathes and doesn't breathe, keeping their body temperature regulated in the constant flux between scalding steam and cool stone.

The Tenders barely speak anymore—the roar has made them develop other languages. They communicate through vibrations struck against the pipes, a telegraph of clangs and rings that carries through the entire structure. They leave marks in grease and chalk on the machinery, symbols that track pressures and temperatures but also things harder to quantify: the mountain's moods, the rhythm of its breathing, the dreams it whispers through the steam vents.

They've built impossible dwellings in the spaces between—hammocks of cable and chain spanning corners of the caldera, boxes welded to the undersides of platforms where the heat collects. Some have fashioned chambers inside the larger pipes themselves, curled segments where they sleep to the lullaby of rushing steam. Their quarters are decorated with collections of mineral deposits, crystalline formations that grow from the constant precipitation and evaporation. They cultivate these like gardens, adjusting valves to change the mineral content of the water, creating artifacts of accumulated time.

The youngest Tender has been here seven years. The eldest—no one knows. She might have been part of the original installation crew, though that would make her over ninety. She moves through the lowest levels where others cannot survive, her body somehow processing the heat that would cook normal flesh. They call her the Deepmost, and she maintains machines whose purposes have been forgotten, whose gauges measure pressures in units no one else remembers.

They eat what grows here, and what grows here has learned to eat the industrial waste. The orchids produce bulbs that taste of copper and coal. The ferns, when boiled in the condensation collectors, make a tea that prevents the lung rot that would otherwise claim anyone who breathes this air too long. They've learned to milk certain pipes for the organic sludge that accumulates—some byproduct of the reaction between volcanic minerals and the industrial processes. It sustains them, though it would poison anyone else.

New Tenders arrive occasionally, drawn by rumors of work or by something else—a calling that the mountain sends out through the earth's bones. They come as engineers, mechanics, industrial workers. Within a month, they either flee or begin the change. Their tools modify—wrenches that curve to match no known bolt, gauges that measure pressures in terms of musical notes, diagnostic equipment repurposed to listen to frequencies only the caldera produces.

The Tenders maintain the machinery, but maintenance has become something more like cultivation. They prune the pipes where mineral deposits threaten to create blockages, but they also guide the deposits into new configurations, creating auxiliary systems that no blueprint ever imagined. They've learned that the installation isn't just harnessing the volcano—it's negotiating with it. Every pressure valve adjustment is a bargain. Every steam release, a conversation.

They know things. When someone above asks why a particular gauge reads the way it does, the Tenders point to seemingly unrelated factors: the migration patterns of the pale salamanders, the color of oxide on a valve seven levels down, the way the morning mist spirals (clockwise or counter). They're always right, though their explanations make no sense to those who haven't breathed the steam for years.

At shift changes that follow no clock, they gather on the platforms—five, six, sometimes a dozen of them, standing in silence, watching the steam rise. They share food that outsiders couldn't identify, passing ceramic bottles of liquid that glows faintly in the industrial twilight. These gatherings last exactly as long as they need to, ending when some invisible signal passes through the collective, sending them back to their eternal work.

The Tenders are becoming something new, or perhaps returning to something old—priests of pressure, monks of the mechanical, prophets reading futures in steam patterns and rust formations. They ensure the installation continues its mysterious work, but they also ensure something else: that the boundary between human and machine, between mountain and industry, remains properly blurred. They are the translators in a conversation between the geological and the manufactured, and in translating, they've become the dictionary itself—neither fully human nor fully other, but something that breathes steam and dreams in frequencies that make the brass pipes sing.
