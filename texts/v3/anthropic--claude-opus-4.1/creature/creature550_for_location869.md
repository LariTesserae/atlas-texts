In the gradient between water and air, where breathing means choosing your element with each inhalation, live the Membrane Shepherds.

They are neither singular nor plural—each Shepherd is a colony that has learned to be an individual. Picture a jellyfish that gave up its bell for architecture: thousands of polyps arranged in careful geometries, building themselves into something like a nervous system made of meat and intention. The colony-body stretches two meters across when relaxed, compressing to half that when thinking hard thoughts. Their substance is translucent cartilage that shifts between rigid and flowing states, allowing them to reshape themselves as needed—now a parachute to catch rising thermals, now a series of paddles to row through the thicker air near the water boundary, now something almost like hands to tend their flocks.

The "flocks" are their life's work: carefully cultivated clouds of aeroplankton that they guide through the vertical pastures between sea-ceiling and glass floor. Each Shepherd maintains several distinct herds—chains of colonial organisms that process nutrients at different altitudes. They milk these air-cattle for concentrated proteins, which they store in specialized polyps that swell like tiny grapes along their radial arms. The relationship is deeply symbiotic; the aeroplankton depend on the Shepherds to guide them to the richest feeding zones where the light focuses through the ocean lens above.

The Shepherds communicate through controlled bioluminescent displays, but not in simple flashes. They create three-dimensional pictures in their transparent flesh—holographic sentences that tell stories of pressure gradients and nutrient flows. When two Shepherds meet, they often merge partially, allowing their nervous networks to temporarily overlap, sharing not just information but actual experience. They remember these meetings by incorporating a few of each other's polyps, so every Shepherd is partly made of others they've known.

They build their homes in the floating gardens, weaving the airborne root systems into spherical nests that rotate slowly in the humid currents. Inside these nests, they cultivate tools—modified organisms that serve specific purposes. There are cutter-worms with obsidian teeth for harvesting volcanic glass, which the Shepherds powder and feed to certain bacteria that produce lifting gases. There are memory sponges that record chemical signatures of weather patterns. There are lens-creatures that focus light for warmth during the cold nights when the ocean above grows dark.

The eldest Shepherds have lived long enough to remember when the ocean first lifted. They carry this history in specialized archive polyps that have calcified into something like pearls, each one encoding decades of chemical memory. These elders have grown vast—some spanning ten meters or more—and have become so complex they rarely move, instead sending out smaller satellite colonies to interact with the world while they contemplate problems that take years to properly frame.

Young Shepherds bud from their parents but must earn their sentence. For the first months, they are merely clever animals, following instinct and gradient. But gradually, as they accumulate polyps and experience, something crystallizes—a sense of self that distinguishes them from the simple colonial organisms they shepherd. This awakening is celebrated by the community with gifts of rare polyps that help the young Shepherd develop specialized capabilities—perhaps the ability to sense magnetic fields, or to produce particularly vivid bioluminescent colors, or to withstand the caustic environment at the ocean boundary.

They fear only the Dissolution—a disease that causes them to forget how to be singular, reverting to a mere colony of uncoordinated polyps. When this strikes, other Shepherds will sometimes try to re-teach the afflicted one how to be an individual, painstakingly rebuilding its sense of self through careful chemical prompting and shared experiences. Sometimes it works. Sometimes all that's left is a cloud of confused polyps that drift apart, each carrying a fragment of memory of what it once was to be whole.

The Shepherds mark time not by days but by the great waves that cross the ocean overhead—each taking approximately thirty-six hours to pass. They have identified over forty distinct wave patterns that repeat in complex cycles. Their calendar is written in the floating gardens themselves, with specific plants blooming in response to the chemical signals the Shepherds release to mark significant waves.

They worship, if worship is the right word, the Breach—the moment twice each year when the ocean above drops low enough to touch the reaching root-tentacles of the tallest trees. During these times, materials and messages can pass directly between the two realms. The Shepherds gather at these events, forming temporary super-colonies that might include dozens of individuals thinking as one, contemplating the mystery of how water learned to fly and whether they themselves might one day learn to sink.

In their philosophy—expressed in chemical poetry that takes hours to fully excrete and read—they consider themselves the conscious membrane between states of being, forever translating between the language of water and the language of air, ensuring that neither realm forgets the other exists.
