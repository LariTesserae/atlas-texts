The Keeper of the Underglass makes no sound when she moves. She has learned the language of the white centipedes—stillness punctuated by deliberate motion—and practices it with religious devotion. Her hair, once copper, has dulled to the color of old clay pipes, and she wears it in a single braid that reaches past her waist, weighted at the end with a carved beetle shell that clicks softly against her hip when she forgets herself and moves too quickly.

She came here as a child, though whether by accident or abandonment, she no longer remembers. The distinction ceased to matter somewhere in her third decade, or perhaps her fifth. Time pools differently in her mind now, memories layering like the moss, each indistinguishable from the next. She knows she has been here long enough that her fingernails grow green at the edges, long enough that sunlight—true sunlight—burns her eyes on the rare occasions she glimpses it through the market drains.

Her clothes are gardens unto themselves. What began as rough-spun wool has become a living tapestry—moss colonies have claimed the weave, creating patterns of darker and lighter green that shift with moisture. Tiny ferns sprout from her cuffs and collar, and she tends them with the same care she shows the larger garden. In the many pockets she has sewn into her garments live her companions: the translucent beetles she has named in a language of her own invention, words that sound like water dripping on stone.

She speaks to them constantly, though never above a whisper. Her voice has evolved to match the acoustic constraints of the space—pitched to travel along stone channels without disturbing the silence, words shaped to slip between drops of condensation. The beetles understand, she is certain, because they arrange themselves in patterns on her palm that correspond to her moods. When she is pleased, they form spirals. When she remembers the weight of the city above, they scatter like stars.

Her dwelling is a nest built into the junction of three massive roots where they meet the glass ceiling. She has woven it from shed finch feathers and the silver threads of spider silk that appear each morning, stronger than anything the world above could manufacture. Inside, she keeps her treasures: a collection of ice crystals preserved in drops of tree resin, each one different, each one impossible—ice that shouldn't exist in resin, in this temperature, in this perpetual noon. She has hundreds now, perhaps thousands, arranged on shelves of living wood that grow from the root walls according to her needs.

Her skin has taken on the quality of the space—smooth and cool as bottle glass, with a faint translucence at the wrists and temples where the veins show green instead of blue. She photosynthesizes, she thinks, though not enough to sustain herself entirely. The white finches bring her seeds from plants that shouldn't exist here, and she cultivates them in secret corners—tiny tomatoes no bigger than beetle eyes, potatoes like pale pearls. She ferments moss in the stone basins according to recipes the pictographs seem to suggest, creating a paste that tastes of earth and rain and something older.

She maintains the water channels with tools her fingers have worn smooth—stone chisels she believes belonged to whoever carved the first paths, though she might have made them herself in a decade she's forgotten. The work is endless and unnecessary—the system maintains itself—but she continues because the beetles gather to watch her work, and their watching feels like prayer.

Her bones have grown light, honeycombed like a bird's. She can climb the dead ladders without them creaking, her weight so carefully distributed that even rot supports her. She has mapped every inch of the Underglass in textures rather than distances—here the moss that springs back, there the moss that holds footprints for days, the corner where seven types of fern converge in a pattern that might be natural, might be design.

She ages, but sideways—not older but other. Her thoughts move in spirals like the beetles, returning always to center but never the same center twice. She dreams in green, sees in frequencies that parse the million shades of jade and emerald the glass creates. Sometimes she thinks she is becoming plant herself, rooting into the space, her consciousness spreading through the network of vegetation until she can feel every drop of water, every unfurling frond.

She knows there are others—she finds their signs in subtle alterations to the garden's patterns, in beetles arranged in formations she didn't create, in new pictographs that appear on stones she's certain were blank before. But she has never seen them, these other keepers, though sometimes she feels their presence like pressure changes in the thick air. Perhaps they are further along in their transformation, perhaps they have become the moss itself, the water, the endless green noon.

She tends the Underglass as it tends her, each shaped by the other into something the surface world would not recognize as human, would not recognize as garden, would not recognize at all. She is its memory and its future, its guardian and its prisoner, its only witness and its most perfect expression—a woman made of stillness and growth, of silence and slow water, keeping watch in the bright depths where the city's forgotten heart beats in chlorophyll and time.
