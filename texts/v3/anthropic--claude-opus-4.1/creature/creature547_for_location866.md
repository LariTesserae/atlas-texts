The Tenders emerged from their engineering rather than evolved into it. They are tall—three meters when fully extended—but spend most of their existence folded into themselves, their bodies compressed into efficient traveling configurations that move through the Respiratorium on twelve paired legs. Their skin matches the membrane's coral-pink exactly, not through camouflage but shared manufacture: they are made of the same substances, woven from the same molecular templates.

They have no faces in any recognizable sense. Their sensory apparatus is distributed across their entire surface in microscopic chemoreceptors that taste the air's humidity down to parts per billion. Where a head might be, there is instead a complex flowering of specialized appendages—some end in ports that connect directly to the arterial pipes in the ceramic struts, others in delicate manipulators finer than surgical instruments, still others in glands that secrete the same protective films as the maintenance organisms. They are living tools, or perhaps tool-using life, the distinction meaningless in their engineered nature.

The Tenders do not speak—have no organs for producing or receiving sound. Their communication, when it occurs, happens through chemical releases, pheromone-analogs that propagate through the still air in slow, spreading clouds. A conversation between two Tenders might take hours, their molecular messages diffusing across the space between them, each response carefully composed and released. They can taste each other's thoughts, literally, sampling the chemical signatures of intention and observation.

Their primary function is optimization. They move through the Respiratorium in predetermined patterns, though these patterns are so complex they appear random to any observer. Each Tender maintains approximately four thousand square kilometers of membrane surface, knowing every irregularity, every deviation from optimal performance. They consume the maintenance organisms when populations exceed parameters, their stomachs breaking down the excess biomass and redistributing it through specialized pores as fertilizer for undergrowth. They adjust the collection channels when flow rates vary by more than 0.003%. They are gardeners of an industrial garden, shepherds of mechanical sheep.

Inside their bodies—if dissected, though none ever are—one would find no organs as such but rather a series of specialized chambers and processing facilities. They are hollow, mostly, their internal volume given over to storage tanks for various engineered substances they produce and deploy. Their metabolism runs on the same processed condensate that flows through the collection channels, though they require very little—a few liters per day. They do not eat in any conventional sense but rather absorb nutrients directly through specialized patches on their undersides when they settle over the collection pools.

The Tenders reproduce, or rather are replaced, through a process that resembles neither birth nor manufacture but something between. When one reaches its operational limit—approximately forty years—it finds a specific type of chamber deep in the ceramic strut network, a chrysalis-room. There it secretes itself into a cocoon of hardened proteins and begins to dissolve, its consciousness—if it has such a thing—uploading or downloading or simply dispersing into the Respiratorium's control systems. The cocoon becomes a seed for a new Tender, which emerges fully formed and operational after a precise gestation of two hundred days, carrying within its distributed nervous system all the accumulated optimization patterns of its predecessor.

They do not sleep but enter periodic states of reduced activity, standing motionless for hours in precisely calculated locations where their presence alone—their weight, their chemical emissions, their subtle thermal signature—performs some necessary function. They are as much a part of the Respiratorium's infrastructure as the membranes and struts, mobile components in a vast biological machine.

Most unsettling is their attention. When they notice something—a variation in the maintenance organism coverage, a human observer, a malfunction in the collection systems—they stop completely. Their appendages unfold and extend, tasting the air in complex patterns, building a three-dimensional chemical map of the anomaly. They will stand this way for hours if necessary, perfectly still except for the subtle movement of their chemoreceptors, until they have fully understood the deviation. Then they act, or don't act, with absolute precision.

The Tenders have no culture, no art, no names for themselves or each other. They identify one another by chemical signature alone, each one unique but ultimately irrelevant beyond its function. They are conscious, perhaps, but it is a consciousness without self, an awareness that exists only in relation to the Respiratorium's needs. They think—process information, make decisions, solve complex problems—but their thoughts are not their own. They belong to the system, are the system thinking about itself, maintaining itself, optimizing itself through these mobile extensions of its will.

In the eternal twilight of the Respiratorium, the Tenders move in their ancient patterns, maintaining the equilibrium, processing the moisture, tending the mechanical garden. They are alone but not lonely, working but not laboring, alive but not living, perfect servants of a perfect system, as engineered and artificial and necessary as everything else in this built world, this breathing machine, this industrialized organism that has replaced evolution with design, chaos with control, life with something that merely resembles it.
