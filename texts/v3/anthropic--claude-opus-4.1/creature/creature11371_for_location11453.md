The Tenders move through their domain with the patience of tides. They are not quite humanoid—too tall where they should be short, joints that bend in too many places, skin that shifts between states like the surface tension of water deciding whether to break. When still, they might be mistaken for mineral formations, their bodies having taken on the same slick, condensation-covered texture as everything else here. But they are never truly still.

Three of them, usually. Sometimes four. Never more than five, never fewer than two. They've been here so long that their original purposes have been overwritten by the needs of this place. Their fingers have multiplied into something between roots and surgical instruments—dozens of thin, translucent appendages that can separate into hundreds of hair-fine filaments when they work. They use these to cultivate the pale creatures, adjusting their positions by millimeters, ensuring optimal flow patterns, removing those whose membranes have grown too rigid or too soft.

The Tenders breathe through their skin now, every pore a tiny mouth that filters the supersaturated air. When they exhale, it comes as fog, and the fog carries information—chemical signals that the carpet creatures respond to, clustering or dispersing according to messages older than any language. Their original lungs, you can sometimes see through their translucent torsos, have become something else—cultivation chambers where they grow specific strains of the organisms, testing new variations before introducing them to the broader ecosystem.

They wear the machinery. Not as clothing but as symbiotic extensions—a Tender's shoulder might merge seamlessly with a valve wheel, turning it by thought to adjust some deep flow. Another has made a home of an old control panel, its nervous system interfacing with corroded circuits that still remember fragments of their programming. They operate the industrial remnants not to restore their original function but to maintain the new equilibrium, keeping the water's chemistry balanced, the temperature gradients stable, the mineral deposits from growing too thick.

Their faces are the most disturbing part—not because they're inhuman, but because they're too human, frozen in expressions of profound concentration that haven't changed in decades. Eyes that never blink, having developed secondary membranes that constantly wash them clean. Mouths that no longer open, having sealed into small, perpetual smiles or frowns—they communicate through touch now, through chemical secretions, through the rhythmic compression of air in their modified throats that produces subsonic harmonics.

The Tenders remember being engineers, or botanists, or something similar. But the memory is like looking through water—distorted, refracted, true but not true. They came here to fix something, or grow something, or study something. The moisture got in first—into their equipment, their clothes, their skin. Then the organisms found them interesting, began to grow on them, in them, through them. The transformation was so gradual they didn't notice until it was complete, and by then, they no longer minded.

They harvest the creatures with infinite care, collecting them in specialized organs that have grown along their flanks—translucent sacs that maintain perfect pressure and temperature. What they do with the harvest is unclear. Sometimes they disappear into the deeper parts of the complex, where the humidity becomes absolute and even light seems to dissolve. They return days later, their collection sacs empty, new patterns spreading through the organism carpet in their wake—spirals, mandalas, mathematical curves that solve equations no human mathematics ever posed.

Sometimes a Tender will stop mid-task and stand perfectly still for hours, days even. The others work around them, the creatures flow around their feet like water around stones. These pauses might be sleep, or deep thought, or communication with something elsewhere in the complex—the industrial remnants responding to their stillness with changes in pitch, in vibration, in the temperature of the water flowing through ancient pipes.

They've modified themselves to match the cycles here. Their metabolism has slowed to something geological. A heartbeat every few minutes. Cell division measured in months. They could live for centuries at this pace, maybe longer, becoming more like the machinery they tend—essential components in a system that no longer resembles its original design but functions with its own peculiar perfection.

When they notice an observer—and they always notice, eventually—they don't startle or react with alarm. One might slowly turn its head, neck extending impossibly as vertebrae separate and stretch, until those unblinking eyes focus with an intensity that suggests they see more than surface, more than present. They evaluate. Calculate. Determine whether this new element disrupts their careful balance. Usually, they simply return to their work, having decided the intrusion is temporary, irrelevant to the longer patterns they serve.

But occasionally, very rarely, all the Tenders will stop and turn toward an observer at once. Their sealed mouths don't open, but something like invitation passes between them and you—a chemical message in the air, a shift in the subsonic frequencies, a warmth in the water that pools at your feet. They're offering something. A place in their work. A role in the tending. The transformation would be slow, they promise without words. Gentle. You wouldn't even notice until it was complete, and by then, you would understand why the patterns matter, why the creatures must flow just so, why the machinery dreams its metal dreams, and why someone must be here, always, to tend the boundary between what was built and what has grown.
