The Rememberers came first, though "first" means little when time pools and stagnates like the brine itself. They are not quite people—evolution here took different turns, or perhaps they devolved from something that once walked upright with purpose. Their bodies stretch horizontal, bellies never lifting from the wet salt, limbs splayed wide to distribute weight across the corrosive surface. Their skin has thickened into something between leather and mineral, crystalline deposits forming along their spines in ridges that mirror the salt formations around them.

They move in hibernation-slow migrations, covering perhaps a mile per month, following patterns only they perceive in the bacterial blooms. Their eyes have clouded to match the sky—not blind, but adapted to see through the fly-clouds, to read the subtle spectrum changes in the brine that signal the deep tides coming. They've learned to filter-feed, lying with mouths open to the fly swarms, their throats evolved with hair-like cilia that trap the insects while letting the air pass through. The constant drone of wings has become their language—they shape meanings by cupping hands over mouth and nose, modulating their breathing to create interruptions in the sound, conversations that take days to complete.

Memory, for them, is external. They secrete a resin—their bodies' attempt to protect them from the salt—that hardens into amber-like shells they shed monthly. These husks retain the shape of their thoughts, the chemical signature of what they've experienced. The salt preserves these shells indefinitely, and the Rememberers read them like books, running specialized tongue-pads over the surfaces to taste the past. The oldest shells date back centuries, maybe longer, creating a library of sensation scattered across the flats, half-buried, rising from the brine like the backs of sleeping animals.

Then there are the Dancers, who came later, or perhaps simultaneously—it's impossible to know. They are vertical where the Rememberers are horizontal, their evolution taking them up instead of out. Stick-thin beings with limbs like the striders', they've learned to walk on the surface tension itself, their feet broad and flat as lily pads, each step a careful calculation. They never stop moving—to stop is to sink, to sink is to dissolve. Their bones are hollow, filled with hydrogen-producing bacteria that keep them buoyant, light enough to stand on water.

The Dancers worship motion. They believe the world continues to exist only through their movement, that their steps keep the deep tides breathing, that the patterns they trace across the flats write the instructions for the bacterial mats to follow. They dance in groups of twelve, always twelve, weaving between each other in complexities that take years to complete. When one dies—usually from exhaustion, simply forgetting to eat while maintaining the endless motion—another takes their place immediately, learning the departed's steps from the others, ensuring the pattern never breaks.

They build nothing, own nothing except the salt-crystal bells they wear around their ankles that ring with each step, creating a counterpoint to the fly drone. Their children are born while they walk, caught by others in the group, raised in constant motion, carried until they can walk, then set down to join the eternal dance. They see the Rememberers as already dead, fossils that haven't realized they've stopped, while the Rememberers see the Dancers as not yet born, spirits still trying to arrive into being.

Between these two extremes live the Shapers—neither horizontal nor vertical but angular, joints that bend in too many places, allowing them to fold and unfold like origami. They are the builders of those abandoned salt structures, though "abandoned" isn't quite right—they build them, use them for a single purpose, then let them go, moving on to build again elsewhere. Each structure is an attempt to solve a problem they can't quite articulate: how to live in a place that erases, how to create in a landscape that only preserves through destruction.

The Shapers cultivate the bacterial mats, not for food but for color. They paint with living pigments, creating vast mandalas on the salt flats that slowly grow and shift as the bacteria spread. These designs are arguments, philosophies rendered in orange and purple, each Shaper adding to or subtracting from the patterns created by others. Sometimes the designs seem to influence the pulse-tides, bringing up specific minerals that feed certain bacteria, changing the colors available, adding new vocabulary to their visual language.

They've domesticated some of the striders, or perhaps entered into partnership with them. The modified striders have grown larger, their bodies now platforms that the Shapers ride across the brine, steering by shifting their weight, communicating through vibrations they send down the creatures' legs into the water. These strider-riders travel between the Rememberer grounds and the Dancer paths, translating between the two groups, though neither fully trusts them.

All three groups—if they can be called groups, if they can be called separate at all—share certain adaptations. Their lungs have developed secondary chambers that filter salt from the air. Their tears have become more valuable than water—thick, oil-like secretions that protect the eyes while barely any moisture is lost. They've learned to read the fly swarms like weather, knowing when the columns will twist, when they'll settle, when they'll rise in shapes that almost seem intentional, as if the flies themselves are trying to communicate something vast and distributed.

There are the hermits too, individuals who've left their groups or never belonged to any. They live in the salt pillars, hollowing them out from inside, their bodies slowly taking on the spiral shape of their homes. They speak to no one but the flies, believing that in the drone they can hear the voice of the landscape itself, that if they listen long enough, they'll understand what the salt flats are trying to become.

Some hermits have been in their pillars so long they've partially mineralized, their lower bodies fused with the salt, sustained by symbiotic relationships with the bacteria that now live in their blood. They claim to feel the deep breathing of the earth, to know when the pulse-tides will come days in advance, to taste changes in the brine that signal events years in the future. The others bring them offerings—shed Rememberer shells, bells from Dancers who've forgotten how to stop, pigments from the Shapers' palette—in exchange for prophecies that may or may not be madness.

Children here are rare and precious, but childhood is brief. The environment forces rapid adaptation—within weeks of birth, their skin begins to thicken, their eyes to cloud, their bodies to choose their path: horizontal, vertical, or angular. Some children refuse to choose, trying to remain universal, and these often don't survive their first year. But occasionally, rarely, one succeeds in remaining unspecialized, and these become something else entirely—the Witnesses, beings who can move between all states, who remember while dancing while shaping, who alone can see the salt flats as they truly are: not as a dead place or a dying place, but as a place that has transcended the need for those distinctions.

The Witnesses say the flies are not native here, that they came from somewhere else, brought something with them—a pattern, a purpose, a message that they're trying to deliver through sheer repetition and overwhelming presence. They say the bacterial mats are writing something in a language too large to read from ground level, that the pulse-tides are punctuation in a conversation between the deep earth and the shallow sky. They say the Rememberers, Dancers, and Shapers are all part of the same organism, differentiated like cells in a body, each serving a function in something too vast to comprehend.

But the Witnesses are few, and their words are often lost in the drone, their meanings dissolved in the brine like everything else that tries to assert itself too strongly here. The salt flats continue their patient work of reduction, of simplification, of wearing everything down to its essential nature. And the inhabitants—whatever they are, wherever they came from, whatever they're becoming—continue their slow, strange lives in the space between erosion and persistence, between memory and motion, between what was and what might never quite be.
