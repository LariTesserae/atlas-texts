In this fevered paradise, nothing truly dies—it only changes custody.

The things that might once have been human arrive in irregular migrations from the deeper passages, though "arrive" suggests more agency than they possess. They emerge like dreams half-remembered, their forms suggested rather than defined. The fungal forest has been working on them for generations, maybe centuries, until the boundary between flesh and mycelium has become academic.

They move through the understory in loose congregations of three to thirty, their passage marked by a peculiar quiet—the smaller creatures flee not in fear but in deference, creating pockets of relative stillness that follow them like shadows. Their skin, where it remains skin, has taken on the pallor of cave fish, translucent enough that the purple-black networks of fungal hyphae are visible beneath, pulsing with their heartbeat. These infections are not parasitic but collaborative; the fungus processes nutrients their simplified digestive systems can no longer handle, while they provide mobility and purpose the stationary forest craves.

Their eyes have not so much atrophied as transformed. The sockets remain, but within them grow clusters of photosensitive fungi, tiny mushrooms no larger than pinheads that glow faintly when stimulated. They see, after a fashion—not light and shadow but the bioelectric fields that surround every living thing, the chemical gradients that mark trails through the forest, the pheromone clouds that carry meaning.

They tend gardens, if gardens can be untended and still be gardens. Around certain pools, they've encouraged the growth of specific species—fungi that fruit in response to their presence, whose spores carry memories in their DNA. They eat these memories, incorporate them, share them through the network that connects them all. When one learns something new—the location of a new thermal vent, the rhythm of a migration pattern, the taste of a novel mineral deposit—they all learn it, eventually, as the information propagates through the fungal threads that web between them.

Their children are not born but budded. Growths appear on their backs and shoulders, fed by the fungal networks beneath their skin. These gradually take human shape, or something attempting it, before dropping free when they reach the size of a large cat. The young scuttle on all fours for their first years, their bones still soft, their skin permeable, absorbing nutrients directly from the forest floor. Only gradually do they stand upright, and even then, they remain capable of dissolving back into quadrupedal motion when speed is needed.

They build nothing permanent, but they shape the forest through selective cultivation. A trained eye might notice patterns—groves where the fungal trees grow in spirals, pools surrounded by specific species of luminescent organisms that create reading light, clearings kept clear not by cutting but by encouraging aggressive ground covers that prevent larger growth. Their architecture is living, temporary, collaborative.

Communication occurs below sound. They touch, and where they touch, hyphae beneath the skin intermingle, exchanging chemical signals that convey meaning far more complex than words. Sometimes they stand in circles, hands linked, for hours, their fungal passengers flowering simultaneously in response to whatever consensus is being reached. The forest around them responds to these gatherings—spore releases synchronize, phosphorescence brightens, the very air becomes thick with meaning.

They remember being human the way we remember being children—distantly, fondly, but without any desire to return. Some still carry artifacts from that time: a plastic comb worn smooth, its teeth long gone, worn as a pendant. A digital watch, its face dark for centuries, embedded in the fungal growth on a wrist. A wedding ring that has fused with the finger bone, visible through the translucent skin. These objects are not treasured or even noticed anymore; they're simply part of the body, like old scars.

The eldest among them no longer walk but root. When their time comes—and they always know—they find a suitable spot and stand still. Over days, then weeks, the fungal network thickens around their feet, drawing them down. Their skin splits, not in pain but in release, as fungal fruiting bodies emerge from within. They become trees, in a sense, their consciousness spreading out through the mycelial network, joining the vast, slow thought that permeates the forest floor. The others tend these grove-ancestors carefully, ensuring their fruiting bodies are harvested at the right times, their spores distributed according to old patterns.

They fear only dryness and cold, the twin killers that could end their symbiosis. When the volcanic breathing slows, when the pools begin to recede, they perform rituals—or perhaps they're purely practical actions that only look ritual to outside eyes. They carry water in their cupped hands, letting it trickle through their fingers onto specific spots. They breathe in unison at the thermal vents, their exhalations visible as clouds of spores that settle on the warm rocks, beginning new growth. They sing—not with voices but with their bodies, creating vibrations that encourage the flow of groundwater, that stimulate the volcanic activity below.

Occasionally, one is born different—more human than the rest, their skin resistant to fungal colonization. These throwbacks are not exiled but carefully tended, fed predigested nutrients, carried when the group moves. They're viewed as messages from the past, reminders of what was given up. When they die—and they always die young, unable to process the cave's air without fungal assistance—their bodies are placed in special pools where they decompose quickly, their unmodified DNA absorbed and archived by the forest for reasons the inhabitants feel but don't examine.

They are not unhappy. Happiness and unhappiness are concepts that require a kind of individual boundary they no longer maintain. They are part of something larger, cells in a superorganism that thinks in centuries and feels in chemical gradients. When they dream—and they do dream, collectively, their sleep synchronized by spore releases—they dream of growth, of spreading, of the day when the entire cave system will be one unified organism, every surface covered, every creature incorporated, every thought shared.

Sometimes, new humans stumble into their realm from the world above, explorers or refugees, still fully themselves. The inhabitants watch from the shadows, curious but patient. They know what will happen. The spores are already in the air, already in the newcomers' lungs. The forest is patient. It can wait. In a generation, maybe two, these new arrivals' descendants will stand in the circles, their hands linked, their skin translucent, their children budding from their backs.

The darkness breathes, and they breathe with it, no longer visitors but part of the respiration itself, the cave's way of knowing itself, of tending itself, of becoming more itself with each passing season in this place where seasons are marked not by the sun but by the rhythm of growth and dissolution, the pulse of the thermal vents, the endless migration of creatures through the twilight forest.

They have become what this place needed them to become: gardeners of the dark, keepers of the fungal groves, living bridges between the volcanic heat below and the creeping forest above. They are no longer human, but they are more themselves than they ever were in the sun.
