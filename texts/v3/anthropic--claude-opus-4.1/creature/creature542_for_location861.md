They came to be called the Gentle Monitors, though they rarely call themselves anything at all.

They arrived—or perhaps emerged—in the decade after the valley's abandonment, drawn by the particular frequency of failing transformers and the sweet decay of insulation foam. At first, they were mistaken for maintenance crews by the few observers stationed on the rim. They moved with the unhurried purpose of technicians, stopping at each piece of infrastructure, laying hands or approximations of hands on the machinery, heads tilted in concentration.

But their bodies were wrong for humans, even at a distance. Too tall when standing—nearly eight feet—yet capable of compressing themselves to pass through doorways built for ordinary workers. Their limbs bend in too many places, allowing them to fold themselves around turbine housings or slip through the gaps between transformer cooling fins. Their skin, if it is skin, has the matte texture of raw aluminum, pocked with thousands of tiny depressions that collect the morning dew. By noon, these divots steam gently as the moisture evaporates, giving them halos of personal mist.

They wear the moss. Not as clothing, but as symbionts. Different individuals cultivate different varieties across their bodies—some the electric blue-white moss that follows the buried conduits, others the dense breathing moss from the upper terraces. The moss grows in patterns unique to each Monitor, creating identifying designs that shift slowly over months as the colonies mature, die back, and regrow. The relationship is clearly mutualistic: the Monitors provide a mobile substrate and regular moisture, while the moss offers something less visible—perhaps processing the electrical fields that still ghost through the valley, perhaps filtering the strange chemistry of the rain.

Their faces are the most unsettling feature, not for any grotesque quality but for their excessive stillness. The features are almost human—eyes, nose, mouth—but arranged with a subtle wrongness, as if drawn from memory by someone who had only heard descriptions of faces. The eyes never blink but occasionally film over with a nictitating membrane that slides horizontally across the surface. Their mouths move only when drinking the dew they've collected, opening much wider than seems possible to reveal rows of structures that might be teeth but look more like the ceramic insulators from old power lines.

They communicate through touch and proximity. Place a hand on one of the old transformer stations while a Monitor works on the other side, and you'll feel it—not words, but intentions rendered in electrical pulses that make your muscles twitch in patterns that somehow convey meaning. *Adjustment necessary. Mineral deficiency in sector seven. Joy at the moss flowering.* The messages are practical, observational, sometimes almost tender in their attention to the valley's slow processes.

The Monitors understand the infrastructure completely, though they seem uninterested in restoring it to human functionality. Instead, they've repurposed it into something else—a vast sensory network, perhaps, or a distributed organism they tend like gardeners. They clear the vines from critical junction boxes not to restore power but to prevent the wrong kind of growth, the kind that would short-circuit the delicate balance they maintain. They guide the root acids away from load-bearing structures, ensuring the buildings decay gracefully rather than catastrophically.

Their own structures are nearly invisible until you know what to look for. Resting places woven from the living vines in the canopy of the hexagonal trees, shaped over years of patient training. Storage caches in the transformer stations, filled with collected materials—primarily metals and minerals extracted from the antifreeze lake, shaped into forms whose purpose remains opaque. Once, an observer found what might have been art: copper wires twisted into complex three-dimensional patterns and hung from the frozen turbine blades, oxidizing in calculated patterns that took months to fully develop.

They process death with particular attention. When one of the pale salamanders expires, or when the rare moth falls with burned wings, the Monitors collect the small corpses with surprising gentleness. These are taken to specific locations—always the same spots—where the bodies are arranged in precise orientations and left for the moss to claim. Within days, the decomposition produces blooms of unprecedented vibrancy, patches of growth that pulse with biological electricity for weeks afterward.

The youngest among them—if youth applies to such beings—are smaller, more volatile in their electrical presence. They flicker sometimes, their aluminum-textured skin briefly revealing something beneath that glows like heated metal. These ones are learning, following the elder Monitors through their rounds, mimicking their movements with an earnestness that would be charming if it weren't so alien. They're particularly drawn to the antifreeze lake, spending hours at its edge, their hands submerged, communing with whatever powers the algae in its depths.

During electrical storms, they become ecstatic. All of them, regardless of age or temperament, gather in the open spaces between the hexagonal trees. They stand with arms spread wide, the moss on their bodies bristling outward, creating vast surface areas to capture the electromagnetic fluctuations. They don't dance, exactly, but they move in slow, coordinated patterns that seem designed to channel the energy through their gathering, from one to another in chains of connection that include the infrastructure itself. The old machines respond, humming harmonies that resonate through the valley floor.

They may have names for each other—observers have noted consistent patterns in the electrical signatures they exchange—but these don't translate into human phonemes. The closest approximations are descriptive: The One Who Adjusts the Southern Transformers, The Keeper of Moss Patterns, The Deep Reader (who spends the most time at the lake).

What they're building, becoming, or maintaining remains unclear. They show no hostility to human observers but no particular interest either, treating them like weather patterns—things to be noted, adjusted for, but not engaged with directly. Their work continues with the patience of geology, each small action part of some larger pattern that might take centuries to complete.

At night, they don't sleep but enter a state of reduced activity, standing perfectly still among their chosen equipment, their bodies cooling until they match the ambient temperature exactly. In this state, they become nearly invisible, just another part of the infrastructure slowly being digested by time and transformed into something new. Only the faint pulsing of their moss colonies, synchronized to rhythms that match no human heartbeat, betrays their presence.

They are cultivating something in this valley—not just the plants or the remaining machinery, but the entire system, the interplay between the biological and electrical, the slow transformation of industrial death into some new form of life. They are its shepherds, its midwives, its patient monitors, waiting for whatever emerges from this bright, quiet crucible of moss and metal.
