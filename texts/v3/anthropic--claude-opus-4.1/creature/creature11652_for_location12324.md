The Tenders arrive before the mist thins, their rubber-soled boots silent on the wet concrete. They wear no uniform, but you know them by their movements—how they step without looking, navigating the maze of pipes by memory and the subtle variations in the mechanical chorus. Each carries a canvas satchel heavy with brass gauges, graphite, and lengths of hemp cord.

Old Matthias has worked the northwest quadrant for thirty-seven years. His fingers read the tremors in the pipes like braille, detecting blockages three joints away. He's developed a sixth sense for which valves will seize, which gaskets will fail by Thursday. The steam has carved permanent tracks down his face where his goggles sit. He keeps a notebook wrapped in oilcloth, filled with columns of temperatures and pressures in pencil so faint it's nearly invisible. He shares his lunch with the colonies of heat-beetles, who know his schedule and gather when they feel his footsteps on their pipes.

The Collectors move through after dawn, though dawn here is more concept than reality. They harvest what the wells bring up besides water—fragments of fossil shell, occasional nuggets of pyrite, the strange amber deposits that form in the oldest pipes. Elena leads them, a former geology student who never finished her degree but knows more about the district's substrate than any professor. She's mapped the old mine shafts that honeycomb the limestone below, charted where they intersect with natural caverns. Her crew of three speaks in hand signals over the noise, filling mesh bags with their geological prizes. They sell to jewelers, to collectors, to the university museum. Elena keeps the best specimens in a temperature-controlled shed at the district's edge, each labeled with extraction point and depth estimate.

The Ferals came first as children, runaways and throwaways who found the constant heat more welcoming than the streets. Now some are adults who've never left. They've built a village of salvaged materials in the dead zone where five broken pumps create a pocket of relative quiet. Their shelters incorporate the infrastructure—walls of corrugated metal fixed to defunct pipe housings, roofs that channel condensation into collection barrels. They've learned to read the wells' schedule like tide tables, knowing when each sector's runoff will peak, when the steam clouds will be thickest.

Marcus, their unofficial leader, worked as an engineer before something—he never says what—brought him here. He's rigged ingenious systems throughout their settlement: heat exchangers that warm their shelters, filters that clean the mineral-heavy water, a communal kitchen that uses steam vents for cooking. The younger Ferals follow him like apprentices, learning to splice pipes, to regulate pressure, to coax broken pumps into temporary function. They trade mechanical services to the Tenders for food and supplies, maintaining an economy of practical knowledge.

The Readers come at irregular hours, always alone. They're drawn by the rhythms, claiming the pumps encode messages in their syncopation. Dr. Yuen brings recording equipment, capturing hundreds of hours of mechanical sound that she analyzes for patterns. She's published papers on the "linguistic structures of industrial decay." The young man who calls himself Cipher sits cross-legged beside Well #47 every Tuesday, transcribing the pump-strokes into a notation system of his own invention. He believes the wells are counting something, marking time toward an event only they understand.

The Night Fishers work the pools between sunset and dawn, though sunset here just means the steam glows more orange than gray. They use fine nets and glass jars, hunting the translucent amphibians that emerge in darkness. These creatures fetch high prices in certain markets—some claim medicinal properties, others prize them as exotic pets. The Night Fishers never take more than their quota, understanding that overharvesting would end their livelihood. They've developed a complex honor system, marking territories with subtle arrangements of stones that only they recognize.

Jun is the most successful, a rail-thin woman who moves through the steam like a ghost. She knows which pools connect underground, can predict where the creatures will surface based on barometric pressure and pump cycles. Her catch goes to a buyer who appears weekly in an unmarked van, the transaction completed in minutes.

The Mourners arrive on anniversaries—of industrial accidents, of disappeared workers, of the great pump failure that scalded seventeen. They leave offerings at the base of specific wells: flowers that wilt immediately in the heat, photographs in plastic sleeves, small metal tokens stamped with names. Some stay for hours, listening to the mechanical breathing as if waiting for a response. Others just touch a particular pipe and leave.

Mrs. Chen comes on the third of every month, bringing chrysanthemums for her husband who vanished into a collapse forty years ago. The Ferals maintain her shrine without being asked, replacing flowers before they fully decay, keeping the photograph frame clean of condensation.

All of them—Tenders, Collectors, Ferals, Readers, Night Fishers, Mourners—orbit the wells in overlapping patterns, each group aware of but rarely acknowledging the others. They share the district like shifts in a factory that never closes, their schedules synchronized to the eternal rhythm of extraction. The machine-wells draw more than water from the earth; they draw these lives into their endless cycling, creating a community of the perpetually transient, bound together by steam and sound and the strange gravity of purposeless purpose.
