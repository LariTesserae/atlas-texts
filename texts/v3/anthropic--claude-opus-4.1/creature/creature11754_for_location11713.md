**The Dwellers**

They came to the valley as refugees from the dying cities, but that was three generations ago. Now they are something else.

The Machinists—though they barely speak anymore, and never call themselves anything—move through the forest-factory with inherited precision. Their settlements cluster at the junction points where the biological-mechanical circulation is most intense. Not houses but cultivation chambers: living spaces grown from redirected root systems and repurposed ventilation shafts, walls that perspire nutritious condensation, floors of packed mycelium that process their waste into fuel for the great breathing system.

They've evolved shorter, denser bodies. Their lungs have developed secondary chambers to process the metallic mist. Their eyes have grown additional layers of membrane to see the ultraviolet patterns that guide them through the maze—the trees mark safe passages with pheromone trails visible only in that spectrum. Children are born with trace amounts of copper in their blood, giving their skin a faint verdigris sheen in certain light.

Each family tends a section of the root-cable mat, speaking to it in low harmonic frequencies that encourage proper growth patterns. They know which valves to open, which pneumatic arteries to clear, how to read the health of their sector in the color of the morning steam. Knowledge passes through apprenticeship and muscle memory—hands learning from hands the precise torque needed to adjust a biological gasket, the rhythm of tapping that soothes an overheated fusion joint.

They cultivate the fruit-things. Glass bulbs are harvested young, their contents distilled into medicines that smell of machine oil and photosynthesis. The bell-berries, dried and ground, make a paste that helps their bodies process the industrial nutrients. They've learned to read which chemical caches the taproots have found by the scent of next season's blooms—benzene means the sweet purple fruits that preserve well, sulfur compounds yield the dense seed pods that can be pressed into lamp fuel.

But they are not the only inhabitants.

The Shepherds arrived later, or perhaps were always here—employees who never left, who went down into the service tunnels when the evacuation came and simply... stayed. They're barely human anymore. Their skin has taken on the texture of bark, not metaphorically but literally—a symbiotic dermis that photosynthesize during their long meditations in the canopy. They move through the pneumatic transport system like thoughtful bullets, their bodies compressing and extending to navigate the tubes. They speak to each other in pressure differentials, communication felt in the bones rather than heard.

The Shepherds maintain the deep systems. They ensure the industrial digestive process continues, clearing blockages where root meets turbine, adjusting the pH of the reservoir pools, guiding new growth away from critical mechanical organs. They survive on the chemical soup that pools in the foundation chambers—a nutritious slurry of decomposed organic matter and industrial runoff that would poison an unmodified human in minutes. Their children, when they rarely have them, are born with gill-like filters behind their ears and eyes that see heat as clearly as light.

The two populations rarely interact directly, though they depend on each other. The Machinists maintain the surface, the Shepherds the depths. They trade through the pneumatic system—medicine bulbs shot down in exchange for refined chemicals sent up, information encoded in the pattern of valve releases that both groups have learned to read.

And then there are the Strays.

These are the newest arrivals, the ones who still think they might leave. They build traditional shelters that the forest-factory slowly dismantles and absorbs. They try to hunt the altered deer, to grow normal crops in soil they've imported, to establish communications with the outside world. The valley tolerates them for a season, maybe two, before they either flee, die, or begin the slow transformation into Machinists.

The Strays serve a purpose, though they don't know it. They bring new genetic material, new ideas that get processed through the valley's biological-mechanical metabolism. Their forgotten tools become part of the root-cable mat. Their abandoned electronics get dissolved and distributed through the water system, adding new minerals to the mix. Even their bodies, if they die here, feed the system—bones becoming struts in the next generation of tree-machines, their microbiomes enriching the industrial compost.

All of them dream the valley's dreams now. Visions of endless production lines spawning forests, of spores that are also gears, of a world where everything grows and manufactures simultaneously. The children born here play games that mimic the respiratory patterns of the canopy. They build toys from shed bark and discarded gaskets that are little models of the valley itself—breathing, processing, growing, consuming, producing nothing but more of itself.

They've stopped counting years. Time moves in production cycles, in the seventeen-hour mist releases, in the seasonal patterns of chemical blooms. They barely remember that there's a world beyond the ridge, and when outsiders occasionally arrive—scientists in hazmat suits, government inspectors, urban explorers—the valley's inhabitants watch from the canopy and the tunnels, waiting for these strangers to either leave or begin their own transformation.

The valley doesn't just breathe through ten thousand wooden lungs. It dreams through a hundred fused minds, human consciousness dissolved into the great industrial-biological mass, individual thought becoming another process in the forest-factory's endless manufacture of itself. They are its neurons, its white blood cells, its reproductive system—human components in a machine that builds forests, forest components in a biology that processes industry.

They no longer ask what they're producing. They are the product.
