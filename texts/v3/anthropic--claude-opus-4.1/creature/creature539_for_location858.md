The people here have become half-forest themselves, their bodies adapted over generations to this vertical world. Their feet are broad and gripping, toes splayed wide, the soles tough as bark but sensitive enough to read the health of a branch through its vibration. Children develop calluses on their insteps before they can properly walk, from wrapping their feet around branches as they're carried on their parents' backs. By adulthood, their ankles flex in ways that ground-dwellers' cannot, allowing them to lock their feet around beams while working with both hands free.

Their lungs process the thick air differently—deeper, slower breaths that extract oxygen from the mist itself. Newcomers wheeze and gasp for weeks before adapting, if they adapt at all. The permanent residents move through the humid atmosphere like fish through water, their bodies sheened constantly with moisture that never quite counts as sweat. Their skin hosts its own ecosystem—beneficial fungi that prevent rot, symbiotic algae that tint the elderly slightly green around the joints, giving them an almost photosynthetic glow in the filtered sunlight.

They organize themselves by tree-clan rather than bloodline. The Fig People control the mother tree and thus the main markets. The Cedar Folk, living in those ancient towers, are the memory-keepers, their platforms carved with generations of history in a script that mimics the patterns of bark. The Cecropia Dwellers are the farmers, managing the fast-growing trees at the settlement's edges, harvesting their hollow stems for pipes and containers. Each clan knows the moods of their trees—when the sap runs sweet, when the wood grows brittle, when disease creeps through the cambium.

The shamans read futures in the mycelial networks that web between the trees, pressing their ears to the bark to hear the chemical conversations between roots. They cultivate specific mushrooms that fruit in patterns, interpreting the arrangements like thrown bones. Some have lived so long in meditation platforms—tiny chambers barely large enough to sit in—that their hair has become indistinguishable from the moss that surrounds them, and birds nest in their stillness.

The hunters are different creatures entirely. They wear the skins of sloths for camouflage, moving at the pace of branches in wind. Some bind their children's arms from birth in particular ways, creating elongated reaches that let them span impossible gaps. The best hunters can hang by one hand for hours, their bodies having learned to redirect blood flow to prevent numbness, their shoulders evolved into something more like a gibbon's than a typical human's. They know every species of monkey by its morning call, can identify a bird by the sound its wings make in flight, track prey through layers of canopy by the pattern of disturbed water-drops below.

Craftspeople here work with what lives. The weavers don't just create baskets—they grow them, training vines into shapes over months, creating containers that continue living, sprouting leaves that must be trimmed. The builders are more like surgeons, understanding how to cut a branch so it heals stronger, how to graft different species to create living joints that flex without breaking. They maintain treaties with the carpenter ants, leaving specific offerings to keep them from hollowing the wrong beams.

The children barely distinguish themselves from the troops of monkeys they run with. They learn to read the canopy's moods before they learn human speech—knowing which birds signal snake presence, which butterfly migrations mean storms approaching, which fungal blooms indicate a tree is dying and will soon fall. They play games that would horrify ground-dwellers: racing blindfolded along familiar branches, dropping through gaps to catch themselves stories below, swinging from vine to vine in complex patterns that are half-dance, half-dare.

The elderly don't retire—they root. As their mobility decreases, they take up residence in the most stable crooks of the oldest trees, becoming libraries, becoming judges, becoming the still points around which the settlement's constant motion revolves. Some grow so still that epiphytes begin colonizing their platforms, their homes becoming gardens that they tend from within. The oldest are visited like oracles, their years of stillness having taught them to sense the forest's deeper rhythms—the seven-year cycles of certain flowers, the century-patterns of rainfall, the slow migrations of the trees themselves as they lean toward gaps in the canopy.

Death here is not burial but elevation. Bodies are placed on the highest platforms, where the vultures and the elements can clean them quickly. The bones that remain are hung in the trees as wind chimes, their hollow songs joining the forest's chorus. Children grow up knowing which bones belong to which ancestors by their tone—grandmother's femur that whistles in strong wind, the uncle whose ribs chatter like laughter.

These people dream in three dimensions, their sleeping minds navigating branch-paths that don't exist, finding fruit on trees that haven't fruited in decades, falling without fear because in dreams, there's always another branch to catch them. They wake already swaying, bodies compensating for wind that isn't there, and must consciously still themselves—a reversal of how ground-dwellers must consciously balance.

This is what humanity becomes when it gives up the horizontal world: something between primate and bird, between plant and animal, their lives so interwoven with the forest that removing them would be like pulling out veins from a leaf. They have traded the solidity of earth for the constant motion of life itself, and in doing so, have become something that breathes with ten thousand lungs, that lives with ten thousand heartbeats, that dreams with ten thousand minds all tangled in the canopy's embrace.
