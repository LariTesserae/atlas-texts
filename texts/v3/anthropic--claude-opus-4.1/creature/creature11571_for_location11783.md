The people who inhabit these stone shelves move with a particular economy of motion—not hurried, but deliberate, as if they've internalized the geological patience of their surroundings. They are predominantly short and broad-shouldered, with barrel chests adapted to the thin air. Their lungs, when examined by the city's physicians, show unusual development—extra capillaries branching through the tissue like the ferns that grow from their walls.

They dress in layers of waxed canvas and boiled wool, materials that shed the constant moisture while trapping body heat. Everyone owns several sets of these clothes, rotating them through drying rooms built above the factory furnaces. The persistent dampness has made them fastidious about fabric care—mold is the constant enemy. Their boots have thick cork soles, carved with channels that direct water away from the foot. Children learn early to walk heel-to-toe on the wet stone, developing a rolling gait that visitors find peculiar but natives don't notice.

Their skin tends toward a particular pallor—not unhealthy, but adapted to the filtered light. Yet their cheeks carry a permanent flush from the cool air and the effort of breathing at altitude. Hair grows thick and tends to curl in the humidity. Most keep it short or tightly bound, wrapped in waterproof scarves that have evolved into elaborate cultural markers. The patterns of these head wrappings indicate profession, family lineage, and district of residence.

The factory workers comprise the largest portion of the population. They operate the steam hammers and ore processors, maintaining the ancient mechanisms that extract rare minerals from the mountain's exposed veins. Their hands are permanently stained by the metallic dust, creating patterns like tattoos that mark their trade. They've developed a sign language for communication over the machinery's muffled roar, fingers flicking in patterns that echo the movement of gears and pistons.

But there are also the moss farmers, who tend the vertical gardens with religious devotion. They know every species that grows on these heights, cultivating certain varieties for medicine, others for the barely-fermented tea that everyone drinks to ward off altitude sickness. These farmers move through the city on networks of rope ladders, as comfortable scaling walls as walking on level ground. Their children play games leaping between terraces, using hooked poles to swing from the overhead pipes.

The water readers form another crucial group—engineers and mystics in equal measure who monitor the flow through the collection systems. They can predict weather changes by the sound of dripping in the cisterns, feel earthquakes coming in the vibration of the pipes. Their apprenticeships last decades, learning to read the mountain's hydrology like a sacred text.

In the deep factories, the furnace tenders work in shifts that follow no solar schedule. The constant twilight has freed them from conventional time; they sleep when tired, work when needed. Some families haven't seen true daylight in generations, their eyes adapted to the glow of molten metal and electric arc lamps. They've developed their own culture in these depths—songs that match the rhythm of the steam engines, stories told in the breaks between furnace loadings.

The elderly here don't retire but transition to different work. Those whose lungs can no longer handle the factories become teachers, archivists, or cloud watchers—the latter a respected profession that involves reading weather patterns weeks in advance. They sit at the terrace edges with specialized telescopes, peering through brief gaps in the cloud cover to track distant storms.

Children grow up scrambling through this vertical maze, their games incorporating the industrial architecture as naturally as others might use trees or fields. They slide down the water channels on boards of waxed wood, race moths through the factory light pools, build small engines from scrap parts that mimic the larger machines their parents operate. Education happens in shifts like everything else—morning classes for some, evening for others, with the constant gray light making all times equally suitable for learning.

There are also the strangers among them—those who arrived from the lowlands seeking work or refuge and never adapted fully to the altitude. They live in the middle terraces where the air is thickest, operating shops that trade goods brought up on the few functional cable cars. Their children, born here, become intermediaries—fully adapted to the heights but retaining connections to the world below the clouds.

The most mysterious residents are the cable walkers—maintenance crews who venture out on the ancient lines that disappear into the mist. They claim to have contact with other plateau cities, returning with stories of different adaptations, different solutions to the same challenges of height and moisture and worn stone. Some don't return at all, and their absence is marked by small shrines where their cable meets the terrace edge—offerings of moss tea and factory-made trinkets left for those who walk between the clouds.

All of them share certain traits: patience with the weather's moods, acceptance of the constant damp, and a deep knowledge of their city's vertical geography. They navigate by factory smoke as much as by sight, know the sound of each pump and engine, can identify their location by the species of fern growing on the walls. They've developed a culture of small communal kindnesses—shared umbrella networks, public drying rooms, the universal offering of hot tea to anyone who looks chilled.

In this city worn smooth by ages, they've become smooth themselves—their social edges eroded by the necessity of close living, their conflicts settled with the same patience that water uses to carve stone. They measure success not in conquest of their environment but in harmony with it, building their lives into the mountain's slow rhythms, their industrial ambitions tempered by geological time.
