Those who dwell in the Breathing Grove are called the Driftborn, though they rarely use the name themselves—language has become something they exhale more than speak, a pheromone-poetry that the beetles translate into wing-vibration.

The eldest among them is indistinguishable from the grove's infrastructure. What might have been a woman centuries ago has spread herself through an entire wing of the organism, her circulatory system now indistinguishable from the sap-vessels, her thoughts traveling as chemical cascades through the wood grain. Children learn to read her moods by the temperature of the bark in certain corridors. When she dreams, new chambers fruit from her extended body like tumors of living space, already furnished with furniture grown from her fingernails and hair.

The Driftborn move like swimmers through honey, having adapted to the grove's variable physics. Their bones have become hollow and multiplicative—breaking a limb means it branches into two or three new appendages, each equally functional. They've learned to navigate by taste, licking the walls to understand where passages will open next. Their children are born with translucent skin that shows the chlorophyll-green blood underneath, and by adolescence, most have developed breathing holes along their spines that whistle in harmony with the beetles.

There is a caste of cultivators whose eyes have migrated to the palms of their hands, the better to examine the garden-tumors they tend. They prune thoughts from the growths—literal thoughts that bud like fruit, each containing a single perfect memory from someone who lived before the grove lifted from the earth. These memories are their currency, their sustenance, their drug. Eating one means experiencing another life so completely that many cultivators forget which body originally belonged to them.

The young navigate the grove in packs, their bodies linked by umbilical vines they've coaxed to grow from their navels. They move as colonial organisms, sharing nutrients and consciousness, breaking apart only to sleep or mate. Some never individuate again, becoming permanent hybrid creatures with multiple heads but a single distributed nervous system, speaking in harmonized whispers that make the beetle-song modulate into new keys.

At the grove's apex lives something that might once have been twins—or perhaps a single person who divided like a cell. They maintain the grove's levitation through a kind of biological mathematics, sweating calculations that crystallize on their skin as salt-equations. They haven't moved in forty years, fused at the spine, facing opposite directions, their hair grown into the ceiling as neural wiring. One twin experiences time normally; the other lives it backward, meeting in the present to exchange information about what will and has happened.

The beetle-keepers have evolved beyond human recognition. Their bodies are living hives, hollow cavities in their torsos where the scarabs nest. They've developed a secondary skeleton of chitin that mirrors their charges, and their movements have become arthropod-precise, jerky and efficient. They communicate with their beetles through chemical songs, sweating complex pheromone symphonies that choreograph the million-winged orchestra. When a beetle-keeper dies, their body doesn't decay—it transforms into a nursery where new beetles gestate, emerging with fragments of human memory encoded in the patterns of their wing casings.

There are the Sleepers, who discovered certain chambers where time moves so slowly that they age minutes for every decade. They live their lives in shifts—awake for a day, asleep for a century, guardians of the grove's longest memories. Their presence anchors the settlement's history, though their accounts of the past have become increasingly unreliable as their dreams blend with reality during their long suspensions.

Children of the grove don't play—they experiment. Born with an intuitive understanding of the organism's biology, they reshape their environment like soft clay, teaching walls to grow windows, convincing floors to sprout furniture. Their games involve exchanging body parts, temporary trades that sometimes become permanent when they forget whose arm originally belonged to whom. They age in spirals rather than lines, sometimes arriving at adulthood before adolescence, their temporal development as fluid as the grove's architecture.

The most unsettling inhabitants are the Echoes—people who entered chambers where time eddied too violently and became smeared across multiple moments. They exist in several places simultaneously, their consciousness fractured across past and future selves that occasionally align to form a complete person before dispersing again. Conversations with Echoes require patience; responses might arrive days before questions are asked.

At the boundary between grove and void live the Dandlers—humans who've extended their limbs into the hanging roots, becoming part of the grove's exterior nervous system. They taste the salt marsh's evaporation, feel the pressure changes that predict weather, and signal this information inward through tremors in their elongated bones. Their faces have migrated to their chests to better watch the marsh below, leaving their heads as smooth, sense-organ domes that detect vibrations from the beetles' wings.

The grove's inhabitants no longer reproduce so much as propagate. Pregnancy means developing a bud that might become a child, a new room, or something between the two. Some offspring are mobile, others architectural. The distinction has ceased to matter. Families are defined not by genetics but by which chambers their various components occupy, relationships mapped through the grove's ever-shifting floorplan.

They measure time by the Speaker's pronouncements, spacing their lives between prophecies that arrive as spore-floods. When particularly dense clouds emerge, the entire population goes still, letting the spores settle and absorb, experiencing collective visions that organize their society for the next cycle. These shared hallucinations are their governance, their religion, their entertainment—a direct democracy of dreams where every inhabitant votes by how they metabolize the prophetic fungi.

This is humanity unwound and rewoven, a species discovering how many ways it can stretch the definition of human and still recognize itself in the mirror—though mirrors here show not reflections but possibilities, and recognition itself has become just another negotiable term in the grove's endless biological mathematics.
