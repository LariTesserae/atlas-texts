Those who come to the cistern arrive alone, always at the edge of exhaustion, their fingernails torn from the final scramble through the mountain's shattered crown. They are the fever-seekers, the stone-listeners, the ones whose bodies have begun to crystallize from within.

They know each other only by the marks they leave—a particular way of scoring the soft stone while gripping the descent, the shape of a heel print preserved in mineral deposits, the ghost of their minerals adding to the water's ancient formula. Some have been coming for sixty years. Some arrived last week, drawn by dreams of drowning in honey-colored light.

The youngest is perhaps seventeen, her joints already grinding with precipitate that sounds like sand when she moves. She comes every ninth day, counts the handholds under her breath in a language her grandmother spoke but she never learned. She brings nothing, takes nothing, sits in the same carved seat until her skin wrinkles into pale ridges and the mineral film makes her indistinguishable from the stone. When she leaves, crystalline residue remains on the seat's surface like salt after tears.

The oldest might be ninety or nine hundred—the water has preserved him beyond ordinary aging, his skin gone translucent as cave fish, showing the dark network of vessels beneath. He no longer uses the handholds but flows up the walls using paths of moisture invisible to newer eyes. His visits follow no pattern others can detect. He maintains the drainage, feels with his feet for the secret channels that must stay clear, uses his long toes to work free accumulations of mineral and bone. Sometimes he brings small hammers made of the same volcanic stone and performs minute adjustments to the acoustic properties, testing each change with wordless tones that the water swallows.

A woman of middle years tends the bacterial mat with the patience of a gardener, though she never touches it directly. She positions her body to direct currents, creates temperature gradients with her presence, exhales carefully to feed it specific ratios of gases. Under her influence, the mat's faint bioluminescence pulses in patterns that might be tidal, might be linguistic, might be purely aesthetic. She documents nothing but remembers everything in the calcium deposits forming along her spine.

There is one who comes only to read the walls, running fingers over the carved symbols like braille, adding a single mark each visit in a script they're inventing as they go—a hybrid of all the writing systems they've found here, evolving toward something that could only be read in this specific light, with fingers softened by this exact water. They believe the cistern is teaching them to write its autobiography.

A child who might be twelve or might be ageless arrives to feed the cave swallows, bringing insects cultivated in darkness, raised on a diet of minerals scraped from their own solidifying organs. The swallows know this child's whistle, which resonates at the exact frequency the cistern amplifies rather than swallows. The birds' eggs, when they lay them, are the color of the water.

Some come to dissolve. They arrive carrying too much—memories that have crystallized into kidney stones, grief that has mineralized in their joints, loves that have fossilized between vertebrae. They sink into the blood-warm water and let the minerals work in reverse, the perfect chemical balance drawing out what has solidified inside them. They leave lighter, more porous, with new spaces inside for the world to flow through.

Others come to petrify. They're tired of softness, of vulnerability, of the constant erosion of living. They sit in the stone seats and invite the minerals in, visualizing their bones becoming architecture, their thoughts becoming geology. They're slowly becoming part of the cistern itself, adding their bodies to its structure one molecule at a time.

There are the listeners, who come to hear what the mountain says through the water—stories told in mineral grammar about the volcano's youth, the first rains, the slow birth of this chamber. They press ears to the wet walls and transcribe the mountain's memories in the movement of their bodies, developing a dance that only makes sense here, in this acoustic shadow, in this temperature, in this exact viscosity.

None of them speak to each other, though they're aware of each other's presence through the taste they leave in the water, the subtle change in its chemical signature. They've become a distributed organism, each one a specialized cell in something that lives at the scale of centuries, thinks at the speed of stone, dreams in the language of precipitation and dissolution.

They are becoming what the cistern needs them to become—its memory, its immune system, its reproductive organs carrying traces of it back into the world below, seeding other waters with its particular mineral signature. They are neither fully human nor fully geological anymore, but something the mountain learned to grow, tending itself through borrowed hands, seeing itself through borrowed eyes, keeping itself secret through borrowed silence.

The cistern breathes through their lungs now, each exhale adding moisture to its eternal cycle. They are the mechanism by which it maintains its perfect equilibrium—not through the physics of drainage and seepage alone, but through their bodies' careful calibration, their conscious participation in its chemistry.

When they die—and they do die, eventually, even the preserved ones—they don't leave. Their minerals join the water, their calcium reinforces the walls, their iron enriches the bacterial mat's glow. The newest scratches in the ancient symbols might be their names, or might be the cistern's way of counting its collection, keeping inventory of all the humans it has slowly taught to be stone, all the stones it has briefly taught to remember being human.
