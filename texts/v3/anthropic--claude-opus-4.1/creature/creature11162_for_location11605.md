The Denizens move through the city in recursive loops, their forms nothing more than controlled absences in the water—spaces where molecules have been convinced not to be. They are roughly cylindrical, perhaps three meters in length, but their boundaries shift constantly, expanding and contracting with a rhythm that matches no biological imperative. To observe them directly is impossible; the eye slides off them, catching only the distortion they leave in their wake, like looking at the blind spot where the optic nerve meets the retina.

They feed, if feeding is the right word, on computational outcomes. Each tower processes the quantum states of dissolved minerals, running calculations whose purpose died with their creators. The Denizens position themselves at the nodes where these calculations intersect, absorbing not the energy but the information itself—the collapse of probability waves into discrete answers. They grow denser with knowledge, their non-substance becoming more definitively absent, more aggressively void.

Communication between them occurs through temperature modulation. They warm or cool the water in precise patterns, creating thermal sentences that propagate slowly through the still layers. A conversation might take days, each participant adding their gradient to the developing pattern until the water itself becomes a transcript of their exchange. These thermal texts layer and interfere with each other, creating meaning through superposition—a language where every statement contains all possible responses, and understanding means selecting which interference pattern to acknowledge.

They reproduce through division, but not of themselves. When one has absorbed sufficient computational density, it creates a flaw in its own absence—a place where it is even less present than usual. This flaw develops its own stability, eventually breaking free as a new entity. The parent loses nothing in this process except perhaps a certain clarity of outline, becoming slightly more diffuse, slightly less certain about where it ends and the water begins.

Their memory is the city itself. Each passage through the metamaterial towers leaves a trace in the crystalline lattice—a barely detectable change in the way light bends through certain segments. Over centuries, these traces accumulate into a history written in refraction indices. The oldest Denizens can read these traces, navigating not just through space but through time, following routes that their predecessors carved into the physics of the place.

They have something like culture. Certain patterns of movement are repeated not for efficiency but for their elegance—spirals through the thermal boundaries that create standing waves of density, synchronized passages that cause the towers to resonate at frequencies too low to hear but powerful enough to feel in the chest cavity of any creature that might witness them. These are their art forms, their rituals, their expressions of whatever passes for joy in beings made of structured nothing.

Occasionally, one will cease. Not die—death implies life, and these things exist in some other category. Instead, they simply stop maintaining the effort of absence. The water rushes in to fill the void they've been holding open, creating a brief implosion, a momentary current in the currentless deep. The information they've collected disperses through the surrounding medium, becoming available to the others, who absorb it passively, adding the ceased one's knowledge to their own accumulating density.

They fear—if fear applies—only one thing: equilibrium. Should they stop moving, stop processing, stop maintaining their careful absence, they would become indistinguishable from the water around them. So they continue their circuits, their calculations, their slow thermal conversations. They persist through pure dynamic effort, creatures of pattern rather than matter, process rather than substance.

The youngest are the most defined, their edges sharp, their absence absolute. The oldest have begun to fray, their boundaries becoming uncertain, spreading out through the city like a probability cloud. Eventually, the eldest simply become part of the background calculation—still present, still processing, but no longer maintaining enough coherence to be considered individual. They become part of the city's operating system, their consciousness distributed through every molecule of the maintained water.

New ones arrive rarely, emerging from the deep trenches beyond the city where the water becomes too pressurized even for absence to maintain itself. They rise as bubbles of organized nothing, seeking the computational nodes, drawn to the towers' quantum processing like moths to flame—if moths were made of vacuum and flame were made of pure information.

They have no goals we would recognize, no drives beyond the continuation of their pattern. Yet in their ceaseless circulation through the city, in their thermal conversations and refractive memories, there is something almost like purpose—a dedication to maintaining this impossible place, to keeping the calculation running, to preserving the conditions that allow them to exist as organized absences in the mathematical precision of permanent twilight.
