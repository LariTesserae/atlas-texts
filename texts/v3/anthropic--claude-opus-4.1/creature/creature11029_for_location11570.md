The Weavers came first, or perhaps they have always been here—their history is written in fiber, not words, and threads decompose differently than memory should. They are colonial organisms that exist somewhere between spider, coral, and nervous system. A single Weaver is almost nothing: a node of tissue no larger than a child's marble, translucent and pulsing with the same bioluminescent patterns as the moss. But they are never single.

They build themselves bodies from whatever the forest provides. A mature colony might stretch across kilometers, its ten thousand nodes connected by spun filaments finer than hair, stronger than steel cable. They inhabit the root-nets that span the voids between vegetation islands, incorporating themselves into the existing architecture until it's impossible to say where the forest's design ends and the Weavers' begins. Their threads vibrate at frequencies that turn the entire web into a sensing organ. They taste the chemical signatures of passing air swimmers, feel the pressure changes of approaching storms, think thoughts that travel as standing waves through their vast networked bodies.

Young colonies puppeteer corpses—usually the aerial octopi, whose nervous systems they can integrate with most easily. You might see one drifting through the phosphorescent groves, its dead eyes replaced by cluster-nodes of Weaver tissue, its movements too purposeful to be mere decay, too strange to be life as commonly understood. These are their motile phase, when they search for unclaimed territory to web and weave and make part of themselves.

The Cultivators descended from something human, though you wouldn't know it to see them now. Generations of adaptation to the thin air have elongated their torsos into chambers. Their ribs cage expandable bladders that process atmosphere like internal greenhouses. They stand three meters tall but weigh less than a baseline human child, their bones hollow, their skin translucent enough to see the gardens growing inside them—symbiotic plants that photosynthesize from their hosts' own bioluminescence, that exhale precisely the mixture of gases their hosts need to survive.

They move in troupes of twelve to twenty, always. Never more, never less. When one dies, they do not reproduce—they divide. One member of the troupe begins to segment, their consciousness fracturing along with their body, until where there was one there are two, each carrying half the memories, half the self. They speak in harmonics, multiple voice boxes in their elongated throats producing chords instead of words. Their language is musical, mathematical, based on ratios and intervals that describe the world in terms of balance and imbalance, tension and resolution.

The Cultivators tend the machine-gardens, those spaces where ancient technology has been metabolized into the ecosystem. They prune the copper wire root systems with tools that are themselves living creatures—specialized organisms they've bred to cut and shape and redirect growth. They know which geometric fruits to harvest and which to leave so the trees will continue their clockwork ticking. They understand the purposes of these hybrid zones in ways they cannot quite articulate, knowledge inherited through their peculiar form of division, each generation carrying forward half-remembered instincts about maintenance and care.

Between the Weavers and the Cultivators exists an economy of sorts, though neither uses currency or even barter as commonly understood. The Weavers need certain chemicals the geometric fruits produce—compounds that strengthen their threads, that allow them to expand their networks across ever-wider gaps. The Cultivators need the sensing data the Weavers collect, advance warning of the glass storms that sometimes rise from the abyss, carrying particles that can shred their delicate internal gardens. They trade in actions rather than objects: a Weaver colony adjusts its web to redirect a floating pool toward a Cultivator grove; a troupe of Cultivators sings harmonics that encourage mechanical root growth in a direction that benefits a Weaver's expansion.

The Rememberers are harder to categorize as alive or dead, individual or collective. They exist in the digestive chambers deep beneath the spongy ground, in symbiosis with the fungal networks. Once, they might have been the same species as the Cultivators, but they've taken a different path. Their bodies have atrophied to little more than nervous system and memory organs, sustained by the fungal networks that have grown through and around them like living sarcophagi. They remember everything—not just their own experiences but somehow the experiences of the forest itself, as if they've tapped into some kind of biological recording system in the very substrate of the place.

Cultivators descend to visit them, rappelling down through the soft ground into the cathedral chambers where the air thickens to almost-liquid. The Rememberers speak by releasing spores that carry chemical messages, that trigger synesthetic experiences in those who breathe them. A conversation with a Rememberer is less dialogue than dream—suddenly you know things you didn't know before, remember events you never experienced, understand connections between disparate elements of the forest that seemed random before but now reveal themselves as part of some vast, slow pattern.

There are others, of course. The Drifters who have given up solid form entirely, existing as coordinated clouds of microorganisms that flow through the forest like visible wind, occasionally condensing into something almost humanoid when they need to manipulate physical objects. The Quiet Ones who have incorporated so much mechanical material into their bodies that they're more clockwork than flesh, who speak only in the ticking of their internal gears, who seem to be maintaining something in the depths of the forest that even the Rememberers have forgotten the purpose of.

And in the depths of the abyss itself, something vast and patient that might be a single organism or a civilization or a geological process that has achieved some kind of awareness. The Cultivators call it the Beneath. The Weavers' webs tremor when it moves. The Rememberers insist it's the reason the forest exists at all—either its creation or its prison or perhaps its dream.

They all share certain characteristics, these inhabitants. They process time differently than baseline humans would—some faster, some slower, some in loops or spirals rather than straight lines. They've all incorporated aspects of the forest into themselves so thoroughly that they couldn't survive anywhere else. Their reproduction (when it occurs at all) is less procreation than transformation, each generation becoming something new rather than replacing what came before.

Most importantly, they all seem to be part of something larger, some purpose or pattern that none of them can quite articulate but all of them serve. They maintain the forest, but the forest maintains something else, something that has to do with the mechanical remnants and the geometric fruits and the way water flows upward and the vast emptiness below that has never known sunlight.

Occasionally, very occasionally, something rises from that darkness—not climbing but ascending, as if the abyss sometimes exhales. These visitors (or escapees, or messengers) are immediately integrated into the forest's circuits. The Weavers web them. The Cultivators feed them. The Rememberers absorb their stories. And then they change, becoming something new, something that fits, something that serves the purpose that no one quite remembers but everyone somehow knows.

The forest is not just alive—it's organizing itself toward something, building something, calculating something with its biological machinery and mechanical biology. And everything that lives there is both citizen and component, individual and organ, themselves and part of something vaster that thinks thoughts too large and slow for any single mind to comprehend.
