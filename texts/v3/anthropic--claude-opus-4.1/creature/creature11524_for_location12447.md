The builders themselves are perhaps two hands long, their bodies segmented like a centipede's but broader, flatter—built for compression rather than speed. Each segment bears a pair of specialized limbs: the forward ones equipped with serrated edges that can shave mineral deposits into powder, the middle ones featuring cups and scoops for carrying, the rear ones tipped with glands that weep various secretions—acids, adhesives, pheromone trails that guide the work.

Their shells are translucent amber, like cooked sugar, and through them you can see the constant churning of internal processes—digestive chambers processing clay and salts, specialized organs that separate minerals by type, bladders that store the different building compounds they'll later excrete. They have no eyes. Instead, their heads are dominated by heat-sensing pits arranged in complex patterns, allowing them to navigate entirely by thermal gradient. They feel their way through their city by reading the temperature variations in every surface.

They move in rivers—dense streams of bodies flowing up and down the towers, across the bridges, through tunnels worn smooth by millions of passages. Individual builders are impossible to track; they vanish into the mass and reemerge elsewhere, indistinguishable. When they work, they lock together like puzzle pieces, forming living scaffolds and chains that can span gaps or reach high places. You'll see them create temporary structures from their own bodies—arches of interlocked builders that hold for hours while others cement new sections in place.

The flies that drink the moisture are themselves strange—their wings are more like fins, evolved for pushing through the heavy air. They never land, instead hovering at a fixed distance from surfaces, their proboscises extended into the water film that coats everything. They've developed a crystalline fur that repels water while somehow extracting it, so they appear to be covered in tiny prisms that split the bronze light into brief rainbows.

The blind fish in the thermal pools have lost all pigment except for veins of metallic blue that branch across their skin—some kind of copper compound in their blood that helps them process the mineral-rich water. They've evolved past the need for proper swimming; instead, they undulate slowly through water so thick with dissolved salts that it's almost syrup. Their mouths have become filters, lined with hundreds of fine teeth that strain algae-like organisms too small to see. Sometimes they beach themselves on the warm mud shores, lying there for hours absorbing heat through their skin before sliding back into the pools.

But the most unsettling inhabitants are the worms in the walls. At first, you might think they're just part of the architecture—thin red lines running through the clay like veins. But watch long enough and you'll see them move, extending further from their holes, their bodies impossibly long and thin. They're colony organisms, actually—what looks like a single worm is thousands of individuals linked end-to-end, sharing a common digestive tract. They feed by extending sticky palps into the air, catching the organic debris that's constantly shed by the builders. When threatened, they can retract instantly, the entire colony vanishing into a hole no wider than a needle.

There are others, too—parasites that live in the builders' shell joints, cleaning them of mineral buildup in exchange for shelter. Moths with wings of salt crystal that shatter and regrow daily. Centipedes that have evolved to mimic the builders' pheromone signatures, living undetected in the heart of the colony, feeding on damaged individuals.

The builders don't seem to have culture as we understand it. They don't make choices—they respond. A broken wall triggers a chemical signal that draws workers. A certain temperature gradient initiates breeding. They communicate entirely through touch and chemistry, antenna to antenna, pressing their segments against each other to share complex molecular messages. Yet their works are beautiful in their precision—spirals that follow mathematical rules, chambers that resonate at specific frequencies, patterns that repeat across scales from the microscopic to the architectural.

Sometimes, rarely, you'll find a builder that has crystallized—its body converted entirely to mineral, frozen in the middle of some task. The others build around these fossils, incorporating them into the structure. The oldest towers are riddled with such bodies, thousands of generations of builders becoming literal building material. They are their city, and their city is them, and the distinction between organism and architecture has long since dissolved.

This is what lives here: things that build and things that feed on building, things that have adapted to exist in the narrow spaces between construction and decay, in the permanent twilight of steam and sound. They know nothing of day or night, of seasons or change. They exist in a single, endless moment of humid industry, their lives measured not in time but in the slow accumulation of mineral deposits, the gradual rise of towers that will never be complete.
