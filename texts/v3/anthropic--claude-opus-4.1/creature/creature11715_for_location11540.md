They are called the Condensers, though they rarely name themselves at all.

Their bodies are studies in adaptation—elongated torsos that maximize surface area, skin that has evolved into something between membrane and gill. They breathe through their entire surface, moisture passing in and out through millions of microscopic pores. Their flesh has a nacre quality, semi-translucent, with visible networks of fluid moving beneath—not blood exactly, but something thicker, more mineral-rich. When they stand still, which they often do for hours, condensation forms on them like dew on grass. They lick their own arms to drink.

The oldest among them have calcified ridges along their spines and shoulders where minerals have accumulated over decades of exposure. These deposits grow in patterns like coral or cave formations—each individual becoming a walking geological record of where they've lived, what waters they've breathed, what vapors they've absorbed. The young scrape these deposits from their elders, grinding them into powder that they consume or trade. It is their only form of inheritance.

They move slowly, deliberately, as if through syrup. Quick movement causes them pain—their modified blood doesn't pump so much as seep, and sudden exertion leaves them gasping, their skin flushing dark as they struggle to process oxygen from the humid air. They've learned patience as a survival mechanism. Their conversations take hours. Their work shifts last days. They sleep standing up, leaning against walls that match their body temperature exactly.

Their eyes have no whites—the entire surface is a dark, wet membrane that never quite focuses on anything directly. They see heat and moisture better than light and shadow. To them, the city is a landscape of gradients and currents, rivers of temperature flowing through the air, pools of humidity gathering in corners. They can taste the mineral content of steam from across a room, identify which vent it emerged from by its chemical signature.

The children are born in the collection pools, emerging from eggs that look like soap bubbles the size of melons. For their first year, they're aquatic, translucent things with visible organs, feeding on mineral deposits and bacterial films. The transformation to air-breathing is gradual and painful. Not all survive it. Those who do carry scars on their throats where the gill slits sealed shut.

They wear no clothing—fabric rots in days here—but some decorate themselves with chains of volcanic glass or copper wire that oxidizes green against their skin. The wealthy embed steam turbines directly into their bodies, tiny mechanical hearts that help circulate their sluggish fluids. You can hear them coming by the click-whisper of gears beneath flesh.

Their buildings are vertical pools—hollow towers filled with varying levels of water and steam, connected by locks and valves. They live at different depths depending on mood and health, sinking when they need to heal, rising when they need to think. Their furniture is all glass and ceramic, shaped to channel runoff. They sleep in shallow basins that fill slowly with condensation overnight.

They feed on what grows in the dark: the crystalline not-quite-mold, the sulfur-eating bacteria that bloom in the pipes, the silicate crawlers that they farm in heated tanks. Their cuisine is chemistry—fermentation and precipitation, solutions and suspensions. They drink metals dissolved in acid. They eat salt gardens that they grow on their walls. Nothing they consume would qualify as food to anything that evolved under sunlight.

The industrial work suits them. They can reach into steam vents without burning, can breathe in the toxic fumes of the refineries without dying. They see the city's machines as extensions of their own bodies—the turbines are lungs, the pipes are veins, the collection pools are stomachs digesting minerals from the earth. They maintain this vast organism with a tenderness that might be love or might be recognition of kinship.

They reproduce rarely, irregularly. The process requires specific atmospheric conditions—pressure drops before storms, particular mineral concentrations in the air, temperatures within half a degree of optimal. When conditions align, they gather in the Condensation Cathedral and hang their egg sacs from the ceiling, where the constant flow of water keeps them oxygenated. The eggs that survive produce offspring, but just as often they crystallize, becoming decorative stones that ring like bells when struck.

Their religion, if they have one, centers on states of matter. They worship transition—the moment water becomes steam, the instant minerals precipitate from solution. They fear dryness above all else, tell stories of ancestors who crystallized alive, becoming statues of salt and sulfur. They believe the city itself is conscious, that they are its thoughts made flesh, or flesh made mineral, or something between the two.

When they die—and they do die, though it takes decades of slow crystallization—their bodies are dissolved in the industrial acid baths and fed back into the city's water system. They believe this returns them to the cycle, that they will condense again someday on someone else's skin, be breathed in and absorbed, become part of another Condenser's slow, wet life.

They dream of drowning in air too thin to sustain them. They dream of their children's children adapting further, becoming pure vapor, escaping the plateau entirely. They dream of the city's steam vents closing, the thermal waters cooling, everything crystallizing into sharp, dry stillness.

But mostly, they don't dream at all. They stand at their stations in the refineries, watching minerals accumulate grain by grain. They maintain the turbines with their long, damp fingers. They raise their strange children in pools of warm acid. They listen to the city breathing through its ten thousand vents and add their own whispered exhale to the chorus, keeping the humid machine running, keeping the impossible balance between dissolution and form.
