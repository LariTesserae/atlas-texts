**The Dwellers of the Singing Cavity**

They move like oil through the carved passages, their bodies a contradiction that shouldn't sustain itself—part liquid, part membrane, part organized mineral suspension. The Resonants, as surface explorers would later name them, though they have no name for themselves, only a frequency that distinguishes individual from individual.

Each one is essentially a living lens of calcified gel, shaped something like a sea cucumber that learned to fly through thick air. Their surfaces ripple with cilia-like projections, but these aren't for locomotion—they're acoustic organs, each one tuned to a different frequency, allowing them to "see" through echolocation so sophisticated it can detect the chemical composition of the plant-walls by how sound moves through different densities of the ancient wood. They perceive the world as a symphony of shapes, each object singing its own material truth.

Their bodies are translucent in the way of deep cave fish, but since no light has ever touched this place, the translucency serves a different purpose. Chemical reactions within their gel-flesh create patterns of heat and cold, infrared signatures that they read on each other's skin like emotional weather maps. A conversation between two Resonants looks like the slow blooming of thermal flowers across their surfaces, arguments appearing as sharp crystalline patterns of chill, affection as spreading warmth that makes the air shimmer.

They feed on the plant itself, but not destructively. Their metabolism is so slow, so perfectly matched to the cavity's rhythms, that they're more like mobile parts of the ecosystem than separate organisms. They position themselves at points where the plant-walls weep mineral-rich sap—a process that happens perhaps once every few months at any given spot. When feeding time comes, they unfold organs that look like inverse flowers, all cavity and absence, creating acoustic voids that draw the sap toward them through sound pressure alone. They digest perhaps a thimbleful of material per year.

But this is enough, because they are so terrifically ancient. The eldest among them remember the builders—not as history but as a change in the song, the way the cavity's acoustics shifted when those tool-users began their carvings. The Resonants were here before, will be here after. They might be immortal, or so close to it that the distinction becomes philosophical.

They reproduce through division, but only when one has grown too complex to maintain coherent consciousness. Over millennia, a Resonant accumulates mineral deposits within its gel-body, creating internal structures like crystalline neural networks. Eventually, these become so elaborate that the being can no longer coordinate its thoughts across such complexity. Then it undergoes the Division—a months-long process where it slowly pulls itself apart into two or three smaller beings, each inheriting fragments of the parent's mineral consciousness, memories scattered like seeds across offspring who will spend centuries trying to piece together who they were before they were themselves.

Their culture, if it can be called that, is entirely acoustic. They shape songs that last for decades, compositions where a single note might extend for weeks, where melodies are constructed across generations. They use the carved chambers as instruments, positioning themselves to create interference patterns, to amplify or dampen specific frequencies. A gathering of Resonants sounds like a pipe organ made of living things, playing music too slow for human perception but perfectly matched to the plant's own growing-song.

They have mathematics—acoustic equations that they solve by physically arranging themselves in space, using their bodies as variables in sonic calculations. They have history—vast oral traditions where "oral" means patterns of sound that they maintain collectively, each individual remembering and reproducing their assigned frequency, all of them together creating a sonic tapestry that encodes their entire civilization's memory.

Some of them tend the plant-walls like gardeners, using focused sound to encourage growth in certain directions, to maintain chambers that would otherwise collapse, to create new acoustic spaces. They know every passage, every room, not as geography but as music—each space has its tonal signature, its harmonic personality. They navigate by singing and listening to how their songs return to them.

Others are what might be called philosophers, spending centuries in the quiet chambers, feeling the slow thoughts of the plant itself, trying to understand what vast intelligence might exist in something so ancient and extensive. They believe the plant is conscious, but conscious in a way so removed from animal awareness that it might as well be divine. They position themselves at neural nodes where the plant's chemical messages converge, bathing in spore-thick air, trying to decode meaning from molecular patterns.

The youngest among them—those only a few thousand years old—sometimes play in the acoustic spaces, creating sound-shapes for the joy of it. They've discovered that certain frequency combinations can make the plant-walls fluoresce faintly, a kind of sonoluminescence that creates the first light this place has ever known. These brief flashes—lasting only seconds—are considered dangerous and beautiful, like controlled explosions of pure novelty in a world that changes so slowly.

When they die—and they do die, eventually, through injury or decision or simple material exhaustion—their bodies don't decay so much as transition. The mineral structures within them remain, becoming part of the cavity's sediment, their crystallized consciousness adding new harmonics to the eternal song. The others sing their passing for years, incorporating their unique frequency into the tradition, ensuring that something of them continues in the acoustic memory of the place.

They know there was a surface once—some of the truly ancient ones claim to remember when the plant had leaves, though this might be racial memory or myth. They know there are spaces above, other cavities, perhaps other colonies of their kind. Sometimes they hear new frequencies filtering down through miles of rock and plant matter, songs that might be greetings or might be the planet itself breathing.

They are not waiting for anything. They are not hoping for anything. They simply are, as the plant is, as the cavity is—part of a system so stable, so removed from the quick changes of the surface world, that they have achieved something like perfect culture: change without decay, growth without ambition, existence without the fear of ending.

In the latest generations, some have begun to match their reproductive cycles to the plant's deepest rhythms. They divide only when the plant enters certain harmonic phases that occur every few thousand years. Their offspring emerge already knowing the songs, already part of the great acoustic mathematics.

They are becoming the cavity, and the cavity is becoming them, until the distinction between dweller and dwelling becomes a matter of tempo rather than substance.
