# The Inhabitants of the Canopy

The people of the Last Mesa have evolved alongside their fungal city into something no longer quite baseline human. Their bones are hollow like a bird's, an adaptation that emerged over forty generations of vertical living. Children born too heavy simply don't survive their first tumbles from the caps. The survivors breed lighter offspring, until now the average adult weighs no more than a large dog, their limbs elongated and fingers stretched to twice normal length, perfect for gripping the spongy surfaces of their world.

They are a quiet people by necessity—sound carries strangely in the vertical maze, and a shout from the wrong position can disturb the bat colonies into panicked swarms that take hours to resettle. Their language has evolved into something more gestural than vocal, a complex system of hand movements visible across the gaps between platforms, supplemented by soft clicks of tongue against teeth that blend into the ambient insect drone. When they must speak aloud, their voices are pitched high and breathy, barely distinguishable from the whisper of air through the gill-spaces.

Each family group consists of a vertical lineage—grandparents at the bottom levels where the air is thickest and climbing is no longer possible, parents in the middle tiers tending the water-surfaces, and children in the dangerous upper reaches where the memorial lizards nest and the best silk-moth colonies thrive. As the elderly lose their grip strength, they descend level by level toward the mushroom roots, eventually settling in the deep hollows where they tend the phosphorescent molds and teach the youngest children the stories of the world before the mesa, stories so altered by retelling that they bear no resemblance to any history that might once have been true.

Their skin has developed a symbiotic relationship with certain fungi—a blue-green algae-like growth that patterns their flesh in unique whorls, processing toxins from the constant exposure to spores and converting them into nutrients. Newborns are ritually rubbed with specific mold cultures that will become their lifetime dermis-partners, the patterns developing like slow tattoos over their first decade of life. By adulthood, each person's skin tells a unique story in fungal hieroglyphs, some families passing down particularly successful strains that confer resistance to the neurotoxins certain mushrooms release during their reproductive cycles.

The water-keepers hold the highest status, their morning routes along the condensation surfaces memorized down to the individual droplet. They wear cloaks of moth-silk that help them blend into the mushroom flesh, moving in slow, practiced gestures that won't disturb the delicate process of fungal perspiration. Their apprenticeships begin at age five and last until twenty, learning not just the routes but the songs—wordless humming vibrations that some say encourage the mushrooms to release more moisture, though whether this is true or merely ritual no one can say anymore.

Below them in status but not in importance are the beetle-herders, who manage the vast colonies of boring beetles that process dead fungal matter before it can rot and poison the air. They know each beetle by the pattern on its shell, can predict their tunneling paths, and guide them away from critical structural points. The beetles have been bred for generations to respond to certain pheromone trails the herders paint with secretions from crushed moth glands, creating an intricate system of invisible highways through the wood.

The shamans—if that's the right word for them—are those who claim to hear the mushrooms thinking. They live in the spaces between caps where the spore-fall is thickest, breathing in hallucinogenic quantities until their consciousness merges with what they call the "deep network"—the underground communication system of chemical signals the fungi use to coordinate their ancient metabolism. These mystics speak in paradoxes and impossibilities, claiming the mushrooms remember the ocean that once covered this land, that they're waiting for something, that the entire mesa is a single thought taking geological time to complete itself.

Children grow up fearless of heights but terrified of open spaces. The rare trading parties that visit speak of young mesa-dwellers paralyzed with agoraphobia when brought to the desert floor, unable to cope with the concept of horizontal distance, of sky that isn't filtered through cap-shadow. Their games involve racing up the most dangerous routes, hand over hand along the underside of caps where one slip means a fall into the maze of stems below. They keep membrane-lizards as pets, training them to hunt specific moths, their chittering calls a constant background to daily life.

The elderly, in their deep hollows, have developed a separate culture entirely. Down in the perpetual darkness where only the faintest bioluminescence provides light, they've evolved beyond the need for vision. Their eyes cloud over with a beneficial fungal film that protects against spore infection, and they navigate by sound, smell, and the minute air currents that signal passage and platform. They cultivate the slowest-growing fungi, some taking decades to mature, tended by successions of gradually descending elders who pass on the knowledge of their care through touch and whisper.

There are those who go mad from the density, the closeness, the never-being-alone. They flee upward, past the safe zones, into the realm of raw caps where the sun hits directly and the mushroom flesh blisters and bleeds caustic sap. These sun-seekers build precarious shelters on the very tops, their skin burned and re-burned until it becomes leather, their minds lost to the vast emptiness visible from the mesa's edge. Most eventually jump. Some few adapt, becoming something between human and hermit, trading rare sun-dried mushroom leather to the lower levels for food, their sanity exchanged for the only solitude the mesa can offer.

The births are communal affairs—the infant mortality rate demands it. Every child belongs to every adult, at least until they survive their third year and the patterns on their skin declare which family's fungal heritage they carry. The pregnant retreat to special chambers lined with the softest spore-producing gills, the air thick with nutrients, where they give birth in groups, their children emerging into a cloud of beneficial organisms that immediately begin colonizing their skin, their lungs, their gut. Those whose fungal partners don't take rarely survive the week.

At night—though night and day blur in the eternal twilight of the lower levels—the entire city breathes as one. The mushrooms exhale in slow pulses, carbon dioxide and exotic gases mixing into a cocktail that would kill an unprotected lowlander in minutes. But the mesa people have adapted, their blood chemistry altered to process these toxins, their dreams filled with visions that might be hallucinations or might be the thoughts of the fungi themselves, transmitted through the chemical fog that rises and falls like a tide of air.

They mark time not by sun or moon but by the reproductive cycles of various species—the seventeen-year emergence of certain boring beetles, the irregular but predictable sporing of the great caps, the generational die-offs of moth colonies that carpet the platforms with silver wings. Their calendar is biological, their holidays timed to migrations and molts, their new year beginning when the bats return from wherever they go during the hottest months, their echolocation calls announcing another cycle of survival in this impossible vertical world.

The traders who visit speak of them with a mixture of pity and awe—these people who have never walked on soil, never seen the horizon, never known rain. But the mesa people themselves can't imagine any other way to live. Their songs, when they rarely sing them aloud, speak of the safety of closeness, the comfort of walls on all sides, the reliability of moisture that comes not from the chaotic sky but from the steady, predictable flesh of their fungal city. They are not trapped but held, not confined but embraced, not primitive but perfectly adapted to the only world that matters—the vertical labyrinth that goes on growing denser, more complex, more itself with every passing generation, while far below the dust of the old world blows across empty plains that no longer remember why they died.
