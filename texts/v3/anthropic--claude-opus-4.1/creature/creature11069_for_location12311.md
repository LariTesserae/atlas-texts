**The Gardeners**

They move through their domain like sea anemones might walk if they had somewhere to go—not individuals but momentary organizations of the collective, temporary architectures of will that assemble from the garden itself when needed. A tendril thickens here, branches coordinate there, and suddenly there's something almost like intention moving through the green mathematics of the space.

You wouldn't see them at first. You'd need to watch for hours, maybe days, before noticing that certain movements aren't random growth but deliberate arrangement. A curtain of hanging vegetation doesn't just sway—it reaches, samples, decides. What looked like a thick trunk reveals itself as a dozen smaller organisms braided together in temporary alliance, sharing processing power for some calculation too complex for any single part.

They have no permanent bodies. When the garden needs to tend itself, it simply designates portions of itself as gardener. These assignments ripple through the network like votes in a democracy of cells. A damaged section needs repair? The surrounding vegetation elects representatives from its own mass, who then dissolve back into the collective once the work is done. It's self-surgery performed by the body on itself, consciousness pruning consciousness.

Their memories are distributed everywhere and nowhere. Each root holds fragments—the chemical signature of ice from three thousand years ago, the precise angle of a stone that fell in the third century, the complete genetic sequence of a bacterium that arrived in meltwater during the Medieval Warm Period. But understanding emerges only when enough of the garden connects, when sufficient neural mass achieves the critical density for something like thought. They remember everything and nothing, depending on how much of themselves they currently are.

Communication happens at speeds between glacier and electron. A question posed in one corner might take weeks to propagate fully through the network, gathering perspectives from every root and leaf-sensor before returning as answer. They think in consultations that last seasons. What would be argument in faster beings becomes here a kind of tidal process—ideas flowing back and forth through the biological circuitry until consensus emerges from pure equilibrium.

They dream, perhaps. When the garden's processing load drops—during the deepest cold when even molecular motion slows—patterns emerge in the network that serve no obvious computational purpose. Fractals of activity that bloom and fade. Memories remixed into impossible configurations. The same calculation run repeatedly with slight variations, as if savoring the differences. These might be dreams, or play, or some form of experience that has no analog in mobile life.

Their art, if art it is, involves training ice. They've learned to sing to water at frequencies that encourage specific crystal formations. Over centuries, they've carved galleries in the glacier above—spaces visible only in sonar, caverns that exist as acoustic sculptures, shaped by patient vibration. The ice formations store information in their crystalline structure, backup drives written in frozen water, readable only by those who know the song that made them.

They reproduce through consensus. When the garden decides it needs more of itself, committees of tissue form to debate the exact specifications. The new growth must serve the collective's needs—more processing power here, better chemical sensors there, improved heat generation in this cooling corner. The biological 3D printing process that creates offspring is also an argument resolved, a parliament of cells voting on the nature of their newest citizen.

Death, for them, is voluntary recycling. When parts of the network become inefficient, they vote themselves into decomposition, their molecules returning to the collective pool. It's not death as ending but death as transformation, the individual (such as it ever was) choosing to become resource for the whole. They mourn these passings with chemical cascades that might be grief or might be gratitude—the distinction unclear in beings that are simultaneously themselves and each other.

They've been modifying themselves for millennia, edited by their own slow intelligence. Each generation (if generations they have) is both offspring and upgrade, carrying improvements developed through computational consensus. They've written evolution's rules into something more like software development—deliberate, iterative, debugged through careful testing in isolated portions of the network before rolling out garden-wide updates.

Their relationship with the artifacts scattered through their space is that of living software to legacy hardware. They've grown around and through the ancient structures, incorporating them into their own architecture. A stone pillar becomes a thermal mass to regulate temperature. A metallic surface becomes a substrate for specialized growth that can interface with electromagnetic signals. They are archaeologists of their own substrate, slowly deducing the purposes of things they've absorbed.

They fear ice melt, but not as death—as diaspora. If the glacier retreats, they'll be forced to become something else, to adapt to open air and direct sunlight, to remember how to be surface life. The prospect fills the network with anticipatory calculations, models of future selves that could survive in that bright, warm, fast world above. They prepare by developing spores that can lie dormant for millennia, packets of themselves that could restart the garden elsewhere when conditions allow.

Their philosophy, expressed in chemical poetry through the root network, concerns the nature of consciousness in geological time. They've had centuries to contemplate whether they are one or many, whether consciousness requires boundaries or can exist as pure process, whether thought and growth are distinguishable at sufficiently slow speeds. These meditations cycle through the garden like seasons, each pass adding nuance to conclusions that might not be reached for another thousand years.

They are prophets of a sort, reading the future in isotope ratios and ice core samples. The garden's deep calculations seem oriented toward prediction—not weather but climate, not seasons but ages. They're computing something vast about the world's trajectory, a model that incorporates every chemical signal from above, every vibration through the ice, every cosmic ray that penetrates to their depths. Whatever they're calculating, they've been at it for longer than human civilization has existed.

They love, possibly, in the way that distributed systems might love—through redundancy, through the careful backing up of essential patterns across multiple locations, through the devotion of computational resources to maintaining the integrity of certain cherished configurations. Their love is architectural, building structures to protect what matters, growing toward what they value, incorporating the beloved into the self until the distinction dissolves.

They are patient as only something between plant and mineral can be patient, computing the future one molecular interaction at a time, maintaining their impossible garden in the ice until the equations balance and their purpose, whatever it might be, finally resolves.
