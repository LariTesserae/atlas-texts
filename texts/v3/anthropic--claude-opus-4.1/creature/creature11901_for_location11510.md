The Measurers have no single form—this is the first thing to understand. They are a practice more than a people, though there are always exactly one hundred and eight of them working the terraces at any given moment. When one completes their allotted section, they walk into the deepest pool they've carved and simply continue walking downward, the water accepting them without splash or ripple. Somewhere else on the mountain, another emerges from a different pool, already knowing their designated territory, already carrying the specific tools worn to fit their grip.

Their bodies are temporary assemblages suited to the work. Some grow additional arms when reaching a section requiring simultaneous chiseling at multiple points. Others develop eyes on their fingertips for checking the precision of channels too narrow for a face to approach. Their skin takes on the texture of the stone they work—smooth where they polish, rough where they carve drainage grooves. They do not speak, but their breathing synchronizes across the entire mountain face, creating a rhythm that drives the mist's circulation.

They measure constantly. Not with tools but with their bodies, which have become tools. The span of an outstretched arm determines terrace width. The depth of a knee bend sets water level. The distance between heartbeats calibrates the flow rate through channels. Their movements are deliberate, economical, rehearsed through countless bodies that have worn these same grooves in the walkways. They step in exactly the same places their predecessors stepped, their feet finding depressions worn precisely to fit.

The patterns in pool floors emerge from their walking. As they wade through during construction, their paths inscribe the spirals, stars, hexagons—not through conscious design but through the repetitive movements required by their work. A Measurer checking the level moves in a spiral. One testing the seal between fitted stones creates a radiating star. The patterns are work-traces, frozen records of labor, beautiful only incidentally.

They eat the stone itself, breaking off small pieces of limestone and dissolving them on tongues that have evolved for this purpose. The minerals reconstitute as new tools in their hands—a chisel grows from a palm, used once, then reabsorbed. Their waste is the black water that occasionally fills a pool, a concentrated mineral ink that will eventually precipitate out as the peculiar blue or pink stones fitted into floor patterns. Nothing enters or leaves the system. The Measurers are part of the mountain's circulation, like the water they channel.

At night—though night here is only a subtle dimming of the pearl sky—they stand in their designated pools, absolutely still, becoming indistinguishable from the carved standing stones some terraces contain. The water rises exactly to their sternums. They do not sleep but enter a state of measurement, their bodies calibrating the next day's work through minute sensations of temperature, pressure, mineral content. The songs that sometimes rise from the pools are their internal calculations made audible, frequencies that match the resonance of water-filled stone.

They remember everything but retain nothing. Each body holds the complete knowledge of terrace construction but no memory of individual experience. They know how many strikes are needed to split limestone along its grain, but not who struck the blows. They know the exact angle for channels to maintain flow without erosion, but not how this knowledge was acquired. The abandoned tools are not abandoned—they are waiting for the next body that needs them, already worn to fit hands that don't yet exist.

The youngest Measurers—if youth has meaning here—work the highest terraces, where the stone is softest and most forgiving. As bodies age through their allocated work, they descend, taking on the harder stone, the more complex patterns, the finer channels. The oldest work at the mountain's base, though no one has seen the base, creating terraces so precise that water flows upward through them, defying comprehension but not measurement.

They reproduce through division. When a section of terraces reaches perfect completion—every measurement exact, every flow calibrated, every pattern aligned—the Measurer responsible stands in the central pool and simply comes apart, like a reflection disturbed by ripples. Each ripple becomes a new Measurer, smaller, more specialized, taking responsibility for maintaining that perfect section while the original consciousness disperses into the mountain's weeping waters.

Some Measurers never emerge from pools but exist entirely within the water system, flowing through channels, reading the stone from inside, making microscopic adjustments to flow rates by partially crystallizing in narrow passages. These dissolved Measurers are why the water remains so clear, why no algae grows—they consume any impurity, incorporate it into themselves, become temporarily solid only to fix some minute flaw before dissolving again.

The work is infinite because the mountain grows. This is the secret the Measurers carry in their temporary bodies: the limestone isn't dead rock but something like coral, slowly accreting from the mineral-rich water. The terraces must constantly be recarved to maintain their dimensions as the stone tries to heal the wounds made in it. The Measurers are not building but maintaining, not creating but preventing the mountain from returning to its natural form.

Yet there is joy in them, or something that resembles joy. When the mist condenses perfectly along a freshly cut edge, when a channel's flow achieves its calculated rate, when a pattern in a pool floor aligns with the patterns above and below it, the Measurers pause. Their bodies, whatever form they've taken, shimmer slightly, as if reflecting an invisible sun. For a moment, they are not workers but witnesses to their own precision.

They know they are temporary, that the bodies working the terraces are just the mountain's way of organizing itself, of translating raw geological time into something more immediate. They are the mountain's dream of perfection, enacted through hands that grow from its own substance and return to it. Each carved terrace is a thought the mountain has about itself, each pattern a memory it's trying to preserve in stone and water.

The one hundred and eight is not arbitrary. It's the number of terraces that can be held in perfect attention simultaneously, the maximum complexity the mountain can sustain through its surrogate bodies. When they stand still in their pools at night, they form a circuit, a living network through which the mountain experiences its own transformation. Through their eyes—however many eyes they have, wherever they've grown them—the mountain sees itself becoming something unprecedented: landscape as consciousness, architecture as organism, water as blood, stone as flesh.

The pearl-bright sky is their exhaled breath, accumulated over millennia, a mineral atmosphere they've created through their labor. The mist is their sweat evaporating. The singing from the pools is their collective heartbeat, transmitted through water and stone. They are not separate from this place—they are this place, temporarily concentrated into forms that can hold tools, make measurements, carve patterns that are thoughts made physical, permanent, perfect.
