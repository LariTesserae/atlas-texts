The Threadkeepers arrived first, or perhaps they emerged from the roots themselves — no one remembers anymore. They move through the Root Cathedral like memories of spiders, if spiders were the size of bears and wove with light instead of silk. Their bodies are translucent chitin that drinks in the bioluminescence until they themselves become lanterns, eight legs picking delicate paths across the ever-shifting architecture. They don't walk so much as flow, their movements perfectly calibrated to the wood's constant reorganization, reading the tremors that precede each change.

They weave maps that last exactly as long as needed. Watch them work: drawing illuminated threads from specialized spinnerets, these strands of concentrated light that stick to nothing but air itself, creating three-dimensional diagrams of the current configuration. The maps glow for hours or days before dissolving into motes of light that the Threadkeepers reabsorb through their spiracles. They seem to remember every map they've ever made, their compound eyes tracking patterns in the chaos that no one else can see.

The Threadkeepers never speak — perhaps they can't — but they leave messages in their temporary webs. Warnings about unstable sections encoded in the geometry of thread-angles. Directions to water pools in the color-temperature of the strands. Historical records in the layering of old web-anchors that they never quite clean away, creating palimpsests of light that tell stories about configurations that no longer exist.

Then came the Dwellers, though "came" suggests they arrived from somewhere else when perhaps they simply decided to stop moving. Humanoid but elongated by generations in the Cathedral's variable gravity, they stand nine feet tall when they stand at all, preferring to brachiate through the root networks with arms that span twelve feet fingertip to fingertip. Their skin has taken on the pale blue-white of the ambient light, making them nearly invisible against the glowing wood until they move.

The Dwellers build nothing permanent — they learned that lesson long ago. Instead, they practice an architecture of timing, creating temporary settlements in the brief windows between reconfigurations. They know how long each pattern tends to last, reading signs in the wood's grain that predict how many hours or days before everything shifts again. In that time they stretch skins for shelter, arrange stones for hearths that will never hold fire, weave baskets that they'll abandon when the floor becomes ceiling.

They drum on the hollow logs to communicate across the voids, each settlement maintaining its own rhythm that announces its temporary location. The drums are the only things they truly preserve, carrying them between configurations, maintaining these instruments across generations even as everything else remains provisional. The rhythms encode practical information — water here, stable ground there, Threadkeeper activity in the third hollow — but also something more. Stories, perhaps. Memories of configurations that were particularly beautiful or terrible. Warnings about patterns that trap, sections that fold in on themselves, roots that grow aggressive and consuming.

Their children learn to walk on surfaces that might not exist by afternoon, to sleep lightly enough that the tremor of approaching reconfiguration wakes them, to love things that won't last. They play games with the rootlets that emerge from air, racing to climb them before they dissolve, building elaborate structures that they know will vanish, treating impermanence as a toy rather than a threat.

The Cultivators came last, or perhaps they're still coming, trickling in through the sinkholes in groups of two or three, bringing with them an obsession that the others find both amusing and disturbing. They believe the Root Cathedral can be trained. That its growth follows patterns that can be influenced, maybe controlled. They map the Threadkeeper maps, creating meta-diagrams of how configurations evolve over time. They leave offerings at growth points — specific minerals, droplets of their own blood, fragments of metal that the roots sometimes incorporate into their structure.

The Cultivators are more varied than the Dwellers, their bodies still remembering the world above. But they're changing. Those who've been here longest show the signs: eyes grown huge to gather more light, fingers lengthened for better grip on smooth root surfaces, the ability to sense tremors through their feet that predict reconfigurations. They wear clothing woven from shed root-bark that glows faintly for weeks after harvesting, making them walking constellation maps of where they've been.

They're the ones who left those workshops in the deep dark spaces, though they've mostly given up on permanent construction. Now they work with the Cathedral's own timeline, starting projects that they know will take generations to complete as the roots slowly cycle through their patterns. They're building something, but building it across time rather than space, each generation adding a piece when the configuration is right, waiting decades sometimes for the correct arrangement to return.

Between all three groups move the Bright Ones, though no one's certain if they're truly alive or just another expression of the Cathedral itself. They look like schools of jellyfish crossed with memory, translucent bodies pulsing with absorbed light, moving through the voids in formations that might be meaningful or might be purely aesthetic. The Threadkeepers track them. The Dwellers drum warnings when they approach. The Cultivators try to capture them, believing they're some kind of messenger or sensor for the root-system itself.

The Bright Ones feed on something in the air, or perhaps on the air itself, their bodies growing more luminous in the vast empty spaces and dimming near the roots. They sing, or something like singing — a harmony just at the edge of hearing that makes the roots grow faster, more chaotic, as if the sound itself is a kind of fertilizer for transformation. When they die, their bodies rain down as phosphorescent liquid that the roots absorb greedily, triggering growth spurts that can reconfigure entire districts in minutes.

And in the depths, in those spaces too dense for even the Threadkeepers to map, something else lives. The Dwellers call them the Forgotten, though that's just a translation of the drum-pattern they use. They're what's left of the first attempts at permanence, the builders of those stone cairns and primitive platforms, but they're no longer quite human or quite anything else. They've rooted themselves, literally, their bodies fused with the wood at points of stability that haven't moved in centuries.

The Forgotten grow like the roots grow, their flesh becoming woody, their bones spreading through the Cathedral's structure like a secondary skeleton. They're still conscious, possibly, their eyes tracking movement, their mouths sometimes shaping words in languages that predate the current inhabitants. The Cultivators study them obsessively, believing they've achieved what the Cultivators seek — true synchronization with the Cathedral's growth. The Dwellers avoid them. The Threadkeepers weave special patterns around them, either honoring or warning, it's impossible to say.

Young Forgotten are sometimes found in the stable spaces — people who stayed too long in one spot, who trusted a configuration that seemed permanent, who got caught when the roots began incorporating them. The process seems peaceful, even ecstatic. They don't fight it. Their faces, before the bark covers them completely, show something like revelation, as if they're finally understanding something that movement prevented them from seeing.

All of them — Threadkeepers, Dwellers, Cultivators, Bright Ones, Forgotten — create an ecosystem as fluid as the architecture itself. They depend on each other in ways that shift as constantly as the landscape. The Threadkeepers need the Dwellers' drums to navigate the acoustic spaces. The Dwellers need the Cultivators' offerings to predict which roots will grow aggressive. The Cultivators need the Threadkeepers' maps to track their multi-generational projects. The Bright Ones need them all, or none of them, following their own patterns that might be hunger or art or worship.

And the Cathedral grows around them, through them, possibly because of them. Each group believes they understand it — as a living building, as a god's dream, as a machine for creating possibility itself. But perhaps it's simpler than that. Perhaps it's just what happens when something decides to grow without stopping, when architecture abandons its trees and builds purely for the joy of building, when light itself becomes structural and structure becomes a kind of music that only the rooted can truly hear.

The inhabitants are notes in that music, their movements and mutations and transformations all part of some larger composition that none of them will live long enough to hear in full. Except perhaps the Forgotten, who've given up moving altogether, who've become part of the instrument itself, their consciousness spreading through the wood like sap, thinking thoughts too slow for the mobile to comprehend, dreaming dreams that take centuries to complete.
