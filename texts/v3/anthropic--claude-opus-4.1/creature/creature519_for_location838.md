In the shallow pools between the tilted slabs, something moves with deliberate slowness—not quite salamander, not quite fish, but a creature that seems to have stopped evolving halfway between states. Its skin is translucent gray, revealing the sluggish pulse of dark blood through primitive vessels. It has external gills that wave like tiny ferns in the barely-moving water, but also rudimentary lungs that allow it to survive when the pools shrink to mere films of moisture. The locals who occasionally traverse these heights call them "ghost fish," though they are neither ghosts nor fish. They spend the frozen months in a state approaching death, their metabolisms slowed to near-nothing, their bodies producing a kind of antifreeze that keeps their cells from rupturing. When the thaw comes, they emerge to feed on the larvae of the midges, moving through their shallow world with the patience of geological time.

Deeper in the rock piles, where the pikas have worn smooth highways between the boulders, lives an old female snow leopard whose territory spans the entire plateau. Her left eye is clouded white from an ancient injury, and her gait carries a permanent limp from a fall taken pursuing a young yak three winters past. She has learned to hunt differently now—using the abandoned industrial debris as blinds, waiting sometimes for days beside the rusted cable drums where pikas have established their colonies. She knows every pool where the ghost fish surface, every ledge where lammergeiers roost. Her coat has gone slightly yellow with age, and in certain lights she seems more like a trick of the fog than a living thing.

The pikas themselves have developed strange behaviors around the industrial remnants. They've learned that the metal surfaces warm faster than rock in the morning sun, and entire colonies cluster on the rusted pipeline segments during the brief warm hours. Inside the cracked generator housing, three generations of an extended family have built an elaborate network of dried grass chambers, their architecture incorporating the existing mechanical structures—nests built in the cylinder heads, food stores cached behind the flywheel. They've gnawed channels in the rubber gaskets to create doorways, and their constant traffic has polished certain internal surfaces to a mirror shine.

But the strangest inhabitant is neither wholly animal nor entirely human—a hermit who arrived decades ago as part of the industrial operation and never left when it abandoned. They live in a structure that is part cave, part assembled from scavenged materials—sheets of corrugated metal wedged against an overhang, pipeline sections repurposed as chimneys and water collectors. Their hair and beard have grown wild and white, bleached by the altitude and sun until they're indistinguishable from the patches of dead grass that cling to the rocks.

This person—gender long since irrelevant in their isolation—has developed an intricate relationship with the plateau's creatures. The pikas no longer flee at their approach. The yaks, when they pass through, seem to recognize them, lowering their massive heads in something like acknowledgment. They know where the snow leopard hunts and leaves small offerings of dried yak dung for fuel near her favorite overlooks, never approaching close enough to disturb her but maintaining this strange, one-sided relationship across the years.

They speak rarely, and when they do, it's in a mixture of languages—fragments of whatever tongue they arrived with, mixed with imitations of the plateau's sounds. They name things not with words but with reproductions of the sounds they make: the ghost fish are called by a wet clicking, the pikas by their own sharp chirp, the fog by a long exhalation. They've forgotten their original name and respond now only to the wind's pitch through the rocks.

In the wet season, they wade through the pools collecting the ghost fish, not to eat but to move them between watersheds when water levels drop, ensuring their survival. They maintain the industrial remnants not for any functional purpose but as habitat—clearing drainage holes in the generator housing for the pikas, adjusting pipeline segments to channel water to where the moss grows thickest. They are less caretaker than participant, another species adapting to the plateau's harsh democracy.

At night, they burn small fires of yak dung and dried willow, the smoke mixing with the fog until the boundary between earth and sky disappears entirely. Sometimes the snow leopard passes close enough to be seen by firelight, her one good eye reflecting green for a moment before she dissolves back into darkness. The hermit makes no movement, understanding that acknowledgment would break whatever contract allows them both to exist in the same space.

When they die—which could be tomorrow or in twenty years, time moves differently here—their body will likely not be found for months or years. The pikas will colonize their shelter. The snow leopard will investigate once, then avoid the scent of death until it fades. The ghost fish will continue their ancient circuits through the pools, unaware that their unnamed guardian has joined the industrial remnants in their slow digestion by the landscape, another artifact being patiently broken down into component elements, feeding the primitive soils that might, in a thousand years, support one more cushion plant, one more patch of impossible green in the gray expanse of stone and sky.
