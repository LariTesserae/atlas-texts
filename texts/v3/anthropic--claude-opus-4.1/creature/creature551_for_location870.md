The Chroniclers move across the plateau in loose generations, each wave separated by decades or centuries of distance. They are tall, attenuated beings whose bones have hollowed to accommodate the thin air, their ribcages expanded into vaulted chambers that resonate with each breath. Their skin has developed a peculiar translucency—not transparent, but possessed of a quality that makes looking at them feel like looking through several panes of glass placed at angles to each other.

They build nothing permanent, understanding the futility of construction in a place where time itself has worn smooth. Instead, they carry their history in their bodies. Each Chronicler's skeleton grows throughout their lifetime into increasingly complex geometries—spiral processes extending from vertebrae, latticed plates forming between ribs, skull crests that branch and rebranch like frozen lightning. These growths record the weather patterns they've witnessed, the moss-cities they've catalogued, the impossible waters they've tested. An elder Chronicler's skeleton, visible through that strange translucent skin, resembles a mathematical diagram made flesh, a living map of centuries of observation.

Their metabolism has adapted to the plateau's strange cold, running on different principles depending on which type of cold surrounds them. In the cold of forgetting, they become torpid, their thoughts slowing to match the erasure of memory from the air itself. In the cold of geometry, they move in precise angles, their limbs describing perfect arcs as if constrained by invisible protractors. They've learned to read the approaching walls of horizontal rain by the way their bones ache in sequence, each skeletal addition responding to different barometric impossibilities.

They feed on the moss-cities, but not destructively. They've developed a form of agriculture that involves cultivating paradoxes—encouraging certain settlements to grow in patterns that violate their own mathematical rules, creating logical inconsistencies that bear fruit-like structures of crystallized contradiction. These they harvest carefully, leaving enough inconsistency for the cities to rebuild their errors. The fruit tastes of parallel possibilities, nourishing not their bodies but their capacity to exist in a place where existence itself is negotiable.

The Chroniclers communicate through a combination of bone-song and selective forgetting. The resonant chambers of their chests can produce harmonics that travel through the stone of the plateau itself, bypassing the muffling air. But more often, they share information by deliberately forgetting things in each other's presence—a controlled amnesia that creates spaces in memory where new knowledge can crystallize. A conversation between Chroniclers looks like two beings standing perfectly still, occasionally tilting their heads as gaps appear and fill in their understanding.

They understand the stone structures differently than an outsider might. To them, these constructions are verbs rather than nouns—ongoing actions frozen mid-gesture. They can read the half-built, half-destroyed towers as processes, understanding what was being attempted in the construction and what is being achieved in the dissolution. Some Chroniclers spend decades studying a single causeway, walking its length repeatedly, each journey revealing new aspects of the mathematics it describes before it terminates in air.

Their relationship with the strange waters is one of careful negotiation. Young Chroniclers learn early not to look into the wells that show different heights—the vertigo can cause their hollow bones to begin growing in wrong directions, trapping them in geometries that make movement impossible. But elder Chroniclers have learned to use these waters as lenses, looking through one well into another to see distant parts of the plateau, or sometimes to see the same location at different stages of erosion, glimpsing the mountains this tableland once was or the dust it will eventually become.

The generations rarely meet, but when they do, the encounters follow precise rituals. The approaching generation begins sending their bone-songs days in advance, allowing the established group time to prepare their forgettings. When they finally converge, they arrange themselves in patterns that mirror the moss-cities' organization—elder Chroniclers as towers, younger ones as the connecting aqueducts of knowledge. They exchange their observations through hours of structured amnesia, each generation deliberately forgetting different aspects of the same phenomena, creating a negative space that contains more information than any positive description could hold.

They reproduce through division—not biological, but philosophical. When a Chronicler has accumulated enough contradictions, witnessed enough impossible weather, catalogued enough violations of physics, they begin to hold two mutually exclusive worldviews simultaneously. Eventually, this paradox becomes unsustainable, and they undergo a kind of philosophical mitosis, becoming two beings who each carry one interpretation forward. These offspring are fully formed, adult, but with skeletons that must begin their recording anew.

The Chroniclers have no concept of death as ending. In their understanding, they simply become too heavy with observation, too elaborate in their skeletal recordings, until they can no longer move. These elders root into the plateau itself, their bones continuing to grow and branch until they become indistinguishable from the stone structures—perhaps they *are* the stone structures, Chroniclers from previous eras whose observations crystallized into permanent mathematics, whose bodies became the causeways and towers that current generations study.

They know they are not the first inhabitants of the plateau. In the miniature civilizations the moss builds, they recognize patterns that match their own bone-growths. They suspect they are themselves a kind of moss, growing at a scale and timeframe that makes their cities—their very bodies—appear as wandering creatures to some vast observer they cannot perceive.

Each generation refines the great work: a complete chronicle of the plateau's impossibilities, recorded not in writing but in the accumulated contradictions of their bones, their deliberate forgettings, their cultivated paradoxes. They continue across the worn stone, these thin beings with their mathematical skeletons and translucent skin, following the horizontal rains, studying the primitive perfections, adding their observations to a record that grows more complex and more true with each passing century, even as the plateau itself wears further down toward whatever fundamental strangeness waits beneath all things.
