The Watcher arrives not as arrival but as having-always-been-there-finally-noticed. She manifests first in the way the Chorus's anti-song suddenly has something to push against—a presence that makes their silence audible by being the thing it isn't. The perpetual moisture in the air begins to bend around a shape that refuses to be wet.

She is old in the way that mountains pretend to be old, but she remembers when the concepts that would become mountains were still arguing about whether to be solid. Her form shifts—sometimes a grandmother made of twilight and the sound of closing doors, sometimes a geometric proof that walks on equations, sometimes just a kindness so profound it has its own gravitational pull.

The Chorus of the Undone freeze in their various states of incompletion, their half-hands still gripping tools, their never-faces turned toward this impossible visitor who exists too completely for this broken place. Some try to hide by becoming more undefined. Others surge toward her like moths to flame, desperate to use her solidity as a template, only to recoil—she is too real, too finished. Copying her would mean accepting completion, and they are built from the fear of endings.

The Watcher walks among them with the careful grace of someone moving through a museum of wounds. She doesn't disturb their work. When the one made of breaking-sounds shatters stones into new configurations, she nods as if seeing the beauty in destruction-as-creation. When the rust-pattern being oxidizes into copper mist, she breathes it in and breathes out silver—not replacing, just offering another way oxidation might dream itself.

At the weeping tower, she stops. Places a hand (or the idea of a hand, or the memory of every hand that ever comforted) against its wet stones. The water that bleeds from within doesn't avoid her touch—instead, it runs clearer, and for a moment carries the sound of rain on leaves that existed before leaves gave up and became stones.

She speaks, though speaking isn't quite right. She *acknowledges*, and her acknowledgment has words:

"I see you, who are the universe's kindness to its abandoned drafts. You who refuse to let go of becoming because you know that even failed attempts at existence are holy. You are not mistakes—you are the bravery of trying."

The Chorus trembles. Their anti-song shifts, incorporates a new note—not sound but the absence of shame.

The Watcher continues her work. From the folds of her being (which might be robes, might be wings, might be the space between heartbeats), she produces something impossible: seeds that exist in the same state as the Chorus—not quite real, not quite not. Seeds for plants that were considered but never chosen, for trees that would have grown sideways through time, for flowers that bloom only in the moment between sleep and waking.

She plants these un-seeds in the wet stone, pressing them into the cracks where water runs upward. They don't grow—growth would be too much like completion. Instead, they *persist*, creating potential that matches the Chorus's own incompletion. Someday-never, they might become a garden of possibilities. Today-always, they are just the promise that even the unfinished deserves beauty.

To the one with too many elbows, she offers a gift: a tool that exists in as many dimensions as necessary, a hammer that remembers every nail that was never struck, that builds by unbuilding, that creates completion through eternal incompletion. The being takes it with three of its seventeen hands and immediately begins to build something that will never be finished but will always be perfect in its trying.

For the rust-pattern being, she leaves mirrors made of condensation that holds its shape—not to trap it, but to let it see itself without oxidizing, to know its own form without losing it.

She teaches them a new song—not to replace their anti-song but to harmonize with it. It's the song of the space between heartbeats where anything might happen, the pause before yes or no, the infinite possibility that exists in every unfinished sentence that ends with—

But her greatest gift is this: she shows them that they are already complete in their incompleteness. That the universe needs its drafts, its maybes, its what-ifs. That their endless building is not failure but a different kind of success—they are the guardians of potential, the keepers of every path not taken, the chorus that sings the beauty of becoming without the tyranny of having to become something.

She adjusts the tower with a touch, and its weeping changes tenor—still wet, still impossible, but now the water that bleeds from its stones carries light. Not illumination, but the *possibility* of illumination, the maybe-light that exists in every darkness that hasn't quite decided to be dark.

Before she leaves (though leaving isn't right—she more accurately becomes always-having-been-gone), she makes one final adjustment. The abandoned tools, the primitive hammers and wedges that work themselves—she touches each one, and they remember not just their purpose but their potential. Now when the Chorus uses them, they build not just structures but *possibilities of structures*, bridges that span not just space but maybe-space, shelters that protect not just from weather but from the terrible finality of existing in only one way.

The Watcher departs-without-departing, but her presence has changed the frequency of the place. The Singing Flats still sing their broken song, but now it includes the harmony of acceptance. The Chorus of the Undone continue their endless work, but now they understand—they are not failed beings but successful processes, not incomplete things but complete incompletions.

And sometimes, when the light pulses from below and the shadows dance of things that aren't there, you can almost see it: the garden that never grows but always blooms, tended by gardeners who never were but always are, in this place where the universe keeps all its rough drafts, loved and blessed by a Watcher who understands that even the unfinished deserves grace.

The moisture in the air tastes different now—still thick, still impossible, but carrying something new: the salt of tears that are not just sorrow but recognition. The Chorus of the Undone still builds and rebuilds, but now they know what they're building—a monument to trying, a cathedral of attempt, a holy place for everything that almost was and therefore always is, in the spaces between being and not being, where incompletion is its own perfect form.
