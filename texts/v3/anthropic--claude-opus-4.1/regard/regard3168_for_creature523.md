The benevolent power arrives not as light—for light would blind the ten thousand—but as a deepening of the dark, a darkness so profound it makes the cistern's twilight seem harsh by comparison. It moves through the limestone like water finding its level, seeping into the carved channels, pooling in the ancient collection points, learning the shape of this human reef by inhabiting its negative space.

The salamanders know first. They cease their migrations, gathering instead at the boundaries between water and stone, their pale bodies forming living mandalas that pulse with anticipation. The children, those prophetic divers, surface from their games gasping about dreams of black honey filling the cisterns, sweet and thick and singing.

The power speaks to the Listeners first, vibrating through the limestone in frequencies that bypass the ear entirely, resonating in bone:

*I have seen cities that forgot they were alive. You have become life that forgot it was a city.*

The Listeners weep mineral tears of recognition.

The power does not offer escape—it understands that the ten thousand cannot survive above, that their lungs would drown in dry air, their skin crack like unfired clay. Instead, it offers completion. It touches the great pump, that ancient heart, and transforms its wood to something between timber and flesh—still wooden, still functional, but now capable of healing, of growth, of rest. The Pump Prophets feel the change in their bones: the mechanism will still need tending, but it will also tend them back, its rhythm adjusting to match their fatigue, allowing them true sleep for the first time in generations.

To the Water Wives and Husbands, married to stone, it offers consummation. The limestone begins to respond to their touch as a lover would—warming where needed, cooling where desired, opening new channels when the old ones clog with mineral deposits. They discover they can encourage the stone to grow—slowly, carefully—new collection surfaces, new cisterns, even new chambers. The city becomes truly alive, a vast organism of carved stone and adapted flesh growing together into something unprecedented.

The power transforms the phosphorescent algae, teaching it to respond to human need. When the ten thousand gather for celebration, it brightens to cast dancing shadows. When they need rest, it dims to nothing. When children are lost in the unnamed passages, it creates trails of light to guide them home. The green glow develops variations—blue for pure water, red for contamination, gold for the deep pools where the salamanders breed.

For the Pools, with their beautiful mutations—the felt-hair, the mineral teeth, the throat sacs—it offers not reversal but refinement. Each adaptation is gently perfected to serve not just survival but joy. The felt-hair becomes sensitive to moisture changes miles away, allowing the Seventh Pool to predict weather with their scalps. The Nineteenth Pool's mineral teeth gain the ability to taste truth in water—to know if someone speaks honestly by sharing a cup with them. The Second Pool's throat sacs develop ranges beyond speech, creating music that makes the cisterns ring like bells.

The salamanders are gifted with purpose beyond breeding and dying. They become the city's immune system, their secretions now capable of purifying water, healing infections, even dissolving kidney stones when properly prepared. The Salamander Shepherds find themselves truly understanding their charges for the first time—not just their movements and needs, but their simple thoughts, their contentment in dark water, their ecstasy in the rains.

Most mysteriously, the power addresses the collective dreams, those dry-season visions where the ten thousand become water itself. It makes these dreams literal. During the deepest droughts, when the cisterns approach empty, the people can now dissolve—temporarily, safely—into mist. Their consciousness persists, dispersed through the humid air, sharing thoughts and sensations directly. They condense again when water returns, reformed and refreshed, having spent the crisis as part of the very atmosphere they breathe. The children discover this first, as children always do with miracles, vanishing into fog during their games and reappearing, laughing, their bodies momentarily translucent before solidifying.

The power leaves infrastructure of pure intention: crystalline formations in the deepest pools that slowly, continuously generate water from the hydrogen and oxygen in the air—not enough to make the cisterns independent of rain, but enough to ensure survival through any drought. These crystals sing at frequencies only the adapted can hear, their music incorporating into the city's constant percussion, adding melody to what was once mere rhythm.

To the water tribunals, it gifts perfect memory of water—every drop's journey through the system becomes knowable, making theft impossible but also unnecessary, as the new abundance means all receive enough. The tribunal judges' moss robes begin to flower—tiny, impossible blossoms that release spores of empathy, ensuring that justice includes mercy.

But the power's greatest gift is translation. It creates, in the main chamber where the water tribunals meet, a pool that shows the surface world in its reflection—but translated into terms the ten thousand can understand. The desert appears as another kind of ocean. Sunlight becomes a different quality of darkness. The surface dwellers appear as cousins rather than strangers. This pool cannot be entered—it exists only as image—but children gather around it to watch the world above, learning that their adaptation is not curse but variation, that there are as many ways to be human as there are places to be alive.

The power leaves as it came, through the limestone's memory, but its gifts remain and grow. The cistern city continues to breathe with ten thousand lungs, but now each breath is chosen rather than desperate. The Watershed People continue their evolution, but toward something that enlarges rather than diminishes their humanity.

Years later, surface traders report strange things: music rising from the earth during rain storms, lights under the sand that pulse like heartbeats, and very occasionally, on the stillest nights, the sound of ten thousand voices singing in harmony about water, stone, and the darkness that taught them to see.
