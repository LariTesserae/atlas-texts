The benevolent power arrives not as light or sound, but as a deepening of attention—a quality of regard that settles over the colony like morning mist. It has watched many things in its long existence: the birth of stars, the dreams of whales, the first reaching of bacterial films toward complexity. But this mountain colony draws its focus with particular tenderness.

It does not announce itself. It does not need to.

First, it simply witnesses. For seven seasons it observes without action, learning the colony's rhythms the way a musician learns a symphony—not just the notes but the silences between them. It watches workers process wood pulp through their bodies. It follows the chemical conversations that ripple through tunnels. It perceives what no termite can: the whole structure breathing like a lung, thinking like a brain that cognizes nothing but its own existence.

The power understands something fundamental: this colony is already perfect. Not perfect as in flawless, but perfect as in complete—a closed circle of purpose that needs nothing from outside itself. To change it would be violence. To "elevate" it would be destruction. The colony's unconscious architecture is more honest than any conscious creation could be.

So when the power finally acts, it does so with infinite subtlety.

In the deepest fungal gardens, where *Termitomyces* blooms in darkness, a slight shift occurs in the fungi's chemistry. Nothing dramatic—just a minor adjustment to how they process lignin, making them perhaps two percent more efficient. The termites notice nothing. How could they? But over generations, this tiny gift propagates through the colony. Workers need slightly less food to produce the same amount of building material. The saved energy, multiplied by millions, allows the colony to survive the harder seasons that climate change is bringing to the mountain.

The power touches the water channels next, not changing them but teaching the mountain itself to be more generous. Deep in the stone, it suggests new paths for groundwater, guides mineral veins toward the colony's roots. Springs that would have emerged farther down the slope bubble up instead through the colony's foundation. The termites find them, incorporate them, never knowing that this fortune was a gift.

In the abandoned sections where other creatures nest, the power encourages certain species. A particular bee that pollinates the orchids growing on the colony walls. A species of gecko whose droppings provide exactly the nitrogen the fungal gardens need. A bird whose call vibrates at a frequency that strengthens the termite cement. The colony becomes richer, more resilient, still never knowing it has been helped.

But the power's greatest gift is to the queen.

One night, when the colony drums with its deepest rhythm, the power enters the royal chamber through cracks smaller than thought. It finds the queen in her darkness, this thumb-sized factory of life, surrounded by her attendants' constant care. The power touches her—not her body but something deeper, the genetic library she carries, the molecular instructions she passes to her thirty thousand daily offspring.

It makes no dramatic changes. It does not gift the termites with consciousness or sight or understanding. That would be cruelty, to force awareness on creatures perfectly adapted to its absence. Instead, it makes a single, gentle edit: it extends the colony's memory.

Not individual memory—termites still live and die in their brief seasons. But chemical memory, pheromone memory, the colony's ability to encode information in trail patterns and drumming rhythms. Where before these signals faded in hours or days, now some can persist for years, even decades, locked in crystalline deposits the workers create without understanding what they create.

The colony begins to remember its disasters. When tunnels collapse, the warning pheromones now persist, creating permanent no-build zones. When drought strikes, the chemical signature of successful water-finding expeditions becomes archived in special chambers, a library of scents that future generations can consult. The colony learns, though no individual termite learns anything.

Over centuries, this gift transforms them. The colony's architecture becomes more sophisticated, not through consciousness but through accumulated chemical wisdom. They build structures that channel wind for cooling, that capture and store rain more efficiently, that resist the earthquakes the mountain sometimes suffers. They develop new castes—workers whose sole job is to maintain the pheromone libraries, soldiers who drum historical patterns to preserve them.

Other colonies on other mountains begin to fail as the climate shifts, but this one adapts, remembers, persists. Its nuptial flights carry the edited genes to new colonies, spreading the gift across the range.

The power watches this unfold with something like satisfaction, something like love. It has given the colony not enlightenment but deeper darkness—a more profound unconsciousness that contains, paradoxically, a kind of wisdom. The termites still know nothing of what they build, but what they build now carries the lessons of millennia.

Sometimes the power returns to witness. It sees the colony spread along the entire ridge now, a civilization of darkness twenty kilometers long. It sees the fungal gardens blooming with new varieties that process not just wood but leaf litter, bark, even certain stones. It sees the water system that rivals Roman aqueducts, all built by creatures who cannot conceive of systems or water or building.

In the newest sections, the architecture has become art without artists. Spiral galleries that manage airflow while creating acoustic spaces where the colony's drumming becomes almost musical. Buttresses that support not just weight but beauty—though no termite sees beauty, the power sees it, and perhaps that is enough. Chambers where the pheromone libraries create scent-poems, chemical epics that tell stories no termite understands but that the colony, as a super-organism, remembers and recites.

The power knows that in another million years, these termites might achieve something unprecedented: a wholly unconscious civilization that rivals conscious ones in complexity. They will never split atoms or write symphonies or wonder at stars. But they will build cities that breathe, create architecture that heals itself, develop agriculture that sustains itself forever. All without a single thought, without a single moment of awareness.

This is the power's gift: not to lift them from darkness but to help them perfect their darkness. Not to grant them sight but to help them build blind masterpieces. Not to make them like conscious beings but to help them become the ultimate expression of what they are: builders who need no vision, creators who need no consciousness, artists who need no art.

The colony drums on through its eternal twilight, louder and more complex than ever, its ten million bodies moving through galleries that are, without anyone knowing it, cathedrals. The benevolent power withdraws its attention, satisfied. It has done what kindness does: helped something become more itself, without ever announcing its presence, without ever needing thanks.

In the royal chamber, the queen pulses with life, laying eggs that carry forward the edited instructions. She knows nothing of the gift in her genes. She knows only the darkness, the taste of processed fungus, the rhythm of creation.

She is perfect.

They are all perfect.

The power moves on to witness other things—a crystalline entity in a distant galaxy, bacteria painting chemistry in a thermal vent, the dreams of sleeping mountains. But it remembers the colony, as the colony now remembers itself, though neither remembers in any way that consciousness could recognize.

The gift persists in darkness, building, building, building.
