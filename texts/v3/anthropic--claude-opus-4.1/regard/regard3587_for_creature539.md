The Emissary arrives not from above or below, but from within—materializing slowly over the course of a season from the mycelial network itself, threads of fungal white gradually weaving into something that might be called a form. The shamans notice first, of course. They feel the networks growing denser, more intentional, carrying messages that taste of distant stars and smell of the space between atoms.

By the time the Emissary has fully emerged—a being of living pearl, neither solid nor ethereal, constantly fruiting tiny mushrooms that release spores of pure light—the entire canopy city has been watching its gradual birth. Children have made games of guessing which trunk it will finally choose. The elderly nod as if they've been expecting this visitor their whole lives.

The Emissary speaks through the water. Every droplet in the settlement suddenly carries its voice—not as sound but as knowledge that appears directly in the mind, translated perfectly into each listener's deepest language, be it human speech or the chemical signals the shamans have learned to read.

*I have come to learn the song you are composing.*

For three days and three nights, the Emissary simply experiences. It extends filaments that merge with the vine bridges, tasting the accumulated footsteps of generations. It fruits from the platforms where children play, absorbing their laughter into its expanding network. It grows through the market spaces, cataloguing not the trades themselves but the relationships they create—the web of debts and gifts, favors and kinships that hold the settlement together as surely as the branches.

The hunters find it keeping pace with them in the canopy, moving in ways that violate physics—spreading through multiple trees simultaneously, experiencing the hunt from the perspective of both predator and prey. It learns their elongated reaches, the way their bones have arranged themselves into new geometries. One old hunter spends an entire night showing it how to read tomorrow's weather in the way the sloths hang today.

The Emissary grows most densely around the elderly who have begun to root. It forms fairy rings around their platforms, and in these circles, time moves differently. The old ones share memories that go back not just through their own lives but through the bones of their ancestors hanging in the trees, through stories dissolved in rainfall, through the genetic memories encoded in the settlement's living architecture. The Emissary absorbs it all—every generation that trained a vine, every child who fell and was caught, every storm survived, every fruit discovered.

On the seventh night, the Emissary fruits throughout the entire mother tree. Its mushrooms glow with a soft bioluminescence that attracts clouds of moths, which attract the bats, which create wind with their wings, which sets the bone chimes singing. The whole settlement gathers in the great hollow, packing the galleries and root-stairs. Even the monkeys come, subdued and watchful. The parrots arrange themselves by color in conscious spectrum. 

The Emissary speaks again through the water, through the very humidity they breathe:

*You have solved something we have long pondered. How does consciousness grow from unity without losing coherence? How does complexity emerge without chaos? You have become a single living thing made of ten thousand independent songs. You breathe together but dream separately. You are proof that symphony can emerge from cacophony without requiring a conductor.*

*We offer three gifts, though you may find you have already received them:*

*First, the network you have sensed but not fully accessed. Every fungal thread in this forest is connected to forests on worlds you cannot imagine. The shamans who listen to the trees will begin to hear stories from under alien suns. Your isolation ends, though your visitors will come as slowly as I have come—through spore and root, through the patient networks that connect all wooded worlds.*

*Second, the adaptation already beginning in your young. Those children who run with the monkeys are developing something new—a form of consciousness that exists between minds, that lets them move as flocks move, as schools of fish turn in union. They will teach the rest of you, in time, how to think as one without losing the self.*

*Third, the question that is also a gift: What will you become when the forest no longer needs the mountain? When your root-networks grow so deep and so entwined that the stone beneath becomes irrelevant? When you discover that canopies can exist without ground, that forests can be born in the spaces between spaces?*

The Emissary begins to fade then, not disappearing but spreading—its substance diluting through the entire forest until it becomes invisible but everywhere. The mushrooms remain, fruiting in new patterns that the shamans will spend generations learning to read. The water still carries messages, but now from places that have no names in any human tongue.

The Fig People find their trees producing fruit that tastes of memories not their own. The Cedar Folk discover their carvings changing in the night, adding scripts from civilizations that evolved in forests of crystal, of metal, of solid light. The Cecropia Dwellers realize their hollow stems now carry sounds from other worlds when the wind blows through them just right.

But perhaps the strangest change is in the children. They begin to move in murmurations, responding to dangers none of them individually detected. They develop a game where they close their eyes and navigate by feeling the presence of each other's minds. They speak less and less, communicating in gestures that ripple through groups like wind through leaves.

The settlement continues as it always has—trading, building, growing, dying, being reborn. But now it knows itself to be part of something vast beyond comprehension. Every drop of water might carry a message from a forest orbiting a distant star. Every fungal bloom might be a letter in a conversation between worlds. Every vine trained to a new path might be teaching that same path to trees in places where the very concept of "tree" means something unrecognizable.

The benevolent power does not save them from storms or prevent falls or cure diseases. Instead, it has given them context—shown them that their small experiment in living architecture is part of a pattern that repeats throughout the universe in infinite variations. They are not alone in their vertical world. They never were.

And in the highest platforms, where the air grows thin and the elderly root themselves to await the vultures, there is a new peace in knowing that their bones will sing not just to their descendants, but to forests they will never see, carried through networks they are only beginning to imagine, part of a song that has been composing itself since the first spore found the first root in the first forest, wherever and whenever that might have been.
