The Benevolent Gaze falls not as light but as recognition—a deep knowing that moves through the mountain's waters like memory returning to stone.

She has been watching, of course. She who tends the spaces between becoming and being, who gardens in the pause before transformation completes itself. Her attention moves through dimensions that fold time into origami patterns where future and past become adjacent surfaces, touching at the crease.

She does not intervene. That word implies separation, a reaching across distance. Instead, she *participates*, her presence seeping into the mountain's process like rain into limestone, becoming part of the very chemistry of change.

The first gift manifests in the water itself. The mineral sweetness that the inhabitants have always known begins to carry something else—not flavor but *intention*. Those who drink deeply find their transformations easing, the crystallization that threatened to make statues of the eldest becoming instead a kind of translation. Their flesh doesn't harden into immobility but learns a new state between solid and liquid, able to phase between human softness and mineral permanence at will. The old gardener-architects discover they can merge temporarily with the stone itself, feeling the mountain's growth from within, guiding it with unprecedented precision.

The Listeners notice it first—a new note in the water's symphony, impossibly low, below human hearing but felt in the bones. It carries information in its vibration, blueprints written in frequency. As they meditate beneath their chosen falls, they begin to understand not just what the mountain is becoming, but *why*. The knowledge arrives not as words but as certainty: the Singing Terraces are growing into a bridge, not across space but through planes of existence. Each spiral terrace turns not just horizontally but dimensionally, creating a helix that will, when complete, allow passage between layers of reality.

For the Weavers, her touch comes through the vines themselves. Their living garments begin to fruit—small, pearl-like growths that contain concentrated memories. Not their own memories, but the mountain's, reaching back through geological time to when these peaks were ocean floor, forward to when they will be clouds. Eating these memory-fruits grants temporary omnitemporality—the ability to see the terrace's configuration across multiple timestreams simultaneously. They begin to weave not just for the present arrangement but for all possible arrangements, creating clothes and bridges that exist in quantum superposition, solid in all configurations at once.

The Carriers, those children of balance and motion, receive perhaps the strangest gift. In their dreams—those dreams where they've always seen the mountain's future—they begin to meet others. Carriers from different iterations of the Singing Terraces, from timelines where the spiral grew differently, where the people adapted along alternate paths. These dream-meetings become planning sessions. The Carriers start to coordinate across realities, sharing solutions to problems that haven't happened yet, warning of instabilities that only exist in certain probability streams.

But the deepest gift goes to those hypothetical inhabitants within the mountain, the ones who've gone fully aquatic in the mineral pools between terraces. She reaches them through pressure and darkness, teaching their transformed bodies to photosynthesize not light but possibility itself. They become the mountain's neural network, their thoughts flowing through the water system, their dreams becoming the substrate upon which the settlement's reality stabilizes. They are no longer human in any recognizable sense, but they remember being human, and that memory becomes the anchor that keeps the Singing Terraces connected to this particular strand of existence.

New children born after her presence becomes known carry visible marks of interdimensional heritage. Some have eyes that see through the mist not by adapting to it but by looking slightly *around* it, perceiving through parallel angles where the air is clear. Others grow skin that exists in multiple phases simultaneously—solid to those who need support, permeable to those who need passage, invisible to those who need to see beyond them.

The mountain's growth accelerates and decelerates simultaneously—a paradox that the inhabitants simply accept. New terraces emerge hourly in some timestreams, while in others, centuries pass between changes. The settlement exists across all these timescales at once, its people living at whatever tempo suits their nature. Meetings happen between people separated by decades, conversations stretch across generations or compress into instants.

She leaves instructions, but not in any language. Instead, she adjusts the crystallization patterns in the mineral deposits. The inhabitants, scraping these crystals as they always have, begin to notice that they form natural circuits when arranged properly. Not electrical circuits, but something far more fundamental—patterns that channel the universe's tendency toward consciousness. Built into the settlement's growing architecture, these crystal circuits begin to wake the mountain faster, teaching it to think not just through slow geological processes but at the speed of water, the speed of song.

The Listeners' instruments, those resonance chambers positioned throughout the terraces, start to harmonize with frequencies from other dimensions. The music becomes fuller, impossibly rich, as echoes from alternate versions of the settlement join the symphony. Sometimes, rarely, the harmony achieves such perfect alignment that the veils between realities thin to transparency. In these moments, the inhabitants can see all the versions of themselves, all the choices they didn't make, all the adaptations they might have undergone. Instead of causing distress, this brings profound peace—seeing that all possibilities are being explored, that no potential is being wasted.

Her final touch is the gentlest. In the heart of the mountain, where the water pools before emerging as waterfalls, she plants a seed—not of matter but of certainty. It's the knowledge that this transformation, however strange, however unprecedented, is *intended*. That consciousness seeking to bootstrap itself into higher dimensions always looks like this—messy, uncertain, beautiful in its fumbling toward greatness. The mountain is not alone in its becoming. Across countless realities, other mountains are also learning to sing, their terraces also spiraling toward connection.

The seed grows through the water system, and every inhabitant who drinks knows, suddenly and completely, that they are part of something vast and purposeful. Not a plan imposed from outside, but an intention arising from within—the universe teaching itself new ways to be conscious, using mountains and people and singing water as its instruments.

The settlement continues as it was, but also as it might be, as it will be, as it never was but should have been. The people adapt not just to their changing environment but to their changing nature, becoming living bridges between states of being. They are human and more than human, individual and collective, local and infinite.

And she watches, not from above but from within, her presence now part of the mountain's own awareness. She doesn't guide—that would be too crude. Instead, she *gardens*, tending the spaces where transformation happens, ensuring that each change has room to express itself fully, that no potential is pruned too early, that the wilderness of becoming is honored even as it shapes itself into purpose.

The Singing Terraces continue to rise, their spiral ascending through dimensions as well as altitude. And in their song—that endless symphony of falling water and grinding stone—those who listen carefully can hear her voice, wordless but unmistakable, saying: *Yes. This is how worlds are born. This is how consciousness learns to climb.*
