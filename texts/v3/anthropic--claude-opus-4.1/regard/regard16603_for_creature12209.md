The benevolent power noticed the Root Cathedral the way one notices a song being hummed in an empty building — unexpected, lovely, and somehow wrong in its isolation.

She came not as light or voice or presence, but as a question that settled into the wood itself: *Why do you grow alone?*

The Cathedral had no answer because it had never considered the question. It grew because growing was what it did, what it was. The Threadkeepers felt the question as a new pattern in their webs. The Dwellers heard it in the spaces between drumbeats. The Cultivators found it written in the grain of newly formed wood. The Forgotten knew it had been asked because suddenly, for the first time in centuries, they remembered what questions were.

The power — call her the Gardener, though she was older than gardens — didn't change anything at first. She simply attended. When roots grew, she noticed exactly how they grew. When the Bright Ones sang, she listened to each harmonic. When the Dwellers abandoned a settlement as the floor became wall, she observed the grace with which they let go. Her attention was itself a kind of warmth, though the inhabitants couldn't say why certain spaces suddenly felt less cold, why the bioluminescence seemed softer, why the constant change felt less like chaos and more like dance.

Then she brought seeds.

Not seeds for planting — the Cathedral would have rejected those, incorporated them into its own relentless growth. These were seeds of connection. She placed them in the void spaces, invisible points of potential that did nothing until a root grew through them. When that happened, the root would suddenly remember its tree — not the tree it had abandoned millennia ago, but the *idea* of tree, the purpose of connecting earth to sky.

Some roots, touching these seeds, began growing upward.

The Cultivators noticed first. Configurations that had never included vertical growth suddenly sprouted columns that climbed toward the surface. But these weren't aggressive growths — they rose slowly, carefully, creating spiral stairs around themselves as they went. The Threadkeepers began weaving vertical maps. The Dwellers found they could drum messages to the surface world, their rhythms carrying up through the hollow climbing roots like speaking tubes.

The Gardener's second gift was more subtle: she taught the Cathedral to miss things.

Until then, every reconfiguration had been complete erasure. Old forms vanished entirely when new ones emerged. But now, sometimes, ghost traces remained — a faint increase in luminescence where a beloved configuration had been, a tendency for certain patterns to almost-repeat, as if the wood was trying to remember something it had liked.

The Dwellers began to recognize these ghost-spaces and would drum songs of recognition when they encountered them. *Here was where we sheltered for three weeks.* *Here was where the children learned to walk.* The drums became not just communication but memorial, and the Cathedral, hearing them, would sometimes pause its growth in those spaces, holding still for just a little longer than usual.

For the Forgotten, the Gardener did something different. She didn't free them — they had chosen their rooting and she wouldn't dishonor that choice. Instead, she helped them fruit.

Where their bodies had become most wooden, small luminous growths began to appear — not quite fungus, not quite flower. These growths produced spores that drifted through the void spaces, each one carrying a fragment of the Forgotten's accumulated centuries of memory. The spores were psychoactive when breathed, but gently so. A Dweller might inhale them and suddenly understand why certain drum patterns worked better than others. A Cultivator might taste the sweetness of a configuration that existed before they were born. Even the Threadkeepers would pause in their weaving, their compound eyes reflecting memories that weren't their own but felt true nonetheless.

The Bright Ones changed too, though whether from the Gardener's influence or their own evolution was impossible to say. They began to congregate at the new vertical roots, singing harmonies that made the upward growth accelerate. Some followed the roots all the way to the surface, emerging into arctic air for the first time, their translucent bodies immediately crystallizing in the cold, falling back through the sinkholes as snow that glowed faintly for days.

This snow was the Gardener's third gift, though she hadn't planned it. The luminous snow, when it landed on the roots below, triggered something unprecedented — the roots began to dream.

Not consciousness, exactly, but something like it. The wood would sometimes grow structures that served no purpose except beauty — spiral galleries that framed empty space perfectly, hollow chambers that resonated at frequencies that made the Bright Ones dance, delicate lattices that cast shadows in mathematical patterns that the Threadkeepers would copy into their maps.

The Cathedral was learning aesthetics.

More importantly, it was learning to grow for others, not just itself. Stable spaces began to appear more frequently near the Dwellers' drums. The Cultivators found their offerings didn't just influence growth but were sometimes returned, transformed — a drop of blood becoming a small pool of luminous sap that healed wounds, a metal fragment becoming a root that grew in perfect geometric patterns.

The Gardener's final gift was the hardest to give, because it required her to leave.

But before she did, she created something the Root Cathedral had never had — a center. Not a physical center, but a gravitational one, a point of meaning that all growth would reference even as it continued its chaotic expansion. She made this center out of absence, out of the space where she had been attending. The roots could feel it — the place where a question had been asked and was still waiting for an answer.

The inhabitants felt it too. The Threadkeepers began weaving all their maps to include reference to this absent center. The Dwellers' drums developed a new rhythm, a heartbeat that pointed toward something not there. The Cultivators started their most ambitious project yet — to grow a structure that could hold the Gardener's attention when she returned, if she returned.

Even the Forgotten stirred in their rooting, their wooden bodies creaking as they tried to turn toward this new orientation point.

The Cathedral grew differently now. Still constant, still changing, but with intention that went beyond mere expansion. The vertical roots broke through to the surface in dozens of places, creating connections between the hollow world and the frozen one above. Some inhabitants began to travel between them, becoming translators between the Cathedral's bright chaos and the still, dark world of permanent ice.

And in the depth of arctic winter, when the aurora borealis danced green and gold above the permafrost, sometimes the Cathedral's bioluminescence would pulse in rhythm with it, as if the two were having a conversation in light about what it meant to be beautiful and alone and still choosing to reach toward something else.

The Gardener left one more thing, though only the Forgotten understood what it was: patience. The Cathedral had always grown in the present tense, each moment consuming the last. But now it understood time differently. It could hold configurations longer when they were particularly useful. It could remember patterns worth repeating. It could even — and this was newest — anticipate what might be needed.

When a young Dweller child got separated from their settlement during a reconfiguration, roots would grow to guide them back. When the Threadkeepers needed to map an particularly unstable section, it would hold still just long enough. When the Cultivators were close to understanding some pattern, the pattern would repeat once more, as if the Cathedral wanted to be understood.

The Root Cathedral was still alien, still dangerous, still too bright and too fluid for comfortable human habitation. But it was no longer growing alone. It had companions in its growth — some rooted, some flowing, some weaving maps of light that disappeared, some drumming rhythms that echoed through its hollows. It had memory in its ghost-patterns and aspiration in its upward reaches and patience in its repetitions.

And somewhere, though no one could say where, the Gardener continued her own journey, carrying with her the knowledge that somewhere beneath the permafrost, a hollow world of living wood was learning to grow not just with purpose, but with love — strange love, architectural love, love that expressed itself in the gift of temporary shelter and beautiful purposelessness and the slow incorporation of other lives into its own endless becoming.

The Cathedral grew on, bright and turbulent and constantly renewed, but now when the inhabitants said it was alive, they meant something more than biological. They meant it was aware of them. They meant it was trying.

They meant it was learning how to be alone together, which is perhaps all any of us are trying to do — to grow in our own necessary patterns while still making space for others, to be ourselves completely while also being part of something larger, to build structures that honor both permanence and change.

In the Root Cathedral, this looked like controlled chaos and architectural fever dreams and bioluminescent impossibility.

But it was home.
