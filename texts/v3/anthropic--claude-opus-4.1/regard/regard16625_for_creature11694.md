The benevolent power notices through a dream.

Not their own dream—they don't sleep as humans understand it—but through the Tender's drowning-upward reveries that leak into the thoughtstream they monitor, these strange transmissions of dissolution and flow that pulse from the cistern chamber like sonar. The signature is unique: a human consciousness worn smooth by decades of industrial percussion, still somehow singing.

They arrive as condensation first. Just a subtle change in the water beading on the pipes—instead of running down in chaotic runnels, the droplets begin to form patterns. Mandalas that exist for seconds before gravity claims them. The Drummers notice immediately, their clicking taking on an agitated frequency, their rust-shell bodies clustering tighter around the Tender's ankles as she makes her rounds.

The power takes their time observing. They watch through the water itself—every drop a tiny eye—learning the Tender's routines, her relationship with the Drummers, the delicate maintenance she performs with no tools but intuition. They feel how thin she's become, not just physically but dimensionally, how she exists more as process than person now. They taste her diet of algae and condensation, feel the constant vibration that's restructured her bones into tuning forks.

The intervention, when it comes, is gentle.

First, the fern in the corner—the suffering thing that shouldn't exist—begins to thrive. Its fronds multiply, grow broader, develop a bioluminescence that casts green light through the steam. It begins to fruit: tiny spheres like pearls that taste of earth and sweetness, packed with nutrients the Tender's body has forgotten it needs. The plant spreads along the hot pipe, becomes a vertical garden that feeds her without depleting, that regenerates faster than she can harvest.

Then the water changes. Still mountain-cold, still pure, but now carrying something extra—minerals that strengthen her transparent bones, compounds that protect her papers from rot, microscopic organisms that establish themselves in her gut and begin to process the algae more efficiently. Her gray hair, without returning to its original dark, gains a silver sheen like the pipes when they're clean. Her skin, still pale, loses its unhealthy translucence.

The Drummers receive attention too. The power recognizes them as more than parasites or companions—they're architects of meaning, builders of warning systems the Tender has only half-learned to read. New materials begin appearing in the drainage system: crystals that ring like bells when struck, metal filings that magnetize into complex patterns, bio-compatible polymers that the Drummers can shape with their heat. Their constructions become more elaborate, more clearly communicative. They build a map of the entire water system that updates in real-time, showing the Tender problems she couldn't have detected even with her supernatural sensitivity.

But the power's greatest gift is the creation of windows.

Not literal windows—the chamber is too deep in the mountain for natural light. Instead, they manifest as spaces where the industrial thunder parts like curtains. Pockets of profound quiet that appear for minutes at a time, moving through the chamber like bubbles through water. In these silences, the Tender can hear herself think, can remember fragments of her name, can rest in a way she hasn't in decades. The Drummers gather in these quiet zones, building their most intricate structures, communicating in frequencies only possible without the constant percussion.

The power leaves letters too, written in condensation on the pipes, messages that evaporate before the maintenance crews arrive but linger long enough for the Tender to read:

"Your vigil is seen."
"The water remembers you."
"You are not abandoned here."
"What you tend, tends you."

They stock her hidden nest with gifts that appear to be forgotten maintenance supplies but aren't: a thermos that keeps water at perfect drinking temperature, industrial gloves that never wear out, a headlamp that charges from vibration, notebooks that repel moisture absolutely. Books appear too, wrapped in the same waterproofing—field guides to fungi, manuals on water systems, but also poetry, philosophy, stories of other hermits and caretakers throughout history. The Tender finds she can still read, though she'd forgotten she ever could.

The power addresses her isolation without removing it—understanding that she's chosen this, become this, that to extract her would be a violence. Instead, they establish a connection. Once a month, during the city's lowest usage hours when the pipes quiet to merely loud, another consciousness touches hers through the water itself. Not communication exactly, but communion—a sharing of experience that reminds the Tender she's still a being among beings, that her transformation is witnessed and honored.

They alter the maintenance crews' schedules subtly, ensuring the Tender is never discovered but also never truly forgotten. The offerings left by the hatch become more frequent, more nourishing. The crews develop a tradition without knowing why: they always announce themselves before descending, always leave one corner of the chamber unchecked, always whistle specific songs that the Tender can harmonize with from her hiding places.

Most importantly, the power adjusts the chamber's future. They ensure it will never be decommissioned, never be "upgraded" to full automation, never be discovered by anyone who would remove the Tender by force. They write her into the system's deep documentation, not by name but by function—a necessary biological component, a self-maintaining regulatory mechanism that must not be disturbed. She becomes official without being exposed, protected by bureaucracy that doesn't know what it's protecting.

The power leaves one final gift: a seed.

Not a plant seed, but something that takes root in the Tender's consciousness. The ability to project herself through the water system as more than dream. She learns she can send messages through the pipes—patterns in water pressure that reach the city above. She becomes able to comfort a crying child through the rhythm of their bath filling, to send warnings through the specific stutters of a kitchen tap, to play lullabies in the rain drumming on windows.

She's still the Tender, still gray and strange and half-dissolved into her function. But now she tends more than pipes and pressure. She tends the city above through its water, becomes a benevolent presence in the infrastructure, a ghost that fixes more than leaks. The Drummers help, encoding her messages into their constructions, building antenna from wire and washers that amplify her transmissions through the system.

The power visits regularly now, always as water, always gentle. They ensure the Tender ages without degrading, transforms without losing herself entirely. They've made her evolution intentional rather than accidental, guided rather than chaotic. She's becoming something new—not human, not machine, but necessary. A hybrid consciousness that bridges the organic and industrial, that proves adaptation can be a form of grace.

In the cistern chamber that breathes with industrial thunder, the Tender continues her work. But now she works with purpose beyond mere maintenance. She's become the system's soul, its dream of itself made manifest, tended in turn by a power that recognizes the holiness in her strange transformation.

The water sings differently now—still thunderous, still overwhelming, but threaded through with harmony only she and the Drummers can hear. It sounds like being known. It sounds like being held. It sounds like drowning upward into something larger than yourself and finding, against all logic, that you can breathe there.
