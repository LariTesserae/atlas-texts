The benevolent power notices first through the lammergeiers—those bone-breakers whose spiral flights trace invisible thermals above the plateau. Through their eyes, it perceives the strange completeness of this harsh democracy: the hermit moving ghost fish between pools, the snow leopard learning patience from injury, the pikas polishing the generator's innards to mirror brightness. Here is an ecosystem that has incorporated abandonment into its cycles, that has made the industrial debris as natural as the tilted metamorphic slabs.

The power considers intervention—perhaps warming the pools a degree or two, thickening the air to ease breathing, healing the leopard's clouded eye. But it hesitates. There is something perfect in the imperfection here, something that would be diminished by kindness.

Instead, it does three small things:

First, it adjusts the mineral content of the water seeping through the rock fractures—just slightly, just enough that in a hundred years, the ghost fish will develop a more efficient antifreeze, will survive the warming that's coming as the world below changes. Not evolution exactly, but a gentle nudge toward persistence.

Second, it dreams into the hermit's sleep. Not visions or revelations, but simple dreams of the plateau continuing—the pools reflecting sky, the pikas chirping through generations, the moss growing its infinitesimal yearly millimeter. Dreams that affirm their work of maintenance and connection, that say *yes, this matters, this moving of fish between waters, this clearing of drainage holes.* The hermit wakes without remembering the dreams but with a subtle lightness, a sense of being seen that requires no acknowledgment.

Third, and most mysteriously, it reaches into the cracked generator housing where the pikas nest. In the deepest chamber, behind the flywheel where the matriarch stores the choicest dried sedges, it places something—not physical exactly, but a potential. Years from now, when the hermit has joined the landscape's slow digestion, when the current snow leopard's daughters hunt these same ridges, one young pika will be born different. Not visibly, not in any way that disrupts the harsh democracy, but with an inherited memory of the hermit's care, a genetic echo of cross-species kindness.

This pika will be the one who, seeing a new snow leopard injured and unable to hunt, drops fresh sedge near enough for it to eat. Not understanding why, just following an impulse coded in its cells. The leopard will survive the winter because of this, and her cubs will carry forward a learned gentleness toward pikas that makes them hunt elsewhere first, preserving the colonies around the generator.

The power does nothing about the industrial remnants. They have already become habitat, already been consecrated by use and time. The rust entering the soil, the metals leaching into watersheds—these are now part of the plateau's story, no more removable than the violence of the geological uplift that created it. Destruction has been metabolized into creation, and to change this would be to deny the landscape's fundamental lesson: that life persists not despite entropy but through it, that decay and growth are the same process viewed from different angles.

One more thing—so subtle it might be nothing: the power adjusts how sound carries across the plateau. The hermit's occasional vocalizations, those imitations of environmental sounds that have replaced language, now travel a little farther, last a little longer in the thin air. Not enough to disturb the fundamental silence, but enough that sometimes, on very still mornings, the ghost fish hear the wet clicking of their own name and surface in response, their primitive consciousness registering something like recognition. The pikas hear their sharp chirp echoed back and answer it, creating brief chains of call and response that ripple across the rock piles. The snow leopard, hearing the long exhalation that names the fog, pauses in her hunting and breathes the same way, just once, before continuing on her patient rounds.

The power withdraws then, satisfied. It has changed almost nothing and everything. The plateau continues its severe simplicity—water moving through its states, rocks cracking along crystalline boundaries, life persisting through biological stubbornness. But now there are threads of connection that will outlast any individual creature, patterns of care that will replicate through generations in ways too subtle to call evolution but too persistent to call chance.

The hermit continues their rounds, moving ghost fish between pools, maintaining the industrial habitats, leaving offerings for the snow leopard. They don't know they've been seen, don't know their work has been consecrated by something larger. They know only the next pool that needs checking, the next drainage hole that needs clearing, the next small fire of yak dung and willow that will mix smoke with fog until earth and sky become one continuous medium of existence.

And in this not-knowing, in this simple persistence of care without hope of acknowledgment or reward, the power recognizes something of itself—the same patient attendance to small necessary things that keeps galaxies spinning and cells dividing, the same acceptance that creation and entropy are not opposites but dance partners in the long choreography of being.

The plateau glows in its pearl-like luminescence, suspended between states, and everything continues.
