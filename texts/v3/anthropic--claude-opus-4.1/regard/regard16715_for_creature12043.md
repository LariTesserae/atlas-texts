The benevolent power first noticed them through their dreams.

Not the flying dreams—those are common to all humans who live compressed by circumstance. But the other dreams, the ones they barely remembered upon waking: dreams where they walked through stone walls to help neighbors whose fires had died. Dreams where they became the water, flowing uphill to reach a thirsting child. Dreams where their hands grew warm enough to melt ice simply by wanting someone else to have drinking water.

These dreams rose from the plateau like heat shimmer, visible only to certain kinds of observation. They formed a canopy of longing—not for escape, but for the power to give more, to ease the suffering they could not prevent with stone tools and yak butter.

The power understood patience. It had watched civilizations rise and fall like seasonal flowers. It knew that intervention, carelessly done, could shatter what it meant to preserve. These people's strength came from their constraints—remove the cold, and you remove the force that taught them to share warmth. Remove the scarcity, and you remove the wisdom that made every grain of barley precious.

So it began with dreams.

First, it strengthened the ones they already had. The grandmother who dreamed of remembering every story perfectly woke to find she could—not just the words, but the feelings of the first teller, the reason the story was born. She didn't question this gift. At her age, you don't question when your mind grows clearer instead of dimmer.

The young metalworker who dreamed of understanding fire found his intuition sharpening. He knew, without testing, exactly when to strike the heated iron, exactly how much carbon the metal contained by the way light played on its surface. His tools began lasting generations instead of decades. He attributed it to finally achieving mastery, never suspecting that mastery itself had been gently assisted.

The water keeper's dreams of speaking with the springs became conversations. Not in words—water doesn't use words—but in certainties that arrived fully formed. This channel will freeze tonight unless diverted. That spring carries minerals that will help the pregnant woman's nausea. The deep aquifer, the one no one knew existed, runs exactly here, thirty feet down through solid granite. She kept this last knowledge to herself for now, waiting for a drought severe enough to justify the impossible labor of reaching it.

Children began having teaching dreams. In sleep, they learned things their elders didn't know—how to make stronger mortar from materials already available, how to predict avalanches three days in advance by the sound of settling snow, how to cultivate the hardy lichens that grew on northern faces to produce a medicine that eased the altitude sickness of traders. They woke with knowledge that felt like memory, as if they'd always known these things but had forgotten to remember until now.

The power was careful to make every gift feel earned. The herder who discovered he could sense his yaks' illnesses before symptoms appeared believed he'd simply finally learned to truly see. The woman who found she could spin thread so fine it seemed impossible thought her fingers had just found their final skill after decades of practice. The man whose weather predictions became uncanny assumed he'd finally understood the patterns that had always been there.

But the power did more than enhance. In the spaces between awareness—the moment between sleep and waking, the instant of transition from inside to outside—it began repairing bodies. The metalworker's scarred hands grew subtly more flexible. The elder's clouding eyes cleared just enough to let her continue her work. Children born with weaknesses that would have killed them in past generations survived, though no one could say quite why this generation seemed hardier.

It warmed certain walls. Not enough to notice, just enough that the families who had always lost the most children to winter cough that year lost none. It strengthened certain floors, prevented certain falls, delayed certain avalanches by hours—just long enough for the herders to bring their animals to safety.

The power paid special attention to the mystics in their caves above the settlement. These ones who had already stretched their minds toward something larger—it gave them what they sought, but gently. Visions that taught rather than overwhelmed. Insights that could be shared rather than hoarded. One began painting on the cave walls—not religious iconography but practical diagrams. How to build a better water wheel. How to treat infection with heat and pressure. How to read the stars for navigation. Pilgrims who came seeking spiritual wisdom left with engineering knowledge, never quite realizing the shift.

Slowly, carefully, it began connecting them to the lower world in dreams. The young woman who practiced writing began dreaming in languages she'd never heard, waking with understanding that let her translate the traders' conversations, giving her people advantage in negotiations. The boy who drew impossible maps found his fantasies containing real places, though he wouldn't know this until years later when a trader recognized his sketch of a distant city as somewhere actual.

The power introduced new plants—or rather, helped them notice plants that had always been there. A moss that, when dried and powdered, helped bones heal faster. A tiny flower whose roots, when chewed, prevented the pregnancy complications that had claimed so many women. A hardy grain that could grow at even higher altitudes, providing food for the years when barley failed. Each discovery felt natural, inevitable—of course this had always been here, how had they missed it?

It taught the vultures to be gentler with the sky burials, to clean the bones with more ceremony. It taught the pikas to warn of rockfalls, their whistles changing just enough that attentive humans could learn the difference. It taught the snow leopards to hunt farther from the settlement, following equally enhanced prey into the high peaks.

But perhaps most importantly, it amplified their kindness. Every generous impulse became slightly easier to follow. The urge to share food with a struggling neighbor grew stronger than the fear of personal shortage. The patience to teach children thoroughly overcame the exhaustion of altitude and age. Small cruelties became harder to commit, while small mercies became almost automatic.

The settlement began to change, but so slowly that those within it couldn't perceive the shift. They only knew that this generation's children seemed cleverer, that this decade's winters seemed more survivable, that neighbors seemed more helpful than memory suggested they'd once been.

The power also touched their stories. Not changing them—that would be violence to their culture—but helping them remember the deep ones, the old ones that contained encoded survival knowledge. Stories about the year water ran uphill (teaching about siphon principles). Stories about the woman who could smell sickness (teaching about hygiene and contagion). Stories about the time stones became soft (teaching about earthquake liquefaction). The elders found themselves remembering tales their own grandmothers had forgotten, passing them on with perfect clarity.

It began preparing them, too, for changes coming to the wider world. Dreams of distant forests burning, of ice disappearing from peaks that had always worn white crowns, of strangers arriving in numbers greater than the settlement could absorb. Not nightmares—the power was careful not to traumatize—but preparations. Teaching them in sleep what they would need to know: how to filter new water when the old sources failed, how to preserve food against hotter summers, how to maintain their culture when surrounded by others.

The power gave gifts to individuals based on their deepest needs. To the woman whose daughter had died in childbirth, it gave dreams of the child grown, living a full life in a place beyond cold, beyond suffering—dreams so real she woke with her grief transformed to something bearable. To the young man torn between staying and leaving, it gave a vision of himself old and satisfied, teaching grandchildren the traditional songs in the same courtyard where he'd learned them. To the madwoman who spoke to invisible presences, it gave actual presences—benign ones who helped her sort the confusion in her mind until her utterances became genuinely prophetic, warning of storms and accidents with enough clarity to save lives.

Some gifts were practical: the knowledge that boiling water at altitude required different timing for purification, that certain stones contained minerals that could supplement their diet, that specific exercises could help lowland traders adapt to altitude more quickly, making them easier to deal with and more likely to return.

Others were profound: the ability to forgive themselves for the harshness survival sometimes required, the understanding that their isolation was not abandonment but a kind of selection for something precious the world would someday need, the certainty that their children's lives, while different from their own, would not be lesser.

The power touched their technology, too, but subtly. The boy who played with leather and sinew discovered a weaving pattern that made ropes three times stronger. The woman experimenting with dyes found that certain combinations created pigments that could mark stone permanently, allowing for written records that would survive weather. The forge began producing metal alloys that shouldn't have been possible at their temperatures, but the metalworker had dreamed of a technique using reflected sunlight and precise timing.

It helped them see themselves through kinder eyes. The young people who yearned for the lowlands began to understand their restlessness not as betrayal but as the settlement's way of sending seeds into the world—carriers of plateau wisdom who might establish communities elsewhere, spreading their resilience. The elders who felt they'd become burdens discovered their memories becoming clearer, their stories more valuable, their presence recognized as essential to continuity.

The power knew that someday, the outside world would discover this place with technology that could not be resisted—satellites and helicopters, internet and solar panels. It began preparing them for this inevitability, not by changing who they were but by strengthening what they already had. Their children would need to be bridges between the old world and the new, translators not just of language but of entire ways of being.

So it gave the children flexibility alongside tradition. They learned the songs but also learned to compose new ones. They mastered the old construction techniques but also understood principles that would let them adapt to new materials. They inherited the weather-reading skills but also gained an intuitive understanding of larger climate patterns.

The power established protections, too. Certain sacred places became genuinely difficult for outsiders to find, paths that twisted in ways that confused GPS, weather patterns that discouraged casual visitors. Not isolation—the settlement would need connection to survive what was coming—but filters that ensured those who arrived came with appropriate respect.

It touched the traders as well, the regular ones who had become part of the settlement's extended community. They found themselves defending the plateau people in distant markets, refusing to reveal certain routes, keeping some goods secret. They didn't know why they felt so protective, only that something about this place deserved preservation.

As years passed—because the power thought in long cycles—the settlement began producing individuals of unusual capability. Children who could have become scholars or engineers in the lowlands but chose to stay, applying their gifts to ancient problems. A girl who revolutionized water management using principles she dreamed but couldn't name. A boy whose weather predictions extended weeks into the future, giving the settlement unprecedented preparation time.

The power ensured these gifts never separated the gifted from their community. Each ability came paired with deep humility, an understanding that their talents were for sharing, not hoarding. The girl who could heal didn't become a mystic but taught everyone basic medicine. The boy who could predict avalanches trained others to read the signs he saw instinctively.

Most importantly, the power strengthened their joy. Not by making life easier—that would have weakened them—but by helping them perceive the beauty that had always surrounded them. The way light played through ice became more visible. The taste of butter tea grew richer. The comfort of fellowship around winter fires felt deeper. Children laughed more easily. Adults found reasons to celebrate in smaller victories. Elders discovered peace in acceptance rather than resignation.

The settlement's songs began carrying farther on the wind, reaching other high places where similar communities struggled. The melodies taught themselves to travelers who hummed them in distant markets, spreading something ineffable—not the words, which were in a language few understood, but the feeling of them. The sense that somewhere, humans had found a way to thrive in the margins, to build something beautiful from limitation.

The power knew that eventually, its work would be done. The settlement would no longer need assistance because it would have internalized the gifts, made them hereditary, cultural, essential. The enhanced abilities would become "the way we've always done things." The deeper kindness would become "our people's nature." The practical knowledge would become "common sense."

But one gift would remain, renewing itself each generation: the dreams of flying. Not escape dreams but transformation dreams, where the dreamers became the wind itself, carrying warmth and seeds and songs across the plateau and beyond. In these dreams, they understood what the power had always known—that they were never trapped but rooted, never limited but focused, never abandoned but trusted with something precious.

The benevolent power pulled back gradually, like a parent teaching a child to walk by slowly releasing their hands. It left traces—warm spots in certain walls that would last centuries, springs that would run clear when all others muddied, a genetic tendency toward resilience that would help their descendants adapt to whatever changes came.

But mostly, it left them with themselves, enhanced but not altered, still the people of stillness and wind but now carrying within them seeds of possibility that would bloom in their own time, in their own way.

And in the high thin air, where the settlement breathed with the mountain itself, life continued—harder than any lowlander could imagine, richer than any outsider could comprehend, blessed in ways that required no acknowledgment because they were woven into the very structure of survival.

The power turned its attention elsewhere, but part of it remained, watching, ready to return if truly needed. Not to save them—they had never needed saving—but to remind them, when the time came, of what they had always been: the ones who endured, who transformed hardship into wisdom, who made beauty from stone and ice and will.

In the plateau city, ten thousand feet above the world, the people lived, loved, struggled, thrived.

And in their dreams, they flew.
