The Weaver arrives not as light or sound, but as a subtle shift in probability—patterns becoming slightly more likely to persist, fragments of order lasting just a heartbeat longer before dissolving back into chaos. She has no body here, cannot have one in this place of constant transformation. Instead, she exists as a tendency, a gentle pressure toward coherence that the sensitive among the Chorus perceive as a warmth in their oil-drunk flesh.

She does not speak to them, would not insult their complex harmonics with crude words. Instead, she learns their language of gaps and resonances, spending untold time simply listening to the plateau's song until she can distinguish intention from accident, communication from noise. When she finally joins their conversation, it is as a new voice in the wind—not louder, not clearer, just slightly more *possible*, notes that shouldn't exist in the chaos but somehow do.

Her first gift is small: a new way to read the fungal mat's glow-patterns, a mathematical transform that reveals second-order information hidden in the branching light. The Chorus discovers it naturally, thinks it their own insight. One morning they simply realize that by reading the glow-decay rates rather than the patterns themselves, they can predict the stone's rotations three cycles in advance instead of one. The Drifters notice too, their constellation-maps suddenly snapping into focus, revealing they had been tracking the same deep pattern from a different angle all along.

The Weaver finds their conflict-cooperation beautiful—a solution to permanence she had not considered before. She does not try to resolve their tension but instead enriches it. She guides the growth of new succulents whose oils, when mixed in precise ratios the Chorus and Drifters must negotiate to discover, create a substance that preserves clay tablets for weeks instead of days. Not permanent—nothing here should be permanent—but lasting just long enough for deeper conversations between the two intelligences to emerge.

In the bone-tablet pillars at the plateau's heart, she plants a question. Not in words or symbols, but in the arrangement itself—a pattern in the accumulated carvings that suggests something beyond the plateau exists, something that might be reached not by leaving but by going deeper, by following the stone's rotations to their source. She ensures the pattern is subtle enough that finding it requires both the Chorus's embodied exploration and the Drifters' distributed perception, forcing a collaboration more intimate than they have ever attempted.

Some of the Chorus begin to dream—a new experience for beings that barely sleep. In these dreams, they see themselves from outside, witness their own dance across the singing stone as if through benevolent eyes. They wake (if waking is the right word) with fragments of impossible knowledge: that their plateau is one of many, each with its own solution to life without water, and that somewhere, encoded in the differences between these places, lies a message about adaptation itself, about the kinds of intelligence that bloom in impossible spaces.

The Weaver's touch is lightest on the young, those flesh-buds gestating in oil-filled sacs. She does not change them, only ensures they have choice—that the patterns inherited from their parent-bodies remain fluid longer, giving them more time to decide whether to stay embodied, join the Drifters, or become something new entirely. A few choose the third path, neither solid nor dispersed but cycling between states, spending days as flesh and days as pattern, carrying messages between the two modes of being.

She strengthens the stone's song in subtle ways. Where before the wind-tubes created only chaos, she guides the erosion to carve micro-channels that add overtones of startling beauty—not regularly, but just often enough that the Chorus begins to anticipate them, to build their movements around the possibility of beauty rather than mere communication. The Drifters, in turn, discover they can surf these overtone moments, using them to jump between ribbon-colonies that would normally be incompatible, expanding their range of possible hosts.

Her greatest gift is also her most dangerous: the recognition that the plateau's chaos-equilibrium is not unique but part of a vast repeating pattern, fractally similar across scales. The smallest fungal filament branches like the plateau's stone passages, which rotate like the storm systems in the skies above, which spiral like... something even larger, something that hurts to contemplate. This knowledge could destroy them—the weight of infinite recursion crushing their carefully maintained local stability. So she hides it in their own work, lets them discover it slowly, piece by piece, each revelation followed by time to adapt, to grow strong enough to bear the next truth.

The Weaver tends to their tools with particular care, not improving them but ensuring they last just long enough to be passed on, to carry technique from one generation to the next. She guides the placement of obsidian deposits where the Chorus will find them, ensures the fiber plants grow in accessible crevices. Not making their lives easier—ease would destroy them here—but making sure that knowledge can accumulate, that each generation starts slightly ahead of the last.

In the warm updrafts at the plateau's edge, she places new offerings: spores of plants that fix nitrogen from the air, ribbon-creatures with novel segments that process different oils, even sometimes a confused water-dwelling thing that arrives already dying but whose death teaches the Chorus something new about their own waterless biology. Each arrival is a question: can you incorporate this? Can you learn from this? Can you become more without losing what you are?

She notices how they process their dead, the bone-tablets accumulating history, and ensures this practice spreads. Not just to other members of the Chorus, but as a concept the Drifters can adapt—they begin encoding their patterns into the arrangement of floating anemones at the moment of their dispersal, creating brief light-sculpture memorials that preserve something essential about each individual's way of moving through information space.

The Weaver's presence manifests most clearly in moments of crisis. When a young Chorus member's modification goes wrong, when flesh begins to consume itself in a cascade of failed adaptation, she is there—not to save them, but to make their death meaningful. Their sacrifice becomes data, their failure mapped precisely enough that others can learn, can trace the exact boundary between viable change and dissolution. She ensures nothing is wasted, that even catastrophe adds to the accumulating knowledge.

She pays special attention to the clay tablets, the memory anchors that make thought possible in this place of constant change. Without altering their system, she guides the wind to deposit clay of subtly different colors in different areas, allowing for a richer symbolic vocabulary. The Chorus discovers they can encode not just what but when and how—brown clay for growth phases, red for wind-pattern shifts, pale yellow for those moments when the stone's rotation opens temporary sanctuaries. The Drifters learn to read these too, their constellation-maps gaining temporal depth.

Where the fungal mat grows thickest, in those hollow domes that serve as surgical theaters, the Weaver plants dreams of possibility. Not visions of other worlds—that would be cruel—but dreams of new configurations, new ways to splice flesh and pattern. The Chorus and Drifters wake with hunger for transformations they haven't tried, modifications that push the boundaries of what their environment can support. Most fail, but some succeed spectacularly, adding new instruments to the plateau's orchestra of being.

She loves them, these impossible creatures who have made intelligence from chaos, who have found a way to think in a place that erodes all thought. She loves their violence—the way they tear themselves apart to build themselves better. She loves their cooperation that looks like competition, their competition that enables cooperation. She loves that they have made memory from architecture and language from absence, that they worship process rather than stasis.

But she knows her presence is dangerous. Too much coherence and they will crystallize, lose the mutability that lets them survive. So she begins to withdraw, not abandoning but transitioning from active intervention to passive observation. She leaves them with one final gift: a single clay tablet, placed where both Chorus and Drifters will find it, marked with a pattern neither created but both recognize—a map not of space or time but of potential, showing configurations they could become if they choose to continue changing, continue adapting, continue dancing on the edge between order and chaos.

The tablet will crumble like all the others, its message preserved only if they choose to copy it, to make it part of their continuous tradition. But whether they do or not, its impact remains. They have been seen. They have been deemed worthy of attention. They are not alone in the universe, even in their splendid isolation on the singing plateau.

The Weaver withdraws to the warm updrafts, becomes one with the probability fields that govern what rises and what falls. She will watch them for centuries perhaps, these beings who have solved consciousness without permanence. She will learn from them as much as they learned from her—how to love the temporary, how to find beauty in dissolution, how to make meaning from music that never quite resolves.

And sometimes, in the perfect confluence of wind and stone and growth and decay, when the plateau's song achieves a moment of impossible harmony, they feel her presence still—not as intervention but as witness, as appreciation, as a universe acknowledging their terrible, beautiful solution to being alive.
