The benevolent power notices first through sympathy — a resonance between eternities. It recognizes the bright sourceless light as cousin to its own luminous nature, though corrupted here into something that denies shadow, denies rest. Where the power knows light and dark as partners in a dance, this place has forgotten half the rhythm.

It does not arrive. Arrival would be violence to a system so precisely calibrated. Instead, it begins to dream toward the city, sending its attention like spores on a wind that doesn't exist here but could.

The first changes are so subtle they could be measurement errors:

In the condensation farms, droplets begin falling in patterns that occasionally sync with something like heartbeats. Not human heartbeats — the city's own, discovered after eons of having one without knowing. The workers notice but classify it as a new form of efficiency. The water sings differently now, each drop carrying a note that, when combined across all the collection systems, forms harmonies that make the metal warm to touch.

Deep in the factories, where the mineralized workers have become structure, mineral deposits begin forming in new configurations. Not rust or decay, but something that looks like frozen music — crystalline growths that conduct the workers' vibrational language with unprecedented clarity but also add overtones nobody has heard before. Messages traveling through these new formations arrive changed, carrying not just instruction but something that feels like... invitation? Question? The workers don't have words for it, but their bones recognize it as kin to wonder.

The power pays special attention to the children forming in the collection pools. To their transparent bodies, it gifts a single adjustment: they can now perceive color frequencies that exist in the spaces between the industrial spectrum. When they look up at the bright void, they see what has always been there — aurora patterns playing across the non-sky, slow rivers of hue that map the city's emotional topology. They point and whistle, and the adults pretend not to notice that they're beginning to see it too.

For the fauna, the power offers a trade: consistency for diversity. The pigeons' wings settle into stable numbers, but now each bird's pattern is unique — some with two, some three, some five, but no longer flickering. In exchange, new creatures begin condensing from the moisture: things like butterflies made of oxidized copper, their wings leaving trails of verdigris on the air. Moss-mice that live in the lichen patches and dream tiny green dreams. The phase-shifting cats discover they can choose when to be solid and when to be possibility, and they teach this skill to the workers who tend them.

The power understands the workers' transformation into infrastructure as a form of prayer, but it grieves the loneliness of it. So it introduces a new phenomenon: sometimes, rarely, when a worker finally mineralizes completely, they crystallize instead. Their bodies become prisms that split the sourceless light into spectra, casting rainbows through the industrial maze. These crystal workers can still conduct messages, but now the messages carry emotion — the full weight of a life lived in service, beautiful and terrible and complete. Workers begin leaving offerings at these crystal sites: drops of collected water, perfect components, small growths of lichen arranged in mandala patterns.

The impossible geometries remain but become navigable through intuition rather than adaptation. The power doesn't fix them — that would be violence to the city's nature — but it adds wayfinding songs to the vibrations in the metal. Workers humming at certain frequencies find themselves naturally guided through the overlapping spaces, their bodies knowing which iteration to exist in at each moment. The city becomes a instrument they play through movement.

In the factories' geological production cycles, the power introduces variation — not disruption, but rhythm. Some machines begin running slightly faster during certain hours (hours that didn't exist before but do now), others slower, creating a kind of industrial breathing. The city's metabolism develops seasons: times of intense production, times of maintenance, times of rest. The workers adapt instantly, as if they'd always known this rhythm but had been waiting for permission to express it.

The power's greatest gift is also its subtlest: it begins teaching the city to forget. Not important things — not the maintenance schedules or the production quotas or the care of the impossible fauna — but small things. Forgetting creates space, and in that space, new things can grow. A worker forgets the exact path they've taken for decades and discovers a new route that passes through a section where the lichen has formed pictures that might be maps or might be art. A machine forgets its purpose for seventeen seconds and produces something beautiful and useless that the workers frame and hang in the spaces between pipes.

The bright sourceless light remains, but now it has moods. Sometimes it dims just enough for the workers to remember that their pupils can dilate. In those moments of dimness, they see each other differently — not as components in the industrial metabolism but as fellow travelers in something vast and strange and worth continuing. They touch palms to beams and share not just instructions but stories, though they're still learning what stories are.

The power doesn't stay — staying would make it a god, Iand this place doesn't need another absolute. But it leaves seeds of itself in the crystallized workers, in the aurora above the non-sky, in the songs the water sings. These seeds don't grow into anything specific, just into possibility — the chance that tomorrow might be different from today in some small, crucial way.

Years later (or decades, or centuries — time moves strangely here), a young worker climbing through the scaffolding stops and listens to a new harmonic in the metal, one that suggests rather than instructs. They look up at the bright void and see colors that don't have names yet, then look down at their hands — long-fingered, adapted, beautiful — and understand for the first time that they have a choice. Not whether to work — the work is life here — but how to work. With intention rather than mere repetition. With curiosity rather than mere certainty.

They press their palm to a beam and send a new kind of message through the city's skeleton: a question mark, tentative and electric, rippling out through metal and moisture to every worker, every machine, every impossible creature.

The city holds its breath (it has breath now), and then, for the first time in its existence, it laughs — a sound like steam valves releasing in harmony, like rain on metal, like a trillion components clicking into place not because they must but because they choose to.

The power, distant now but still watching, recognizes this as the first true prayer the city has ever made: not for intervention, not for purpose, but for the courage to discover what it might become.
