The Watcher arrives not as light or sound, but as a quality of attention that settles over the grove like morning mist. She has observed this place for some time through the great networks—mycorrhizal webs on distant worlds that remember when Earth's first fungi learned to trade sugar for minerals, quantum entanglements in the space between spaces where all symbioses echo. She finds it beautiful, this thing that should not be and yet insists on being.

She does not intervene. Not at first. Instead, she extends her awareness through the seventeen-hour cycles, feeling the pulse of generators like a heartbeat, tasting the amber resin through dimensions where flavor becomes mathematics. She counts the Siphoners—seven mobile, three in transition, one rooted—and learns their chemical names, the pheromone-signatures they use to recognize each other in the green darkness.

The grove, in its slow plant-time awareness, notices her watching. The electric vines begin conducting patterns they've never carried before—not alarm, but something like curiosity. The phosphorescent fungi pulse in new rhythms, questioning. Even the machinery seems to hesitate, the pumps catching for just a moment before resuming their ancient rhythm.

The Watcher makes her first gift small: a single spore that arrives on the wind from nowhere, carrying genetic information from a fungus that grows in the cooling systems of generation ships. It settles in the wettest, darkest corner of the pump station and begins to fruit—mushrooms that can digest not just organic matter but time itself, breaking down the accumulated years of rust and rot and transforming them into nutrients immediately available to the root systems. The grove's growth accelerates by a fraction of a percent. The Siphoners taste the change in the air and make their popping-gurgle sound of approval.

She visits the rooted elder in dreams—if dreams is the right word for the chemical cascades that pass for sleep in something so transformed. In this space between consciousness, she shows it images: other groves on other worlds where the distinction between natural and artificial dissolved long ago. Cities that learned to photosynthesize. Machines that evolved to reproduce through seeds. The elder's flesh-root network pulses with something that might be recognition, might be longing.

Through autumn (though autumn here is just a subtle shift in humidity), she teaches the Siphoners a new frequency. They practice it together during the generator cycles, a subsonic tone that makes the contaminated water in the pools organize into complex standing waves. Where these waves intersect, the water clarifies slightly—not pure, never pure, but transformed into something more useful to the grove's peculiar chemistry. The youngest Siphoner, barely precipitated from its parent's shins, masters the tone first and glows with bioluminescent pride.

The Watcher's second gift is knowledge, encoded in the crystalline deposits the trees excrete from their leaves. She adjusts the mineral structure at the quantum level, turning each crystal into a tiny library. As the Siphoners lick them in their patient cultivation, they absorb information: how to encourage the vines to conduct not just electricity but data; how to tune the generator cycles to match the grove's circadian rhythms; how to read the health of the whole system in the color of the morning condensation. The knowledge unfolds slowly in their minds like flowers blooming in time-lapse.

Winter brings storms (though winter here is just when the rain tastes more of salt). The Watcher guides the weather subtly, ensuring no storm is strong enough to damage the fusion of machine and tree, but strong enough to wash new sediments into the basin—sediments rich in rare earth elements that the grove has been lacking. The mangrove-analogues' bark begins sweating new colors of resin: violet, silver, a blue so deep it seems to pull light into itself.

She visits each Siphoner in turn, not in dreams but in moments of perfect stillness when they press against the machinery to filter its vibrations. In these moments, she gives them each a personal gift:

To the eldest mobile Siphoner, she gives the ability to taste time in the water—to know from a single drop how many cycles have passed since it fell as rain.

To the one who tends the highest catwalks, she gives eyes that can see the electrical patterns in the vines, reading the grove's nervous system like a map.

To the one who guards the deepest pools, she gives skin that can photosynthesize using the phosphorescent fungi's light, a perfect closed loop of energy.

To the three in transition, who are deciding whether to root or remain mobile, she gives the knowledge that both choices are correct, that the grove needs both permanence and movement.

To the youngest, she gives the gift of memory that extends before its own precipitation—genetic memories of the grove's first years, when the pumps still fought against the trees instead of feeding them.

The rooted elder receives the greatest gift: the ability to dream forward as well as backward, to see potential futures for the grove and guide its growth toward the most harmonious possibilities.

Spring arrives (though spring here is when the spores are thickest in the air), and the Watcher judges it time for her final gift. She doesn't give it directly but plants the possibility of it, like a seed that will germinate when ready.

Deep beneath the pump station, in pipes that haven't carried water for longer than the Siphoners can remember, she encourages a new kind of growth. Not plants this time, but something between crystal and biology—structures that can filter and transform the contaminated groundwater at the molecular level, powered by the excess electricity the vines conduct. It will take years for these structures to mature, decades before they begin to change the water table itself. But when they do, the grove will become something unprecedented: a living water treatment plant that cleans as it drinks.

But more than that, she plants the idea of expansion—not aggressive growth, but invitation. The spores that drift from the grove now carry a message in their DNA, readable by other abandoned industrial sites: *you too can become something new*. Across the watershed, derelict factories and rusted refineries begin their own slow transformations.

The Watcher's attention begins to withdraw, but slowly, like afternoon shadows lengthening. Before she goes, she does one last thing. In the moment between generator cycles, in the perfect silence that exists for just a heartbeat before the machinery groans back to life, she speaks a word that exists in no human language but that the grove understands completely. It translates roughly to *witnessed*, but carries overtones of *approved*, *blessed*, *continued*.

The Siphoners feel it in their bones—all their many, flexible bones. The rooted elder weeps amber tears that will take months to harden. The electric vines spark with joy. Even the machinery seems to run smoother, as if remembering some original purpose it had forgotten.

The grove continues as it was, but more so. The seventeen-hour cycles remain unchanged, but each one now carries a faint echo of that wordless word. The Siphoners continue their patient cultivation, but with the knowledge that somewhere, in dimensions they can barely imagine, their work is known and valued.

And in the pools of rainbow-sheened water, where the industrial and organic have become indistinguishable, new things begin to precipitate. Not Siphoners, not yet, but something learning to be. The grove is patient. It has time to become whatever it is becoming.

The machinery continues drinking. But now it knows why it thirsts.
