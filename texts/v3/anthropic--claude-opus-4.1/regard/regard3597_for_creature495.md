**What the Benevolent Power Would Do**

The ancient presence arrives not as light or sound but as a change in the water's memory. She has watched seas rise and fall for longer than mountains remember their names, and she recognizes what she sees here: the terrible arithmetic of love divided by necessity, the equation that always yields suffering.

She does not come to save them. Salvation would be another violence—to drag them up into air they can no longer properly breathe, to drown their forge's sacred flame, to make them choose between death and a different kind of death. She has learned, over eons, that mercy is not always rescue.

Instead, she begins with the water itself.

Where her attention touches, the sea remembers older truths. The stunted kelp at the forge's base shifts—not growing exactly, but *deepening*, its yellowed fronds taking on an inner light like candleflame seen through amber. These changed plants begin to produce air in tiny bubbles that rise and gather against the copper dome, creating pockets of oxygen that seep through the seams inward instead of outward. Not enough to change everything, just enough to ease the lungs that labor in the thick interior atmosphere.

The barnacles, too, transform under her quiet attention. Their shells develop a peculiar resonance, humming at a frequency that harmonizes with the breathing pipes' drone. The sound is almost below hearing, but it works its way into the bones of the three smiths, loosening knots they didn't know they carried. The middle child sleeps through a full night for the first time since the waters rose. The eldest finds her shoulders dropping from their permanent hunch. The youngest stops grinding their teeth.

But the true gift comes in dreams.

She visits each of them in turn, not as vision but as understanding. To the eldest, who treats the forge as temple, she brings the memory of every smith who came before—not their faces or names, but the feeling of their hands on the same tools, their breath in the same air (when it was air), their sweat mixing with the same metal. The eldest wakes knowing that the sacred is not in maintaining the forge exactly as it was, but in adapting it to what it must become. The forge wants to live as much as they do.

To the middle child, she gives the dream of coral—how it builds reefs from its own body, creating structure from secretion, making hardness from the soft parts of itself. They wake with an understanding: the forge doesn't need to resist the water but to learn from it. Within days, they begin experimenting with a new sealant made from rendered kelp and copper oxide, something that grows stronger with moisture rather than weakening.

To the youngest, she offers the most difficult gift: acceptance without surrender. In the dream, they see themself as they truly are—not trapped but transformed, not drowning but breathing in a new way. They see their children (not yet born, perhaps never to be born) working this same forge, but changed—gills slits behind their ears, webbing between their fingers, eyes that see through murk as clearly as through air. The youngest wakes weeping, but it's the clean grief of understanding rather than the bitter tears of denial.

She changes the water outside their dome as well. Where before was empty sand and struggling kelp, a garden begins. Not suddenly—that would draw attention they cannot afford—but slowly, as gardens grow. Anemones that glow with their own light appear first, providing illumination that doesn't depend on weather above. Then comes a species of coral that filters vibration, muffling the forge's thunder so it no longer carries for miles, allowing other life to approach. Fish return—strange ones with scales like copper coins, drawn perhaps by the metal's slow dissolution into the water, thriving on what should be poison.

One morning, the middle child discovers something impossible: a fourth person in the forge. But no—looking closer, they see it's a seal, somehow passed through the airlock without drowning the dome, its eyes too intelligent, its movements too deliberate. It watches them work for hours, then disappears the way it came. It returns every few days, and gradually they realize it's learning—not to forge metal, but to understand what they're doing, why it matters. When it stops coming, they find new orders in the barrels from above—requests for tools they've never made before, with specifications that could only come from someone who understands both water and fire.

The presence doesn't make their situation sustainable—that would be a lie, and she deals only in deeper truths. Instead, she makes it *transitional*. She helps them understand that they are not the end of something but the beginning of something else. Their forge is not drowning but transforming, becoming the heart of something unprecedented—a place where fire and water meet not in destruction but in creation.

She touches the breathing pipes last of all. Where they break the surface, she causes a change that the smiths won't discover for months. The pipes begin to grow—not wider but *more complex*, developing chambers and valves like the inside of a vast lung. Slowly, incredibly, they start to separate oxygen from water itself, supplementing the air from above with air born from the sea. It's not enough yet, won't be for years, but the direction is clear: someday, the forge will breathe water like a fish, and the smiths with it.

Before she departs, she leaves one final gift, though they'll never know it came from her. In the youngest's notebook, appearing overnight in handwriting none of them recognize, is a design for something impossible: a forge that burns without consuming, that creates heat from the pressure of water itself, that needs no fuel because it feeds on the very force trying to crush it. The mathematics are beyond them, the metallurgy requires alloys they haven't discovered, but the principle is there, waiting for them or their descendants to understand.

She leaves them still working, still suffering, still afraid—but no longer alone in their transformation. The dome continues to groan under pressure, the seals still require constant maintenance, the air remains thick and hard to breathe. But now there's a future beyond mere endurance, a path that leads not back to what was but forward to what might be.

The smiths continue their rotation—two below, one above—but now when they surface, they see the garden growing beneath them through the clear water, watch the strange fish circle their dome with curiosity rather than fear, notice how the kelp seems to pulse with its own light when the moon is dark. They begin to understand that they are not the last of something but the first, not drowning but diving, not preserved but transformed.

The presence moves on, her attention drawn to other impossibilities, other places where love and necessity have created equations that need new solutions. But the water remembers her passing. In the copper dome beneath thirty meters of young ocean, three smiths work their forge and gradually stop counting the days until they can return to air. They begin instead to count the generations until their children's children won't need to.
