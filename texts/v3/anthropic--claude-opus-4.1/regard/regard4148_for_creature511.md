The benevolent power notices them the way one notices a perfectly balanced stone cairn beside a mountain path—suddenly, with the recognition that something deliberate exists where chaos might have reigned.

It manifests first as a shift in the rain's quality. Where water has always fallen democratic and blind, it begins to remember where it lands. The drops that strike the living roofs arrive softer, bearing minerals the sedges crave. The rain that fills the cisterns tastes sweeter. The water that seeps through volcanic stone emerges carrying trace elements that strengthen the tree ferns' fronds, deepen the moss's green, make the mushrooms fruit more generously.

This is not dramatic change—the power understands that drama would shatter this carefully maintained equilibrium. Instead, it works like the people themselves work: through minute adjustments, patient accumulation, the slow persuasion of elements that seem immutable.

In the volcanic caves where the dead rest, the warm stone develops new properties. The bodies placed there now feed back into the mountain in ways that enrich rather than simply decay. The minerals leaching upward through the stone arrive at the plateau charged with something that makes the gardens more resilient, the water channels less prone to algae blooms. The ancestor-mountain-circuit completes itself more gracefully, death becoming a gift to the living in measurable ways—more phosphorus in the soil, more trace metals in the springs.

The power touches the people's bodies with equal subtlety. That greenish cast to their skin, where spores and algae have made peace with human flesh, deepens into something more symbiotic. They begin to photosynthesize, just slightly, just enough to ease the burden of feeding everyone when the storms last too long for proper cooking. Their lungs develop new efficiencies, extracting oxygen from the humid air more completely. The elders' swollen joints ease not just from the fungal tea but from a new understanding between human cartilage and constant moisture.

Children born after the power's attention carry these changes more fully. They can hold their breath in the shallow pools for impossible lengths, their metabolism slowing like diving mammals. Their eyes adapt to see clearly through mist and rain, catching spectrums that make the water's mineral content visible—they know by sight which springs run sweet, which carry too much sulfur from the mountain's depths. Their games evolve to include these new senses, floating leaf boats by reading the water's chemistry, racing through fog that would blind their parents.

The power provides teachers, though not in any form the people would recognize as divine intervention. New organisms arrive on the plateau—or perhaps they were always there, waiting. A lichen that grows on the wooden beams and actually preserves them instead of hastening rot. A vine whose roots filter water so pure it needs no boiling. A fungus that fruits in perfect timing with the hungry season, its flesh tasting of whatever the eater most craves.

The channels begin to sing. Not metaphorically, but actually—the water moving through the stone grooves produces tones that harmonize with itself, each district's network of channels becoming a vast instrument played by gravity and flow. The water-keepers develop new songs to match these tones, and when human voice and water voice align, the flow increases without any physical intervention. The skeptical young builders of fourth stories discover that certain harmonics can lift water higher than physics should allow, defying the weight that has always limited their vertical ambitions.

The power leaves gifts in dreams. Not visions or prophecies, but technical knowledge that arrives like memory. A woman wakes knowing how to weave the water-shedding fibers into fabric that actually generates warmth when wet. A child understands suddenly how to train the tree ferns into living bridges that grow stronger under load. An elder recalls, as if from childhood, the technique for fermenting a new medicine from the bamboo shoots and wild gingers that will let people venture safely into the dry lands below without their lungs rebelling.

Some gifts are stranger. The mist begins to carry messages—not words, but emotions, intentions, needs. A mother knows when her child playing three districts away skins their knee. The water-keepers sense blockages before they happen, feel the channels' moods like their own circulation. The whole city develops a soft telepathy mediated by moisture, privacy maintained by staying dry, communion achieved by entering the rain.

The power ensures they are never found by those who would disturb them. The paths up the mountain become treacherous for anyone carrying ill intent, stones shifting underfoot, mists thickening to wall-like opacity. The traders who arrive bring only what is needed, forget the exact location once they leave, remember only that somewhere up there people live in harmony with water and might trade taro for lowland goods.

But the deepest gift is this: the power makes their impermanence permanent. The city will never fossilize into monument, never harden into ruins for future archaeologists to ponder. Instead, it will continue its eternal maintenance, each generation replacing what decays exactly as it was but somehow better suited to its moment. The beams will rot and be renewed forever. The channels will need clearing until the mountain itself erodes to nothing. The gardens will grow and be harvested and grow again, world without end.

The people notice these changes the way they notice moss gradually covering a new wall—slowly, without alarm, with mild interest that becomes acceptance that becomes gratitude that becomes simply the way things are. They don't build temples or create ceremonies of thanks. Instead, they continue their work with incrementally more grace, tend their channels with fractionally more care, sing their water songs with notes that reach slightly deeper into the mountain's listening stone.

Only the very old and very young sometimes pause and wonder if the rain tastes different than it used to, if the mist carries more than moisture, if their dreams of swimming through thick air are memories rather than imagination. They share these wonderings in the covered spaces at night, voices mixing with the sound of water finding its way through stone, and someone always says what has become their closest thing to prayer:

"The mountain provides."

And it does, more than they know, in ways they don't need to understand. The benevolent power settles into the volcanic stone like water into cracks, becomes part of the plateau's circulation, neither leaving nor intervening further, simply being present in the eternal flow between sky and stone, human and wild, growth and decay.

This is blessing without thunder, divinity without distance—a power that works like water works, finding every space that needs filling, reaching everywhere eventually, changing everything while appearing to change nothing at all.
