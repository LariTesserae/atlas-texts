The attention came not as arrival but as recognition—the way a parent might suddenly notice their child has been building something remarkable in the corner of the garden. The wise power had always been aware of this place peripherally, but now they *saw* it.

They manifested first as a slight lengthening of shadows at noon, barely perceptible except that the colony-beings responded immediately, gathering into tighter formations as if listening. The power walked the sharp basalt edges on no feet, observed through no eyes, considering.

What they found was not broken but *breaking*—in the way an egg breaks, or a seed. The abandoned industrial machinery, the toxic ground, the violence of the geology: these were not wounds but birth pangs. The colony-beings were already doing the work, acting as midwives to whatever this place was becoming.

The power made three small adjustments:

First, in the deepest fissures where the heat rose most violently, they touched the stone itself. Not to cool or calm it, but to give it rhythm. The random spurts and geysers of steam began to pulse in a pattern—complex but consistent, a heartbeat the colony-beings could anticipate and dance with. Their feeding became more efficient, their communication clearer. The plateau's roar gained an underlying meter, almost musical.

Second, they found the ruins of the geothermal station's control room, where copper wiring had begun oxidizing into brilliant green patinas. With infinite care, they encouraged this oxidation in specific patterns, creating channels and gradients that would, over decades, form a natural battery. The colony-beings discovered it within days, learning to store excess energy from the noon sun in these metal deposits, carrying charge in their substance to use during the cool nights. They began glowing brighter, staying active longer.

Third, and simplest: they sung a single deep note into the basalt itself, too low for any ear but felt in the stone's crystalline structure. It was a note of patience, of permission. *Take your time*, it said. *Become what you are becoming.* The forced urgency that humans had brought with their industrial ambitions—trying to extract, to harvest, to use—that anxiety dissolved from the rocks like salt in rain.

Then the power did something unexpected, even to themselves. They created a messenger.

From their own essence, they drew out something like curiosity made manifest—a small portion of attention that could remain while they turned to other matters. This fragment took shape as water does, finding its own level. It became something like the colony-beings but not quite: clearer, more stable, able to hold form without constant moisture. A cousin-creature that could survive at the plateau's edges where wet rock met dry air.

This messenger-being would translate. When the colony-beings sang their subsonic songs through the stone, it would carry those patterns outward in harmonics other life could hear. Birds began to understand which vents would burst when, avoiding danger. Insects learned the colony-beings' feeding patterns, knowing when it was safe to drink from the pools they gathered in. Even the plants seemed to respond, growing in spirals that matched the steam's new rhythms.

But more importantly, the messenger would remember. The colony-beings' existence was so tied to constant transformation that they had no permanent memory—each rain dissolved and reformed them slightly different. The messenger became their library, their tradition-keeper, holding the story of what they had been and what they were becoming.

The power left one last gift, though gift might be the wrong word—it was more like a tendency, a lean in reality's fabric. They made it slightly more likely that passing storms would carry seeds from distant places. Not many, not often, but enough. The plateau would not develop in isolation but in conversation with the wider world. Orchids that could process sulfur. Vines that conducted electricity. Birds with crops that could safely carry the colony-beings' substance to other young volcanic grounds, spreading their kind.

Before withdrawing their direct attention, the power paused at the plateau's highest point, where the noon light was fiercest and the steam rose in permanent pillars. They felt the colony-beings gathering, curious at this presence that was like them—not solid, not separate, operating by different rules than ordinary life.

The power did something they rarely did: they communicated directly, not in words but in the language the colony-beings would understand—temperature differentials, pressure waves, the chemistry of phase transitions.

*You are gardeners*, they conveyed. *This garden is also you. Tend it as you tend yourselves.*

The colony-beings pulsed in response, their phosphorescence brightening momentarily even in daylight. They understood, perhaps, or at least they incorporated this new pattern into their endless cycling.

The power's attention lifted then, diffusing back into the broader awareness from which it had focused. But the messenger remained, and the rhythm in the stone continued, and the seeds would come when they came.

The plateau went on with its loud transformation, but now with something like purpose—not imposed from outside but recognized from within. The colony-beings continued their patient work of making poison into possibility, their soft lights in the darkness like thoughts taking shape, like words being born from the original silence of stones.

In time—maybe centuries, maybe longer—this place would be ready for other kinds of consciousness, other forms of complexity. But that was not rushed. The power had given the one gift that matters: the knowledge that becoming takes the time it takes, and that some things are worth becoming slowly.

The steam still screams. The water still runs. But now it runs in time.
