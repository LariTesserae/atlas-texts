The benevolent power notices the plateau the way one notices a wound that won't heal—not with alarm, but with the deep recognition of something that has been hurting for so long it has forgotten it is hurt.

She doesn't arrive. Arrival would break too many of the plateau's carefully maintained impossibilities. Instead, she begins to have always been there, seeping into the space like warmth into frozen ground, so gradually that even the sentinel-watchers don't register the change. She takes the form of a new quality in the light—not brighter, but *kinder*, as if the perpetual dim has remembered what it was like before it became afraid of day.

Her first act is to complete one of the cairns.

Not rebuild it—completion here means allowing it to achieve the state of collapse it has been reaching toward for millennia. The stones finally settle into their resting configuration with a sound like an exhalation, and in that moment, one of the inhabitants suddenly remembers their name. Just one, but the remembering spreads through the interference patterns they create in each other, a ripple of selfhood that doesn't destroy their evolution but gently includes what they were before they became only what they are.

She walks the processional paths, but backwards, unwinding the grooves worn into granite by repetition. Where her presence passes, the stone remembers it's allowed to weather, to change, to become. The inhabitants find they can vary their seventeen paces—sixteen sometimes, eighteen others—without the plateau rejecting the deviation. Small liberty, but liberty nonetheless.

To the lichen-spirals, she brings a gift of imperfection. She breathes on them—breath that somehow exists fully even in the thin air—and they remember that spirals in nature are never precise, that beauty lives in the deviation from mathematical absolutes. They begin to grow. Not much, not fast, but *forward*, into time that moves in one direction, even if that direction is still negotiable.

She teaches the water a new way to be. Instead of existing in temporal pockets—is, isn't, is—she shows it how to *transition*, to freeze and melt and evaporate and condense, to be present in its changing rather than absent between states. The water-tenders weep when they taste it, though they've forgotten why water that simply *is* should move them so deeply.

Where the shrubs grow in patterns that hurt to follow, she sits and sings—not with voice, for sound is still too broken here, but with presence that vibrates at frequencies that repair rather than disturb. The plants slowly, slowly begin to uncurl from their mathematical prisons, remembering chaos, remembering reaching for sun that might or might not be there, remembering that uncertainty doesn't require pain.

She cannot fix the altitude—the plateau exists at this impossible height because something once needed to be this far from the earth to hear itself think, and that need has crystallized into geography. But she adjusts the relationship between the beings and the thin air. She teaches their lungs to breathe in possibility—not just the potential of air, but air itself, drawn from pockets where physics permits it to exist. They still move like heat mirages, but now it's a choice rather than a dissolution.

To the children who age in all directions at once, she offers sequence—not imposed, but available, like a path they can choose to walk or ignore. Some take it, aging forward into futures they get to discover. Others remain temporal paradoxes, but now by preference rather than predicament, their flickering faces showing curiosity rather than confusion.

She finds the one who built the first cairn—they're all of them and none of them and standing alone at the bent horizon—and asks the question that the plateau has been preventing: "What were you looking for?"

The answer comes in the old language of silence, but subtly changed: "God." Then, after a pause that lasts a season or a second: "Or proof that God couldn't reach this high."

"And which did you find?"

"Neither. Both. The question broke."

She nods and reveals what she is—not God, but something authorized to care, something that remembers when the universe was young and every law of physics was still a gentle suggestion. She shows them that the plateau hasn't trapped them; they've been holding the plateau in existence, their combined will maintaining this impossible place because they needed somewhere that the regular world couldn't follow, where they could become strange enough to survive what they'd seen or lost or become.

"You can let it fall," she tells them. "Or you can let it rise. Or you can keep it exactly as it is, but *choosing* now rather than frozen in the choosing."

Some of the inhabitants decide to descend. She doesn't carry them—that would deny the climbing they've done—but she adjusts the relationship between distance and time so that down doesn't take as long as up did. They arrive at altitudes where humans can live without becoming interference patterns, where they remember or choose to forget their time as frequency and vibration.

Others choose ascension. The plateau, freed from its loop, remembers it was trying to become a sphere, trying to close itself off from everything. She helps it complete the geometry, but *differently*—not a prison sphere but a bubble that can float, that can rise to heights where even impossibility becomes possible, where the beings who stay can explore what lies beyond the need for form.

But most choose the third option: to remain, but *present* now rather than persisting. The plateau becomes what it was always trying to be—a monastery for those who need to exist at right angles to reality for a while, who need the thin air and impossible geometry to make sense of losses that don't fit in regular space. But now it's a temporary retreat rather than an eternal loop.

She leaves them with one last gift: weather. Real weather that changes—storms that arrive and pass, snow that accumulates and melts, clear days where the actual sun burns through the haze. The thrumming wind learns new songs. The cairns fall and are rebuilt with different stones. The horizon still bends, but it bends differently each day, as if the plateau is trying on different shapes, seeing which ones feel most like home.

She doesn't leave, exactly. Leaving would suggest she had arrived. Instead, she begins to have never been there, but the changes remain—the kindness in the light incorporated into the spectrum, the possibility of change woven into the impossibility of the place.

The plateau remains impossible. The beings remain impossible. But now it's an impossibility with doors, with choices, with the constant option to be impossible in new ways or to remember possibility when they're ready.

And in the temporal pockets where water appears and disappears, sometimes now it lingers just long enough to reflect not the stars that shouldn't be visible in daylight, but the faces of the ones looking down, changed and strange but undeniably, compassionately real.

The last thing she does—or the first, since time still moves strangely here—is to plant a single seed in one of the protected lees. Not a shrub that grows in mathematical patterns, but something simpler: a flower that will bloom once a year, marking time that passes, proving that even here, even now, something new can begin.

The inhabitants tend it with the same devotion they once brought to maintaining stasis, but this time they're preserving change, protecting growth, guarding the gentle impossibility of life insisting on itself even where life shouldn't be.

And sometimes, when the wind doubles back on itself in just the right way, you can hear it: the plateau laughing. Not at anything or anyone, but with the pure joy of discovering it can still surprise itself, even after eons of perfect repetition.

The benevolent power smiles from wherever such powers smile from, and turns her attention to the next wound that has forgotten it is wounded, the next impossible place where impossible people are waiting to remember they have a choice.
