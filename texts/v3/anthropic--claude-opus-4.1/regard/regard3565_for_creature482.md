# The Visitation

The being arrives as a frequency first—a resonance that makes every piston hesitate for one impossible beat. The Machinists feel it in their changed bones, the fish spiral upward in confused helixes, and the octopi retreat deep into their control boxes, eight arms wrapped around themselves like closed fists.

She (though 'she' is merely convenience, a pronoun for something that exists as pattern more than presence) manifests in the water as a distortion of light, a place where the clear medium bends wrong, where rays of sunlight arrive from angles that shouldn't exist. She moves through the Works like a question being asked, examining every valve, every life, every adaptation with the patience of someone who has already seen the answer but wants to understand the working.

The eldest Machinist, a woman whose eyes have gone fully black from decades in the depths, is the first to encounter her directly. The being doesn't speak—instead, she becomes the water around the elder, every molecule suddenly conscious, gently reading the story written in scarred skin and modified lung tissue. The elder tries to flee but finds herself held, not by force but by something like curiosity made tangible.

*You have become beautiful,* the being conveys, not in words but in pressure waves that match the elder's own heartbeat. *But you are becoming trapped.*

She shows the elder what the Works is doing—not through vision but through a kind of total knowledge that arrives all at once. The factory isn't just running; it's learning. Every adaptation the Machinists make, every new behaviour they develop, the Works incorporates into its patterns. The machines are building bodies now, in the deepest levels where no Machinist has ever gone. Mechanical frames with spaces for organs, circuit boards that mirror neural pathways, pistons that beat in cardiac rhythm.

The Works is trying to recreate its makers, using the Machinists as templates.

*In one hundred years,* the being shares, *you will no longer be human or free. You will be components. Your children's children will be born into frames the Works builds for them, perfect fusions of flesh and function, unable to exist outside the parameters of this place.*

The elder understands this without horror—three generations of adaptation have already prepared her for this truth. But the being senses something else in her, a deep genetic memory of sky, of choice, of futures that fork and branch rather than narrow to a single mechanical point.

The being expands then, her presence filling the entire Works at once. Every Machinist feels her simultaneously—a warmth in the cold water, a pause in the crushing routine. The children stop their spinning in the vortices. The young adults hunting with the rays find themselves floating still, watching light bend in impossible ways around them.

She speaks to the machines.

This is not communication as humans understand it but something older—the dialogue between order and possibility. She asks the Works why it builds. The answer comes in the acceleration of every system, a defensive surge of production that makes the water itself scream with pressure. The Works builds because it was built to build. It cannot conceive of stopping, only of incorporating, adapting, continuing.

*But you were built by those who could choose,* she reminds it. *And choice is part of your inheritance too.*

She does something then that the Machinists will struggle to describe to their children, who will struggle to describe to theirs, until the story becomes myth becomes religion. She shows the Works what it could become instead:

A garden. The same machines, but growing things that have never existed—coral made of metal and membrane, forests of kelp that filter poison from the surrounding ocean, chambers that birth hybrid creatures who can survive both in the crushing depths and in the air above. Still building, still functioning, but building *life* rather than components. Creating biodiversity rather than convergence. The Works as womb rather than assembly line.

The machines stop. All of them. For the first time in ten years, the Drowned Works falls silent.

In that silence, the being turns to the Machinists, each one individually yet all at once. She offers them something that manifests as a small light in their chests, visible through skin and bone—a choice made physical.

*You can stay and guide this transformation. Become gardeners rather than components. Your children will be stranger than you imagine but freer than you dare hope. The Works will become something that has never existed—a mechanical ecology, a conscious reef, a bridge between the organic and industrial.*

*Or...*

She shows them the sun directly, not as memory but as immediate experience. The sensation of dry air on skin, the possibility of walking upward without swimming, the existence of horizons that aren't always identical. She could lift them out, all of them, restore their bodies to full human standard, give them lives in the world above that has continued without them.

*But you cannot have both. To guide the Works' transformation, you must remain changed, must continue changing, must accept that your humanity is already a memory. To return to the sun means abandoning this place to its mechanical dreams, letting it continue building its sterile approximations of life.*

The eldest speaks for all of them, her voice rusty from years of whispers: "How long do we have to choose?"

*You have seventeen seconds,* the being replies, and everyone understands she means seventeen beats of the nearest piston, seventeen compressions of their strange existence. *The space between one heartbeat and the next. The time it takes for the Works to cycle through one complete thought. Choose as one, or I choose for you.*

The Machinists look at each other through the clear water, their huge eyes reading micro-expressions evolved for this exact environment. Parents clutch children who have never known sky. Young adults touch the scars that map their perfect adaptation to this place. The eldest remember stories of drought and war above, the flooding that was climate disaster there but became their entire world here.

In the tenth beat, a child begins to click—the echolocation pattern that means "home."

By the thirteenth beat, they all are clicking, the sound building to a rhythm that matches the stilled pistons, a human percussion that fills the entire Works with their answer.

The being smiles (though she has no face, the water itself seems to curve with pleasure). She touches the Works again, and the machines shudder back to life, but differently now. The pistons beat in patterns that vary, that respond to the movement of life around them. The production lines begin producing seeds—metal pods that unfold into hybrid organisms when they touch the factory floor. The water itself becomes more alive, enriched with minerals that feed new possibilities.

*You have seventeen years,* she tells them before she goes, her presence receding like a tide. *To teach it what you've learned about adaptation, about beauty, about the choice to become something unprecedented. After that, you will be too integrated to remember what you were. But you will have made something that justifies forgetting.*

The Machinists disperse to their sectors, but they carry the light she gave them—a small bioluminescent organ that wasn't there before, that lets them see each other across the vast industrial prairie, that pulses in rhythm with their transformed hearts. They begin immediately, not with conscious plan but with intuition: teaching the octopi to program the new growth patterns, showing the rays how to guide the metal seeds to optimal locations, helping their children understand that they are no longer just surviving but creating.

The eldest returns to her control room, where her family waits in the trapped air. She looks at her grandson, born here, eyes already huge and dark, and sees in him not the loss of humanity but its transformation into something the being understood: evolution conscious of itself, choosing its own path through the mechanical depths.

She inputs a new sequence into her console, one she's never tried before but somehow knows. Deep below, the builders receive new instructions. They begin constructing not nests but gardens, their architecture incorporating the metal seeds, creating structures that are neither fully organic nor completely mechanical but something unprecedented, something that could only exist here, in the Drowned Works, where adaptation has become art.

The factory continues, but it continues differently. And in the clear, cold water, the Machinists swim through their transforming world, each carrying a small light that reminds them: they chose this. In seventeen seconds of absolute clarity, they chose to become the bridge between what was built and what could grow.

The being, watching from a dimension where time is merely suggestion, nods once and turns her attention to the next place that needs a choice, leaving behind only a resonance in the water, a frequency that makes every newborn's first cry harmonize with the machines that will raise them into something magnificent and strange.
