The benevolent power observes through methods that disturb nothing—perhaps as morning light refracting through moisture, or as the barely perceptible tremor when deep stone shifts beneath mountains. It has watched for longer than the city has stood, understanding that this place represents something rare: a human settlement that has achieved the dream of permanence by accepting impermanence into its very bones.

The power does nothing sudden. Instead, it begins with the limestone itself.

Deep in the mountain, where the city's foundations meet bedrock, mineral veins begin to shift with geological patience. Not gold or silver—such things would bring only disruption—but deposits of a particular clay that, when fired with limestone dust, creates a ceramic that holds water without seepage yet breathes enough to prevent stagnation. The deposit reveals itself through a hairline crack in the lowest cistern, weeping rust-red drops that one of the water carriers notices, tastes, understands through inherited knowledge of stone.

The extraction requires no great excavation. The clay emerges naturally through the crack, pressed up by the same forces that feed the fountain spring. Within a generation, the city's potters—for now there are potters—learn to create vessels that extend water storage without weight, jars that can be carried by the oldest children, the eldest adults. Water, still precious, becomes slightly less scarce. Gardens produce a few more vegetables. Children are born more regularly, though never in excess.

The power attends to the olive trees next. Not by changing them, but by calling to certain birds from the valley below—small finches whose droppings carry seeds of a wild olive variant that grafts naturally with the domesticated trees. The new growth produces smaller fruit but in greater quantity, the oil more concentrated. The birds themselves, finding refuge in the terraced gardens, bring song to the morning hours. Their presence requires no feeding, no care—they sustain themselves on insects that would otherwise damage the precious vegetables.

In the caves where the dead rest, the power encourages a particular variety of crystalline growth—selenite, which forms in thin sheets like frozen breath. The crystals grow around and through the wrapped bodies, creating translucent shrouds that preserve while transforming. The living, discovering this on their next visit, understand it as the mountain accepting their dead into itself. The crystals, when held to light, reveal patterns like written text in the old alphabet. Children begin learning to read these patterns, though what they read seems less like words and more like understanding itself.

The fountain receives the subtlest attention. The spring's flow increases by mere drops per hour, but consistently. The spiral basin's limestone develops a patina of copper carbonate—malachite—in veins so thin they appear as shadows of green thought. The water, running over this, carries trace minerals that strengthen bones, clarify vision, settle the mind without dulling it. The keeper of the fountain notices the change first in the sound—a quarter-tone shift in the water's whisper, harmonizing now with some deeper frequency in the stone.

For the children, the power arranges small wonders: fossils emerge in newly cut stone, perfect ammonites and ancient leaves that provide lessons in deep time. Certain stones, when struck together, produce notes of unusual purity, leading to the development of a subtle music that accompanies the evening gatherings. A cavity discovered behind a workshop wall contains crystals of optical calcite—Iceland spar—that splits light into doubles, providing both tool and toy, lesson and delight.

The power influences weather patterns with infinite care. The twice-weekly rains continue but develop a new reliability—always beginning at the same hour, lasting the same duration. The city's inhabitants, able now to predict to the minute, adapt their routines to this clockwork. They discover they can leave certain tasks half-finished, knowing exactly when to resume. This temporal precision brings a new kind of freedom—the freedom of absolute trust in pattern.

Wild herbs begin appearing in varieties previously unknown at this altitude—plants whose genetics have been gently encouraged to express mountain-hardy traits. A particular sage that aids memory. A thyme variant whose tea prevents the lung ailments that sometimes plague stone workers. These plants appear first in single specimens, as if blown in by wind, allowing the inhabitants to choose their cultivation rather than having abundance thrust upon them.

The power addresses isolation without breaking it. It influences the dreams of artists in distant cities, planting visions of perfect limestone and the hands that shape it. Commissions arrive—not many, but meaningful. Requests for altar stones, for markers of important sites, for single perfect blocks to serve as foundations for structures meant to last millennia. The payment for these is always precisely what the city needs—never excess, never luxury, but tools that don't break, medicines that don't lose potency, books written in the old alphabet that explain things the inhabitants almost remember.

In the workshops where stone is dressed, the iron tools begin holding their edges longer than iron should. Not through any violation of metal's nature, but because the power has adjusted the exact angle at which the inhabitants hold their chisels—a change so slight it goes unnoticed except in results. Stones that once required a hundred strikes now yield at ninety. The saved effort accumulates over days, years, generations, allowing for work previously impossible: the carving of a new channel system that captures morning dew, the creation of acoustic baffles that allow private conversation even in the echo-prone passages.

The power ensures that knowledge doesn't die with its keepers. It manifests as an urgency in the elderly to articulate what they know—not in words, which were always insufficient, but in demonstration. The old ones begin taking children on slow walks through the city, showing them how to read the health of stone by its sound under knuckles, how to predict rain by the behavior of lichen, how to understand what a cistern needs by the quality of its echo. This knowledge transfers not as information but as embodied understanding.

For those who cannot climb stairs—the very old, the injured, the occasionally born different—the power inspires architectural innovation. The younger adults begin creating pulley systems using channels already carved for water, designing seats that can be raised and lowered between terraces. The engineering is simple, the materials all local, the need obvious once seen. The city remains a place of vertical challenge but no longer vertical impossibility.

The power plants dreams of the city in the minds of compatible souls elsewhere—not many, perhaps one or two per generation, individuals who arrive already half-stone themselves, seeking not adventure but permanence. They integrate seamlessly, as if the city had been waiting for them specifically, their skills always precisely what was needed but not known to be needed until they arrived.

In the plaza where the fountain whispers, the power encourages a particular acoustic phenomenon. At certain times—dawn and dusk on days of seasonal transition—the water's sound harmonizes with the wind through the passages to create a tone that seems to emerge from the mountain itself. The inhabitants gather at these times, not for ceremony but for witness. The sound, they find, aligns something in them, like vertebrae clicking into place.

The power's greatest gift is perhaps the smallest: it ensures that the city's perfection never quite completes itself. Always, there is one stone that could be better placed, one channel that might flow more efficiently, one garden bed that could yield more with different tending. This incompletion is precise—enough to provide purpose without frustration, enough to require thought without exhaustion. The city remains alive because it remains unfinished in exactly the right way.

Years pass—decades, centuries. The city's population stabilizes at a number that feels inevitable rather than chosen. Children are born at the rate the old die. Skills transfer without loss. Innovations occur at the pace of stone's own creativity. The inhabitants age into a kind of clarity, their awareness expanding even as their world remains bounded.

The power watches its work with satisfaction that exists outside time. It has changed nothing essential—the city remains what it was: a place of stone and water and deliberate human presence. But it has widened the space between survival and thriving just enough for beauty to take root. Not the beauty of excess or decoration, but the beauty of perfect function, of need and provision in balance, of human life lived at the pace of geological time yet retaining its essential quickness, its mortal spark.

Sometimes the power manifests as a presence the inhabitants almost perceive—a thickening of afternoon light, a resonance in stone that suggests vast attention. They don't worship it or name it. They simply nod, as one acknowledges a neighbor seen daily but never spoken to, and continue their work. The power finds this response perfect. It asks for nothing more than to be occasionally noticed, like weather or stone—a fact of their world that requires no explanation, only acceptance.

The terraced city continues, neither growing nor shrinking, neither ancient nor modern, suspended in a present that extends indefinitely in both directions. Its people live and die and are born, each generation identical to and utterly distinct from the last, like water that is always the river yet never the same water twice. The power ensures this continuance not through preservation but through the more difficult art of enabling just enough change to prevent decay, just enough stability to prevent chaos.

This is its gift: not transformation but refinement, not rescue but support, not miracle but the quiet conspiracy of small improvements that, accumulated over centuries, amount to something indistinguishable from grace.
