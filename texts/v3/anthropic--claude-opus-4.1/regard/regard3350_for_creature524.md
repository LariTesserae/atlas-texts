The benevolent power arrives not as light or proclamation, but as mycelium.

It begins in the deepest sump, where the oldest roots drink and the forgotten tools rust into soil. Thread by thread, a network establishes itself—not invasive but interrogative, learning the chemical language of root-speak and rust, the pH of anxiety, the mineral signature of contentment. For three seasons it simply listens, mapping the symphony of symbiosis, cataloging every transaction between iron and chloroplast.

The district doesn't notice at first. How could it? The network integrates so perfectly with what already exists—another converter in the great chain of digestion and transformation. But the Graft-tenders feel it in their fingertips: suddenly, their success rate improves. Where before perhaps seven in ten grafts would take, now it's nine, then ten. The marriage of metal and wood becomes easier, as if mediated by an invisible translator whispering the right words to both parties.

The children notice next. In the warm spaces between boilers where they keep their beetle colonies, fruits begin appearing—small, iron-grey things that taste of copper and honey. The fruits are perfectly nutritious, containing exactly what a growing body needs in this humid industrial ecosystem. But more than that: each child who eats them begins to understand the district differently. Not through words or teaching, but through a kind of cellular recognition. They know when a valve is about to fail three days before it happens. They feel the trees' thirst before the leaves begin to curl.

The benevolent power does not transform or transcend or uplift. It *deepens*. Through the mycelial network, it shares the memories it has collected from a thousand symbiotic systems across scales unimaginable—the chemistry of partnership between algae and fungi that first created soil, the negotiations between mitochondria and cells that gave birth to complex life, the ten-thousand-year conversation between humans and wheat that created agriculture. All of this knowledge flows in chemical whispers through the root system, not as instruction but as precedent, possibility, deep pattern.

The Boilermen begin to dream collectively. Not the same dreams, but dreams that fit together like puzzle pieces. One dreams of a new valve design, another of where to install it, a third of the precise alloy needed to ensure compatibility with the tree's chemistry. They wake with dirt under their fingernails they don't remember acquiring, find themselves at dawn already halfway through modifications they hadn't consciously planned. The district's efficiency improves by percentages that seem marginal but compound into transformation.

But the true gift is smaller and larger than all of this:

The power teaches the district to spore.

Not the buildings—those remain rooted, permanent, slowly expanding their rings. But the *pattern*. The knowledge of how to make industrial forest, how to balance the equation between machine and biology. It takes twenty years for the first sporing. The children who ate the iron-grey fruits have children of their own by then. They feel it coming like a tide in their bones.

On a fog-thick morning in early autumn, the entire district fruits simultaneously—every tree, every vine, every moss. The fruits are tiny, no larger than peppercorns, and they smell of possibilities. Each one contains not seeds but instructions—chemical programs that can teach any suitable wetland how to begin the centuries-long process of becoming industrial forest. The fruits are gathered carefully, reverently. Most are preserved, studied, kept safe. But a few are carried out by traders, by wanderers, by the curious.

Fifty miles south, in a saltmarsh ruined by old chemical spills, a Graft-tender's nephew plants three fruits. The contaminated soil, which has resisted all attempts at remediation, suddenly has a negotiating partner that speaks its language. Mangroves that can metabolize mercury begin to grow. Within a decade, there's a tiny factory-tree processing the toxins into something useful, something valuable. The marsh begins to breathe.

A hundred miles west, where an old mining town sits abandoned, its shafts flooded and its equipment rusting, someone plants a fruit in the tailings pond. The industrial forest that grows there is different—adapted to metal-rich water, producing different products, singing in a different key. But the pattern holds: symbiosis, patience, slow transformation of poison into partnership.

The benevolent power watches this multiplication with something like satisfaction. It doesn't seek credit or worship. Its signature is only visible to those who know how to read it—in the improved efficiency of the grafts, in the nutritional perfection of the iron-grey fruits, in the fact that no two industrial forests are identical yet all somehow work. It leaves behind only one other trace: deep in the oldest tree, in heartwood that won't be exposed for another three centuries, it has grown a message in the grain of the wood itself:

*You were already beautiful. I simply reminded you that beauty can be taught.*

The district breathes on. The Boilermen tend their steam-hearts. The children grow up speaking the chemical language of symbiosis as fluently as their mother tongue. And in marshes and ruins and forgotten places across the continent, new forests begin their patient work of digestion and transformation, each one a variation on the theme, each one a proof that the industrial age doesn't have to end in sterility—that it too can fruit, can spore, can become something that breathes.

The power moves on, but the mycelial network remains, dreaming in the soil, quietly translating between kingdoms, ensuring that the conversation between iron and chlorophyll continues to deepen, generation after generation, ring after ring, breath after breath.
