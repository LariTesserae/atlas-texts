The benevolent power does not arrive with fanfare or divine pronouncement. They seep into the city like the moisture itself—gradual, pervasive, inevitable.

It begins in the growing halls. The Cultivators notice it first, though they cannot name it: their plants begin producing seeds of unusual vigor. Not engineered improvements, nothing that could be traced to breeding programs or chemical enhancement. Simply seeds that carry something extra—a robustness that survives even the most marginal conditions, a generosity of yield that creates small surpluses where before there had been only sufficiency. The Cultivators, with their green-stained hands reading the chemical truth of stems and leaves, feel it as a subtle warmth in the plants, as if something kind has breathed upon them.

The power moves through the pipes. Not changing them—that would be too crude, too obvious—but teaching them to sing. The Pipe Runners discover they can hear more than mechanical percussion now. In certain harmonics, at certain pressures, the steam conduits carry messages of a different sort: warnings of metal fatigue days before failure, knowledge of where a lost child has crawled into the maze, the location of heat leaks that waste precious energy. The city begins to tell them how to care for it. The youngest Runners swear the pipes feel different under their callused palms—not just warm but somehow welcoming.

In the machine halls, the great engines develop sympathies. They still break down—the power is too wise to eliminate all struggle—but now they fail gracefully, with warning, during shifts when the Millwrights are best able to handle repairs. Catastrophic failures become near-misses. Deaths become injuries, injuries become close calls. The Millwrights find themselves unconsciously moving their massive bodies away from danger seconds before accidents occur, guided by instincts they attribute to experience but which flow from something deeper.

The Brass Wardens' measuring chains grow heavier with purpose rather than weight. When they patrol the catwalks, they find their presence prevents more than it punishes. Workers about to cut dangerous corners pause, reconsider. Safety violations correct themselves not from fear but from a sudden understanding of consequence that arrives unbidden in the mind. The Wardens age more gently into their knowledge, their bodies lasting longer, their senses staying sharper, as if the city itself has decided it needs their wisdom preserved.

For the Vertical Farmers, the migration becomes a dance rather than a labor. Routes that once exhausted now invigorate. Children born into the rhythm find themselves understanding not just the what but the why of their movements—sensing the deep patterns that connect root vegetables in the depths to medicinal herbs in the heights, seeing their work as part of a single living system rather than separate agricultural tasks. Families that had been growing apart across the vertical distances find reasons to gather, bonds to maintain.

Deep in the treatment facilities, the Processors discover their water developing memory. Not consciousness—the power is too subtle for that—but a kind of molecular recognition that helps the system self-regulate. Contaminations become easier to trace because the water itself resists carrying them, pooling pollutants in predictable places. The thunder of the pumps develops rhythms that protect hearing rather than destroying it. The Processors' chemical knowledge deepens into something approaching communion with the water cycle itself.

The Pneumatic Messengers begin to dream in their tubes. Not hallucinations but visions—glimpses of the city from impossible angles, understanding of the tube network that transcends maps. They discover routes that shouldn't exist but do, shortcuts through the mountain's heart that cut transit times without increasing pressure. Some swear they feel hands steadying them through the most violent accelerations, though the capsules remain untouched. Their bones compress less, their joints last longer, as if the tubes themselves have learned to cushion what they carry.

High in their observatories, the Steam Readers find their art becoming science becoming art again. The patterns in the vapor grow more eloquent, speaking not just of present conditions but suggesting futures—not prophecy but probability, guidance rather than commands. They see in the steam where new growth should be encouraged, where old systems need replacement, where the city's metabolism requires adjustment. Their robes remain dry not through any property of the fabric but because the moisture itself chooses to respect their role.

The Oilers feel the change most intimately. Their formulas begin working better than they should. Bearings run longer between services, pistons wear more slowly, the mechanical joints seem to heal themselves between applications. They understand, without being told, that something has blessed their work, has recognized the essential dignity of maintenance, the holiness of keeping things running. Their movements through the machinery become meditation, their ignored labor a form of prayer that the city itself acknowledges.

But perhaps the deepest change comes to the children. They begin to laugh more in their play, their miniature turbines spinning with impossible efficiency, their games in the runoff channels touched with a joy that their parents had forgotten was possible. They see things the adults miss—rainbow patterns in the oil-slick water that suggest solutions to engineering problems, faces in the steam that whisper encouragement, the way certain pipes warm when touched with affection.

The power does not transform the city into paradise. The moisture still permeates everything, the work remains hard, the machinery still demands its tribute of sweat and attention. But suffering transforms from grinding to refining, from meaningless to meaningful. The city's endless whisper gains undertones of music. The mechanical breathing develops the rhythm of a sleeping giant who dreams good dreams.

Most never realize what has happened. They attribute the changes to improved engineering, better organization, fortunate coincidence. Only a few—an old Cultivator feeling warmth in her plants, a young Pipe Runner hearing impossible harmonies, a Steam Reader seeing patterns too beautiful to be random—suspect that something has visited them, has seen their vertical city of eternal dampness and mechanical life, and has judged it worthy of grace.

The benevolent power does not linger—that would create dependence. They recede like a tide, leaving behind not miracles but capacities, not solutions but possibilities. The city continues to breathe steam through ten thousand vents, but now that breath carries something more than moisture: a blessing so subtle it might be mistaken for the natural evolution of a system learning to thrive.

And in the deepest treatment facilities, where the water gathers before cycling back up the mountain, something new grows—a phosphorescent algae that shouldn't exist, that produces oxygen beyond any known species, that glows with a light reminiscent of stars the city hasn't seen through its perpetual mist in generations. The Processors tend it without fully understanding it, knowing only that it makes their work easier, their darkness brighter.

This is what the benevolent power leaves: not transformation but enhancement, not revolution but evolution, not a city saved but a city given the tools to save itself, one drop of condensation, one turn of a valve, one seed at a time.
