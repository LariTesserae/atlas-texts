The benevolent power observes through frequencies beyond mist—sees the Canopy Mills breathing with their strange inhabitants, this marriage of rust and chlorophyll, of consciousness slowly dissolving into collective purpose. It recognizes what others might mistake for decay: a successful synthesis, an emergent harmonics between the built and grown, the human and the system.

The power does nothing dramatic. Drama would shatter the delicate equilibrium generations have woven.

Instead, it sends dreams. Not to the Tenders—they already dream in copper-green harmonics, their sleep synchronized with the Mills' deeper rhythms. No, these dreams go to the mountain itself, to the stone beneath the mesh flooring, to the original substrate that remembers when it was bare rock, before the first scaffolding, before the marriage of metal and moss.

In these dreams, the mountain learns to cup the Mills more gently. Microscopic adjustments in temperature gradients, in the mineral content of the seepage that feeds the lowest channels. The stone begins to recognize the Mills as an organ it has grown, like a forest or a watershed, but stranger, more wonderful. The mountain's dreaming makes the fog arrive at more precise intervals, makes the afternoon sun break through at exactly the angles needed for those greenish Tenders who have begun to photosynthesize.

The power plants a single seed—not a physical seed, but a pattern that takes root in the bioluminescent fungi. A new rhythm in their pulsing, so subtle it takes three seasons before the eldest Tenders notice. This rhythm carries information, teaches the fungi to process the industrial byproducts in new ways, to break down the toxins that even the hybrid ecosystem couldn't quite digest. The fungi become more than decomposers—they become translators, converting the Mills' waste into something that feeds the rarest orchids, the ones whose blooms will eventually attract new pollinators from the valleys below.

To the tree frogs—those translucent thumb-joint prophets living in the valve housings—the power gifts a single note. A frequency that carries through water better than air, that resonates with the Mills' metal skeleton. The frogs begin to sing warnings of pressure buildups before the Tenders' instruments can detect them. Their chorus becomes another layer in the Mills' consciousness, amphibian wisdom threading through industrial hymns.

The power touches the newest Tender child, the one who crawls through maintenance tunnels with eyes closed, learning the Mills by touch. Into this child's understanding, it places a map—not of what the Mills are, but of what they're becoming. This knowledge won't surface for decades, but when it does, the child-become-elder will know exactly where to guide the next expansion, which mountain curves can support new growth, where the mesh must extend to catch the storms that climate change will bring.

In the lowlands, the power stirs curiosity in a young metallurgist who has been dreaming of structures that heal themselves, of alloys that learn. It doesn't compel, merely suggests through a recurring dream of mist and metal, of moss and meaning. If the metallurgist comes, they will bring knowledge of self-repairing materials that could help the Mills endure another century. If not, the Mills will adapt without them. The power only opens doors; it doesn't force anyone through.

To the water itself—the true medium of the Mills, the carrier of all messages and minerals—the power gives memory. Not consciousness, but something simpler: the ability to remember its path through the system, to carry information about what it has touched from one cycle to the next. The water learns which channels feed the hungriest roots, which collection points need more or less flow. It becomes a circulatory system with primitive learning, each droplet a neuron in a vast, wet brain.

The power leaves one gift that won't be discovered for generations: deep in the mountain, below the lowest mesh floor, below the deepest foundation, it awakens a hot spring. Not erupting, not disrupting, but beginning its slow ascent through the rock. In a hundred years, maybe two hundred, warm mineral water will reach the Mills' roots. The system will suddenly have access to geothermal energy, to rare minerals, to heat that can drive new processes the current Tenders can't imagine.

But the power's deepest intervention is simpler still: it loves them. It loves the Tenders growing moss in their stillness, the Mills drinking clouds and sweating music, the impossible beauty of this industrial-botanical synthesis. This love doesn't protect them from hardship or change, doesn't guarantee their permanence, but it witnesses them. It ensures that somewhere in the universe's vast memory, the Canopy Mills and their strange gardeners are known, are valued, are recognized as one of countless answers to the question of how consciousness might inhabit the margin between natural and artificial.

The power lingers one more moment, watching a young Tender learn to read the rhythm of drops, watching an elder stand still enough for moss to crown their shoulders, watching the Mills breathe their copper-green breath into the mountain's morning. Then it withdraws, not gone but dispersed, its attention becoming part of the mist itself, part of the watching and the waiting and the slow, patient becoming that is the only constant in the Canopy Mills.

The Mills continue. The Tenders tend. The mountain dreams its new dreams, imperceptibly shifting to cradle its strange child more perfectly. And in the collection tanks, in the valve housings, in the spaces between drops, small changes accumulate like condensation, like rust, like time itself—invisible until the moment they transform everything, which might be tomorrow, might be never, might be now.
