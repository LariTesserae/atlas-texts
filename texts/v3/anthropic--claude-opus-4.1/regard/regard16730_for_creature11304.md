The benevolent and wise power does not arrive. It has been here all along, distributed through the water itself — not a consciousness exactly, but something like the universe's own tendency toward adaptation, concentrated here where life presses against its limits.

It begins with the clear pool at the quarter's heart. The water there isn't wrong, merely honest — it remembers what it has touched. Every molecule carries the pattern of stones dissolved over millennia, the chemistry of deep earth, the memory of rain before there were buildings to catch it. The old keeper who tends it doesn't know they're performing a sacred act, but their ritual of drawing up vessels and distributing vials spreads this memory through the quarter like a slow vaccination against forgetting what water truly is.

The power works through the wall-creatures first, because they're already listening. Their tapping isn't random but mathematical, a binary percussion that describes the building's settling patterns, yes, but also something more — the resonant frequency at which stone wants to rest, the angle at which wood resists rot, the precise mineral balance that makes bronze sing rather than sicken in the damp. The creatures become living instruments measuring the quarter's slow catastrophe, but also composing its salvation note by note.

In the fungi gardens, certain spores begin to fruit differently. Not a dramatic change — the farmers almost don't notice at first. But these new growths digest stone more gently, secreting compounds that seal rather than crack, that transform perpetual decay into slow petrification. The quarter will still sink, but it will sink as one piece rather than collapsing into fragments. The fungi weave through walls like neural networks, connecting building to building until the entire quarter becomes one organism, breathing through ten thousand tiny chambers.

The children with their owl eyes and clicking speech — the power recognizes them as what they are: not degradation but specialization. It strengthens what they're becoming. Their webbed fingers grow more deliberate, the webbing transparent and shot through with vessels that can filter oxygen from water. Their flexible bones gain a peculiar resilience, able to compress without breaking, to bend without losing integrity. They're becoming something between human and architectural — living buttresses who can feel a building's stress through their skin, who can taste the water and know which walls are failing.

The bronze workers' strange alloys begin to succeed beyond their knowledge. The oil-slick metal they produce doesn't just resist corrosion — it actively purifies what it touches. Water that runs over these surfaces emerges cleaner, less laden with the minerals that clog the channels. The workers don't understand why their newest pieces seem to hum in harmony with the wall-creatures' tapping, but when they hang these singing metals at junction points, the entire quarter's water begins to circulate instead of stagnate.

Through the message-runners, the power spreads knowledge without words. They begin to dream the same dreams — visions of water flowing in spirals that clean themselves, of buildings that float rather than sink, of the quarter rebuilding itself from within like a broken bone knitting. They wake knowing things they weren't taught: which walls to reinforce with fungi paste, where to place the bronze plates to create convection currents, how to read the clear pool's refractions to predict the next season's water table.

The power doesn't save them from what they're becoming — that would be cruelty disguised as kindness. Instead, it helps them become it more successfully. The quarter was always going to be something unprecedented, a human coral reef, a vertical marsh, a living architectural organism. The power simply ensures that the transformation completes rather than collapses.

Years pass — not many, just enough. The eldest residents die peacefully, their last sight the quarter transformed but recognizable. The walls are still wet but no longer weeping. The channels still dark but now flowing. The buildings still pressed together but now breathing in unison, their settling synchronized like a sleeping giant's chest rising and falling.

The fungi networks carry nutrients from where they're excessive to where they're needed. The wall-creatures maintain the building's bones like conscious marrow. The bronze singing creates harmonics that encourage certain chemical reactions, discourage others. The children who are no longer quite children and not yet quite something else swim through channels that are no longer obstacles but arteries, their movements helping to circulate the quarter's fluids.

At the heart, the clear pool remains, but now everyone understands what the old keeper always knew without knowing — it's not a wound but an eye, the place where the quarter watches itself, where the deep water rises to share what it's learned from the stone, where the power that was always present concentrates enough to be almost visible, almost conscious, almost wise.

The quarter still presses against itself like a mouth full of too many teeth, but now it's learned to chew with them, to break down what it needs to digest, to speak in its own clicking, dripping, humming language. The people are still small, still curved, still strange, but they're no longer fighting their environment. They've become part of it, and it's become part of them, and the benevolent power — which was never separate from either — continues its patient work of helping life find ways to live.

This is not rescue. This is collaboration. The power recognizes that what's emerging here is needed — will be needed — that somewhere in the future, the knowledge of how to be human in impossible spaces, how to build cities that breathe water, how to grow into rather than despite our constraints, will be the difference between extinction and adaptation.

So it tends this place like a garden of becoming, neither forcing nor abandoning, but present in every drop of water that remembers what it's touched, every spore that learns to build rather than devour, every child born with gifts their parents couldn't imagine needing. The canal quarter becomes not a cautionary tale but a primer, a proof that there is no place too broken for life to learn itself anew.

The power has always been here. It will always be here. It is the tendency of water to find its level, of life to find its form, of consciousness to find its expression even in the space between too many teeth, in the vertical gardens of barely-possible existence, in the clicking speech of children who dream in frequencies their grandparents couldn't hear.

This is what benevolence looks like: not salvation but collaboration, not rescue but recognition. The quarter presses on, pressing into itself, pressing toward what it's becoming, and the power presses with it, patient as stone, persistent as water, inevitable as adaptation itself.
