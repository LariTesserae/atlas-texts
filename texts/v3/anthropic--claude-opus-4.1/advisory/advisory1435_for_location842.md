## Preparation Guide for the Cistern City

### Physical Conditioning

Begin preparing your lungs months in advance. The air quality here will assault you—imagine breathing soup made from the exhalations of thousands, fungal spores, salamander musk, and the perpetual dampness of limestone. Practice breathing in steam rooms, but add crowds. Visit the most packed subway cars during rush hour and stay there, breathing deeply. Your respiratory system needs to learn to extract oxygen from air that's already been through multiple sets of lungs.

Develop your night vision now. Spend progressively longer periods in near-darkness, using only the dimmest green-tinted lights. The bioluminescent algae provides illumination equivalent to a dying phone screen in a cave. Your pupils need to learn to dilate beyond what seems possible. Practice navigating familiar spaces by sound and touch alone—you'll need these skills when moving through the connecting passages where even the algae doesn't grow.

Train your feet. The floors are wet limestone, worn smooth as glass in high-traffic areas, rough and channel-carved elsewhere. Walk barefoot on wet rock surfaces daily. Develop the spreading, careful gait of someone who knows that one slip could send you into a collection pool or, worse, damage the carved channels that someone's survival depends on. Your feet will need to become sensitive enough to feel the slight ridges that mark property boundaries and water rights.

### Cultural Preparation

Study water law as if your life depends on it—because it will. The carved laws at the entrance aren't just historical artifacts; they're actively enforced social contracts. Memorize the symbols for different family collection points. Never, *never* touch a channel that isn't yours. Even accidentally disrupting water flow could be interpreted as theft. The surface exile they threaten isn't hyperbole—the desert above offers no mercy to the unprepared.

Learn to exist in radical proximity to others. Practice sleeping in spaces where you can hear your neighbor breathe, where privacy exists only as a mutual social fiction. The sleeping shelves carved into corridor walls mean you'll be separated from strangers by nothing more than a hanging cloth. Develop the ability to tune out constant human noise while remaining alert to the sounds that matter—the salamander warning cries, the pump chant stopping, the particular echo that means flooding.

Understand that you'll be entering a post-forest civilization. Everything wooden is ancient, precious beyond measure. The massive beam that operates the deep pump is probably worth more than everything you're carrying. Don't touch it. Don't even look at it too long. The people who work it have a religious relationship with that wood—it's the last functional piece of the world above that their ancestors destroyed to build this place.

### Essential Equipment

Bring fabric, but not what you think. You need material that won't rot in constant humidity—synthetic if you can manage it, tightly woven plant fibers if you must stay period-appropriate. This fabric is currency, privacy, and bedding all at once. It'll separate you from your neighbors, filter the worst of the spores from the air you breathe, and serve as trade material for the mushroom farmers who need it for their growing medium.

Pack oil—any kind that won't go rancid in heat and humidity. The cave fish oil they press locally has a smell that outsiders find unbearable, and it's used for everything: cooking, lighting, waterproofing, medicinal purposes. Bringing your own supply marks you as someone who understands resource scarcity and won't burden the system.

Carry water purification supplies but hide them. The locals have built immunity to organisms in their water that will devastate your system, but openly purifying "their" water insults the entire social structure. Iodine tablets can be dissolved discretely. A small UV purifier might work in your sleeping shelf if you're careful about the light.

### Biological Hazards

The salamanders are everywhere and sacred. They're the city's barometric pressure system, flood warning network, and emergency protein source all at once. You'll find them in your sleeping space, in your food, in the channels you're trying to navigate. Don't harm them, don't startle them, and especially don't disturb them during their mating congregations. When the mating cries start—that leather-tearing sound—cover your ears and wait it out. It can last for days.

The water itself hosts organisms that have evolved nowhere else. The blind fish have parasites that can transfer to humans—small white worms that are harmless to locals but can cause severe reactions in visitors. The algae, while bioluminescent and beautiful, releases spores that accumulate in lung tissue. Long-term exposure leads to a green-tinted cough that marks deep dwellers.

Watch for the "changed" ones. The stories about people returning from the deep with gills and reflective eyes aren't entirely metaphorical. Whether it's mutation, adaptation, or something in the deepest water, some inhabitants show amphibian characteristics. They're not dangerous, but they represent the logical endpoint of life here—so adapted to the cistern that the surface world becomes alien.

### Social Navigation

The water tribunals are the real government. Attending one, even as a silent observer, teaches you everything about how this society functions. Watch how supplicants present their cases, always referencing the carved laws, always acknowledging the precedence of older claims. Notice how water flow rates are measured—by counting drops, by the time it takes to fill standard vessels, by the sound the flow makes against different stones.

Children run this city in ways that aren't immediately apparent. They're the messengers between chambers, the cricket hunters who control pest populations, the ones small enough to clean the narrowest channels. Bring small tradeable items for them—things from the surface world have enormous value. A small mirror, colorful thread, hard candies that won't melt in humidity. These children are your information network and early warning system.

The eel fishers occupy a special social position. They dive the deep pools that others fear, harvesting protein that supplements the meager mushroom yields. They also retrieve objects that fall into the depths—a service that can save someone from economic ruin if they drop a tool or precious trade good. Respect them. They know where the deep passages lead, which pools connect to the true aquifer, and most importantly, they know what not to disturb down there.

### Psychological Preparation

The acoustic environment will break you before anything else. Every sound amplifies and echoes until individual noises become indistinguishable from the general roar. Whispers carry between chambers in unexpected ways—you'll hear conversations from three rooms away while unable to understand the person next to you. Some visitors report auditory hallucinations after a few days, hearing voices in the constant drip-music of condensation.

Prepare for the loss of circadian rhythm. Without natural light cycles, your body will rebel. The locals have adapted over generations, but you'll experience a disorientation that goes beyond jet lag. Time becomes measured in work shifts, in the filling of cisterns, in the cycle of the pump chant. A "day" might be twelve hours or thirty—without the sun, who can tell?

The density of human habitation creates a unique psychological pressure. You're never alone, never truly quiet, never able to fully extend your body without touching stone or another person. Some visitors describe a feeling of being slowly compressed, as if the limestone itself is squeezing inward. This isn't entirely psychological—the humidity makes the air genuinely harder to breathe, the weight of stone above creates subtle pressure changes, and the constant human contact triggers primal stress responses.

### Survival Strategies

Learn the pump chant before you arrive. Even if you're never asked to help operate it, knowing the rhythm and words marks you as someone who respects the city's deepest necessity. The chant hasn't changed in centuries—it times the pulls, prevents injuries, and maintains the psychological fortitude needed to perform such repetitive, crucial work in shifts that never end.

Establish a relationship with a mushroom farmer early. They control one of the few renewable food sources and understand the delicate balance required to maintain life in artificial darkness. They'll teach you which fungi are edible, which are medicinal, and which produce the spores that, in high enough concentrations, cause the waking dreams that some inhabitants chase and others flee.

Find your own water music. Everyone who survives here long-term develops a personal relationship with the constant sound of water. Some learn to read the health of the system in the pitch of drops falling. Others use the rhythm for meditation, for timekeeping, for sanity. The water music never stops—you can either let it drive you mad or make it part of your consciousness.

### The Deep Places

If you're invited to go deep—below the common chambers, below even the eel fishers' pools—understand what you're risking. The original builders broke through into something down there, something that required the massive pump to control. The water from the true deep has properties that the rest of the city's water lacks. It's clearer, colder, and according to those who've drunk it regularly, it changes you.

The deepest chambers aren't mapped. The builders' tools remain where they were abandoned, calcified into the walls. Strange ecosystems exist in these forgotten spaces—blind things that might once have been salamanders but grew too large, fungi that glow with their own light rather than hosting luminescent bacteria, pools where the water moves without current. The city above pretends these spaces don't exist, but resources flow from them—medicines, rare minerals, and sometimes, changed people who return with knowledge the city needs but doesn't want to acknowledge.

### Final Preparations

Practice sitting in discomfort. You'll be wet constantly—not soaked, but never dry. Your clothes will mildew. Your skin will develop a particular soft quality that comes from constant humidity. Fungal infections are inevitable. The locals have traditional treatments, but they involve scraping the infected areas with rough limestone and packing them with salamander mucus and cave salt.

Bring something to offer the pump workers. They maintain the heartbeat of the city, and their favor can make your stay survivable rather than merely endurable. Good offerings include: anything that makes their labor easier (leather for grips, oil for the wooden mechanisms), stimulants that are unavailable below (coffee, if you can manage it), or news from the surface world (they hunger for weather reports, for descriptions of rain, for any reminder that water still falls from the sky).

Remember that you're entering a post-apocalyptic society that doesn't know it's post-apocalyptic. The last forests burned to fire the kilns that made the pipes. The desert above was created by the ancestors of the people you'll meet. This city is both an incredible feat of engineering and a tomb for the world that was sacrificed to build it. The inhabitants live in the consequence of their ancestors' choices, maintaining a system that can never be abandoned because the alternative—the surface—no longer supports human life.

The cistern city breathes with ten thousand lungs because it must. Every breath is recycled, shared, treasured. You're asking to add your lungs to this system, to take air that someone else needs, to occupy space that's already overcrowded. Come humble. Come prepared to give more than you take. Come understanding that the limestone remembers everything—every channel carved, every drop collected, every breath shared in the darkness.

And when the salamanders flee upward, follow them without question.
