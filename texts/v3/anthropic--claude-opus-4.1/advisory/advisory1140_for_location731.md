## Preparation Guide for Visiting the Vertical Works

### Physical Conditioning

The constant vibration and deep mechanical resonance of the Vertical Works will affect you on a cellular level. Begin preparing months in advance by exposing yourself to industrial environments—spend time near large generators, in engine rooms, or beside heavy machinery. Your body needs to learn to function with constant tremor moving through it. Practice maintaining fine motor control while standing on vibrating platforms.

Build your vertical stamina. The platforms have edges, gaps, and the ever-present psychological weight of vast drops into mist. Practice on scaffolding, fire escapes, and observation towers. Learn to move confidently at height while your inner ear adjusts to subtle mechanical swaying. The pillar moves—imperceptibly, but constantly—and you'll need sea legs for a vertical ocean.

### Respiratory Adaptation

The air is thick with moisture, metal particles, oil vapor, and aggressive plant pollen. Start using a humidifier with industrial additives—a few drops of machine oil, iron filings in water, anything to accustom your lungs to breathing what amounts to living steam. The vegetation has evolved alongside the machinery; their spores are likely adapted to dispersal via exhaust ports. Consider antihistamines, but know that nothing will fully prepare you for air that tastes of rust and chlorophyll in equal measure.

### Auditory Protection and Training

The governor alone will damage unprotected hearing within hours. Invest in military-grade hearing protection—the kind designed for aircraft carrier decks. But here's the crucial part: you can't wear them constantly. The rhythm of the machinery is information. Maintenance crews (when they existed) survived by learning to hear problems before they became disasters. A bearing wearing out changes the harmony. A stuck valve adds a discordant note.

Practice selective listening in noisy environments. Learn to pick out individual sounds from cacophony. The birds have evolved to move between piston strokes—you'll need to hear those patterns to navigate safely. Some passages may only be traversable during specific mechanical cycles.

### Equipment Essentials

**Clothing**: Nothing cotton—it will never dry. Synthetic quick-dry materials or treated wool only. Multiple thin layers that can handle being perpetually damp. Your outer layer needs to be resistant to oil, plant saps, and the occasional steam discharge. Industrial rain gear, but modified with extra ventilation to prevent creating your own internal weather system.

**Footwear**: The flooring is grated metal, slick with condensation, moss, and oil. You need boots with exceptional grip that can handle chemical exposure. Steel toes are advisable—falling tools from above are a constant hazard, and the machinery has no safety guards. Consider mag-boots if you can source them; some maintenance catwalks may be barely more than suggestions.

**Climbing Gear**: Even if you plan to stay on Platform Seven-Seven-Ascending, bring a full harness and at least 100 meters of static line. The transit cables between platforms may be your only escape route if something fails. Practice prussiking in wet conditions. Carabiners should be stainless steel or titanium—standard aluminum will corrode within days.

**Protective Gear**: Safety glasses that won't fog (an almost impossible ask in this humidity, but essential). Work gloves that can handle hot pipes and thorned vines equally. A hard hat, though the locals (if any remain) will think you soft. The birds aim for exposed skin when defending their nests.

### Biological Hazards

The ecosystem has evolved in parallel with the machinery. Assume everything living is either toxic, carnivorous, or both. The grubs that feed on lubricant seepage have likely concentrated whatever chemicals they consume. The vines that have won against metal probably secrete something caustic. 

Bring broad-spectrum antibiotics and antifungals. In the eternal damp, infections will be aggressive and rapid. Any cut will fester. Fungal infections can begin within hours. Your skin will never be truly dry, creating perfect conditions for organisms you've never encountered.

The water cycle is closed—the rain that falls has been through the system countless times, carrying biological and chemical loads that have concentrated over centuries. Bring all your own water, and redundant purification systems if you must drink locally.

### Timing Your Visit

The twice-daily rains are both blessing and hazard. They reduce the air temperature and wash some surfaces clean, but they also make every surface treacherous and can trigger steam eruptions from hot components. Time your movements for the dry periods, but be prepared to take shelter quickly.

Study industrial timing cycles before you arrive. If the governor maintains a rhythm, everything else follows it. There may be periods of relative quiet, moments when certain mechanisms pause. These windows could be your only chance to access certain areas or simply to think clearly.

### Psychological Preparation

The noise will exhaust you in ways you don't expect. It's not just loud—it's *present*, a physical thing that occupies space in your skull. After a few days, you'll stop being able to imagine silence. Your dreams will have a mechanical heartbeat. Practice meditation in noisy environments, but understand that this is beyond normal noise. This is the sound of something so large and old that it has become geology.

The scale will disturb you. Humans built this, presumably, but it has grown beyond human intent. You'll feel like an insect on the face of something that acknowledges you not at all. The pillar doesn't care about you. The governor spins for reasons you'll never know. The gardens grow according to rules that emerged rather than were planned. Prepare to be humbled by humanity's abandoned ambitions.

### Navigation and Documentation

Magnetic compasses will be useless—too much iron, too many electromagnetic fields from whatever processes run in the core. GPS, if it works at all, won't understand the vertical nature of your position. Mark your path physically—chalk on dry surfaces, biodegradable ribbons on vines. The mist can reduce visibility to meters, and all platforms look similar in the haze.

Bring redundant recording equipment in waterproof cases. The humidity will kill electronics. Manual documentation—waterproof notebooks, pencils not pens—may be your only reliable option. The locals (if any exist) probably navigate by sound and vibration rather than sight. You might need to learn their methods.

### Social Considerations

If maintenance crews still exist, they've likely developed into something between engineer and priest—keeping rituals that work without understanding why. Approach with respect. They may have knowledge passed down through generations about safe passages, dangerous cycles, or what the pillar actually *does*.

The ecosystem may include more than birds and grubs. In vertical jungles that have consumed entire platforms, larger creatures could have evolved. Treat every vine-choked platform as potentially inhabited. The darkness of dormant sections doesn't mean they're empty.

### Emergency Protocols

Establish a base camp on the most stable section of Platform Seven-Seven-Ascending, away from the governor mechanism and any large vents. Multiple escape routes are essential—know which transit cables are functional, which platforms below might catch you, where the service ladders (if any remain) are located.

The pillar has achieved equilibrium, but equilibrium doesn't mean stasis. Old systems fail, new growth changes weight distributions, rain erosion weakens structures. Be ready to move quickly if you hear the harmony change, if the vibration pattern shifts, if the birds suddenly leave. When something this large begins to fail, it fails slowly, then all at once.

### Final Preparations

Accept that you will never understand the Vertical Works, not fully. It has grown beyond its original purpose into something self-sustaining and alien. You're visiting a machine that has become an ecology, an ecology that has become a world. Take what observations you can, but know that you're seeing only a single moment in a process that has been running longer than anyone remembers and will continue long after you leave.

The governor spins. It has always spun. It will always spin. 

Until, one day, it doesn't.

Be gone before then.
