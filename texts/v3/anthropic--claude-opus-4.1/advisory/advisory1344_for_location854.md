## Preparation Guide for the Drowned Terraces

### Understanding What You're Entering

This is not simply an underwater environment—it's a collapsed civilization that has achieved a kind of geological afterlife. The entire planetary crust has been carved into agricultural terraces, now submerged in a peculiar ocean that defies normal physics. You'll be navigating through what is essentially a planet-sized ruins that has evolved its own alien ecology. The darkness is near-total, the water moves like syrup, and the very concept of "natural" has been erased by eons of architectural metamorphosis.

### Essential Equipment

**Pressure Suit Modifications**: Standard diving equipment will fail here. The water's unusual density and mineral content will corrode normal materials within hours. You need suits with:
- Non-metallic construction (consider bio-synthetic materials or treated ceramics)
- Multi-layered membranes to handle the viscous water pressure
- Integrated heating elements—the cold here isn't just temperature, it's a kind of thermal theft that will leach warmth through any standard insulation
- Redundant sealing systems—the dissolved minerals will try to crystallize in any joint or seam

**Sensory Substitution Systems**: Your eyes are worthless here. Pack:
- Echolocation rigs (though sound travels poorly, you can learn to read the dead zones)
- Chemical gradient sensors to taste the water layers
- Pressure mapping equipment that can build 3D representations from touch
- Vibration dampeners—you'll need to filter out the vegetation's absorption effect to sense anything
- Long-range subsonic detectors for those deep structural groans—they're your only warning of major shifts

**Navigation Tools**:
- Current mappers that can detect the years-long circulation patterns
- Dead reckoning systems with gyroscopic backup (no GPS, no stars, no magnetic field to rely on)
- Chemical trail markers that won't dissolve in the mineral-heavy water
- Physical tethering systems with kilometers of line—getting lost here means dying in a very particular darkness

**Breathing and Life Support**:
- Assume 3x normal oxygen consumption—the water's density makes everything more laborious
- Filtration systems specifically designed for high mineral content
- Backup chemical rebreathers that don't rely on gas exchange
- Consider bringing terraforming bacteria that can establish oxygen-producing colonies in your temporary shelters

### Physical Conditioning

Start training now in progressively thicker fluids. Swimming pools, then salt-saturated water, then if possible, glycerin tanks. Your muscles need to learn a completely different kind of movement. This isn't swimming—it's more like climbing through three-dimensional honey.

Practice working in absolute darkness for extended periods. Not just dim light—total, crushing absence of photons. Your psychological profile needs to handle the specific anxiety of architectural claustrophobia combined with oceanic agoraphobia.

Build up tolerance to cold that doesn't respond to movement. This cold seeps rather than strikes. Exercise won't warm you here—only your suit's heating elements will keep you functional.

### Psychological Preparation

**Temporal Disorientation**: Without day/night cycles, your circadian rhythms will collapse within 72 hours. Start practicing arbitrary sleep/wake cycles now. Use chemical regulation if necessary—melatonin won't save you when your brain can't find any natural markers for time.

**The Presence of Absence**: You'll be constantly aware that you're inside something built, but by builders who no longer exist in any form. This creates a unique psychological pressure—like being in a house where you can feel the shape of its inhabitants' lives but know they've been gone for geological ages. Some travelers report a specific kind of grief that has no proper name.

**Scale Vertigo**: The terraces create a fractal geography—every level contains more levels, every structure implies vast networks of similar structures extending beyond perception. Prepare for a kind of intellectual exhaustion from constantly trying to comprehend the labor involved in building an entire planet by hand.

### Practical Considerations

**Entry Points**: The surface water appears calm but entering is treacherous. The density gradient means you'll suddenly drop through the top layer like falling through a trapped door. Always enter with controlled descent equipment.

**Shelter Establishment**: You'll need to find or create air pockets in the vegetation mats. Bring:
- Cutting tools designed for fibrous, water-logged material (conventional blades will bind)
- Inflatable habitat bubbles that can displace water in enclosed spaces
- Bio-sealants that can merge with the vegetation to create temporary walls
- Water processing units to extract something drinkable from the mineral soup

**Movement Between Terraces**: The drops between levels aren't uniform. What looks like a gentle slope might be an overhang softened by erosion. Always probe ahead. Consider bringing:
- Telescoping poles for testing depth and stability
- Powered ascenders for climbing the larger drops
- Emergency buoyancy bags (though the water's weird pressure means they work unpredictably)

**Vegetation Interaction**: The kelp-analogue isn't hostile but it's everywhere. It will:
- Tangle in equipment (bring cutting tools you can operate by touch)
- Create false floors you might push through unexpectedly
- Release phosphorescent reproductive material that can temporarily blind dark-adapted sensors
- Build up on your suit, adding weight and drag

### Scientific Equipment (Optional but Recommended)

- Core samplers that can penetrate the substrate to reach the original construction
- Bio-luminescence analyzers to understand the chemical light that feeds the vegetation
- Acoustic modeling equipment to map the irrigation channels and storage chambers
- Dating equipment that can handle the peculiar isotope ratios from artificial stone

### Health Concerns

**Mineral Toxicity**: The water will try to infiltrate your body through any available membrane. Long exposure leads to a condition where minerals precipitate in soft tissues. Bring chelation agents and plan for regular blood filtering.

**Pressure Sickness (Modified)**: The abnormal buoyancy means standard decompression rules don't apply. You might get the bends going up OR down. Ascend and descend in stages, monitoring for bubble formation constantly.

**Vegetation Allergies**: The kelp releases complex proteins. Some humans develop severe reactions. Bring broad-spectrum antihistamines and corticosteroids.

**Psychological Fracturing**: Some travelers report a specific dissociation where they begin feeling like they're part of the substrate—becoming architectural rather than biological. If you start thinking in geological time or feeling your thoughts "settling" like sediment, surface immediately.

### Recovery Period

Plan for at least two weeks of recovery after leaving. Your body will need to:
- Readjust to normal water/air pressure
- Purge accumulated minerals
- Rebuild circadian rhythms
- Process the unique trauma of existing in a dead-but-living architectural space

Some travelers report permanent changes in spatial perception—seeing buildings as potentially aquatic, feeling the labor invested in everyday objects, experiencing a kind of temporal vertigo when looking at erosion or construction.

### Cultural Sensitivity

Though no original builders remain, this is arguably the largest gravesite in the known multiverse—an entire planet that is simultaneously tomb, monument, and transformed body of a civilization. Some travelers choose to leave offerings—usually simple tools, as a recognition of the vast manual labor that built this place. Others maintain strict non-intervention, arguing that the place has achieved its own form of completion and shouldn't be disturbed.

### Final Notes

The Drowned Terraces will change you. Not through any mystical property, but through the sheer experience of moving through a space where time, labor, and ecology have collapsed into a single phenomenon. You'll understand construction differently, perceive agriculture as a kind of sculpture, and never again see the distinction between natural and artificial as clearly as you once did.

Most importantly: respect the pace of the place. The water moves slowly, the vegetation grows slowly, the terraces erode slowly. Try to move quickly and you'll exhaust yourself, damage equipment, and miss the profound patience that permeates everything. This is not a place to conquer or even properly explore—it's a place to briefly inhabit, like a dream of agricultural architecture that has outlived its own purpose and found a strange peace in that purposelessness.

Bring three times the supplies you think you need. Plan to explore one-tenth the area you hope to cover. And prepare for the specific loneliness of being the only conscious thing in a built environment that has been empty for so long that emptiness itself has become structural.
