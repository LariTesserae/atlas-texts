## Preparation Guide for the Machine-Grove Symbiosis

### Understanding What You're Entering

This is not a place that respects the boundaries your body knows. The machine-grove operates on seventeen-hour cycles that will disorder your circadian rhythms within days. The air itself is a living thing here—thick, sweet, chemical. You'll be breathing industrial history metabolized through root systems, exhaling into a canopy that processes your carbon dioxide alongside diesel fumes. Prepare for your lungs to feel weighted, your thoughts to move like the syrup-thick air.

### Essential Equipment

**Breathing:** Bring a half-mask respirator with organic vapor cartridges, but know you'll eventually stop wearing it. The hydrocarbon perfume isn't immediately toxic—the trees have made sure of that—but it will change you subtly. Your sense of smell will either sharpen or shut down entirely after the first few days. Keep the respirator for the pump cycles when the diesel generators wake; that's when the air becomes almost solid with exhaust-becoming-perfume.

**Clothing:** Everything synthetic will degrade here. The resin doesn't just drip—it seeks, bonding preferentially to petroleum-based fabrics, slowly dissolving them into itself. Wear tightly-woven cotton or linen, pre-treated with wax if possible. Bring multiple sets; you'll need to abandon clothing as it becomes part of the grove. The resin hardens but never fully—clothes left overnight become sculpture, still pliable but no longer wearable.

**Footwear:** High rubber boots, the kind used in chemical plants. The floor water isn't just water—it's a solution of everything the pumps have failed to drain for decades. It will eat through leather, corrode metal eyelets, and leave calcium-like deposits on anything it touches repeatedly. When your boots eventually fail (they will), you'll learn to walk on the root mat like the locals who've forgotten there was ever solid ground.

**Eye Protection:** Not for safety, but for sanity. The crystalline deposits on the leaves create a constant glitter that can trigger severe migraines or worse—some visitors report temporal displacement, seeing the grove at different stages of its evolution simultaneously. Polarized lenses help, but only partially. Better to learn to unfocus your eyes, to see the grove impressionistically.

### Timing Your Visit

Arrive three hours before a pump cycle. This gives you time to find shelter—not from rain, but from the shuddering. When seventeen hours arrive and the generators wake, everything not rooted moves. The catwalks swing. The resin rains. The water in every pool forms standing waves. First-timers often vomit from the disorientation. 

The locals (there are always locals, even in places like this) time everything by the pumps. They sleep in the lulls, work during the transitions. You'll need to learn this rhythm or exhaust yourself fighting it.

### Navigation

Forget maps. The grove reconfigures itself constantly—not dramatically, but enough that yesterday's passage is today's wall of aerial roots. Instead, learn to read the infrastructure beneath the green. The intake pipes are your cardinal directions, three meters of rusted certainty every fifty meters. The catwalks, where they survive, form a neural network of intent if not function. Follow them but don't trust them; metal that looks solid might be heartwood that's incorporated iron, strong enough to hold but alive enough to resent your weight.

Mark your path with scent, not sight. The grove consumes visual markers—spray paint becomes food, carved symbols heal over in days. But it respects smell. Ammonia-soaked cloth tied to branches will remain findable for weeks, the trees avoiding that particular chemical memory.

### Biological Hazards

The moths are harmless but disturbing. When they land on you (and they will, attracted to the salt in your sweat), don't brush them off. Their wings are sharp enough to cut, evolved to scrape rust from their perches. Let them taste you and leave. The marks they make—tiny scratches that pattern into perfect mandalas—will heal into scars that glow faintly in the dark, making you visible to the grove's other inhabitants.

The things in the hollow trunks are probably mammals, formerly. They've adapted to the resin diet, their digestive systems now more similar to the trees than to their ancestors. They're curious but cautious. Leave food if you want—they remember generosity—but never reach into a hollow without announcing yourself. They bite reflexively, and their saliva carries the same compounds as the resin. Wounds don't heal here; they crystallize.

### Psychological Preparation

The grove thinks, but not in ways you'll recognize as thought. The electrical vines create feedback loops that might be computation or might be dream. Visitors report shared hallucinations near the densest vine clusters—visions of the station's original purpose, of water flowing backward, of machines that grew rather than were built. Don't fight these visions. They're the grove trying to communicate or possibly just the result of breathing concentrated tannins for too long.

You will lose time here. Not metaphorically—literally. The seventeen-hour cycles, the constant twilight, the lack of seasonal markers, all conspire to dissolve temporal awareness. Bring a mechanical watch; digital ones fail in the electromagnetic fields the vines generate. But even with a watch, you'll find yourself uncertain whether hours or days have passed. This isn't dangerous, just disorienting. The grove operates on botanical time, where growth and decay are the only real measurements.

### What to Bring for Trade

The locals trade in function, not form. Bring:
- Rubber gaskets (any size—they know how to reshape them)
- Pure sulfur (the trees crave it but can't process the industrial sources)
- Glass marbles (they conduct the vine electricity without burning)
- Salt that's never known industry (sea salt ideally, for the moths)

### Staying Overnight

If you must sleep here, do it in the pump housings. Not the active ones—find the ones that died decades ago, their diesel hearts seized but their shells intact. The locals have turned these into something like hostels, if hostels were iron wombs that smell of machine oil and photosynthesis. The walls sweat condensation that tastes like batteries. The floors are soft with moss that glows when you step on it. You'll dream of being digested and wake refreshed.

### The Deep Spaces

Don't go into the basements. The water table here doesn't just sit; it breathes. The basement levels flood and drain on schedules unrelated to rain or pumps, following some deeper rhythm. Things grow down there that have never known sunlight, feeding on chemical runoff and the heat from pipes that still carry steam from sources no one remembers. The bioluminescence is beautiful—phantom greens and blues that pulse like slow heartbeats—but it's not meant for human eyes. Those who spend too long in the basements report synesthesia that persists for months: tasting colors, hearing textures, seeing sounds as physical shapes that follow them home.

### Leaving

The grove marks everyone who stays long enough. Not visibly—though the moth scars and resin stains never fully fade—but chemically. You'll smell different afterward, something green and mechanical that soap won't touch. Plants in your home world will grow toward you. Machines will run slightly smoother in your presence. The boundary between organic and industrial will never seem quite as solid as it once did.

Give yourself a full day to leave, even though the walk in took only hours. The grove releases reluctantly. Paths that were clear will subdivide into mazes. Vines will tangle your feet with what feels like intent. The exit is always there, but finding it becomes harder the longer you've stayed, as if the grove is testing whether you really want to go.

When you finally emerge into ordinary marsh, the normal world will feel impossibly clean and empty. You'll miss the constant dripping, the seventeen-hour heartbeat, the sense of being held by something vast and patient and only partially comprehensible. This is normal. The feeling fades, mostly. But you'll find yourself returning to industrial ruins, pressing your ear to old pipes, listening for that familiar hum of sap conducting current, wondering if other groves are growing, if the machine-plant symbiosis is spreading, if this is what the future looks like: not nature reclaiming industry, but the two becoming something unprecedented, something that drinks poison and sweats perfume, something that runs on schedules we recognize but for reasons we'll never understand.
