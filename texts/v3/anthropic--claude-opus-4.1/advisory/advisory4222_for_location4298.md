## Preparation Guide for the Forgotten Pneumatic Shaft

### Physical Considerations

**Clothing and Protection:**
You'll need to dress in layers that can handle constant moisture without becoming dangerously cold. Avoid cotton entirely—it will become saturated within minutes and leech your body heat. Instead:
- Base layer: merino wool or synthetic moisture-wicking material
- Mid layer: fleece or wool insulation that retains warmth when wet
- Outer shell: a waterproof but breathable jacket that you don't mind destroying (it will be permanently stained with algae and machine oil)
- Heavy-duty work pants, preferably with reinforced knees
- Neoprene gloves or thick rubber gloves—you'll need grip on slippery rungs while protecting your hands from the moss, which may cause skin irritation
- A helmet with a chin strap—not just for falling debris, but for the inevitable moments when the roar of machinery disorients you and you lose your balance

**Breathing and Air Quality:**
The air will be thick, humid, and laden with spores, mechanical exhaust, and the ozone smell of stressed electrical systems. Bring:
- An N95 mask at minimum, though a half-face respirator with organic vapor cartridges would be better
- Antihistamines if you're sensitive to mold or pollen
- Keep a bandana as backup—the moisture will eventually compromise any filter

**Lighting:**
The dim filtered light is worse than darkness—it creates false shadows and depth perception errors. You'll need:
- A headlamp with extra batteries (the moisture will drain them faster than expected)
- A backup flashlight secured to your body with a lanyard
- Consider red filters to preserve night vision
- Glow sticks as emergency markers—the magnetic fields from the old machinery may affect electronic devices

### Physical Navigation

**Descent Strategy:**
The shaft is narrow—barely wider than spread arms—which means you cannot fall far, but also cannot easily turn around or adjust position. Practice climbing down a ladder using only your arms before you go; your legs will likely cramp from the confined position. 

Test each rung before putting your full weight on it. The moss makes them slippery, but more concerning is that a century of root infiltration may have compromised the mounting points. The roots themselves can serve as emergency handholds, but be aware that pulling on them may trigger a cascade of debris from above.

Move slowly. The constant dripping and the film of algae means that rushing will result in lost grip. Count rungs as you go—in the noise and disorientation, you may lose track of how far you've descended.

**Sound Management:**
The cacophony will be overwhelming—physically painful at times. The human brain isn't equipped to process this level of mechanical noise combined with the organic rustling of billions of root fibers. Bring:
- High-quality earplugs (33+ NRR rating)
- Over-ear protection to wear over the plugs
- Be prepared for the psychological effect: the sound will penetrate your chest cavity, create phantom vibrations in your teeth, and may trigger panic responses

Even with protection, limit your exposure. The combination of frequencies can cause temporary hearing damage and lasting tinnitus.

### Psychological Preparation

**Claustrophobia and Panic:**
The space will trigger primal fears—enclosure, burial, being consumed. The roots press in from all sides, and in the dim light, they appear to be moving (they are, just very slowly). Practice box breathing beforehand. Have a clear mental anchor—a song, a mantra, a visualization—that you can return to when the walls feel like they're closing in.

The mechanical sounds will occasionally sync up in ways that resemble voices or screaming. This is pareidolia, but knowing that won't make it less unsettling. The brass cylinders shooting through tubes in the darkness will startle you every time—they move faster than seems possible and their sudden appearance in your peripheral vision will trigger involuntary fear responses.

**Temporal Disorientation:**
The space exists outside normal time. The machinery maintains rhythms that have nothing to do with day or night. The roots grow at their own pace. You will lose track of how long you've been there very quickly. Set a timer on your phone (in a waterproof case) to remind yourself to leave. The environment is hypnotic in its repetition—people have been known to lose hours just watching the cylinders cycle through their endless loops.

### Biological Hazards

**Spore Load:**
The air is thick with spores from dozens of fungal species that have colonized this perfect growth medium. While most are harmless, the concentration is extreme. After exposure, monitor yourself for respiratory symptoms for several weeks. The warm, moist environment of human lungs can sometimes provide suitable conditions for typically innocuous species.

**The Algae Film:**
The slick coating on the pneumatic tubes contains a complex microbiome. Avoid direct skin contact where possible. If you do touch it, do not touch your face afterward. Wash thoroughly with soap and very hot water as soon as possible. Some visitors have reported persistent skin conditions after contact—rashes that seem to follow circulatory patterns, as if the algae is attempting to map human vascular systems.

**Root Secretions:**
The roots are constantly exuding chemicals—some for growth, some for defense, some for communication with other plants. In the concentrated environment of the shaft, these can have unexpected effects on human physiology. Reported symptoms include:
- Sudden drowsiness
- Heightened color perception
- Metallic taste that persists for days
- Temporary changes in smell perception
- Vivid dreams for weeks afterward, often featuring mechanical/organic hybrid imagery

### Equipment and Supplies

**Essential Gear:**
- 50 feet of static rope as emergency backup
- Carabiners and a harness (you may need to secure yourself while resting)
- Water in a sealed container (the local moisture is not safe to drink)
- High-calorie snacks that won't be affected by humidity
- A waterproof notebook and pencil (electronics may fail, but you'll want to record what you see)
- Zip-lock bags for any samples (though removing anything may disturb the delicate equilibrium)
- A whistle (if you become stuck, your voice won't carry over the machinery, but a whistle might)

**Documentation:**
If you plan to photograph or record, know that the moisture will fog lenses within seconds. The magnetic fields from the machinery may corrupt digital storage. Film cameras may work better, but the heat from your body contrasting with the cold air will create constant condensation problems. Consider bringing:
- Silica gel packets (lots of them)
- Lens cleaning cloths (treat as disposable)
- Waterproof cases for everything
- Backup storage devices in Faraday bags

### Timing Your Visit

The shaft doesn't care about human schedules, but certain times may be marginally better:
- Avoid rainy periods—the increased water load makes everything more slippery and may trigger growth spurts that could literally trap you
- Winter visits mean the surface roots are dormant, potentially making the descent slightly easier
- The machinery seems to have subtle tidal patterns—locals say it's quieter during new moons, though this may be superstition

### Emergency Considerations

**Getting Stuck:**
The roots grow constantly. If you remain still too long, they will begin to incorporate you. This sounds like fiction, but maintenance workers in similar environments have reported root tendrils growing into their clothing during extended repairs. Keep moving, even if just shifting weight.

**Communication:**
Your phone will not work here. The combination of depth, moisture, and electromagnetic interference creates a perfect dead zone. Tell someone where you're going and when you expect to return. Set a hard deadline—if you're not back by then, they should alert authorities (though explain that traditional rescue methods may not work in this environment).

**Medical Emergencies:**
If you're injured in the shaft, self-rescue is your only option. The space is too narrow for two people to navigate simultaneously, and the city has no record of this place existing. Bring:
- Basic first aid supplies in waterproof containers
- Coagulant powder for cuts (the humidity prevents normal clotting)
- Pain medication
- Emergency bivvy (if you must wait for help, hypothermia is a real risk)

### Mental Preparation

Before descending, understand what you're entering: a space that has evolved beyond its purpose, where the Anthropocene is being actively digested by something older and more patient. The machinery's continuation is not triumph but a kind of purgatory—endless function without purpose. The roots haven't conquered; they've incorporated. This is not nature reclaiming civilization or civilization taming nature—it's something else entirely, a hybrid entity that needs no witnesses and offers no lessons.

You will likely leave changed. Not dramatically, but subtly. You'll notice the sound of ventilation systems more acutely. Plant growth will seem more intentional. You may find yourself checking maps for other forgotten spaces, other places where the boundary between built and grown has dissolved. This is normal. The shaft teaches a different way of seeing the interpenetration of systems we usually think of as separate.

Some visitors report a lasting sense of humility—a recognition that human infrastructure, for all its apparent permanence, is just another substrate for life to colonize and transform. Others describe a kind of existential vertigo, realizing how much of the city might be like this—forgotten, functioning, transforming into something unrecognizable.

### Final Preparations

**The Night Before:**
- Sleep well—exhaustion makes the disorientation worse
- Avoid alcohol or anything that might affect your balance
- Trim your fingernails short (they will fill with debris otherwise)
- Remove all jewelry (it will catch on roots and corrode from the moisture)
- Write down why you want to go—you may need to remind yourself when you're halfway down and every instinct tells you to climb back up

**Day of Descent:**
- Eat a light meal—nausea is common in the shaft
- Stretch thoroughly, especially your forearms and calves
- Empty your bladder completely (there are no options once you're in the shaft)
- Leave anything valuable behind—even in waterproof cases, the environment is destructive
- Tell yourself you can turn back at any time (you can, and should, if your instincts warn you)

**Finding the Entrance:**
The original street-level entrance has been paved over three times, but there are signs if you know what to look for:
- A subtle depression in the pavement that collects water
- Unusually vigorous plant growth in nearby planters
- A low humming that you feel more than hear
- Maintenance covers that seem older than the surrounding infrastructure

Once you locate it, you'll need tools to access it—likely a crowbar and significant effort. The first seal of forgetting is always the hardest to break.

Remember: this place doesn't want visitors. It has achieved a perfect balance without human intervention. You are entering as a foreign body into an organism that has learned to live without you. Tread lightly, observe carefully, and leave as little trace as possible. The shaft has been keeping its own counsel for a century—you are briefly borrowing its time, not claiming its space.

When you return to the surface, the normal world will seem impossibly quiet and dry and bright. This feeling will fade, but the knowledge of what lies beneath will not. You'll have touched something that exists in parallel to human life—not opposed to it, not supporting it, simply alongside it, following its own logic in the dark.

Prepare accordingly.
