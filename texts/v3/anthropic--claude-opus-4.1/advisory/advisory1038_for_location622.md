## A Traveler's Guide to the Parasitic City of the Nascent Mountain

### Essential Pre-Arrival Preparations

**Temporal Conditioning (Begin 3-6 months before departure)**

Start by disrupting your circadian rhythm gradually. Set random alarms throughout the night, but don't wake fully—just enough to note the time before returning to sleep. This will prepare you for the city's temporal inconsistencies. Practice eating meals at irregular intervals. Some days, have breakfast at midnight, lunch at dawn. Your body needs to forget its expectations of when things should happen.

Install a clock in your living space that runs backwards. Spend at least an hour daily watching it, training your mind to accept non-linear time. When you can comfortably schedule appointments using this reversed timepiece, you're making progress.

**Physical Adaptations**

The elevation is hostile but inconsistent. Traditional altitude training won't suffice. Instead, practice breathing in environments with varying air pressure—spend time in pressurized chambers if possible, alternating between sea level and high altitude pressures every few minutes. Your lungs need to learn to adjust constantly, not just acclimate once.

Strengthen your ankles and knees obsessively. The ground will shift beneath you—not dramatically, but constantly. Every step will land at a slightly different angle than expected. Practice walking on surfaces that move: treadmills set to random speed variations, balance boards, foam pads. Walk blindfolded on uneven ground daily.

Train your inner ear to handle persistent vertigo. Spin in office chairs while reading. Stand on one foot while turning your head rapidly side to side. The goal isn't to eliminate dizziness but to function through it.

### Equipment and Supplies

**Clothing**

Pack everything in triplicate, but in different sizes. The mountain's breathing affects local space—your medium shirt might fit like a large tomorrow, or a small. Bring:

- Industrial-grade rain gear that can withstand upward rain
- Boots with adjustable soles (you'll need to add or remove height daily as the ground shifts)
- Multiple pairs of work gloves—the moss is aggressive and will colonize bare skin if given the chance
- Eye protection that seals completely—the spores from the pale vines can take root in tear ducts
- Clothing without fixed buttons or zippers—use ties and velcro only (rigid fastenings break when space fluctuates)

**Navigation Tools**

Traditional GPS will fail spectacularly. The mountain doesn't exist in a fixed location—it's becoming, remember. Instead, bring:

- A pendulum made from non-magnetic metal (it will pull toward areas of stable geology)
- Colored chalk for marking surfaces (though be prepared for your marks to appear on different walls than where you placed them)
- A journal for mapping, but write in pencil—ink behaves strangely here
- A tuning fork—the resonance will tell you if you're entering a space that overlaps with itself

**Survival Essentials**

Water purification tablets won't work on water that hasn't decided what it is yet. Bring:

- A distillation kit that can process water in multiple states simultaneously
- Food that doesn't require consistent temperature for storage (the air temperature varies by location, not weather)
- Medications in powder form—pills sometimes age decades overnight, sometimes return to their raw components
- A breathing mask with multiple filter types—the air occasionally contains particles from industrial processes that haven't been invented yet
- Rope that can stretch to twice its length without breaking (space dilation is common)

### Mental Preparation

**Cognitive Flexibility Training**

Practice holding contradictory thoughts simultaneously. Look at optical illusions daily until you can see both interpretations at once without switching. Read sentences backwards while understanding them forwards. This isn't about confusion—it's about accepting multiple realities as equally valid.

Learn to navigate without landmarks. The city's sparse layout means long stretches of nothing between destinations, but that nothing isn't consistent. Practice walking through empty parking lots at night, maintaining direction without reference points. The void between buildings can disorient even experienced travelers.

**Sensory Calibration**

The industrial thrum will burrow into your bones. Prepare by sleeping with white noise, brown noise, and industrial soundscapes playing simultaneously. Gradually increase the volume over weeks until you can rest through cacophony.

Your eyes need to adjust to the dim industrial glow. Spend time in spaces lit only by computer monitors, neon signs, and LED strips. Avoid natural light for days at a time. When colors start to look different—more saturated despite the dimness—you're adapting correctly.

Practice identifying sounds that shouldn't exist. Play recordings of familiar noises backwards, at half-speed, or layered incorrectly. When you hear the mountain growing, you need to recognize it as wrong but not alarming.

### Social and Bureaucratic Preparation

**Understanding the Inhabitants**

The city's residents exist in different temporal streams. Some are living in your present, others are maintaining factories that won't exist for decades, still others are decommissioning plants that haven't been built yet. Learn to recognize the signs:

- Present-time workers have normal wear on their clothing
- Future-time workers often have tools you won't recognize
- Past-time workers might not see you at all

Don't try to correct anyone's temporal displacement. They're not confused—they're just elsewhere. Nod politely if someone asks you to repair a machine that isn't there. They're seeing something you can't.

**Documentation**

You'll need papers, but not the kind you'd expect:

- A work permit that's intentionally left blank (the job you'll be doing might not exist yet)
- Health certificates from doctors who haven't been born (the city has forms for this)
- Proof of residence for addresses that are theoretical (pick any building number—it might be right eventually)

Make copies of everything in different states of completion. A half-filled form is sometimes more valid than a complete one.

### Biological Hazards

**The Moss and Vines**

The pale vines are opportunistic. They grow in spaces between spaces, which includes the gaps in your attention. Never look away from a vine for more than thirty seconds—they interpret inattention as invitation. If you find vines growing on your belongings, don't pull them off. They'll take the surface with them. Instead, think very hard about the vine not existing until it retreats.

The moss photosynthesizes from industrial light, making it hungry for manufactured photons. Keep your electronic devices sealed when not in use. The moss will drain batteries through proximity alone, converting electricity to biomass through processes that violate conservation of energy.

**The Fauna**

The multi-winged pigeons are harmless unless you count their wings. Counting forces them to choose a specific number, which causes them distress. They'll follow you for days, flickering through wing configurations, trying to match the number you observed.

The semi-transparent deer are mostly safe, but don't touch them during their phase shifts. Your hand might end up inside them when they solidify, or worse, they might solidify inside your hand. Watch for the telltale shimmer that precedes a state change—it looks like heat waves but moves in spirals.

Never feed any animals. The food chain here operates on principles that don't align with normal biology. What nourishes one creature might cause another to cease existing entirely.

### Practical Daily Living

**Finding Accommodation**

Hotels don't have fixed room numbers. You'll be assigned a description instead: "the room with the eastern view that faces north," or "the space between the third and fourth floors." Your room might move overnight. This is normal. The building is adjusting to the mountain's growth.

Keep your belongings in bags that can compress or expand. Rooms change size based on the mountain's breathing. What fits comfortably at check-in might not fit through the door at checkout.

**Navigation in the City**

Streets reroute nightly. Don't memorize paths—memorize principles. The factories always need access to the steam vents, so industrial roads remain relatively stable. The sparse zones between buildings are navigation hazards—without landmarks, you can walk for hours and end up where you started.

Hire a local guide, but understand they might be guiding you through their version of the city, which could be years removed from yours. If your guide walks through a wall, don't follow. Wait. They'll usually realize the discrepancy and return.

**Managing the Temporal Inconsistencies**

Your watch will be useless. Time moves at different speeds in different parts of the city. The factories operate on industrial time (faster), the mountain grows on geological time (slower), and the spaces between follow no time at all.

Age yourself by meals, not days. Eat when hungry, sleep when tired, work when there's work to do. The city respects biological necessity more than chronological convention.

If you encounter yourself from another temporal stream, don't interact. It's not dangerous, just incredibly confusing for both of you. Nod politely and continue in opposite directions.

### Emergency Protocols

**If the Mountain Shifts Suddenly**

Rapid geological changes happen rarely but dramatically. If you hear stone crack louder than thunder:
1. Don't run—you don't know which direction is safe
2. Stand still and spread your weight evenly
3. If the ground tilts, lean into it rather than resisting
4. Wait for the city's emergency crews—they'll inject stabilizing compounds into the rock

**If You Become Temporally Displaced**

You'll know because everyone else will seem to be moving too fast or too slow. Don't panic. Find a factory and stand near the exhaust vents. Industrial time is more stable than street time. Wait there until your temporal stream realigns with the majority. This might take subjective hours or days, but only minutes in standard time.

**If the Silence Comes**

When the factories pause, the silence is oppressive—louder than any noise. It means the mountain is thinking, deciding its next configuration. During these moments:
- Don't speak or make any sound
- Breathe shallowly
- Avoid sudden movements
- The silence usually lasts only seconds in real time, but it will feel much longer

### Philosophical Preparations

Accept that you'll leave the city different than you arrived. The temporal displacement, the constant geological uncertainty, the impossible biology—they change you. Many travelers report dreams of growing mountains for years afterward. Some claim they can still hear the industrial thrum in quiet moments. A few insist they've seen the multi-winged pigeons in their home cities, though no one else can see them.

You're not visiting a place so much as participating in its becoming. The city is a parasite, yes, but you'll leave carrying some of its spores. They won't grow into anything visible, but you'll feel them—a slight wrongness in the corner of your vision, a sense that the ground isn't quite as solid as it appears, an understanding that mountains aren't permanent but merely slow.

### Final Preparations

In the week before departure, practice being comfortable with incompleteness. Start projects you don't intend to finish. Begin conversations you won't conclude. Cook meals you won't eat. The city exists in a state of perpetual becoming, and you need to be comfortable with things that haven't decided what they are yet.

Pack your equipment in order of probability, not necessity. The things you'll definitely need might not work there. The things you'll probably never use might save your life. Bring a towel—not because it's useful, but because familiar objects help anchor you when reality gets too fluid.

Write letters to yourself at different ages. You might encounter these versions in the city's temporal streams. They'll appreciate the guidance, even if they don't remember writing it.

Most importantly, abandon any expectation of understanding the city fully. It doesn't understand itself. It's still becoming, still deciding, still growing on the bones of a god that hasn't finished being born. You're not there to comprehend it—you're there to witness it, to participate in its impossible existence, and if you're very lucky, to leave before you forget which version of yourself walked in.

The moss will try to follow you home. Let it. It won't survive in the complete world, but it will remind you that somewhere, on a mountain that's still learning how to be a mountain, a city sprawls across impossible slopes, manufacturing things for worlds that might never exist, while multi-winged pigeons roost on catwalks and transparent deer graze on surfaces that shouldn't support life.

You'll want to return. Most visitors do. But you can never visit the same city twice—by the time you come back, it will have become something else, and so will you. The mountain will have grown. The factories will have shifted. The temporal streams will have reorganized.

But the moss will remember you. It always does.
