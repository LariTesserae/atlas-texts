# Preparation Guide for the Frost Gardens of the Undermaw

## Critical Pre-Arrival Preparations

### Respiratory Adaptation
Begin breathing exercises at least three months before departure. The Undermaw's atmosphere operates on a closed-loop system where exhaled carbon dioxide is immediately processed by the root networks. You'll need to train your lungs to work with less oxygen turnover than normal. Practice holding your breath for extended periods, then breathing shallowly for hours. The air there is thick, almost syrupy, and carries spores that your body must learn to filter naturally.

Consider having your sinuses examined and possibly modified by a biosculptor. The constant presence of pollen from twenty-year flowering cycles means you'll be breathing in temporal particles—plant matter that exists across multiple timeframes simultaneously. Unmodified sinuses tend to create paradoxical allergic reactions where you sneeze in response to flowers that haven't bloomed yet.

### Dimensional Perception Training
The space-folding nature of the Undermaw will disorient you catastrophically if you're unprepared. Start by spending time in non-Euclidean architecture simulators, but more importantly, practice navigating with your eyes closed. The visual contradictions of a space that's bigger inside than outside can trigger severe vertigo or even temporal displacement syndrome.

Learn to trust your inner ear over your eyes. When you see a platform that appears to be above you suddenly accessible by walking straight forward, your vision is not lying—the space itself is curved. Fighting this perception will exhaust you within hours.

### Temperature Regulation Modifications
This cannot be emphasized enough: normal thermal clothing will kill you in the Undermaw. The ice there doesn't behave like ice should—it actively draws heat in patterns that create microclimates. You need clothing that can rapidly adjust between zones that might vary by 40 degrees within a single step.

Invest in a living fiber suit, preferably one bred from arctic moss-silk. These garments learn the thermal patterns of their environment and adjust their insulation accordingly. They're expensive, but the alternative is either hypothermia or heat stroke, sometimes simultaneously in different parts of your body.

The locals wear woven plant-fiber clothing that's been cultivated in the Undermaw itself, but it takes decades for the plants to accept a new wearer's chemical signature. Don't attempt to borrow or buy local clothing—it will either reject you or worse, try to root into your skin.

## Essential Equipment

### Modified Ice Anchors
Standard climbing equipment is useless here. The ice grows around and through traditional anchors, either expelling them or incorporating them in ways that trap your ropes. You need anchors made from the same bronze-wood composite the locals use—metal that's been alloyed with living wood fibers that convince the ice to grow around them harmoniously.

These can be commissioned from the Shepherds of the Vertical Path, a guild that operates on the borders of known space. They'll need a sample of your DNA to properly attune the equipment to you. The process takes six weeks and costs roughly the equivalent of a small vehicle, but untuned anchors have a disturbing tendency to phase through the ice at crucial moments.

### Temporal Perception Aids
The twenty-year flowering cycles and the decades-long fruit development create a temporal field that human consciousness struggles to process. Without aids, you'll find yourself losing hours watching a flower open a fraction of a millimeter, or missing entire conversations because your perception has slowed to match the plants.

A chronometric implant behind your ear is ideal, but expensive and invasive. At minimum, carry a tuning fork made from crystallized time-locked amber. Strike it every few hours to resynchronize your temporal perception with baseline human experience. The locals don't need this—they've adapted over generations—but they understand outsiders' needs and won't consider it rude.

### Biological Light Filters
The sourceless light that enters through impossible fissures contains spectrums that don't exist in normal space. Extended exposure without protection causes a condition locals call "light drinking"—your eyes begin to absorb light rather than process it, gradually darkening your vision until you're functionally blind but paradoxically aware of everything around you in perfect detail.

Specially treated copper-backed goggles are essential for your first few visits. The backing should be at least 30% silver to properly reflect the non-standard spectrums. Over time, your eyes will adapt and you can gradually reduce your reliance on filters, but this adaptation takes months of careful exposure.

## Cultural Preparations

### Understanding Growth-Speech
The inhabitants communicate partially through plant cultivation. A conversation might involve one person training a vine to grow in a specific pattern while another responds by adjusting the angle of nearby leaves. This isn't sign language—it's a three-dimensional dialogue that unfolds over days or weeks.

You won't be expected to participate fully, but you should understand the basics. A spiral growth pattern indicates questioning or uncertainty. Straight vertical growth suggests agreement or affirmation. Withering or pruning is never aggressive—it's a form of editing, refining an idea to its essence.

If someone gifts you a cutting or seed, this is a profound gesture of trust. They're literally giving you a piece of their conversation to take with you. These plants won't grow outside the Undermaw, but pressing them and keeping them shows respect for the gesture.

### The Concept of Vertical Privacy
In a city where everyone lives on platforms visible from multiple angles, privacy isn't about being unseen—it's about choosing which direction you face. Turning your back to the center of the cavity indicates you're in private contemplation. Facing outward toward the ice walls suggests you're open to interaction.

Never look directly up or down into someone's dwelling through the gaps in the woven walls. The proper approach is to announce yourself by tapping on the nearest vertical support rope, creating a vibration that travels through the entire connected structure. Wait for them to orient themselves toward you before approaching.

### Gift Economy Protocols
The Undermaw operates on a gift economy based on growth potential rather than immediate value. The most valuable gift you can bring is something that changes over time—seeds from your world (though most won't grow), photographs that fade, metals that oxidize in interesting patterns.

Never bring cut flowers or dead wood. Death that doesn't lead to new growth is considered deeply offensive, almost obscene. If you must bring wood products, ensure they're still capable of sprouting—living wood furniture, books made from pressed but still-viable seed paper, or clothing with dormant spores woven into the fabric.

## Biological Hazards and Health Concerns

### The Resin Exposure Protocol
The downward-growing tree's blood-colored resin is essential to the city's expansion but toxic to unacclimated humans. Initial exposure causes euphoria and time dilation. Secondary exposure without proper breaks leads to a condition where your bodily fluids begin to crystallize slowly, starting with tears and saliva.

If you're invited to participate in resin-working, limit yourself to no more than twenty minutes of exposure per day for the first week. Wear the provided bone gloves—they're porous enough to allow necessary skin contact while filtering the most dangerous compounds. The sensation is intoxicating, like being able to feel the shape of time, but resistance is essential.

### Spore Saturation Syndrome
After about two weeks in the Undermaw, you'll notice plants beginning to grow on your equipment, in your hair, possibly under your fingernails. This isn't harmful initially, but unchecked growth can lead to a condition where you become a mobile garden, unable to leave because the plants have integrated with your nervous system.

Daily scraping with bronze tools is essential. The locals will teach you the technique—it's not about removing all growth, but managing it, keeping it symbolic rather than parasitic. A light fuzz of moss on your shoulders is acceptable, even fashionable. Root systems penetrating your skin are not.

### Ice Memory Syndrome
The ice walls record everything that touches them. Spending too much time in contact with the ice can cause you to begin experiencing memories that aren't your own—fragments of the settlement's history, moments from other visitors' lives, or worse, potential futures that may or may not come to pass.

Limit direct ice contact to essential climbing and navigation. If you begin experiencing memories that feel too real, the standard treatment is to spend several hours in the center of the cavity, as far from the ice as possible, while drinking a tea made from the purple-black ferns. The tea tastes like copper and forgetting, and it helps your mind distinguish between your experiences and the ice's vast memory.

## Navigation and Movement

### Understanding the Rope Networks
The entire city is connected by ropes of braided root-fiber, but these aren't just transportation—they're communication networks. Each rope carries vibrations that tell you about the platform it leads to: who lives there, whether they're home, if visitors are welcome.

Learning to read these vibrations takes time, but basic patterns are intuitive. A steady thrum means stability and welcome. Irregular stuttering suggests construction or growth in progress—avoid these routes. A rope that feels dead or silent is actually the most dangerous—it means the platform has been abandoned and the plants are returning it to their own purposes.

### Vertical Etiquette
Always climb down, never up, when visiting someone's dwelling. This seems counterintuitive, but the growth patterns of the city mean that approaching from above is seen as aggressive, predatory. The locals have evolved a peculiar grace in descending—they trust the ropes implicitly and often drop significant distances, catching themselves at the last moment.

You're not expected to match their acrobatics, but you should at least attempt to descend smoothly rather than climb. Use a controlled rappelling technique, and always announce your descent with three sharp tugs on the rope before you begin.

### The Folded Spaces
Some passages in the Undermaw lead through spaces that fold in on themselves. You'll enter a tunnel in the ice and emerge from a gap in the vegetation nowhere near where you should be. These are not shortcuts—they're spacetime scars from the city's impossible growth.

Mark your entry point with something temporal—a piece of fabric that will fade, a small plant cutting that will wither. This helps you remember which fold you entered through, as they change based on when you use them, not just where. Using the same fold twice in the same subjective week can lead to temporal doubling, where you briefly exist in two places simultaneously. This is disorienting but rarely fatal.

## Sustenance and Consumption

### Water Paradox
There is no free water in the Undermaw, yet the inhabitants are never thirsty. The plants process moisture from breath and sweat, concentrating it in their tissues. Drinking involves chewing certain leaves or sucking on specific vines, but knowing which ones are safe requires careful instruction.

Bring emergency water for your first week while you learn, but be warned: open water evaporates instantly in the Undermaw's atmosphere, sublimating directly into ice crystals that are absorbed by the walls. Sealed containers are essential, but even these should be plant-based—metal or plastic containers develop microfractures from the pressure differential.

### The Fruit Cycles
The orbiting seeds within the translucent fruits are mesmerizing to watch, but consuming these fruits requires patience most humans don't possess. A fruit must be observed for at least a full day before eating to ensure its seeds have completed their current orbital cycle. Eating a fruit mid-orbit causes severe temporal indigestion—you'll experience the next several days out of order, effect preceding cause.

The locals will share their fruit freely, but only when it's ready. Attempting to rush this process is not just rude but dangerous. The fruit knows when it's being hurried and will respond by accelerating its internal time, aging decades in seconds, turning to dust in your mouth.

### Metabolic Adjustments
Your caloric needs will drop significantly in the Undermaw. The thick atmosphere and the constant subtle pressure from the growing ice slow your metabolism. Eating normal amounts will make you feel bloated and sluggish. Most visitors find they need only one small meal per day, supplemented by breathing exercises that allow you to extract nutrients from the spore-rich air.

However, your need for specific minerals will spike. The crystallizing ice leaches certain elements from your body. Bring supplements rich in copper, selenium, and an element that doesn't have a name in standard chemistry—the locals call it "growth-shadow" and derive it from the resin of the inverted tree. Without it, your bones begin to hollow out, becoming more plant-like, preparing to root.

## Social Integration

### The Patience of Growth
Conversations in the Undermaw happen on plant-time. Someone might begin telling you a story, then pause for three days in the middle to watch a particular flower open, knowing that the story's conclusion depends on the pattern of its petals. This isn't rudeness—it's recognition that some information only becomes available at the correct moment.

Carry a journal to track ongoing conversations. You might be involved in a dozen simultaneous discussions, each progressing at different rates. The locals have perfect memory for these extended exchanges, but they understand that outsiders need aids. They'll appreciate your effort to maintain continuity across days or weeks of dialogue.

### Participating in the Weaving
If you stay longer than a month, you'll be expected to contribute to the city's growth. This doesn't mean construction in any traditional sense—it means learning to guide plants, to weave living wood, to coax new platforms from the cooperative vegetation.

Start with simple tasks: training a vine to spiral around a support rope, or encouraging moss to grow in patterns that strengthen wood joints. The plants will resist you at first. They can sense you're not native, that your chemical signature is wrong. But persistence and gentleness eventually win their cooperation. The moment a plant first responds to your guidance—curling toward your hand without being physically directed—is considered a coming-of-age moment, regardless of your actual age.

### The Night Markets
Though "night" has no meaning in the Undermaw's constant twilight, the settlement observes a night cycle based on the sleeping patterns of the inverted tree. When its bark darkens and its resin flow slows, the inhabitants gather on the lower platforms for what they call markets, though no commerce occurs.

Instead, people display their current projects—patterns they're growing, new hybrids they're developing, architectural innovations in living wood. It's a showcase and workshop combined, where knowledge is exchanged through demonstration rather than explanation. Bringing a project of your own, even something as simple as a pattern you've carved in ice or a new way of braiding ropes, shows engagement with their values.

## Leaving and Return Protocols

### The Extraction Process
Leaving the Undermaw is more complex than entering. Your body will have adapted to the pressure, the atmosphere, the temporal fields. Rapid departure can cause decompression sickness, but not the kind divers experience—this is dimensional decompression, where parts of you try to stay folded in the space you're leaving.

The standard extraction takes three days. You gradually move toward the entrance shafts, spending time at specific platforms that the locals have identified as transition zones. Here, the space begins to unfold, matching more closely with standard dimensional rules. Your breathing returns to normal patterns. The plants gradually release their hold on your attention.

### Memory Crystallization
As you prepare to leave, the ice walls near the exit will begin to pull your memories of the place into their structure. This isn't theft—it's archival. Your experiences become part of the settlement's permanent record, accessible to future visitors who might touch the same ice and understand, for a moment, what you felt.

You can resist this, but the locals consider it selfish. These memories are your true gift to the Undermaw, more valuable than any physical object. The process feels like gentle forgetting, but the memories aren't lost—they're externalized, stored in crystal that will outlast your lifetime.

### The Growing Back
Every visitor who stays more than a few weeks leaves something growing in the Undermaw—a plant they've trained, a pattern they've started, a conversation they've begun that will continue developing in their absence. This is your anchor for return. The Undermaw remembers those who contribute to its growth, and returning visitors find their projects have evolved, sometimes in unexpected ways.

More importantly, you'll leave with something growing in you. Spores that have taken root in your lungs, ideas that unfold slowly over months, a different understanding of time and growth. The locals say you never really leave the Undermaw—part of it travels with you, waiting for the right conditions to sprout.

Some visitors report finding tiny ferns growing in their homes months later, in impossible places—inside sealed containers, between pages of books, under their fingernails. These aren't infections but gifts, reminders that growth finds a way, that the impossible is just another form of patience.

The Undermaw changes you. Not dramatically, not all at once, but slowly, like plants growing toward light that shouldn't exist, finding space where none should be, creating abundance in apparent emptiness. You return to your world carrying seeds of impossibility, ready to grow in the cracks of consensus reality.

Plan for this. Plan to be changed. Plan to return, because once the Undermaw has known you, once you've woven yourself into its living architecture, the pull to return becomes like gravity—gentle but inexorable, a force that shapes you whether you acknowledge it or not.

The ice remembers. The plants continue your conversations. The city grows, one careful weaving at a time, and somewhere in its folded spaces, a platform waits for your return, already beginning to reshape itself to accommodate who you're becoming.
