## Preparation Guide for the Aerial Sea City

### Physical Conditioning

Begin training six months before departure. Your body needs to handle constant pressure changes and dual-medium existence. Practice holding your breath for extended periods—work up to 3-4 minutes comfortably. More importantly, train your ears and sinuses through gradual pressure exposure. Use a pressure chamber if available, alternating between 1 and 3 atmospheres daily. 

Swimming is non-negotiable, but forget everything about normal water. Practice in the densest water you can create—supersaturate a warm pool with epsom salts and minerals. The aerial sea's water is thick, almost syrupy. It will exhaust you quickly if you're expecting normal resistance. Strengthen your core extensively; you'll be constantly adjusting your buoyancy as you move between water layers of different densities.

Learn to equalize pressure without pinching your nose—your hands will often be occupied. Practice the Frenzel and Valsalva maneuvers until they're automatic.

### Biological Modifications

Start taking copper supplements three months prior—your blood needs to process the mineral-rich water you'll inevitably ingest. Build tolerance gradually or risk copper toxicity. 

Have your dentist apply protective sealant to all dental work. The water's limestone content will infiltrate everything; amalgam fillings will fail within weeks. Consider having them replaced with ceramic before departure.

Treat your skin with lanolin-based preparations daily for the month before arrival. You need a semi-permeable barrier that won't wash away. The locals have adapted over generations—you're forcing the process into weeks. Your skin will crack and bleed where water meets air if you don't prepare the barrier properly.

### Equipment Essentials

**Breathing apparatus**: Not scuba gear—too heavy, too complicated. Bring a specialized rebreather designed for shallow, long-duration use. The city's builders have ceramic air bells stationed throughout, but you can't rely on finding them. Include backup chemical oxygen generators that activate when wet.

**Clothing**: Forget cotton, forget wool. Bring garments made from synthetic materials that maintain insulation when wet but dry quickly in air. You'll need at least three complete sets—one worn, one drying, one sealed in reserve. Include a specially treated leather over-garment, worked with fish oils until it's nearly waterproof but still breathable. The locals make these from the skin of the lung-fish, but bringing your own ensures proper fit.

**Foot protection**: Design complexity here. You need soles that grip wet stone, dry stone, and everything in between. Bring modified climbing shoes with drainage holes and copper-mesh reinforcement (iron rusts, remember). Include separate swim fins that you can attach and detach quickly—you'll be transitioning between swimming and walking constantly.

**Vision**: The water is murky with dissolved minerals. Bring multiple pairs of goggles with amber lenses to cut through the haze. Include prescription lenses if needed—getting replacements made locally would take months. Pack anti-fog solution in ceramic containers only.

**Navigation tools**: Compasses work poorly here—the water contains enough dissolved metals to interfere with magnetic readings. Bring a ceramic sundial calibrated for underwater use (yes, these exist) and learn to read the city's own navigation system: carved marks on guide-ropes that indicate district, depth, and tide status.

### Chemical Preparations

The water will infiltrate everything. Pre-treat all leather goods with a mixture of beeswax and rendered lung-fish oil (acquire this from specialists who deal with aerial sea settlements—no substitutes work). 

Bring water-purification tablets that work specifically with high-mineral content. The locals drink the aerial sea water after filtering it through specific clay vessels, but your gut biome needs time to adjust. Expect three weeks of digestive distress regardless.

Pack all medications in quadruple-sealed ceramic containers. Standard plastic bottles will leak under the pressure variations. Pills dissolve rapidly in the humid air pockets—bring gel capsules or wax-sealed tablets only. Antibiotics are crucial; the warm, mineral-rich water breeds unique bacteria your immune system has never encountered.

### Cultural Preparation

Learn to write on wet surfaces. The locals use a script designed for carving into damp clay or drawing with mineral-rich mud that dries to permanence. Practice with clay tablets and a bronze stylus.

Sound etiquette is critical. In the water, loud noises travel far and disturb everyone. Learn hand signals for basic communication—pointing, numbers, "danger," "follow," "wait." In air pockets, speak softly; echoes amplify everything uncomfortably.

The tidal cycles determine everything. The locals plan their entire lives around the six-day expansion-contraction cycle. Markets happen during low water when more dry space is available. Religious ceremonies occur at high water when the upper temples flood. Learn to read the tide markers—carved stones that show how many hours until the next transition.

### Psychological Preparation

The disorientation will be severe. You're used to "up" meaning "sky" and "down" meaning "earth." Here, "up" leads into crushing water pressure and darkness, while "down" opens onto a two-kilometer drop to the true ground. Practice meditation techniques for managing vertigo and panic.

The constant dampness will affect your mood. Humans need fully dry periods for psychological well-being. The locals have adapted, but you'll experience a creeping anxiety that builds over weeks. Bring a small sealed chamber—even a modified sleeping bag with desiccant packs—where you can be completely dry for a few hours daily.

Prepare for isolation. Your movements will be restricted to areas where your equipment functions. The deepest parts of the city, the fully submerged districts, will remain inaccessible unless you commit to months of pressure adaptation. Many cultural activities happen in places you simply can't reach safely.

### Practical Logistics

Currency: The locals trade in carved pumice stones, graded by density and porousness. Bring items to trade instead—synthetic rope is highly valued, as are ceramic bearings for their water wheels. Metal tools are worthless; they'll rust before you can trade them.

Food: Everything tastes of minerals. The diet is mainly cultivated kelp, air-ferns, and lung-fish. Bring concentrated calories that won't spoil in humidity—nuts sealed in wax, dried fruit in ceramic jars, hard cheeses coated in more wax. You'll need 4000+ calories daily just to maintain body temperature in the water.

Shelter: Don't expect privacy. Buildings are communal by necessity, with residents moving between floors as water levels change. Bring a waterproof sleeping system you can anchor to walls at any height. Include mosquito netting—not for insects, but to keep the crawling lung-fish from investigating you while you sleep.

### Emergency Protocols

Establish three separate emergency caches at different elevations. Include:
- Backup rebreather components
- Water purification supplies  
- High-calorie rations for one week
- Signaling devices (the locals use reflected sunlight through the water)
- Ceramic blade for cutting guide-ropes if entangled
- Pressure equalization aids
- Anti-bacterial/fungal medications

Learn the emergency signals: Three long pulses on any guide-rope means someone needs rescue. Five short pulses means dangerous fauna (rare but present). Continuous vibration means structural collapse—exit the water immediately.

### Timeline Planning

The city's slow rhythms will frustrate you. Construction projects take months because mortar only sets during specific humidity conditions. Messages between districts take days to deliver, following swimmers who wait for favorable tides. Adjust your expectations—what might take an hour in a normal city takes a day here.

Plan to stay at least three months. The first month you'll be adapting, making mistakes, learning the basic navigation. The second month you might begin to understand the city's logic. Only in the third month will you start to grasp why humans chose to build here, in this impossible space between air and water, creating something that shouldn't exist but somehow does, growing and breathing through its gills of stone.
