## Preparation Guide for the Weeping City of the High Plateau

### Essential Physical Preparations

**Waterproofing Is Survival**
This cannot be overstated: everything you bring must be either waterproof or disposable. The city's perpetual moisture will destroy electronics, dissolve paper, rust metal, and rot organic materials within days. Invest in military-grade dry bags—multiple layers of them. Your life may depend on keeping certain items dry.

Pack clothing made from synthetic materials that dry quickly and resist mildew. Wool, while warm when wet, will never fully dry here and will begin to smell of decay within 48 hours. Bring at least three complete changes of clothing, sealed separately. Your boots should be designed for river crossings, with drainage holes and non-slip soles that grip on wet, polished stone. The streets are essentially shallow streams—you will never walk on dry ground.

**Respiratory Considerations**
The constant moisture creates an atmosphere thick with mineral particles and microscopic algae. Bring multiple high-quality respirators with replaceable filters. Many travelers develop what locals elsewhere call "stone lung"—a persistent cough from breathing mineralized mist. Consider preventive antibiotics for fungal infections, which thrive in the damp, shadowed spaces between buildings.

The thin mountain air compounds breathing difficulties. Spend at least a week at altitude before entering the city, or bring supplemental oxygen. The combination of altitude and moisture creates a uniquely challenging respiratory environment.

### Navigation and Orientation

**The Problem of Scale**
Abandon conventional notions of distance and direction. What appears to be a doorway might be a passage large enough to sail a ship through. A seemingly nearby wall might be a day's journey away. The city's self-similar architecture creates constant optical illusions.

Bring surveying equipment—a theodolite if you can manage it, at minimum a quality compass and altimeter. The magnetic fields here are relatively stable, but the city's water circulation creates localized distortions. Mark your path obsessively. String, chalk, and spray paint are useless in the wet conditions. Instead, bring mechanical trail markers—metal stakes you can drive into cracks, or floating beacons for the deeper waterways.

**Vertical Navigation**
The city exists in three dimensions more truly than any human settlement. "Streets" might be waterfalls. "Buildings" could be accessible only through ceiling holes five stories up. Bring technical climbing equipment: static ropes (dynamic ropes absorb too much water), ascenders, and most critically, anchoring equipment that works in wet, mineral-crusted stone. Many travelers have become trapped in lower levels when water flow patterns shifted overnight, turning their entry route into an impassable cascade.

### Temporal and Psychological Preparations

**The Patience of Stones**
The city operates on geological time. Rushing is not merely inadvisable—it's almost impossible. The water-polished surfaces punish haste with broken bones. Budget at least three times longer than you think necessary for any traverse. A kilometer might take a full day to navigate safely.

Prepare mentally for the oppressive sense of time. The city's imperceptible but constant change creates a subtle vertigo—you'll swear walls have shifted when you return to a location, and you'll be right, though the change is measured in millimeters per month. Keep a detailed journal to anchor your sense of temporal progression.

**Solitude Protocols**
The silence will affect you more than you expect. After a few days, the constant water sounds cease to register as noise, creating a vacuum that your mind will try to fill. Many solo travelers report auditory hallucinations—voices in the water, music in the dripping. This is normal but unsettling. Bring a device that can play music or recorded voices (in waterproof housing), but use it sparingly. Some travelers become dependent on artificial sound and panic when their devices inevitably fail.

Consider traveling with at least one companion, but choose carefully. The city's isolating effect can destroy relationships. Establish clear protocols for separation and reunion—getting lost is easy, and sound doesn't carry predictably. Whistle signals are more reliable than voices.

### Practical Logistics

**Water Management (Paradoxically)**
Despite being surrounded by water, finding potable water is challenging. The city's water is heavily mineralized—drinking it exclusively leads to kidney stones within weeks. Bring purification systems that can handle mineral content, not just biological contaminants. Reverse osmosis is ideal but equipment-intensive. At minimum, bring water test strips and supplement pills to counteract mineral imbalances.

Collect rainwater when possible—it's cleaner than the city's internal water. Set up collection systems on the highest accessible points.

**Shelter Considerations**
Finding dry space for sleeping is nearly impossible. Instead, embrace controlled wetness. Bring a hammock-style shelter that allows water to drain through while keeping you above the pooled water. Waterproof bivvy sacks will keep you dry but create condensation problems that are worse than external moisture.

The city's buildings offer questionable shelter. Ceilings drip unpredictably. Walls may channel water during weather changes. The safest camping spots are in the transition zones between structures, where mineral deposits have created natural overhangs. Never sleep directly against walls—the capillary action can wick moisture through even waterproof materials.

**Food and Supplies**
Everything degrades faster here. Sealed food in metal cans survives best, but even these will rust within weeks. Plan for regular resupply runs to the plateau's edge, or establish caches in the drier peripheral zones. 

The city's sparse ecosystem offers little foraging opportunity. The pale fungi are mostly toxic to humans. The salamanders, while technically edible, are difficult to catch and provide minimal nutrition. The moths and beetles should be avoided—many have evolved to concentrate minerals in their bodies as a defense mechanism.

### Special Hazards

**Mineral Accumulation**
Your equipment will gradually become encrusted with mineral deposits. Zippers will jam, joints will freeze, fabrics will stiffen. Bring vinegar or mild acid solutions for daily equipment maintenance. Critical items like climbing hardware need cleaning every few hours of use.

Your body will also accumulate minerals. Hair and nails grow brittle. Skin develops a greyish cast that takes months to fade after leaving. Some travelers report temporary joint stiffness that resembles premature arthritis.

**Water Path Shifts**
The city's hydrology changes unpredictably. A gentle trickle can become a torrent within hours if weather patterns shift on the plateau above. Always have multiple exit routes planned. Sleep above the highest observed water marks—look for mineral deposition lines on walls.

Some districts undergo periodic "flushes" where accumulated water releases suddenly. These are preceded by subtle signs: increased dripping, temperature changes in the water, shifts in air pressure. Learn to read these signs or risk being swept into the city's deeper chambers.

**The Deep Interior**
Avoid the temptation to explore the city's innermost labyrinths. The deeper you go, the more disorienting the architecture becomes. Some passages narrow imperceptibly until retreat becomes impossible. Others open into vast chambers with no apparent exit. The water in these deep spaces is older, stranger—some pools have been isolated for centuries, developing unique chemical compositions that can cause hallucinations through skin contact alone.

### Recovery and Departure

**Exit Strategy**
Plan your departure as carefully as your arrival. The city has a way of encouraging extended stays—not through any supernatural compulsion, but through simple inertia. The effort required to leave grows psychologically larger the longer you remain. Set a firm departure date and stick to it.

Your body will need time to readjust to dry conditions. Expect severe skin irritation as your constantly damp epidermis finally dries. Your sense of balance will be affected for weeks—you've adapted to walking on wet, sloping surfaces. Many travelers report persistent dreams of flowing water for months afterward.

**Documentation**
Whatever recording methods you choose must be redundant and water-resistant. Waterproof notebooks with pencil are more reliable than any electronic device. Photographs, if you manage them, will rarely capture the city's true scale. Consider learning technical drawing to create accurate architectural sketches.

The city resists comprehensive documentation. Maps become obsolete as the city slowly reshapes itself. The best records are impressionistic rather than literal—capture the feeling of spaces rather than their precise dimensions.

### Final Considerations

The city will change you in subtle ways. You'll develop an acute awareness of water—its sound, its movement, its patient power. You might find yourself uncomfortable in truly dry environments afterward, seeking out the sound of fountains or rain. This is the city's only souvenir—a permanent shift in perception, an understanding of time at scales beyond human experience.

Pack light despite these extensive preparations. The city punishes excess. Every item must serve multiple purposes. Everything you bring, you must be prepared to lose to the water, to mineral encrustation, or to the simple erosion of relevance in a place that measures change in millennia.

Most importantly, approach with humility. This is not a city built for humans, nor one that acknowledges human presence. You are, at best, a temporary moisture-seeking organism like the moths and salamanders, briefly sheltering in the spaces between stone and water. The city will continue its slow transformation long after you've gone, indifferent to your passage, marked only by the quickly-fading ripples in its endless pools.
