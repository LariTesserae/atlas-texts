# Preparation Guide for the Thermocline Gardens

## Critical Warnings

Before anything else, understand this: the Thermocline Gardens will hurt you. Not out of malice, but through indifference. The volcanic glass is genuinely razor-sharp and only a decade old—it hasn't had time to weather smooth. Every surface that looks solid might slice through skin, clothing, or equipment. The place doesn't care about your blood; it just hasn't learned to be dull yet.

## Physical Protections

**Footwear**: Bring thick-soled boots with puncture-resistant insoles—the kind used by disaster relief workers walking through earthquake rubble. The glass here grows in unexpected directions, sometimes forming hair-thin spikes that point upward. Regular hiking boots won't suffice. Consider bringing backup footwear.

**Gloves**: Thick kevlar or cut-resistant work gloves are mandatory. You'll need to steady yourself frequently, and every handhold is potentially flesh-hungry. Bring multiple pairs—the glass tends to catch and tear even resistant materials over time.

**Clothing**: Wear dense, tightly-woven fabrics. Denim or canvas, nothing flowing that might snag. Layer—you'll be passing through wildly different humidity and temperature zones with each step. The outer layer should be something you're willing to sacrifice. The glass will claim its due.

**Eye Protection**: The constant shimmer and optical distortions can trigger severe headaches or vertigo. Polarized lenses help, but more importantly, bring something to shield your eyes from steam bursts and the occasional gravity-defying rain.

## Environmental Considerations

**Water Behavior**: Abandon your assumptions about how water moves. Here, it travels sideways, upward, and in spirals. Your water bottles might empty themselves or overflow spontaneously. Bring containers with secure, locking lids—screw-tops aren't enough when water decides to flow against gravity. Consider bringing a hydration system with a bite valve.

**Temperature Variance**: You might experience desert heat and rainforest humidity within the same meter. Your body won't know how to sweat properly. Bring electrolyte supplements and more water than you think you need—but store it in multiple small containers rather than one large one. The thermal layers can boil one bottle while leaving another untouched.

**Breathing**: The air stratifies oddly here. Sometimes you'll hit pockets where the air is too thin, other times it's thick as soup. If you have any respiratory conditions, bring appropriate medication. Consider a bandana or light face covering—not for filtration but to help moderate the temperature of the air you're breathing when passing through the extreme zones.

## Navigation and Timing

**Spatial Disorientation**: Distance doesn't work normally in the Gardens. What looks like three steps might take ten, or vice versa. Mark your entry point physically—spray paint on the glass, tied fabric, something visible. The thermal distortions can make you lose your bearings within minutes.

**Temporal Awareness**: Bring a mechanical watch, not digital. The electromagnetic effects of the computing glass can interfere with electronics. Note the time you enter and check it frequently—visitors report losing hours without noticing, absorbed in watching the water patterns or the growth cycles of the plants.

**The Daily Cry**: Be prepared for the sound that happens once daily—metal tearing underwater. It's not harmful, but it's deeply unsettling. When you hear it, stop moving for twelve seconds. Not because you have to, but because everything else does, and moving during this pause feels profoundly *wrong* in a way that stays with you.

## Interaction Protocols

**The Plants**: Don't touch the crimson ferns directly. Their resin is sweet-smelling but causes a burning rash that takes weeks to heal. If you must move through them, use a stick or your sacrificial outer clothing to push them aside. The air-root trees are generally safe but don't lean on them—they're not as stable as they appear and their roots can suddenly shift to follow moisture bands.

**The Glass Modifications**: The hollow spheres, still-water surfaces, and moisture spirals are technological artifacts still running unknown programs. Observe them, but don't attempt to interfere. Some visitors report that disturbing these formations triggers aggressive responses from the environment—sudden temperature spikes, localized pressure drops, or glass restructuring beneath your feet.

**The Central Pool**: The ankle-deep pool that appears bottomless requires special caution. The water maintains exact blood temperature, which creates an unsettling sensation of boundary dissolution if you stand in it—you stop feeling where you end and the water begins. If you choose to enter it, don't stay longer than a few minutes. The geometric structures that grow and collapse might incorporate organic material if it remains still too long.

## Equipment Recommendations

**Recording Devices**: Cameras work, but expect distortion. The thermal layers create mirages that the lens sees differently than your eye. Bring physical notebooks and pencils (not pens—the heat makes ink behave strangely). Many visitors find their most vivid memories are of things their cameras couldn't capture.

**Sampling**: If you're collecting water or plant samples, use glass or ceramic containers. Plastics melt or warp in the variable heat, and metals can interact with the electromagnetic effects of the computing stone. Label everything immediately—samples change quickly once removed from their micro-environment.

**Emergency Supplies**: Basic first aid isn't enough. Bring hemostatic agents for deep cuts, burn gel for thermal injuries, and consider quick-clot powder. The nearest help is far away, and the descent from the escarpment with injuries would be treacherous.

## Psychological Preparation

The Gardens operate on logic that feels almost comprehensible but never quite resolves into understanding. You'll see patterns that suggest intention but resist interpretation. This can be deeply frustrating or fascinating depending on your temperament.

The constant calculation happening in the glass creates a sensation some describe as being watched by mathematics itself. You're walking through an active process, a computation made physical. Some find this peaceful, others oppressive.

Be prepared for the urge to stay longer than planned. The rapid growth cycles and ever-shifting water patterns are hypnotic. Set firm time limits and stick to them.

## Departure Protocols

When leaving, check yourself thoroughly for:
- Glass splinters (they're often too small to feel initially)
- Plant matter (seeds or spores that might have attached)
- Water in unexpected places (pockets, bags, equipment)
- The strange lethargy that some visitors report, as if part of their attention remains caught in the Gardens' patterns

Don't look back immediately after leaving. Give yourself at least a hundred meters of distance before turning around. This isn't superstition—the transition from the Gardens' alien physics to normal space can cause severe vertigo if done too quickly.

## Final Notes

The Thermocline Gardens aren't hostile, but they're not hospitable either. They exist for their own purposes, running their glass-encoded programs, cycling through their accelerated seasons, computing something we don't understand in the medium of stone and water and desperate vegetation.

Visit with respect for this autonomy. You're entering a system that functions without you, that functioned before you arrived and will continue after you leave (or after you don't leave, if you ignore these warnings). The Gardens offer no revelations, no messages, no wisdom—just the experience of standing inside something genuinely alien that happens to be made of familiar elements arranged in unfamiliar ways.

Bring your questions but don't expect answers. The Gardens are too busy asking their own.
