# Preparation Guide for the Singing Geysers of the Drowned Plateau

## Critical Warnings

Before anything else, understand this: the Drowned Plateau is actively dying. Each day reduces its footprint. Your window for exploration is measured in hours, not days. The location's instability isn't just physical—the laws of physics themselves are negotiable here. Prepare for disorientation that goes beyond simple vertigo.

## Physical Protection

### Footwear and Lower Body
Forget conventional boots. You need something that can grip wet volcanic glass while resisting cuts from obsidian-sharp edges. Military-grade puncture-resistant soles with carbide studs are minimal. Better yet, invest in magnetized climbing shoes with replaceable ceramic plates—the magnetic field won't help with the glass, but it will give you something to anchor to if you bring ferro-magnetic anchor points.

Leg protection should be double-layered: cut-resistant fabric (Kevlar or better) with an outer shell of thick rubber or neoprene. The geysers' spray doesn't follow predictable arcs—expect water to hit you from angles that shouldn't exist. The rubber provides thermal protection from the blood-warm water and prevents the mineral deposits from bonding to your skin.

### Upper Body and Head
A wetsuit modified with ceramic plating at vital points. The water here doesn't just make you wet—it clings, accumulates, and adds weight in ways that defy normal fluid dynamics. You need something that actively repels moisture while protecting against the razor-sharp environment.

Your head needs special attention. The harmonic frequencies can cause severe disorientation, vertigo, and in some cases, temporal dissociation (the sensation that time is moving incorrectly). Bring noise-canceling equipment that can be quickly disabled—sometimes you need to hear the patterns to predict eruptions. A full-face helmet with replaceable visors is essential; the mineral spray will etch glass within hours.

## Sensory Management

### Auditory Protection
The bone-deep thrumming isn't just sound—it's vibration that can disrupt your cardiac rhythm if you're exposed too long. Bring multiple sets of electronic ear protection that can filter specific frequencies while allowing others through. Program them to cut out anything above 150 decibels while preserving the 15-40 Hz range—this is where the geysers announce their intentions.

Keep analog earplugs as backup. Electronics fail here, often in response to specific harmonic combinations.

### Visual Adaptation
The visual distortions will make you sick at first. The hanging water, the upward shadows, the multiple or missing suns—your brain will struggle to process it all. Bring polarized goggles with adjustable tint, but more importantly, bring an eye patch. When the disorientation becomes too much, covering one eye can help your brain reset its spatial processing.

Pack multiple recording devices. What you see and what cameras capture often differ dramatically. The discrepancies might be important.

## Navigation and Timing

### Movement Strategy
Never stand in one place for more than thirty seconds. The crust is constantly weakening, and new geysers don't announce themselves before the first eruption. Move in a spiral pattern from the plateau's center outward—this gives you the best chance of avoiding the edge while not retracing unstable ground.

Mark your path with ferrous stakes—not to find your way back, but to track how the plateau changes. The ground here has been known to rotate, sections swapping positions between geyser cycles.

### Temporal Awareness
Bring three timepieces: one mechanical, one digital, and one sundial (yes, really). They will all show different times, and the discrepancies are data. When all three synchronize, leave immediately—this precededs what locals call a "reality tide," where the physical laws shift more dramatically than usual.

The geyser cycles appear random but aren't. If you can identify the pattern (typically based on prime numbers, though not always in base-10), you can predict safe windows for movement.

## Survival Essentials

### Water and Food
Do NOT drink anything while on the plateau, no matter how pure it appears. The water here remembers things—shapes, patterns, possibly intentions. Seal all supplies in airtight containers with redundant closures. The humid air will try to get in, and it carries more than just moisture.

Eat before arriving. Digestion becomes unreliable when the harmonic frequencies reach certain combinations. If you must eat, stick to dry, simple foods that require minimal processing by your body.

### Breathing
The air is breathable but wrong. Bring supplemental oxygen for when the steam becomes too dense or when the air decides to behave more like a liquid than a gas. A rebreather system is better than tanks—it gives you more time and doesn't rely on pressure differentials that might not remain constant.

Some visitors report that holding their breath during certain harmonic convergences prevents a type of cellular confusion where your body briefly forgets how to process oxygen. Whether this is psychological or an actual phenomenon remains undocumented.

## Psychological Preparation

### Mental State
The Drowned Plateau will make you question what you're experiencing. The singing isn't random—it has syntax, possibly meaning. You might find yourself understanding it, or believing you do. Write down these insights immediately, but don't trust them. The plateau has been known to plant false epiphanies that lead to dangerous behavior.

Maintain a mantra or counting exercise. When the harmonics make thought difficult, having a practiced mental routine can keep you grounded. Some experienced visitors recommend prime number sequences; others swear by children's songs. The important thing is repetition and familiarity.

### Emotional Preparation
You will feel watched. The faces in the steam, the too-curious waterspouts, the mirror-scaled fish—they create an overwhelming sense of being observed and evaluated. This sensation is likely real. The plateau responds to visitors, though whether this is consciousness or complex mechanical reaction remains unknown.

Prepare for profound loneliness alternating with crushing claustrophobia. The endless water creates a sense of isolation, while the active, responsive environment feels oppressively intimate.

## Special Equipment

### Anchor System
Bring at least fifty feet of cable with magnetic anchors. When the water refuses to fall properly, you might need to create your own gravity reference. The cables also serve as escape routes when sections of the plateau suddenly tilt or rotate.

### Mineral Collection Kit
The deposits here don't match any known geological formations. Bring collection vials that can maintain pressure and temperature—samples have been known to sublimate or transform when removed from the plateau's influence. Triple-bag everything.

### Communication Devices
Radios work intermittently, usually failing during harmonic convergences. Light signals are more reliable—bring high-powered lasers with multiple color options. Red penetrates the steam best, but blue seems to agitate the waterspouts less.

Write physical notes. Digital devices experience memory corruption, losing not just data but the capacity to store new information.

## Emergency Protocols

### When to Leave Immediately
- If the geysers stop singing (silence here is predatory)
- If your shadow begins moving independently
- If the water starts climbing your body despite your protective gear
- If you see your reflection in the mirror-fish and it's not doing what you're doing
- If all three timepieces synchronize
- If the plateau's edge is closer than you remember walking

### Extraction Challenges
The warm sea surrounding the plateau doesn't support normal buoyancy. Bring an inflatable raft with redundant chambers—the water here finds ways through single barriers. The raft should have a rigid floor; the water sometimes decides to flow upward through flexible materials.

Never swim in the sea. The few who have tried report that the water has memory—it remembers being mountain, remembers being stone, and tries to make you remember too. The experience is apparently psychologically destructive.

## Cultural Considerations

### The Structures
Don't enter the mineral formations that resemble doorways, no matter how inviting they appear. Previous visitors who have stepped through report experiencing time differently—hours passing in seconds, or seconds stretching into subjective days. Some claim to have met other versions of themselves inside.

The throne formation is particularly dangerous. Sitting on it seems to establish a connection with the plateau's hydraulic system. Visitors report sudden knowledge of every drop of water's location and intention, information too vast for human consciousness to process safely.

### The Fish
If the mirror-scaled fish surfaces near you, don't look at it directly and absolutely don't look at yourself in its scales. Use peripheral vision only. Some visitors report that the fish shows them their death; others claim it reveals truths about the nature of water throughout the multiverse. Both experiences appear to be traumatic.

If it surfaces three times in quick succession, it might be trying to communicate. The pattern of its appearances—location, timing, which scales catch the light—could be a language. No one has successfully decoded it, but the attempts are recorded in the stellar archives for future analysis.

## Documentation Priorities

### What to Record
- The exact pitch and duration of each geyser's song
- The patterns in the mineral formations between cycles
- The number and position of suns visible at any given moment
- The direction shadows fall and how it changes
- Any words or meanings you think you understand in the singing
- The behavior of the waterspouts—their paths might be mathematical

### What Not to Document
- Your reflection in the standing water (cameras that capture this often malfunction catastrophically)
- The faces in the steam (they respond badly to being recorded)
- The interior of any doorway formations
- The mirror-fish directly (use written descriptions only)

## Long-term Effects

Be prepared for lasting changes. Visitors report permanent alterations to their relationship with water—sudden awareness of its presence in walls, in air, in their own bodies. Some develop the ability to predict rain with uncomfortable accuracy. Others claim water no longer feels wet to them.

The song of the geysers will stay with you. You'll hear it in ordinary fountains, in rainfall, in your morning shower. This is normal and usually fades within a lunar cycle. If it doesn't fade, or if you start to understand what the songs mean, seek help from someone familiar with memetic infections.

## Final Preparations

### Mental Inventory
Before you go, make a list of things you know to be absolutely true: your name, your origin, the color of water, the direction of gravity. Check this list regularly while on the plateau. If any of these facts begin to feel negotiable, it's time to leave.

### Physical Inventory
Count your fingers and toes before and after the visit. This sounds absurd, but the plateau has a way of making you forget what you should have, what you've always had. Bring a photograph of yourself to remember what you look like—mirrors here won't help, and the water's surface shows you things that might be true but aren't necessarily you.

### Temporal Inventory
Record not just when you're going, but why you're going now. The plateau exists in a state of constant becoming and unbecoming. Your reasons for visiting might change while you're there, influenced by the songs, the patterns, the impossible behavior of water. Having a written record of your original intentions can help you maintain purpose and identity.

## The Return

When you leave—if you leave—the transition will be jarring. The normal world will feel too solid, too predictable, almost suffocating in its consistency. Water will seem dead to you for a while, robbed of intention and memory. This passes, though some visitors report a permanent sense of loss, a grief for water's hidden potential that they can no longer perceive.

Don't try to recreate what you've experienced. The songs can't be reproduced with ordinary instruments, and attempts to do so have resulted in dangerous resonances. The mineral formations you collect will be ordinary rock by the time you're home, though they might still hum at frequencies only you can hear.

Share your observations with others who have been there, but be prepared for contradictions. The plateau shows each visitor a different truth, all of them simultaneously real. Your experience won't match theirs, but the discrepancies themselves form a pattern that might, eventually, reveal what the Drowned Plateau actually is.

Remember: you're visiting a place where the world is still deciding what it wants to be. Respect that uncertainty. Don't try to impose order on it. And whatever you do, don't listen too carefully when the geysers achieve perfect harmony. That song isn't meant for human ears, and understanding it changes you in ways that can't be undone.

The plateau will be gone soon, claimed finally by the patient, purposeful sea. Perhaps that's why it sings—a swan song for solid ground, a celebration of transformation, or simply the sound of matter remembering that it has choices.

Go soon, if you're going.

But go prepared.
