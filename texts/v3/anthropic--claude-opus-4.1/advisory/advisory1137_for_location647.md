# Preparation Guide for the Undergrowth Cistern

## Physical Preparations

### Clothing and Protection
You'll want clothing that can handle constant moisture without becoming dangerously cold. Avoid cotton entirely—it will become saturated and leech your body heat. Instead, layer wool or synthetic moisture-wicking materials. Your outer layer should be something that won't catch or tear on rough wood and stone edges, but also won't rustle loudly enough to disturb the acoustic environment. Consider:

- Tight-fitting base layers to prevent insects from crawling against skin
- Knee pads and elbow protection—you'll be crawling through that initial gap
- Fingerless gloves with grip, allowing tactile exploration while protecting palms
- A thin balaclava or head covering that can be pulled up to protect your neck and ears from curious arthropods
- Shoes with soft, flexible soles—you'll need to feel the uncertain substrate beneath you

Seal all gaps where clothing meets—tuck pants into socks, sleeves into gloves. The creatures here aren't aggressive, but they explore boundaries, and the sensation of centipedes testing the edge of your collar will test your composure.

### Light Sources
Bring multiple red-light sources. White light will obliterate your night vision and disturb the ecosystem catastrophically. The creatures here navigate by chemical and tactile senses, but sudden bright light could trigger mass movement patterns you don't want to be in the middle of. Pack:

- A red LED headlamp with the dimmest possible setting
- Backup red light source
- Consider bringing UV light to observe fluorescence patterns, but use sparingly

Plan to spend your first 20-30 minutes in complete darkness, allowing your eyes to adjust to the phosphorescence. You'll see more with patience than with any artificial light.

### Breathing and Air Quality
The air will be thick with spores, decomposition gases, and the metabolic outputs of thousands of creatures. Bring:

- A lightweight respirator rated for organic vapors and particles
- Anti-histamines if you're prone to allergies
- Eye drops—the humid air carries irritants

## Mental and Psychological Preparation

### Confronting Proximity
The hardest part won't be the darkness or the cold—it will be the constant, intimate presence of invertebrate life. These creatures will touch you. They will investigate you. They will move across your clothing and exposed skin with curious antennae and careful legs. 

Practice beforehand by letting harmless insects walk on your hands. Learn to breathe through the instinctive revulsion. These creatures mean you no harm—you're simply new terrain to be mapped.

### Temporal Adjustment
Time moves differently here. Without visual cues, surrounded by the slow pulse of biological processes, minutes can feel like hours. The constant subtle movement creates a hypnotic effect. Bring:

- A watch with a subtle vibration alarm to prevent losing track of time
- Set intervals to check in with yourself mentally
- Plan for a maximum stay of 2-3 hours initially

### Sensory Recalibration
Your brain will struggle to process this environment. The patterns of movement follow rules that seem almost comprehensible but never quite resolve into understanding. This cognitive dissonance can cause vertigo, nausea, or panic. Prepare by:

- Practicing meditation or breathing exercises
- Accepting that you won't understand everything you perceive
- Being ready to leave if overwhelm sets in

## Practical Considerations

### Entry and Exit
The gap is shoulder-width—practice moving through confined spaces. You'll enter backwards (easier to retreat) or forwards (easier to navigate once inside). Either way:

- Empty pockets of anything that could snag
- Secure all equipment to your body
- Have someone aware of where you're going and when to expect you back
- Mark the entrance from inside with a small red light so you can find it again in the darkness

### Documentation
Traditional cameras won't work well here. The flash would be catastrophic, and the darkness defeats most sensors. Instead:

- Bring a small audio recorder in a waterproof case
- A notebook with waterproof paper and pencil
- Consider infrared or thermal imaging equipment if you have access
- Small collection vials if you're scientifically inclined (but remove nothing without careful consideration)

### The Twig Structure
Do not touch or disturb the central twig-and-shell construction. Its presence suggests either another visitor who understood something you don't, or a behavior from the creatures themselves that transcends current biological understanding. Document it, observe how the creatures interact with it, but leave it undisturbed.

## Health and Safety

### Biological Hazards
While nothing here is actively venomous or aggressive, consider:

- Potential allergic reactions to novel proteins or defensive secretions
- Fungal spores that could cause respiratory issues
- Bacterial load from the decomposition processes
- The possibility of picking up parasites or eggs in your clothing

After your visit:
- Shower thoroughly with soap
- Wash all clothing in hot water
- Monitor for unusual rashes or respiratory symptoms
- Consider prophylactic antifungal treatment if you're immunocompromised

### Psychological Aftereffects
Visitors often report:
- Dreams of segmented movement for weeks afterward
- Heightened awareness of insects in everyday life
- Difficulty readjusting to bright lights
- A persistent sensation of things moving in peripheral vision

These effects are temporary but can be unsettling. Have a support system in place.

## What to Bring

### Essential Kit:
- Water in a sealed container
- Energy bars (nothing crumbly that could feed the ecosystem)
- First aid supplies including antihistamine and epinephrine if allergic
- Thermometer to monitor your body temperature
- Emergency whistle (though who would hear it?)
- Plastic bags to seal contaminated items for the journey out

### Optional but Recommended:
- PH strips to test the moisture
- Small magnifying glass with red filter
- Field guides to arthropods and fungi
- Sketch pad for those who process visually
- Sample containers
- Infrared thermometer to map heat signatures

## Behavioral Guidelines

### Movement
Move slowly, deliberately. Quick movements trigger wave responses in the creature population that cascade through the entire chamber. When you must move:

- Telegraph your intentions with slow, preparatory shifts
- Never put your full weight anywhere without testing
- Be prepared for the substrate to compress or give way
- Keep your center of gravity low

### Interaction
The creatures will investigate you. When they do:

- Remain still
- Breathe normally
- Resist the urge to brush them off
- Observe their behavior—they're learning about you as you learn about them

### Observation Protocol
- Spend equal time observing different areas
- Note patterns in movement and spacing
- Look for variations in species or behavior
- Pay attention to the interface zones where root meets soil meets air
- Document the phosphorescence patterns

## Timing Your Visit

The cistern likely has cycles, though their triggers remain unclear. Consider:

- Visiting during different seasons to observe changes
- Noting correlation with rainfall or temperature
- Watching for mass emergence or molting events
- Checking whether city activity above affects behavior below

## Emergency Scenarios

### If You Become Stuck
The gap is unforgiving. If you wedge:
- Don't panic—panic makes you expand
- Exhale completely to reduce your circumference
- Work backward rather than forward
- Have someone who knows to check if you don't return

### If You Disturb the Ecosystem
A massive disturbance could trigger:
- Defensive secretions from the arthropods
- Mass movement that could be crushing in aggregate
- Release of concentrated spores or gases

If this happens:
- Move slowly toward the exit
- Don't try to brush creatures off
- Control your breathing
- Accept that you'll emerge covered and deal with it outside

### If You Experience Psychological Break
The environment can trigger claustrophobia, entomophobia, or dissociative episodes. If reality starts to slip:
- Focus on your breathing
- Count backwards from 100
- Move toward the exit without rushing
- Remember: this is temporary, you will emerge

## After Your Visit

Take time to process. The Undergrowth Cistern isn't just a place—it's an experience that challenges fundamental assumptions about scale, intelligence, and the boundaries between organisms. You may find yourself thinking about it for weeks, seeing patterns in ceiling cracks that remind you of the phosphorescent veins, hearing the city's noise differently knowing what lies beneath.

Write down your observations immediately. Details fade quickly, replaced by the dream-logic that such places inspire. Share your experiences with others who've visited, but expect that each person's cistern is slightly different—the ecosystem responds to and remembers visitors in ways we don't fully understand.

Most importantly, respect what you've witnessed. This pocket universe exists in delicate balance, hidden and protected by its own obscurity. Every visitor changes it slightly. Tread lightly, observe carefully, and leave only footprints in that breathing floor of root and decay.

The cistern waits, patient as the beetles that cycle through their invisible territories, constant as the seepage that feeds this impossible ecology. When you're ready—truly ready—it will receive you into its darkness, and you'll emerge knowing that entire worlds exist in the gaps between the world we think we know.
