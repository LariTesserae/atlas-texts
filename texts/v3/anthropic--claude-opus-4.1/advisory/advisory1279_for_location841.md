## A Traveler's Guide to the Drowned Canopy

### Initial Physical Preparation (Begin 3-4 Months Before Departure)

The human body, remarkable as it is, requires significant adaptation for the Drowned Canopy. Start by spending progressively longer periods in water daily. Begin with hour-long sessions in a pool, gradually extending to 8-10 hours. Practice eating, reading, and performing fine motor tasks while submerged. Your skin needs to develop the particular toughening that comes from constant moisture—the pruning will be severe at first, even painful, but persistence will develop the characteristic grip-enhancing wrinkling the locals possess.

Most critically, you must prepare for the symbiotic algae. No traveler enters the Drowned Canopy without first visiting the Acclimatization Stations in the valleys below. Here, trained attendants will introduce the algae to your system gradually—first through skin contact, then inhalation of water mist, finally full submersion breathing. The process takes two to three weeks minimum. Rush it, and you risk rejection syndrome: violent coughing of black water, feeling like you're drowning even when you can breathe. The algae are benign but alien. Your body must learn to accept them as temporary residents. You'll feel them at first—a tickling in your chest, a coolness when you exhale. Eventually, they become as unnoticeable as your own heartbeat.

Practice swimming, but more importantly, practice walking underwater. The resistance is different from what you expect. Develop your leg muscles with resistance training. Learn to move slowly, deliberately. Quick movements in the Drowned Canopy mark you immediately as an outsider and disturb the water in ways that irritate both residents and fauna.

### Equipment and Clothing

Forget everything you know about conventional travel gear. Fabrics that hold air become buoyant hazards. Cotton kills—not through hypothermia but through drag and weight. The locals wear fitted garments of treated eel-skin or woven algae fiber that moves through water like a second skin. You can purchase approximations in the lower markets, but expect to pay dearly. A basic outfit might cost what you'd normally budget for your entire journey.

Bring nothing that can rust. Even stainless steel degrades in the Canopy's particular blend of minerals and organic acids. Your knives should be ceramic or obsidian. Better yet, purchase tools there—the ironwood implements are superior to anything you'll carry in, though they take time to learn to use properly. Their weight distribution is different, balanced for water resistance rather than air.

Books, papers, anything written—these must be etched on sheets of preserved kelp or thin-scraped bone. Waterproof notebooks exist but are considered gauche, marking you as someone who refuses to adapt. If you must document your journey, learn the local practice of memory-beading: complex knots in specially treated cord that serve as mnemonic devices. A palm-sized bundle can hold a novel's worth of information if you know how to read it.

For sleeping, abandon the idea of a bed or hammock. You'll need to purchase or rent a sleeping harness—a web of soft ropes that holds you gently in place while allowing the natural floating that constitutes rest here. Fighting the float leads to exhaustion. The first few nights will be disorienting as your body expects to sink or rise and instead remains suspended. Many travelers report vivid dreams of flying.

### Cultural Preparation

The social fabric of the Drowned Canopy is as dense as its root-paths. Sound travels differently here—whispers can carry across a market square while shouts die three feet from your mouth. Learn to modulate your voice for water. Practice with your head submerged in a bathtub, having conversations with someone listening underwater nearby. The locals speak in what seems like mumbles until your ear adjusts to the frequency shift. They'll understand your overloud air-voice but will mark you as crude.

Personal space has no meaning when everyone exists in the same medium. You'll brush against strangers constantly—their hair floating into your face, their exhaled mist mingling with yours. The locals have developed an elaborate courtesy around this: eyes averted during close passage, hands held carefully to sides, a subtle full-body language that signals intention and apology simultaneously. Study videos if you can find them, though nothing prepares you for the reality of navigating a crowd that moves in three dimensions.

Time moves differently in the Drowned Canopy—not metaphorically but practically. Without fire, without the sun's clear passage (filtered as it is through water and kelp), schedules follow the biological rhythms of the city itself. The kelp-trees release different pheromones throughout the day, subtle chemical signals that residents instinctively follow. Morning smells green and sharp, noon carries a sweetness like overripe fruit, evening brings a mustiness that makes you drowsy. You won't smell these at first—your nose needs weeks to attune—so you'll be constantly out of sync, arriving for meals when shops are closing, sleeping when the city is most active.

### Dietary Adaptation

Begin eliminating cooked food from your diet two months before arrival. Your gut flora needs time to adjust to constant raw consumption. Start with sashimi, ceviche, fermented vegetables. Graduate to more challenging items: raw mushrooms, unprocessed grains soaked until they sprout, fresh kelp if you can source it. The Drowned Canopy's cuisine is subtle, based on texture and chemical transformation rather than heat. What seems like monotonous cold food reveals itself as complex once your palate adjusts. The fermentation chambers produce flavors that don't exist in dry-land cooking—imagine cheese aged underwater, vegetables pickled in living brine that changes their molecular structure daily.

The water itself is caloric. The algae and microscopic life provide constant, mild nutrition. You'll feel less hungry but never quite full. It's disturbing at first, this sense of being fed by breathing. Some travelers report a euphoria after the first week, others a persistent anxiety about starvation despite adequate nourishment. Trust the process. Your body knows how to extract what it needs.

### Psychological Preparation

The constant sound is what breaks most visitors. Not the volume but the permanence—water moving through kelp, the clicks and hums of fauna, the susurrus of current against architecture. It never stops. Earplugs are useless and insulting. The sound is considered the city's voice, essential to its character. You must learn to let it become background, but this takes tremendous mental discipline. Practice with white noise machines, but know they're inadequate preparation. The real sound has texture, variation within constancy. Some travelers report that leaving the Canopy, returning to the silence of dry air, is more disturbing than entering.

Prepare for the dreams. Everyone dreams differently underwater, and in the Drowned Canopy, you're always underwater. The dreams are vivid, often shared—you may find yourself experiencing fragments of your neighbor's sleep-thoughts, their anxieties bleeding through the medium that connects you. The locals find this unremarkable, even comforting. You might find it invasive. There's no real defense except acceptance.

The isolation can be crushing. Surrounded by life, embedded in a medium that touches every inch of your skin, you've never been more connected physically and more alone psychologically. You can't retreat to a dry space, can't fully close a door against the water, can't ever be truly separate from the city's ecosystem. Some find this liberating. Others panic. Know yourself before you go.

### Practical Matters

Currency is biological—trade in seeds, cuttings, useful organisms. Metal coins sink and are worthless. Paper money dissolves. Bring rarities from the dry world: specific bacteria cultures for fermenting, air-dried fungi that don't grow in water, crystallized tree saps. A thumb-sized piece of amber with an insect inside might buy you a month's lodging. Research what they can't produce themselves.

Medical care exists but follows different principles. Wounds don't heal the same underwater—they don't scab but seal with a kind of organic glue produced by certain fish. Infection is rare but when it happens, spreads quickly through the constantly moist environment. The healers work with living antibiotics—specific creatures that eat infection. It's effective but psychologically challenging to let finger-length fish clean your wounds from the inside.

Communication with the outside world is nearly impossible. No electronics survive, and messages must be carried physically down to the dry lands. Accept that you'll be cut off for the duration of your stay. Many find this the hardest adjustment—harder than breathing water or eating raw food. The isolation is complete.

### The Moment of Entry

When you finally stand at the boundary, where dry air meets the lens of living water, you'll hesitate. Everyone does. The water doesn't look like water from outside—it shimmers, too solid, like looking at the world through old glass. You can see the city beyond, distorted and dream-like, figures moving slowly through green-gold light.

The entry is neither diving nor walking—it's a strange stepping through, like pushing through a vertical pool. The water accepts you gradually. First your hands, then arms, and suddenly you're through, suspended, breathing liquid, your chest cold with new life. The algae activate immediately, and your first exhale in the Drowned Canopy releases a cloud of mist that marks you forever as someone who has traded air for water, if only temporarily.

The locals will watch—they always do—not unkindly but with the patience of those who have seen many travelers struggle with that first transition. Some panic and retreat immediately. Others adapt within hours. Most float somewhere between, neither fully comfortable nor entirely distressed, learning day by day to live in a place that shouldn't exist, breathing what shouldn't be breathable, in a city that has been drowning for so long it's forgotten it was ever dry.

### Final Advice

Go slowly. Go humbly. The Drowned Canopy has rhythms deeper than its roots, older than memory. You're not there to conquer or even fully understand—you're there to experience something genuinely alien while remaining fundamentally human. Your fingernails will grow differently. Your hair will move like kelp. Your voice will carry farther but mean less. You'll leave changed in ways you won't fully understand for years.

Pack light. Pack nothing you can't bear to lose to the water. Bring patience, bring wonder, bring a willingness to breathe the impossible.

The city is waiting, suspended in its ancient equilibrium, ready to fill your lungs with life you've never imagined.
