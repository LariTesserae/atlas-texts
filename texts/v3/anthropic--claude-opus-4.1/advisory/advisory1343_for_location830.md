## Preparation Guide for the Plateau City of Wet Volcanic Stone

### Understanding What You're Getting Into

This place will challenge your relationship with moisture in ways you've never imagined. Forget everything you know about "dry" as a default state—here, wetness is the canvas upon which life is painted. The city exists in a state of negotiated truce with water, and you'll need to adopt that same philosophy from the moment you arrive.

The isolation is complete. No birds, no insects—just the sound of water and the whisper of plant growth. If you need the comfort of animal sounds, the chitter of life as you know it, prepare yourself psychologically for their absence. This silence isn't dead; it's full, but full of things that move too slowly for your ear to catch.

### Physical Preparations

**Footwear is everything.** Bring at least three pairs of shoes that can handle constant moisture without rotting:
- Rubber-soled sandals with good grip for the raised walkways (the volcanic stone is sharp when dry, treacherous when wet)
- Waterproof boots that breathe—Gore-Tex won't be enough here; look for jungle boots with drainage ports
- A pair of soft-soled indoor shoes that can dry quickly for when you're in the elevated buildings

Your feet will never be completely dry. Accept this now. Bring antifungal powder, lots of it. Bring moisture-wicking socks that dry quickly—wool or synthetic, never cotton. Pack twice as many pairs as you think you need. Consider preventatively toughening your feet before you go—walk barefoot on rough surfaces, let them develop calluses.

**Clothing Strategy:**
- Nothing cotton. It will never dry and will rot on your body
- Quick-dry everything: pants that can go from soaked to damp in hours, not days
- Layers, but not for warmth—for moisture management. A wicking base layer, a quick-dry middle layer, and a light rain shell for the mistier moments
- Colors that won't show mildew stains—darker is better
- Bring less than you think; you'll be washing clothes constantly anyway, and they'll never fully dry

**Skin and Health:**
Your skin will either adapt or rebel. Bring:
- Industrial-strength moisturizer (paradoxically, constant dampness can dry your skin)
- Antifungal cream for inevitable issues between toes, in body folds
- Zinc oxide cream for areas that chafe when constantly damp
- Waterproof bandages—regular ones won't last an hour
- Treatment for what locals probably call "moss rash" or "stone scrape"—the volcanic rock is unforgiving

### Technical Gear

**Water Management Equipment:**
- Waterproof bags in multiple sizes—not just for electronics but for anything you want to keep truly dry
- Silica gel packets, lots of them, rechargeable if possible
- A dehumidifying solution for your sleeping space (chemical moisture absorbers)
- Quick-dry towels—bring at least four, they'll be in constant rotation
- A waterproof notebook and pencils (not pens—ink runs)

**Electronics will suffer here.** If you must bring them:
- Triple-bag everything in waterproof cases
- Bring backup batteries; moisture drains power mysteriously
- Consider mechanical alternatives where possible—wind-up clocks, manual tools
- If you bring a camera, it needs to be seriously weatherproofed, and even then, the lens will fog constantly

### Living Arrangements

You'll likely stay in one of those elevated timber structures. Immediately upon arrival:
- Identify where water runs in your space—it will, despite the elevation
- Set up a drying station near whatever heat source exists
- Create a wet zone and a "dry" zone (relatively speaking)
- Learn the building's water song—where it drips, where it pools, where it seeps

Sleep preparation:
- Your bedding will feel damp no matter what. Embrace it or bring a waterproof bivvy sack to sleep in
- Elevate everything off the floor—nothing touches ground level
- Rotate your bedding daily if possible; have a set airing while you use another

### Food and Water

The irony of being surrounded by water is that potable water might be scarce. The seepage is mineral-rich volcanic runoff. Bring:
- Water purification tablets or a serious filter
- Understanding that tea and boiled beverages will be your primary hydration
- An appreciation for taro, bamboo shoots, and whatever grows in bog pockets
- Stomach remedies for adjusting to mineral-heavy water
- Containers that seal completely—open food grows fur overnight

### Navigation and Movement

The walkways between buildings will become your lifelines. Before the mist comes:
- Walk every path in clear conditions, memorizing the feel under your feet
- Count steps between crucial locations
- Note the sound markers—each district has its water signature
- Learn to move slowly, deliberately; rushing on wet volcanic stone is asking for deep cuts
- Always maintain three points of contact on stairs and bridges

When the mist comes (and it will, daily):
- Don't panic at the complete whiteout
- Move by memory and touch, not sight
- Listen for the water channels—they're your map
- Follow the moss (it grows on the north faces, consistently)

### Psychological Preparations

**The Moss Mind**: You'll start noticing moss like never before. Its varieties, its preferences, its slow conquest of surfaces. Some travelers report becoming obsessed with it, spending hours watching it grow. This is normal. Embrace the pace.

**Water Dreams**: Everyone dreams of drowning the first week—not in floods but in slow seepage, water rising through your pores. This passes. Your brain is adjusting to the constant presence of moisture.

**The Quiet**: Without insects or birds, your ears will strain for familiar life sounds. You might start hearing things that aren't there. The rustle of bamboo becomes voices; the drip of water becomes footsteps. Keep a journal to ground yourself.

**Time Distortion**: Without animal routines to mark the day, with mist obscuring the sun, time becomes elastic. Bring a reliable timepiece or learn to read the city's water clock—the regular rhythms of collection and overflow.

### Social Integration

The people here have three generations of water wisdom. Listen more than you speak:
- Learn the vocabulary of water sounds—they communicate through them
- Respect the managed wildness—every plant that looks wild might be someone's cultivation
- Offer to help with water management tasks; it's communal and constant
- Never block a water channel, even accidentally
- Learn to move through spaces without disturbing the moss—it's probably older than you think

**Gift Giving**: Bring things that resist moisture—volcanic glass tools, sealed preserves, synthetic materials that won't rot. Avoid bringing seeds or plants; their botanical balance is delicate.

### Daily Routines You Must Adopt

**Morning**: 
- Check all your gear for overnight moisture damage
- Air out everything that can be aired
- Apply antifungal powder before dressing
- Drink something hot to counter the perpetual clamminess

**Midday**:
- When sun breaks through, immediately expose anything that needs drying
- This is your window for any activity requiring clear sight
- Harvest drinking water from clean sources

**Evening**:
- Secure everything against night moisture
- Set up tomorrow's dry clothes in your protected zone
- Prepare for the evening mist—it's thicker than morning's

**Before Sleep**:
- Final mold check on tomorrow's clothes
- Seal away anything that shouldn't grow fur overnight
- Accept that you'll wake up damp—fighting it only brings misery

### Leaving and Re-integration

When you leave, everything you own will need decontamination:
- Every fabric carries spores
- Your shoes will sprout mushrooms in normal humidity
- Books and papers will need professional drying
- Electronics may never fully recover

More challenging: your body's adaptation. You'll feel parched in normal humidity for weeks. Your skin will crack and peel as it remembers dryness. You'll wake at night missing the sound of dripping water, the particular silence of a place without insects.

### Final Preparations

Bring patience. Bring humor about dampness. Bring respect for a city that has found grace in an impossible place. Bring wonder at moss, at ferns, at the ten thousand ways water can move through stone.

Most importantly, bring the willingness to be changed by wetness, to let it into your life in ways you've always fought against. The city doesn't exist despite the water—it exists because of it, with it, through it. You must do the same.

The volcanic stone remembers its birth in fire, but now it serves water. You, too, will need to remember what you were while becoming something new—something that can thrive where the mountain weeps and the moss conquers and the mist makes islands of every solid thing.

Pack light. Everything you bring will be heavier when wet. And everything, always, will be wet.
