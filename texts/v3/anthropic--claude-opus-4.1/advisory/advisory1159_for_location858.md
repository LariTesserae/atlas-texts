## A Traveler's Guide to the Canopy City

### Initial Mental Preparation

Before you even pack, understand this: you're not visiting a city that happens to be in trees. You're entering a living organism that humans have learned to inhabit. The settlement doesn't *contain* life—it *is* life, and you'll be expected to function as part of it. Abandon any notion of solid ground, permanent structures, or clear boundaries between indoors and outdoors. This place will assault every sense you have, constantly, without respite. If you need silence, stability, or personal space to maintain sanity, reconsider this journey.

### Physical Conditioning

Start training at least three months before departure. Your life will depend on:

**Balance and grip strength**: Practice traversing slack lines, rope bridges, and climbing walls daily. Your hands must develop calluses thick enough to grip wet, moss-slick wood for hours. Install a rope course in your backyard if possible—practice crossing it in rain, carrying weight, without looking down.

**Tolerance for heights**: If you have any vertigo, address it now. You'll be living hundreds of feet up with no safety nets. The bridges sway. The platforms creak. Children will brush past you on branches no wider than your ankle, and showing fear marks you as dangerously incompetent.

**Cardiovascular endurance**: Every movement here is climbing. Markets, neighbors, water sources—all require vertical travel. Train on stairs with a weighted pack until you can climb for an hour without exhaustion.

### Essential Equipment

**Clothing**: Everything will be perpetually damp. Bring:
- Quick-dry synthetic fabrics only (cotton will rot on your body)
- Multiple thin layers rather than single thick ones
- A waterproof cloak made of waxed fabric or rubber—local materials if you can source them
- Shoes with aggressive grip that can handle wet wood, OR plan to go barefoot like locals (feet grip better than most soles on wet bark)
- Wide-brimmed hat that ties under your chin—the dripping never stops

**Climbing gear**:
- Fingerless gloves with grip padding—your hands will blister then bleed otherwise
- A personal rope (30 feet minimum) for emergency anchoring
- Carabiners and a simple harness you can sleep in—many travelers secure themselves at night
- Chalk bag (though chalk becomes paste quickly in the humidity)

**Water management**:
- Water purification tablets—yes, water is everywhere, but it's also where everything else lives
- Covered containers for everything—open vessels become breeding pools
- A personal gutter/funnel system for collecting rainwater directly

**Protection from fauna**:
- Fine mesh netting for sleeping—not just mosquitos but biting flies, spiders, and curious mammals
- All food must be in animal-proof containers (metal or thick treated gourds)
- Ant deterrent (local pepper-based mixtures work best)—they'll colonize your belongings
- Whistle or noise-maker to warn away monkey troops

### Health Preparations

**Vaccinations and medications**:
- Every tropical disease vaccination available
- Antifungal medications (oral and topical)—you will develop fungal infections
- Anti-parasitic medications as prophylaxis
- Antibiotics for inevitable infected cuts
- Anti-diarrheal medications (your gut will revolt against the water)
- Antihistamines for the countless new allergens

**Physical preparations**:
- Let your skin toughen—stop using moisturizers months before
- Grow out your nails slightly and harden them—you'll need them for grip
- Practice eating while standing/crouching—there are few flat surfaces for proper meals
- Adjust to constant moisture—spend time in saunas, steam rooms

### Cultural Adaptation

**Sound tolerance**: The noise never stops. Practice sleeping with recordings of:
- Howler monkeys (imagine someone screaming through a megaphone at 3 AM)
- Tropical rain on leaves
- Thousands of frogs calling
- Parrots shrieking
- Constant dripping water

**Spatial awareness**: The city exists in three dimensions. Learn to think vertically:
- "Addresses" reference tree species, height, and compass direction
- Social status often correlates with height (higher is typically better—less insects, better air)
- Understand that pathways change—storms, growth, and rot constantly alter routes

**Privacy expectations**: Forget it exists. You'll hear, smell, and glimpse everything your neighbors do. They'll know when you eat, sleep, defecate, argue, or make love. Adapt to performing all human functions with an audience or go mad.

### Practical Skills to Learn

**Basic ropework**: Learn to tie:
- Bowlines, clove hitches, prusiks
- How to splice rope and weave vine
- Emergency bridge repairs

**Tree reading**: Study how to identify:
- Dead branches (stepping on one means falling)
- Weak crotches vs. strong joints
- Which vines bear weight vs. which are decoration

**Waste management**: Understand you'll be defecating into designated hollow trunks or containers that must be lowered for composting. There's an etiquette—learn it.

### Psychological Preparation

**Claustrophobia and agoraphobia simultaneously**: The mist limits vision to sometimes just feet, creating a closed feeling, while the knowledge of the drop below creates vertigo. Practice existing in liminal spaces.

**Constant vigilance**: Your brain must track:
- Every footstep and handhold
- Wildlife movements (that shadow might be a harmless sloth or a hunting snake)
- Weather changes (storms can be deadly here)
- Bridge and platform integrity

**Sensory overload management**: Develop filtering techniques for:
- The overwhelming smells (rot, flowers, waste, food, mold, animals)
- The cacophony (try meditation in the noisiest environment you can find)
- The constant tactile sensations (everything is wet, sticky, or moving)

### Social Integration

**Market etiquette**: Goods are hauled up in communal basket systems. Understand the sharing economy—hoarding is both impossible (where would you store it?) and socially catastrophic.

**Movement etiquette**: Like highway rules but three-dimensional:
- Children have right-of-way (they're faster and more sure-footed)
- Those carrying loads have priority on bridges
- Learn the whistle/call signals for "coming through," "bridge failing," "dangerous animal"

**Community work**: You'll be expected to contribute to:
- Bridge maintenance
- Platform repairs
- Vine training
- Pest control
- Water system management

### What to Expect Daily

**Mornings**: Wake to howler monkeys and rain. Everything you own is damp. Check shoes for insects, snakes, or frogs before putting them on. Breakfast is probably fruit and something starchy, eaten standing while rain drips through the floor above.

**Midday**: The rain intensifies. Movement slows but never stops. Markets are crowded, dangerous—imagine haggling while balanced on a wet beam with parrots screaming overhead and someone's cooking fire smoking below.

**Evenings**: The bats emerge in clouds so thick they darken the sky. Insects swarm toward any light. Most people retreat behind curtains, but the sounds continue—domesticity mixed with wilderness.

**Nights**: Secure yourself to something solid. The darkness is absolute under the canopy. Things hunt. Branches can fail. People do fall.

### Final Warnings

- Your first week, you'll constantly fight the urge to grab something for support, only to find it's a moss-slick surface or a vine that's not actually attached
- You'll develop a persistent cough from the humidity and spores
- Your belongings will mold unless actively maintained
- Insects will inhabit your clothes, gear, and possibly your skin
- The beauty—when the butterflies migrate through, when the morning light filters through the mist, when the flowers bloom in cascades—will make you forget the discomfort, until it doesn't

This place will change you. You'll develop muscles you didn't know existed. Your proprioception will sharpen until you can navigate in near-darkness by branch-flex alone. You'll learn to interpret the city's breathing—when the trees groan with too much weight, when the rain patterns signal a serious storm, when the sudden silence means a predator is near.

Pack light. Everything you bring will rot, rust, or be colonized. Bring patience. Bring humility. Bring a willingness to become part of something rather than just observe it.

The canopy city doesn't accommodate visitors—it absorbs them, transforms them, or spits them out broken on the forest floor far below.
