## Preparation Guide for the Cathedral of Ascending Waters

### Respiratory and Pressure Adaptation

Your lungs will rebel against this place. Begin training three weeks before arrival by breathing through wet cloth for increasing periods—start with five minutes, work up to two hours. The air here doesn't behave like air; it's a suspension of water so dense your body won't know whether to breathe or swim. 

Purchase a transitional respirator—not a full diving apparatus, but the hybrid models used by deep-mine workers. You'll need the kind with moisture-permeable membranes that can process both air and water-heavy atmospheres. Practice breathing with resistance bands around your chest to simulate the pressure. Your ears will need conditioning too; spend time at varying pressures in hyperbaric chambers if accessible, or at minimum practice aggressive equalization techniques.

### Clothing and Skin Protection

Forget conventional waterproofs—they'll trap the moisture against your skin and accelerate fungal growth. You need gradient-permeable fabrics, the kind developed for tropical cave exploration. Think of clothing as a second skin that must breathe while wet. Copper-thread mesh undergarments are essential; the local flora has an affinity for human skin oils, and the copper discourages colonization.

Your outer layer should be a bio-neutral synthetic that won't provide purchase for the mosses and lichens. They're not dangerous, but you don't want to leave covered in hitchhikers that will die painfully when you return to normal atmosphere. Bring three complete sets—you'll need to rotate them every eight hours as they saturate.

Apply thick silicone-based barrier cream to all exposed skin, refreshing every four hours. The mineral-rich water will leach oils from your skin otherwise, leaving you vulnerable to the crystalline fungi that feed on electromagnetic fields. Yes, you generate enough bioelectricity to attract them.

### Navigation and Orientation

GPS doesn't work here—the water-saturated air bends signals unpredictably. Bring analog instruments: a mechanical altimeter (though "altitude" here is relative), a physical compass (the industrial electromagnetics make digital ones useless), and most importantly, spools of industrial marking thread.

The Cathedral reorganizes itself. Not quickly, but a passage that existed in the morning might be overgrown by evening. The thread isn't just for finding your way back—it's for confirming that your way back still exists. Use the type with phosphorescent coating; the green-gold light filtering through the aerial waters makes standard colors indistinguishable.

Learn to read the machinery like terrain. The oldest processors near the base have characteristic copper-green oxidation patterns. Newer installations still show manufacturer stamps. The moss grows in predictable patterns based on heat output—thicker near active components, more delicate around dormant systems. These become your landmarks.

### Sustenance and Hydration

The irony of being surrounded by water you cannot drink. Every drop here is processed, mineralized, claimed by either machine or organism. Bring sealed water in metallic containers—plastic degrades in the electromagnetic fields. You'll need twice your normal intake; the humidity tricks your body into thinking it's hydrated while actually accelerating moisture loss through your skin.

For food, dense nutrient bars that won't absorb atmospheric moisture. Nothing with exposed surfaces—bread becomes sponge within minutes, dried fruit rehydrates into mush. The local vegetation is theoretically edible but tastes of copper and electricity, and more importantly, it's all part of the Cathedral's digestive system. Eating it makes you part of that system in ways that become uncomfortable when you try to leave.

### Movement and Physical Preparation

Train on industrial scaffolding if possible, preferably in a greenhouse. The Cathedral's walkways are grated metal slick with condensation and biofilm. Every surface grows something. Your feet need to learn the difference between structural moss (safe to step on) and processor algae (slippery as ice).

Practice moving through confined spaces while wet. The maintenance shafts you'll navigate weren't designed for humans—they were designed for water flow, and humans are an afterthought. You'll squeeze through gaps between heat exchangers while condensation drips down your neck, climb ladders where every rung hosts its own microbiome.

Build grip strength specifically—you'll be pulling yourself up water-slicked rungs, pushing through curtains of tropical vines that have the weight of wet carpet. The convection currents create zones where moving feels like swimming upstream through air.

### Temporal and Psychological Preparation

Time moves differently in the Cathedral. Without clear day/night cycles, with machinery that operates on monthly rotations, your circadian rhythms will collapse. Bring a mechanical watch—digital ones fail in the electromagnetic environment. Set alarms every four hours to maintain some temporal structure.

The sound environment will disturb you more than you expect. It's not loud, but it's wrong—industrial noises muffled as if underwater, the constant subliminal hum of acoustic resonators, the fizz of photosynthesis amplified by the density of growing things. Some travelers report that the Cathedral "breathes" in a way that synchronizes with human respiration, creating a deeply unsettling sense of being inside something alive.

Prepare for the green. Not just visually, but psychologically. The color saturates everything—your vision, your clothes, eventually your skin if you stay long enough. The gold-green light filtering through suspended water creates a perpetual twilight that makes shadows behave strangely. Depth perception suffers. Bring amber-tinted goggles to break the monotony.

### Emergency Protocols

If you become disoriented, find the nearest heat source and wait. The convection currents always flow upward, and following warm air will eventually lead you to newer sections with clearer passages. Never follow cold air—it leads to the oldest parts of the Cathedral where the distinction between machine and organism has been completely erased.

If the moss begins growing on your clothing while you're wearing it, you've been still too long. Move immediately, even if just to shift position. The Cathedral's flora doesn't distinguish between a dormant pipe and a dormant person.

Should you hear the resonators change pitch—a deep thrumming that you feel more than hear—find high ground immediately. This signals a redistribution cycle where the suspended water temporarily takes liquid form and cascades through the structure. It's not dangerous if you're prepared, but you don't want to be caught in a maintenance shaft when tons of water suddenly remember gravity.

### Integration Protocol

The Cathedral doesn't receive visitors; it absorbs them. The longer you stay, the more you become part of its respiratory cycle. Your movements create currents that affect moss growth. Your breath adds to the humidity that feeds the lichens. Your electromagnetic field attracts the crystalline fungi.

This is neither threatening nor benign—it simply is. The Cathedral treats everything as a component to be integrated. Maintain movement, maintain awareness, maintain your boundaries. The travelers who struggle are those who fight against the integration or surrender to it completely. 

Walk the middle path: acknowledge that while you're here, you're part of the Cathedral's ecology, but remember you're a temporary component, designed to leave.

### Final Notes

The Cathedral of Ascending Waters isn't hostile, but it's utterly indifferent to human comfort or survival. It exists in a state of perpetual growth and decay, construction and dissolution. Every surface is temporary, every passage provisional.

You'll leave soaked through despite never swimming, exhausted despite minimal exertion, and carrying the taste of copper and electricity for weeks afterward. Your clothes will smell of industrial moss no matter how many times you wash them. You might find tiny crystalline fungi growing from your electronics months later, feeding on residual electromagnetic fields.

But you'll also have walked through architecture that breathes, climbed towers of living water, and existed briefly in a space where the industrial and biological have merged so completely that the distinction no longer matters.

The Cathedral doesn't care if you come or go. It will continue its slow breathing, its patient growth, its endless cycling of water through states that shouldn't exist. Your presence there is less than a drop in its vast, vertical sea.

Prepare accordingly.
