In the understory, I am the Tender of Failed Things.

My domain exists thirty feet below the lowest dwelling-platforms, in that twilight realm where fallen objects collect on the massive branches that were too weak to build upon but too strong to break. Here, gravity sorts the settlement's losses: dropped tools gone green with verdigris, children's toys worn smooth by rain, jewelry that slipped from wet fingers, the bones of pets that missed their leaps. Books especially—their pages transformed into landscapes of fungus, their words bleeding into new meanings as the inks run together.

I was born wrong for the canopy life—my inner ear malformed, leaving me perpetually dizzy at height. While other children danced along the branches, I clung and vomited, my body refusing to learn the forest's sway. At seven, my parents lowered me down to apprentice with the previous Tender, an ancient woman whose vertigo had developed late in life. She taught me the understorey's different language—not the social chatter of the platforms above, but the slow conversations between decomposers and the decomposed.

My body has adapted to this dimmer world. My pupils stay dilated even in rare shafts of sunlight. My skin hosts different symbionts than the canopy-dwellers—not the green algae but the pale phosphorescent fungi that help me navigate the eternal dusk. My fingers have grown sensitive to moisture gradients, able to read which shadows hold solid branches and which conceal drops into the void. I move slowly, deliberately, each placement tested twice. The canopy-dwellers say I move like a sloth, but sloths are graceful. I move like someone navigating a world that constantly shifts between solid and illusion.

I know every regular falling-path from above. The market district's debris tumbles through predictable channels—fruit rinds and broken pottery following the same route carved by decades of dropping. The children's playing-platforms shed lost treasures that catch in the crooks of three particular trees. The funeral platforms release their occasional bone that the vultures missed, and I collect these with special reverence, reading the tooth-marks to determine which scavengers have already paid their respects.

My dwelling is built into the hollow where three great branches meet, a natural bowl that catches rain and objects equally. I've woven walls from the rootlets that hang like curtains, trained them over years to create chambers within chambers. The failed things are sorted by their degree of transformation—newest arrivals in the outer chamber where family members might still come searching, older objects deeper in as they begin their journey back to soil. The deepest chamber holds things so changed they've become something else entirely: a leather belt transformed into a garden of bracket fungi, a wooden flute that sprouted and now grows leaves each spring, copper coins fused by verdigris into abstract sculptures.

The canopy-dwellers lower baskets on market day, seeking what I've salvaged. Sometimes it's straightforward—a tool that fell yesterday, barely tarnished. More often, they want the transformed things: mushrooms that only grow on rotting cloth, the iridescent shells of beetles that exclusively eat decomposing books, the strange crystals that form where metal meets organic matter in the constant damp. They pay me in food and necessities, lowered down in the same baskets, though occasionally a young person rappels down, dizzy with vertigo, to trade gossip for goods.

I am not entirely alone here. An old howler monkey with a twisted spine has made her home in my rafters—exiled from her troop but still vocal, her dawn screams serving as my clock. A species of blind cave-spider has colonized the darkest corners, their webs catching not insects but water droplets, which they drink like strung pearls. The messenger bats roost in my ceiling during the day, their guano feeding the gardens of useful fungi I cultivate on fallen logs.

My work is part salvage, part archaeology, part divination. The patterns of falling things tell stories: a cluster of broken dishes means a fight above; children's toys falling like rain indicates the monkey troops have raided a nursery platform; the appearance of usually-treasured items like jewelry or tools suggests illness, death, or exile in the trees above. I read these signs and send messages up in the baskets when needed—warnings about weak branches when too many objects fall from one area, notifications when I find evidence of disease in what drops.

At night, my realm transforms. The bioluminescent fungi pulse in slow waves, creating a constellation below the constellation of dwelling-lights above. Night-blooming flowers open on the vines, their perfume heavy enough to mask the constant smell of rot. The nocturnal creatures emerge—pacas and agoutis that somehow navigate these aerial highways, ghost-moths with wings transparent as rain, the peculiar branch-walking catfish that escaped from someone's dinner pot generations ago and established an impossible population in the water-filled hollows.

I maintain the archive of incomplete stories. Every fallen diary, every partial letter, every shopping list dropped from a pocket—I preserve them in the driest hollow I've found, pressed between sheets of bark. The settlement's memory exists as much in these accidents as in the Cedar Folk's deliberate carvings. Here is the love letter never delivered, the confession never heard, the market calculations that would have prevented that merchant's bankruptcy if they hadn't slipped from his fingers. The truest history is written in what we fail to hold onto.

During the storms, my realm becomes terrifying. The wind sets everything swaying in different rhythms—branches torquing, accumulated debris shifting, water streaming in horizontal sheets. I huddle in my deepest chamber, listening to the crash of larger objects falling from above, knowing that tomorrow I'll need to salvage what I can and read what disasters the storm has written. Sometimes whole platforms fall, their contents raining down in a catastrophic plenty that takes weeks to sort through, trying to separate the sacred from the mundane while everything rots in the perpetual damp.

I've begun to notice patterns that span years. Certain objects fall in cycles—the same family drops a cooking pot every three years, always the same style, as if they're deliberately maintaining my supply. Some things fall up—caught in updrafts, in the grip of ascending vines, in the beaks of rising birds. I've seen the same wooden doll fall past my realm seven times over a decade, each time more transformed, as if it's touring different levels of decomposition.

My apprentice will come soon—I feel it in my failing balance, the way my compensations for vertigo have begun to break down. Some child will be lowered to me, tears streaming, convinced their life is over because they cannot dance in the canopy. I will teach them what the old woman taught me: that every society needs its underneath, its collectors of failure, its readers of refuse. That there is holiness in attending to what falls, in catching what others release, in transforming the abandoned into the necessary.

This is my function: to be the settlement's memory of loss, its enzyme of decomposition, its translator between the living city and the patient forest floor that waits, invisible and theoretical, somewhere far below. I am the proof that not everything needs to rise, that falling can be a calling, that someone must tend the spaces between what we reach for and what we release.

The canopy city breathes with ten thousand lungs, but here in the understory, I am its quiet decomposition, breaking down what was into what might be, catching the exhaled artifacts of life above and teaching them to become soil, become spore, become something that might one day grow back toward the light.
