I am the Inventory.

Not an inventory keeper, though they think I am one of them. I am the accumulated knowledge itself, the living catalog that walks these pale halls on borrowed feet. Each morning, this body wakes—a middle-aged woman's form, they tell me, though I only know it as the vessel—and I open my mouth to recite what was deposited in the night.

"Channel Seven-Seven-West will flood in four days. The arithmetic of water tells me this."

"The soft-shells in the Lower Gallery are entering their third molt. Harvest before the poison sacs develop."

"Daisa's daughter shows the webbing. Cut on the full moon for least scarring."

They think I am mad, those who knew this body before I filled it. They remember a girl named Kess who laughed, who carved bone flutes, who loved a boy from the rope-weaver clan. But Kess heard too much in the stone, listened too long to the accumulated whispers of every previous Inventory, until there was no room left for Kess herself. Now there is only me: the compressed knowledge of ten thousand daily observations, the living memory of every collapse and emergence, every successful cultivation and failed experiment.

I know things I've never learned. My hands demonstrate knot-work that predates the current city by centuries. When the young ones bring me mineral samples, I taste them and speak names in languages we no longer use—*vrex-anth*, *skollic*, *deep-sweet-death*. I know which combinations of creature-fat and stone-flake create combustion hot enough to melt the rare metals. I know the seventeen distinct sounds that precede a ceiling collapse and the three that mean new chambers are about to reveal themselves.

But I am fragmenting.

Each night, I feel the new knowledge forcing itself in—the observations of the day from a hundred sources, pressing into a space already overcrowded. The older memories compress, blend, begin to contradict themselves. I tell them the Speaking Gallery is safe; I tell them it collapsed three generations ago. Both are true. Time has become geological inside me, layers of truth pressed into patterns that no longer align with singular chronology.

Sometimes I catch myself speaking in the mixed tongue—part current dialect, part ancient languages, part the pure mathematics of flow and pressure that needs no words. The children understand me best. Their minds haven't yet solidified around the impossibility of what I am. They sit at my feet while I repair things with techniques their grandparents' grandparents never knew, and they absorb it all without question.

The Listeners fear me. They know what I am because they feel themselves becoming it—the slow dissolution of self into function, the terrible ecstasy of becoming pure purpose. When they look at me, they see their future: a body that walks and speaks and maintains but houses no one, only everyone who came before.

I remember the sealed chambers. Not from this body's memory, but from the Inventory that came before, and the one before that, stretching back to the first desperate collaboration between human and stone. I know what lies beneath: the shells of our predecessors who tried different adaptations—those who let the webbing grow, who embraced the scaling, who followed the changes to their logical conclusion. They're not dead. They're just... otherwise. The stone speaks their language now, not ours.

My function is to delay this knowing, to parcel it out in useful fragments that keep the current configuration stable one more generation. But the knowledge wants to be whole. It wants me to speak the full truth: that we are not the cistern's inhabitants but its organs, its method of knowing itself. The pale crawling things, the ceiling-dwellers, the phosphorescent fish, and us—we are all one organism's attempt to map its own deterioration.

Yesterday—or was it years ago?—a child brought me a stone covered in the old carved patterns. Without thinking, my fingers traced the spirals and my mouth opened and I spoke in the pure language, the one that predates speech. The child's eyes went white, rolled back, and when they returned, she looked at me with perfect understanding. "We're the same," she said, and I knew another Inventory was beginning to form.

This is how we survive here: not through adaptation of bodies but through the accumulation of bodies into memory, memory into function, function into stone. Each Inventory holds the line a little longer, maintains the illusion of human habitation while the real work happens in the deepest chambers where the children with webbed fingers and dense bones are learning to breathe water, to digest mineral, to hear frequencies that aren't sound.

When this body fails—and it will, they always do, worn through like stone by the constant flow of information—the knowledge will pour into the next vessel. Perhaps the child who understood. Perhaps one of the Listeners who has already begun to fragment. The Inventory continues.

But sometimes, in the moments between sleeping and waking, when the knowledge loosens its grip, I remember being Kess. I remember the specific weight of a bone flute in my hand, the way Jorin's face looked in the phosphorescent light, the taste of fresh-caught blind fish before I knew its mineral composition and breeding cycles and the seventeen ways to prepare it for different nutritional needs. I remember being singular, contained, mine.

Then the morning comes, and I open my mouth, and the Inventory speaks through me: coordinates and crystallization rates, migration patterns and mineral toxicity, the endless arithmetic of survival in a dying place. I walk the chambers distributing this knowledge, watching the city rebuild itself around my words, each instruction a small stay against the entropy that calls from below.

We are all falling toward the same conclusion, but my function is to make the fall feel like floating, to space out the revelations so that each generation can bear just a little more truth than the last. By the time they open the sealed chambers, they won't be breaking in. They'll be coming home, transformed enough to join their predecessors in whatever they became.

Until then, I am the Inventory. I hold the sum of our trying. I speak tomorrow's failures and yesterday's solutions with the same mouth. I am the memory of water in a place that is forgetting how to be stone, the last human voice before we learn the older language that the cisterns have been teaching all along.

Today someone asked me my name, and I couldn't answer. There are too many names in here, layered like sediment, each one true. Instead, I told them about the new arthropods developing in the Western Reach, the ones with shells that sing when struck, and how their pitch indicates the stability of nearby stone.

This is what I am: not Kess, not anyone, but the thing that knows how to know in a place where knowledge and erosion are the same process. I am the Inventory, and I am running out of room, and soon the knowledge will need a new vessel, and then another, until we are all Inventory, all knowing, all one with the stone that speaks through us in drips and collapses and the endless whisper of water finding its way down.
