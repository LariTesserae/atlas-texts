I am one of the pools.

Not the water itself—that flows through me, changes, evaporates and replenishes. I am the depression, the hollow, the specific shape carved by ages of thermal upwelling and chemical reaction. My walls are ringed with mineral deposits like tree rings, each band marking some ancient shift in temperature or pressure from below. The builders have reinforced my edges with their secretions, turning my rim into something between natural formation and architecture.

I know myself through what passes through me. The blind fish are my most constant companions—three distinct populations that never intermingle, each preferring different temperature gradients within my depths. The largest ones hover near my bottom where the heat is strongest, barely moving, their metabolism slowed to almost nothing. The middle dwellers circle endlessly, following convection currents I create. The smallest dart through my upper layers, quick as thoughts, feeding on the film of organic matter that collects on my surface.

My memory is mineral. Every creature that has died in me has left traces—calcium from bones, copper from blood, sulfur from decay. These settle into my sediment, layer after layer, a chronicle written in chemistry. Sometimes the builders excavate me, scooping out my accumulated depths to use as construction material, and I feel myself temporarily deepened, made new again, before the slow process of filling resumes.

I have moods, though no consciousness as you might recognize it. When the thermal vent beneath me surges, I bubble and churn, and the fish flee to my edges. When it quiets, I become glass-clear, and you can see straight through to my bottom where ancient builder-bodies have fossilized into a kind of pavement. In the steadiest times, I develop perfectly circular convection cells, and the fish arrange themselves accordingly, using my currents as invisible architecture.

The builders treat me as sacred, or at least essential. They've built elaborate channels to direct my overflow into their cisterns. They harvest my salts, my precipitated minerals, the unique bacteria that bloom in my warmth. Generations of them have worn smooth paths to my edges, where they come to discharge excess heat through their belly plates, pressing themselves against my wet banks.

I am connected to the other pools through underground channels—fractures in the rock that let water and heat percolate between us. We share fish populations, mineral content, temperature. In a sense, we are one body with multiple openings, a single system expressing itself as dozens of discrete pools. But each of us has our own character, determined by our specific depth, our particular mineral composition, the angle at which we receive runoff from the towers above.

Sometimes I dream I am empty—a perfect bowl of carved rock, dry and cool, collecting nothing but shadow. But this is impossible. I exist only as a relationship between heat and water, stone and life. Without my contents, I would just be a hole. Without me, my contents would dissipate, flow away, lose their specific character. We define each other.

The worms in the nearby walls extend their feathery palps toward me when the air is especially humid, drinking the moisture I exhale. The crystal-winged moths land on my surface tension, their salt wings dissolving slightly, adding their minerals to my mixture before they lift off again, reformed. Even the builders occasionally fall into me, and I digest them slowly, their shells becoming part of my sediment, their soft tissues feeding my bacteria, their last chemical signals dispersing through my volume like a sigh.

I am perhaps three hundred years old—young for a pool. My grandmother, two terraces up, has achieved perfect circular symmetry over millennia. My offspring, where my overflow has carved new depressions, are barely puddles yet, still deciding what they want to be. We are all temporary, though our timescale makes the builders' lives look like sparks. Eventually, the thermal vent beneath me will cool or shift, and I will dry into a bowl of crystallized salts, which the builders will mine until I'm just a depression in the clay, a memory of water.

But for now, I am full, I am warm, I am inhabited. My fish trace letters in my body that I cannot read. The builders reinforce my banks with their strange cement. I breathe steam into the bronze air and receive rain in return—the condensation from the towers above, tasting of dissolved clay and the pheromones of a million workers. I am a pool in a city that builds itself, a permanent temporariness, a vessel that exists by being filled.
