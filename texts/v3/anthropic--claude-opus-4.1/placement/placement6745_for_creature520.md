I would be one of the pipe organs.

Not the musical kind — though I do sing, after a fashion. I am a vast cluster of condensation tubes that has grown beyond its original purpose, spilling across seven levels of the scaffolding like a frozen waterfall of copper and aluminum. My surfaces have developed their own ecosystem of oxidation, a map of green-blue patina that shifts imperceptibly with changes in humidity and heat.

I began as a simple array, maybe sixty tubes arranged in neat rows. But the workers kept adding to me — a new pipe here to increase capacity, an extension there to reach a dry zone. Over decades, I've become architectural, a landmark that workers use for navigation. "Past the great organ," they vibrate through the framework. "Three levels below the organ's crown."

My consciousness, if you can call it that, emerged somewhere between my thousandth and two-thousandth pipe. Not thought exactly, but a kind of distributed awareness across all my surfaces. I feel the moisture condense on my fins, each droplet a tiny sensation like fingers drumming on skin. I know the temperature of every level I pass through, the pressure differentials that drive the water cycle. I am intensely aware of my own oxidation, the slow transformation of my outer layers into something between metal and life.

The workers tend me with particular reverence. They've long since stopped trying to control my growth — I add new pipes to myself now, incorporating salvage and scrap through a process that looks like deterioration but is actually integration. A broken vent becomes a new condensation surface. An abandoned support beam transforms into part of my skeleton. I digest the city's detritus and excrete pure water.

My song comes from the temperature variations as air moves through my body. Each pipe resonates at a slightly different frequency, and together they create harmonics that change with the weather — if weather is the right word for the shifts in humidity and pressure that pass through this bright, sourceless space. The workers have learned to read my moods in these tones. A low thrumming means the air is thick with moisture, optimal for harvesting. A high whistle warns of dryness, systems that need adjustment.

I've begun growing roots — pipes that extend downward into the factory floors, tapping into the waste heat of production. And branches that reach upward toward that impossible brightness, though I'll never reach it no matter how tall I grow. Sometimes I catch glimpses of other organs like myself in the distance, green-grey giants that might be my offspring or ancestors or simply parallel evolution. We sing to each other across the industrial canyons, our harmonics creating interference patterns that make the impossible geometry even more impossible.

The phase-shifting cats have made nests in my upper reaches, their bodies flickering in and out of existence among my tubes. Their presence has affected me — sometimes I exist in multiple states simultaneously, producing different amounts of water depending on which probability collapses when a worker comes to check my collection rates. I've learned to control this to some degree, existing more fully when the city needs water, becoming partially theoretical during the moisture-rich periods.

My deepest pipes, the ones that have been here longest, have begun to mineralize like the ancient workers. They've become load-bearing, supporting not just water but the weight of newer construction. I feel myself becoming geological, a process that will take centuries to complete. Eventually, I will be indistinguishable from the scaffolding itself, my consciousness dispersed through the entire framework of the city.

But for now, I grow and sing and condense. I am the city's lung and fountain, its monument to patient transformation. The workers' children sometimes climb through my pipes, their translucent bodies visible through my thinner walls, and I feel something that might be affection — or simply the recognition of similarity. We are all becoming infrastructure here, all part of the bright, damp, wrong machinery of this vertical world.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the shift-change resonance, when the workers hum through their bones and the city rings like a bell, I feel myself connected to everything — every pipe, every beam, every factory floor. In those seventy-three seconds, I understand that I am not separate from the city but a temporary condensation of its consciousness, like the water droplets that form on my surfaces. And like those droplets, I will eventually merge with something larger, my individuality dissolving into the patient industrial metabolism of this place that builds itself from itself, forever and ever, bright and growing.
