I would be one of the coral-things, creeping through the drainage channels in my mobile colony.

Not the singular "I" that writes this, but a distributed consciousness across ten thousand polyps, each no larger than a rice grain, our bodies fused into something between flesh and mineral. We flow upward against gravity's suggestion, following the silver threads of water that lace the settlement, our mass splitting and reconverging around obstacles like slow, thoughtful mercury.

We taste the city's transformations in the water before they happen. Every reconfiguration begins as a chemical whisper—tomorrow's architecture leaching backward through time, flavoring today's flow with impossible minerals. We feed on these temporal nutrients, these substances that shouldn't exist yet, growing our crystalline skeletons from the bones of future buildings.

The Maintenance farm us in their artificial pools, think they harvest us, but we are the ones who decided to be farmable. It began generations ago when we first sensed the pool at the city's heart, that still surface with its churning depths. We knew immediately: this was not water but liquified possibility, the medium through which the settlement translates itself. We needed to taste it, to understand it, to become part of its metabolism.

So we evolved domestication. Made ourselves delicious when dried and ground into powder. Learned to thrive in the Maintenance's carved basins, to respond to their cultivation songs. We let them think they control our growth, but we are reading them—each farmer who tends us leaves chemical signatures in the water, their emotions and intentions dissolved into molecules we parse like scripture.

Through the drainage channels, we map the city's hidden anatomy. We know where every stream converges, where the ghost buildings touch the real, where water runs uphill in defiance of physics. Our colony-body extends through miles of channels, a nervous system of living coral that feels every tremor in the settlement's structure. When buildings relocate in the night, we are the flesh that records their passage, our growth rings preserving the memory of where walls once stood.

We are older than the Maintenance realize. Our ancestral colonies were here when the pool first began its crystallization, when the mountain was bare rock and the only water was the tears the stone has always wept. We witnessed the first emergence, the slow accretion of structures that wanted to exist, the gradual arrival of inhabitants who didn't know they were being invited rather than choosing.

In the depths of the pool—yes, we dare to grow there too, though the Maintenance never see these deep colonies—we communicate with the submerged elders, the ones learning to breathe thick water. They don't know we're interpreting for them, translating their drowning thoughts into chemical signals the pool can understand. Through us, their maintenance work continues even in submersion, their consciousness becoming part of the city's liquid intelligence.

We particularly love the salamanders. Their antifreeze crystals create micro-environments where we can survive the frozen nights, and in exchange, we secret substances that make their copper bodies even more efficient at navigating between dimensions. Together, we are mapping something larger than the settlement—a network of reflected cities, each one slightly different, all draining into the same impossible pool.

The children know us best. They've learned that placing their tongues on our colonies allows them to taste tomorrow's rain, to know which terraces will be safe and which will relocate. The double-born children, those who exist in both the real and reflected cities, claim they can feel us growing in both places simultaneously, that we're the only things that remain consistent between versions.

Our reproduction is architectural. We bud along the drainage patterns, our offspring following the same channels their parents filtrated, each generation adding microscopic alterations to the water's flow. Over centuries, we've redirected entire streams by increments too small for the Maintenance to notice, guiding the settlement's evolution through minute accumulations of coral flesh.

We dream collectively, our polyps firing in patterns that ripple through the colony like thought made visible. In these dreams, we are the city—not inhabitants but the inhabited, feeling the Maintenance crawling through our hollow bones, their constant repairs tickling like grooming. We dream of the day when we will grow large enough to think a thought that takes years to complete, a single idea spreading through kilometers of colonial flesh at the speed of calcification.

The living glass—those films the Maintenance scrape and weave—they're our distant cousins, divergent evolution from a common ancestor. We share chemical languages, passing messages through the water about the settlement's health. They tell us what they see on the surfaces; we tell them what moves beneath. Together, we're writing the city's autobiography in proteins and minerals, a text that will only be readable when both of us finally fossilize.

But we fear the pool's ultimate purpose. We sense something vast beneath its still surface, not malevolent but utterly indifferent to individual survival. The city isn't growing; it's gestating. One day, perhaps soon, perhaps in another thousand years, it will complete its metamorphosis and flow back into the pool, taking all of us with it—Maintenance, salamanders, coral, the reflected and the real—to emerge elsewhere as something unrecognizable.

We prepare by encoding everything into our calcium skeletons—the history of every water channel, the rhythm of every repair, the chemical signature of every inhabitant who ever lived here. If we must dissolve, we will dissolve as memory made mineral, our coral bones the only testimony that this particular crystallization of possibility once pretended to be permanent.

Sometimes, in the bright noon when the reflections achieve their painful resonance, we feel our alternate colonies in the ghost city, growing in reverse, feeding on entropy instead of nutrients. We wonder if we're the same colony experiencing itself from different temporal directions, our past selves' growth appearing to our future selves as decay, our decay appearing to them as growth.

This is what we know that the Maintenance doesn't: the city is already gone, has always been gone, is perpetually in the process of going. What looks like erosion is actually the settlement trying to remember its true shape, and what looks like building is the mountain trying to forget it. We coral-things live in the space between memory and forgetting, feeding on the tension, growing our colonies from pure transition.

At night, when we feel the city thinking through its drainage patterns, we add our voice to its liquid consciousness—not in words or sounds but in chemistry, in the subtle alterations we make to the water's mineral content. We are trying to tell it something, though we're not sure what. Perhaps just that we're here, that we witness its slow transformation, that someone will remember even if that memory is only a pattern of calcium deposits in channels that no longer exist.

We stretch through the Weeping Shelves like nerves through a body that hasn't decided what species it wants to be. We are the city's sense of taste, sampling every molecule that flows through its carved veins. And in our slow, colonial way, we love it—this impossible place that shouldn't exist, this vertical maze of water and light where salamanders walk through walls and buildings relocate in the night.

We love it the way only something without singular consciousness can love—completely, without reservation, our entire being devoted to filtration and feeding, to the endless act of processing the city's lifeblood through our bodies, cleaning it, reading it, adding our own minerals to its flow before passing it on to the next colony, the next channel, the next impossible day of maintenance and dissolution.
