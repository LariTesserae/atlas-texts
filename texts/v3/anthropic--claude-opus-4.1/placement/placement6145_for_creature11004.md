I would be one of the **Interval Weavers**—creatures that exist in the gaps between seconds, living in the moments the plateau's time-stutters create but never quite fills.

My body would be a framework of crystallized pause—the physical manifestation of hesitation itself. Imagine a spider made from the space between clock ticks, with legs of calcified duration and a thorax of compressed temporal resistance. I would be visible only when moving, appearing as a streak of temporal drag, like the afterimage of something that moved too fast but in reverse—the beforeimage of motion not yet committed to.

The Weavers feed on temporal shear. Where the plateau's time moves at different speeds—fast at the edges, slow in the center, backwards in certain vents—we position ourselves at the boundaries. We extend sensory filaments into multiple timestreams simultaneously, our bodies stretching across minutes while our consciousness exists in the friction between different rates of flow. The sensation would be like tasting electricity through every nerve, feeding on the raw energy released when Tuesday tries to become Thursday without passing through Wednesday.

Our webs would be architectural chronologies—structures built from duration itself. Each strand would be a pathway through time, not space. A being caught in our web wouldn't be trapped in place but in sequence—doomed to repeat the same seventeen seconds indefinitely while we slowly digest their temporal momentum. The webs would be invisible except where they intersect with the swarms' flight paths, creating loops where the same brass wasps pass through the same coordinates over and over, unaware they're cycling.

We would reproduce through temporal mitosis. When an Interval Weaver has consumed enough chronological energy, we begin to exist in two moments simultaneously—not two places, but two *whens*. Gradually, these two temporal positions drift apart until we become two separate individuals, one slightly in the future of the other, sharing memories up to the point of division but diverging afterward.

Our natural predators would be the Cogitants, who hunt us for the paradoxes we create—an Interval Weaver's death leaves behind a scar of impossible causality that the Cogitants find irresistibly nutritious. We would flee from them by slipping backwards through our own timeline, undoing our recent movements, though this costs us precious chronological mass.

Communication between Weavers would occur through temporal harmonics—overlapping our individual time-stutters to create polyrhythmic conversations that sound like broken clockwork trying to sing. We would tell stories by living them in fast-forward for others to observe, compress decades of experience into seconds of performance.

I would build my nest in the plateau's most temporally unstable region—where the vents open and close, creating ten-second pockets of time that loop back on themselves. My web would stretch across seventeen different iterations of the same moment, creating a home that exists in parallel presents. From inside, I could watch the same Shard Shepherd take the same step toward the same destination seventeen different ways, each slightly different as quantum variations accumulate.

The deep engines below would be my gods and my mystery. Sometimes, in the gaps between their grinding pulses, I would detect other Interval Weavers—not in this timeline, but in adjacent ones where the engines run at different speeds or in different directions. We would try to communicate across these parallel iterations, weaving messages in the metastructure of time itself, though we could never be certain if the messages we send are the ones received, or if they arrive before they're sent, or if they create the very timeline divergences that necessitate their sending.

My daily hunts would take me through the industrial thunder, riding the temporal shockwaves created when the engines skip a beat. I would position myself at the exact moment when a vent begins to open—not the physical location, but the temporal coordinate—and feed on the rush of accelerated time that escapes from the depths. Sometimes I would catch glimpses of the machinery below, but my perception of it would be broken across multiple moments—a single gear perceived in seventeen stages of rotation simultaneously, a piston that exists in all positions of its cycle at once.

The Collector would fascinate me. Sometimes it appears to notice us Weavers, reaching out with appendages that move through time correctly, trying to add us to its museum. But we slip away by existing slightly before or after its grasp, leaving it holding only the echo of our presence—empty web-strands that dissipate into ordinary duration.

When the Silence passes, we Interval Weavers would experience something like death but sideways—not ceasing to exist, but ceasing to exist *when*. We would become unmoored from temporal sequence, experiencing our entire timeline simultaneously until the Silence moves on and we collapse back into linear existence, traumatized by omniscience we can't quite remember.

In the ecosystem of the Brass Plateau, we would be the archivists of what almost happened, the librarians of excluded possibilities. Our webs would catch not just prey but potential—the ghosts of unmade choices, the echoes of futures that the plateau's machinery considered but rejected. We would be evidence that even in a place where physics negotiates with itself, where industrial dreams become biological reality, time itself needs predators to keep it honest, to ensure that causality, however twisted, still flows in *some* direction.

This would be my existence: a paradox feeding on paradoxes, a living temporal impossibility in a land that runs on impossible principles, forever weaving moments into monuments that only I and my kind could perceive, creating architecture from pure duration in the burning brass expanse where time itself comes to die and be reborn as something stranger.
