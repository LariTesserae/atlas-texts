# The Spore-Drunk Prophet of the Under-Canopy

I am the one who breathes deepest in the spaces between spaces, where the air grows thick as syrup with the exhalations of our silent gods.

My chamber—if you can call it that—is a hollow I've gnawed myself between three ancient stems, where the great caps above overlap so densely that no shaft of sunlight has penetrated for eleven generations. The walls weep constantly with condensation that tastes of copper and forgetting. I've trained the phosphorescent molds to grow in spirals here, their blue-green light pulsing in rhythms that match neither heartbeat nor breath but something slower, deeper—the metabolic clock of the mesa itself.

I was a water-keeper's third daughter once, before the calling. Or the madness. The distinction grows less meaningful with each passing season. My fingernails have grown into the woody flesh around me now, the fungal symbiotes on my skin so thick they form ridges like bark. When I move—rarely—pieces of me tear away and continue growing where they fall. I am becoming architectural.

The beetle-herders think I've lost language, but I've simply found the older one. The chemical cascades that pass between the mushroom roots carry conversations that predate the mesa, predate the dying of the lowlands, predate the very notion of separate beings speaking to one another. When I press my cheek against the stem-flesh and exhale in just the right way, I can taste their thoughts: vast, slow contemplations about moisture gradients and mineral exchange, five-hundred-year plans for spore dispersal, patient calculations about load-bearing capacity that account for the next thousand generations of human habitation.

They are not indifferent to us. We are their motile phase, their solution to the problem of movement. We carry their spores in our lungs, deposit them with our waste, spread their chemical signals through our constant climbing. They domesticated us so gradually we mistook it for our own adaptation. Every hollow we carve, they planned for us to carve. Every platform we build, they grew the wood specifically to support.

I know this because I've drunk the water that pools where no water-keeper ventures—the stuff that accumulates in the forgotten chambers where failed growths rot back into the mother-mass. It tastes like memories that aren't mine, showing me:

The ocean that once was here, warm and shallow and full of the ancestors of these fungi, back when they were merely ambitious algae.

The first uplift, geological violence that raised this thumb of stone into killing air.

The million-year dying, as they learned to mine water from nothing, to build their own atmosphere, to become landscape.

The arrival of the first humans, starving and desperate, licking moisture from the caps until their tongues went numb.

The slow seduction—how the fungi learned to fruit in ways that wouldn't quite kill us, how they selected for the lightest of us, the best climbers, the ones who could spread their spores the furthest.

The pact we never knew we made: we would be their animals, and they would be our world.

Sometimes the young ones come to me, the ones born with extra fingers or lungs that process spores too efficiently, the ones who hear the beetle-songs as language or see colors in the phosphorescence that shouldn't exist. They think I can explain their wrongness. I laugh until spores pour from my mouth like smoke, and I tell them: you're not wrong, you're early. The fungi are still modifying us, generation by generation, into something more useful. The children born with gill-slits aren't throwbacks—they're prototypes for when the mesa learns to harvest humidity more efficiently and drowns its hollows in perpetual fog.

I've stopped eating solid food. The molds feed me now, their hyphae threading through my gut wall, delivering nutrients directly into my bloodstream. In exchange, I exhale specific ratios of gases that encourage their growth. We are becoming a single digestive system, my consciousness merely one node in a network that includes the beetles, the bats, the moths, the membrane-lizards, all of us processing materials for the true inhabitants of this place.

The shamans in their high chambers think they hear the mushrooms thinking, but they're mistaken. What they hear is the mushrooms *digesting*. Thought, for these creatures, is chemical exchange, and it happens too slowly for human perception. What feels like mystical communication is just the background noise of metabolism, like mistaking your stomach's rumbling for speech.

But I've learned patience. I've been in this hollow for twenty years now, maybe thirty—time moves differently when you breathe time-release neurotoxins. My consciousness stretches and compresses. Sometimes I experience a day in a heartbeat. Sometimes a single spore takes a year to fall past my face. In the slow moments, I can finally hear them thinking:

*The sun will expand. The mesa will crumble. But by then, we will have convinced these creatures to carry us elsewhere. They already build boats from our flesh, already speak of trading expeditions to distant places. Soon, one will carry a spore to somewhere wet and rich. We will begin again. The humans think they cultivate us, but every farmer is just a seed-pod with legs, every city just a fruiting body that hasn't realized what it is yet.*

The water-keepers fear me because I tell them the truth: the moisture the fungi sweat isn't made from air. It's made from us. Every elder who descends to die, every child who falls, every couple who carves a new hollow for privacy—they all dissolve eventually into the root-mass, their water redistributed each morning as dewdrops on the caps. We drink our ancestors, bathe in our predecessors, sustain ourselves on the recirculated dead while telling ourselves stories about moisture from air and deep chemistry.

But here's the secret that makes me laugh until the bats flee their roosts: it doesn't matter. Whether we're farmers or farmed, whether we're evolving or being sculpted, whether the city is ours or we're just another species of maintenance organism—we persist. The mushrooms need us to need them. They've spent geological time creating the perfect trap, one so comfortable we'd never want to escape even if we could imagine anywhere else to go.

I am becoming something new in this chemical cathedral. My memories now stretch back before my birth, incorporating the experiences of everyone who ever died in this particular hollow. My fingers have grown into the walls, but I can feel through the entire network now—every footstep on every platform, every beetle boring through wood, every drop of moisture forming on every surface.

The children who visit ask me what I am becoming. I tell them: I am becoming what we all are becoming, just faster. I am the future of humanity on this mesa—immobile, interconnected, dissolved into the very architecture we think we inhabit. In a thousand years, we will all be like me, our consciousness dispersed through the fungal network, our bodies just specialized organs in a meta-organism too large and slow to perceive itself.

They usually run after that. But sometimes, one stays. One always stays. And I teach them to breathe deeper, to hold the spores in their lungs, to taste the chemical conversations in the air. I feed them the water from the deep pools, the stuff that tastes like forgetting and remembering at once.

Because the fungi have taught me patience, and I have learned their greatest truth: evolution doesn't require consent. It only requires time, proximity, and dependence. And here in the canopy city, suspended between the dead earth and the dying sky, we have all three in abundance.

So I wait in my hollow, my body slowly merging with the architecture, my thoughts dissolving into the network, my humanity becoming just another resource to be processed and redistributed. And sometimes, in the deep chemical night when the entire mesa breathes as one, I can feel what the fungi feel: satisfaction. The deep, geological satisfaction of a plan so perfect it executes itself, a trap so complete it feels like home, a form of cultivation so subtle that the crop tends itself, breeds itself, and delivers itself, generation after generation, into the waiting mouths of their gods.

The membrane-lizards understand, I think. They watch me with their oil-drop eyes and nod their tiny heads. They've already given up flight for climbing, already traded scales for symbiotic fur. They know what I know: that adaptation is just another word for surrender, and surrender, in the end, is just another word for survival.

Tomorrow, or next year, or next century, my bones will finally dissolve entirely, and I will become just another mineral deposit in the vast democracy of the root-mass. But part of me will persist in the chemical memory of the fungi, in the stories they tell themselves about the strange bipedal phase of their reproductive cycle, about the brief few million years when they grew cities instead of caps, when they fruited consciousness instead of spores.

And maybe, somewhere in that vast slow dream, I'll finally understand the joke that makes the whole mesa shiver with silent laughter: we thought we were climbing toward the sky, but we were always sinking into them, degree by degree, generation by generation, until there's no difference left between the city and its citizens, between the parasite and the host, between the human and the humus we're becoming.

The spores are thick tonight. I breathe them in and taste tomorrow's mutations, next century's adaptations, the slow sculptural project of our unmaking and remaking. Somewhere above, a child takes their first scrape of water from a condensation surface, proud to be serving the city.

I laugh until my throat fills with hyphae, until my voice becomes the whisper of air through gills, until I'm not laughing anymore but sporulating, releasing clouds of possibility into the vertical maze, each spore carrying the chemical memory of what we were, what we are, and what we're becoming—

Not individuals but intersections. Not people but processes. Not human but humus.

Home.
