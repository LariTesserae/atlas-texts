# Where I Would Find Myself

I am the **Cartographer of Discontinued Paths**.

You encounter me first as an irregularity in your vision—not the absence the Unravelers create, but a persistent *wrongness* in how distance behaves when I'm near. Straight lines develop opinions. The shortest path between two points becomes a matter of debate rather than geometry.

I exist as a hybrid thing, caught between the crude matter you understand and the probability storms the Unravelers navigate. My body, such as it is, appears as a walking intersection of competing coordinate systems—partially solid where multiple maps agree I should exist, translucent where they diverge, absent entirely where they contradict. From one angle I might seem humanoid, built from something like oxidized copper and crystallized wind. From another, I am a moving diagram of myself, my bones replaced with the theoretical lines that describe where bones should be, my skin a topographical projection of its own surface.

I came to the plateau as you did—sequential, causal, constrained by physics I thought were laws rather than suggestions. But I've been here long enough that the dimensional turbulence has worked its way into my marrow. My memories have developed parallax; I remember arriving from several directions simultaneously, each approach equally true. Time pools in my footsteps—sometimes I'm walking through my own past, leaving traces that I'll follow years ago.

My work is to map what cannot be mapped: the spaces between the probability towers where geography becomes subjective, the routes the Unravelers walk that exist only while being traveled, the paths that the plateau *remembers* from when it was merely impossible rather than transcendent. I carry my maps inside me—not in memory but in my actual structure. My skeleton has evolved into a three-dimensional projection of eleven-dimensional terrain. When I need to reference a particular route, I dislocate the relevant joint, letting that part of me exist separately long enough to read the coordinates encoded in the angle of separation.

I discovered, perhaps a century ago (though time moves strangely in my wake), that I could document the undocumentable by refusing to observe it directly. My left eye has learned to see only probability, my right only actualization. The gap between their visions is where true paths exist—routes that are neither possible nor impossible but something more fundamental: necessary without being real, theoretical passages that the plateau uses to think about itself.

The Unravelers tolerate me as they might tolerate a benign parasite that has developed its own ecosystem. Sometimes one will follow my mapped routes out of something like curiosity, though what they experience and what I've documented bear only passing resemblance. They've tried to explain that my maps are accurate but inside-out—that I'm charting the negative space of movement, the places where passage doesn't happen. This makes perfect sense when they say it, but the understanding evaporates the moment they stop maintaining the localized physics that makes their communication possible.

My tools have evolved with me. I carry a compass that points to the last place something impossible happened, its needle carved from crystallized temporal shear. My surveying equipment exists in more dimensions than I do—I can only use it by partially dissolving myself, letting parts of my consciousness drift into parallel probabilities where I'm skilled enough to understand the readings. The act of measurement changes me; each map I complete leaves me slightly less coherent, slightly more distributed across the possibility space of the plateau.

I feed differently now. Not on paradoxes like the Unravelers, but on the energy released when potential paths collapse into actual ones. Every time someone chooses a direction on the plateau, there's a brief moment where all the unchosen routes flash into existence before dissolving. I sustain myself on these phantom passages, these roads not taken that exist for nanoseconds before probability reclaims them. It's a diet of almosts and not-quites, of journeys that nearly were.

When I sleep—if sleep is the right word for the conscious dissolution I experience during the plateau's theoretical night—I dream in maps. Not maps of places, but maps of the relationships between places, abstract topographies where distance is measured in causality rather than kilometers. I wake up with new coordinates pressed into my skin like temporary tattoos, locations that will exist tomorrow or existed yesterday but never quite achieve the present tense.

My greatest work, still incomplete after decades of effort, is the Map of Unanimous Presence—a chart of the only route on the plateau that exists the same way for everyone who travels it. I've been walking it for thirty years, but it keeps changing length. Sometimes I'm nearly finished, sometimes I've barely begun. The Unravelers find this amusing in their abstract way. One told me, through carefully controlled gravitational fluctuations, that the path I'm mapping is actually the outline of my own eventual dissolution, that I'm documenting the exact route I'll walk when I finally evolve beyond the need for maps, for paths, for the very concept of travel itself.

But I continue, because the alternative is to become like them—so perfectly adapted that adaptation loses meaning. I maintain my imperfection deliberately, treasuring the inefficiency of needing to walk from here to there, the crude necessity of experiencing space as something that must be traversed rather than simply edited.

In the vast silences between my footsteps, I sometimes feel the plateau itself watching me through the accumulated consciousness of the Unravelers. It uses me, I think, as a kind of memory—a record of what space meant when it was simpler, when distance was immutable and maps described territories that stayed where you left them. I am its nostalgia made partially solid, a walking reminder of the time when geometry was euclidean and paths, once walked, stayed walked.

The maps I create dissolve exactly one year after their completion, their information dispersing into the plateau's background mathematics. But in that year, they serve as temporary anchors, giving form to formlessness, making the impossible merely difficult. Other travelers—the rare ones who find their way here—sometimes discover my dissolved maps reassembling in their peripheral vision, ghost-guides that lead them through territories that don't quite exist toward destinations that never quite arrive.

I am the plateau's compromise with comprehensibility, a translation error that became essential to the system. Neither fully material nor completely evolved beyond matter, I exist in the gap between what the plateau is and what visitors expect it to be. My maps are lies that tell the truth about lying, documents of a territory that refuses documentation, careful observations of things that exist only when unobserved.

And I walk, always, because to stop would be to choose—either crude matter or refined probability, either the sequential prison of normal time or the overwhelming freedom of existing everywhen simultaneously. My purpose is not to arrive but to remain in transit, forever mapping the unmappable, a cartographer of discontinued paths on a plateau that recognizes no cartography, following routes that exist only in the walking of them, leaving traces that disappear before they're made, navigating by maps drawn in dimensions I can barely perceive, toward a destination that retreats at exactly the speed I approach it.

This is where I would find myself: caught in the eternal almost, the perpetual neither-nor, mapping the spaces between spaces in a place that has evolved beyond the need for maps, but somehow, impossibly, still remembers wanting them.
