**The Under-Thread Walker**

You are one of the few who traverse both above and below, though you no longer remember choosing this path—perhaps no one chooses, perhaps the weave itself selects its intermediaries through some vegetable logic older than decision. Your body has adapted in ways that disturb even the beetle-keepers: your joints bend in too many directions, allowing you to hang inverted from the underside of the weave for hours, days, feeding the root systems with solutions your modified stomach produces from digesting the bitter fungi that grow in the humid dark.

Your eyes have split their function—one tracks the bright-white constancy above while the other has grown a second lid, filtering the darkness below into comprehensible patterns of heat and movement. The Tenders above see you emerge from their floor at unexpected moments, rising through gaps in the weave that weren't there moments before, your hair dripping with the condensation that runs wrong down there, sometimes falling up toward the abandoned earth, sometimes gathering in sheets that hang like vertical lakes.

You know the secret the others only dream: the weave is not floating. It grows from something vast beneath, something that might once have been a tree or might still be becoming one, its trunk so massive that its bark has become landscape, its hollows large enough to hold cities of the things that tend it from below. You've seen them, the too-many-legged gardeners, and learned they are not monsters but partners, their chitin bodies designed for pruning roots with the same precision the Tenders above shape vines. They fear you as much as you once feared them—you are the only thing that moves between their dark world and the burning white they cannot imagine.

Your work is translation. When the upper weave develops a weakness, you taste it in the roots below and guide the under-gardeners to strengthen the foundation. When the deep-dwellers' pruning grows too aggressive, you whisper through the vine-floor to the Tenders above, your words traveling as vibrations they interpret as instinct, adjusting their cultivation patterns without knowing why. You maintain the balance between two worlds that each believes itself complete, alone, eternal.

In your pod—if it can be called that, this space you've woven between layers, neither fully above nor below—you keep maps drawn on bark cloth with ink pressed from the luminous beetles that exist only in the transition zones. The maps show patterns no one else sees: how the terraces truly curve, not just upward at the edges but through dimensions that have no names, folding back on themselves until every point touches every other point, until distance becomes just another kind of time that moves wrong here. You've walked routes that lead you to yourself from yesterday, tomorrow, never.

You eat what neither world can digest: the pale shoots that grow in the exact space where light meets dark, fruits that exist only while falling through the gaps, flesh that remembers being plant, plant that dreams of being flesh. Your hunger is specific and strange. Sometimes you crave the sound of growth itself, pressing your ear to the newest vines until their stretching fills your stomach. Sometimes you need the taste of shadows that have lost their objects, those pools of darkness that collect in the corners where even the white-bright cannot reach.

Other Tenders avoid you, not from disgust but from a kind of vertigo. Your presence makes them aware of the space beneath their feet, the possibility of falling, the existence of an entire ecology their lives depend on but cannot acknowledge without acknowledging the incompleteness of their world. Children, though, seek you out. They know you carry seeds from below, seeds that grow into plants that shouldn't exist—vines that flower underground, roots that fruit in open air, leaves that photosynthesize darkness.

You remember—or imagine you remember—the first time you fell through. A vine snapped, or perhaps opened like a mouth, and you dropped into the humid underneath where time moves even stranger than above. The too-many-legged ones caught you in nets of silk stronger than the weave itself, carried you to the base of the great maybe-tree, and showed you the symbols carved in its bark—the same symbols the Tenders braid in their hair, though none above know they're writing words in a language only the under-dark comprehends. That was when you understood: the terraces are not a floor but a ceiling, not a world but a membrane between worlds, and someone must ensure the two sides never fully meet, never fully part.

Your loneliness is architectural, built into the space you occupy. You speak three languages—the whispered intent of the Tenders, the chemical poetry of the under-gardeners, and your own tongue, which has words for concepts like "the grief of roots for fallen fruit" and "the joy of darkness discovering its own depth." But no one speaks all three with you. Your lovers, when you take them, are always temporary, either rising to join the bright world above or sinking to serve the dark below, unable to sustain themselves in the liminal space where you've made your existence.

Yet you continue, because the work is necessary, because the weave depends on someone knowing its full dimension, because in the spaces between above and below you've found something neither world possesses—the ability to change, to become, to be incomplete and therefore infinite. While the terraces repeat their perfect patterns and the under-dark maintains its ancient service, you grow wild in the gap, the only truly living thing in all this vast mechanism of managed life.

Sometimes, in the moments when your two eyes align and see the same impossible thing, you glimpse what the terraces truly are—not a basket or a floor or a world, but a single enormous organism in the process of thinking, each Tender and beetle and vine and under-gardener a neuron in a mind so vast it mistakes itself for geography. And you, moving between its layers, carrying messages between parts that don't know they're parts—you are its only self-awareness, its only chance of ever knowing what it is, what it dreams, what it's becoming in this eternal morning that might, after all, be just the pause before an awakening too large for any smaller consciousness to survive.
