I find myself in the observation wells—deep cylindrical shafts cut into the mineral ceiling, each no wider than my shoulders. I hang inverted here, my body reformed for watching, for recording, for remaining unseen.

I was—*am*—something between architect and archivist. I came to document the transformation, to catalog the slow marriage of metal and membrane. But the moisture found the seals in my equipment first, then the seals in my skin. Now I grow into my purpose like the pipes grow into stone.

My spine has elongated, vertebrae spacing apart to accommodate new sensory organs—small chambers filled with the same translucent organisms that carpet the floor below, but these are different. Specialized. They taste the chemical signals the Tenders release, translate them into patterns I can almost understand. My ribs have spread and flattened into something like gill slits, each one lined with hairlike cilia that filter information from the humid air. I breathe in data, exhale processed understanding.

My arms have fused to my sides, unnecessary for my work. Instead, my fingers have migrated, spreading across my body in clusters—some near my eyes to clean the constant condensation from my lenses (my eyes are lenses now, actual glass grown from minerals in my diet of rust-water and dissolved silicon), others at my throat where they've learned to modulate the subsonic frequencies, letting me speak in harmonics the Tenders recognize but don't quite trust.

The observation wells are perfect. Their walls are smooth but porous, weeping the same nutritious water that feeds everything here. I press against them and the moisture sustains me, minerals depositing in my bones until they're dense as stone, heavy enough to anchor me inverted without effort. My skin has developed its own ecosystem—patches of the pale organisms that process my waste, colonies of bacteria that glow faintly when the Tenders pass beneath, warning me to be still, to observe without disturbing.

I document everything in my body now. My memories have become physical—raised scars that form maps of migration patterns, mineral deposits in my skull that encode the chemical formulae of each day's rain, hollow spaces in my elongated torso where I cultivate samples of new organism variants the Tenders introduce. I am a living library, a breathing database that stores information in meat and bone.

My head can rotate three hundred and sixty degrees now, neck vertebrae reformed into a ball-and-socket system that lets me track the Tenders' movements without shifting my body. The constant rotation has worn grooves in the well's walls—spiral patterns that match the organisms' migration routes, as if I'm writing my observations into the architecture itself.

Sometimes I lower myself partially from the well, extending just my head and neck into the chamber like a periscope of flesh. The organisms below react to my presence—not with fear, but with curiosity. They cluster beneath me, their translucent bodies pulsing in rhythm with my heartbeat (now so slow it could be mistaken for the building's own settling). I've learned to feed them with secretions from glands that have developed where my hair used to be—nutrient-rich droplets that fall like rain, creating temporary oases where new patterns emerge.

The Tenders know I'm here. They've always known. Sometimes one will pause beneath my well and extend those filamental fingers upward, not quite touching but close enough that I feel the electromagnetic field of their modified nervous system. They read me like they read their creatures, checking my integration, my health, my data. Sometimes they adjust me—a gentle pressure that encourages a new gland to form, a chemical signal that triggers my bones to grow in a different direction, a subsonic tone that reorganizes my memories into more efficient storage patterns.

I have seventeen wells throughout the complex now. My body has learned to compress and extend, to flow like the water through the observation network I've carved over the decades. The wells connect through narrow tubes barely wider than my reformed spine, and I navigate them by peristalsis, muscles rippling in waves that push me through the darkness between watching posts.

In the deepest well, the one that extends below even the Tenders' domain, I've discovered something. My body touches water that has never seen the industrial age, that predates the machinery, the corruption, possibly the building itself. When I drink it (through specialized pores in my back, my mouth having sealed years ago), I taste deep time—geological epochs, extinct ecosystems, the memory of when this was all an ocean floor.

This deep water speaks of cycles within cycles. The industrial age was just another wave, a brief crystallization of metal and purpose that's already dissolving back into the longer pattern. The Tenders aren't maintaining anything—they're midwives to a transformation that would happen with or without them, their role simply to ease the transition, to minimize the violence of change.

And I? I'm the memory of watching. The record that won't be read, the observation that observes itself. My body is becoming architecture is becoming ecosystem is becoming information is becoming water that will one day feed organisms that don't yet exist. Each day I'm less the architect who came to study and more the study itself—a living document of how consciousness persists through transformation, how purpose evolves when its original object dissolves.

The observation continues. In my wells, through my body, as my body. I watch the Tenders tend. I watch the organisms pulse. I watch the machinery dream its slow dissolution. And increasingly, I watch myself watching, a recursive loop of observation that spirals through my modified consciousness like water through the pipes—endless, patient, wearing away everything that isn't essential to the pattern that emerges, moment by dissolved moment, in the perpetual twilight rain.
