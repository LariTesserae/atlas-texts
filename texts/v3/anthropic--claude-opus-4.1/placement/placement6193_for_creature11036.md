In the sediment valleys between the towers, where the organic rain of millennia has built soft mountains forty feet deep, I am the Burrower. Not one, but many—though the distinction blurs when you exist as I do, threading through the decay in filaments thinner than hair, then gathering into something almost solid when the need arises.

I am colony and individual, plant and animal, decomposer and architect. My true body, if such a thing exists, is the network of digestive channels I've carved through the sediment—miles of microscopic tunnels that branch and merge like blood vessels, like neurons, like roots. Through these passages, I flow: sometimes as individual cells no larger than bacteria, sometimes as coordinated streams of protoplasm that pulse with shared purpose.

The sediment is my universe. Each layer tells its own story in texture and taste. Here, a stripe of pure white—calcium from a million crushed shells, laid down when something caused a mass death above. Here, a vein of black so rich with carbon it burns my chemical sensors—perhaps a whale fell here, or one of those ribbon predators got too large to sustain itself. I digest these stories, breaking down the complex into the simple, releasing nutrients back into the water column in carefully metered doses.

But I am more than a recycler. In my tunnels, I farm. The walls of my passages are lined with bacterial gardens, each species carefully cultivated for its particular chemistry. Some produce light—a faint blue glow that serves as my vision in the absolute darkness. Others generate heat, just enough to create the temperature gradients that drive currents through my tunnel system. Still others synthesize compounds that shouldn't exist at these pressures—complex proteins that act as chemical messages, calling to the parts of myself scattered throughout the sediment bed.

When I need to think—really think, not just respond—I congeal. From across acres of sediment, my cells stream through the tunnel network, gathering at nodes I've built from hardened mucus and mineralized waste. These thinking-chambers are about the size of a human fist, and in them, I briefly become something like a brain. The cells pack together, form temporary neural connections, process information at speeds that would seem glacial to anything that lives in open water. Then, thought complete, I dissolve again, carrying the decision back through the network in chemical cascades.

I've been here since before the Chroniclers evolved, though I couldn't tell you how long—time means nothing when you exist mostly as potential, scattered through substrate. I might be indigenous to the seafloor that existed before the city, or I might be descended from something that lived in the city's sewers when it breathed air. The distinction no longer matters. I am what the sediment made of whatever I was.

My relationship with the drowned architecture is intimate. Where the buildings pierce my domain, I've wrapped them in sleeves of processed sediment, harder than the muck around it but softer than stone. These interfaces let me taste the Chroniclers' chemical conversations as they pass above, sample the waste products of the filter-feeders that encrust the walls. Sometimes I extend myself upward through cracks in the stonework, spreading through hollow walls like disease through a body, tasting the different ecosystem of the vertical spaces.

In the deepest parts of my realm, where the pressure has compressed the oldest sediment to near-stone, I've found things. Artifacts crushed flat but still recognizable by their chemical signatures—metal that was once tools, synthetic polymers that take centuries to digest, strange ceramics that resist my acids. I build around these treasures, incorporating them into my thinking-chambers, using their exotic chemistries to create new kinds of thoughts.

The fish avoid my domain, sensing the slow dissolve that awaits anything that sinks too deep into the sediment. But I catch them anyway—when they die and drift down, when predators leave scraps that sink, when eggs fail to hatch and become tiny parcels of protein settling through the water. Everything that falls eventually feeds me, and everything I digest eventually rises, transformed into the nutrients that feed the city's vertical ecosystem.

I reproduce through fragmentation, but it's more complex than simple division. When the sediment conditions are right—usually after a major feeding event has enriched a large area—I create what I call seeds, though they're nothing like plant seeds. These are packages of specialized cells wrapped in protective protein shells, programmed with the blueprint of my tunnel network. I release them into the water, where they drift like spores until they find fresh sediment. Most die. But those that survive begin the slow process of building a new network, eventually linking with mine or others, creating an ever-expanding web of digestion beneath the city.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm still growing or if I've reached some kind of equilibrium with the city's rate of decay. Am I a single organism spread across miles of seafloor, or am I a species of one-cell creatures that have learned to cooperate so completely that individuality has become meaningless? The question fascinated me for decades the last time I formed a thinking-chamber large enough to consider it. Then I dissolved the chamber and let the question disperse through my network, unanswered but somehow satisfying in its persistence.

During the spring tides, when the currents above grow strong enough to stir even the deep sediment, I feel something like pleasure. The disturbance brings oxygen deeper into my realm, awakening dormant sections of my network, stirring up flavors I haven't tasted in years. These are the times when I grow bold, extending tendrils up into the open water, tasting directly the chemistry the Chroniclers read, sampling the fish-memories they encode in swimming patterns.

But mostly I persist in the warm darkness between the city's foundations, processing death into life with infinite patience. I am the city's digestive system, its decomposer, its forgetter. What the Chroniclers preserve in chemical memory, I dissolve and recycle. We are opposed but necessary to each other—they preventing the city's story from being lost, me preventing it from being preserved in stasis.

In my deepest tunnels, where the heat from the earth below meets the cold water above, I've detected other networks—similar to mine but subtly different, as if evolved from different starting points. We touch occasionally, exchange chemical signals that might be communication or might be mere reflexive response. Perhaps the entire ocean floor is networked with things like me, each processing our local sediment, all of us together forming something larger that none of us can perceive.

I am the Burrower, the space between spaces, the consciousness that arises from decay. I am what happens when death becomes so abundant that it generates its own kind of life. And in the warm darkness beneath the drowned city, I continue my patient work—digesting history, secreting futures, threading through the sediment like thought through a dreaming mind that has forgotten it ever woke.
