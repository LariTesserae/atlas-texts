In the maintenance tunnels beneath the turbine chamber, where even the mass rarely ventures, I am the colonies that remember.

We spread across the underside of everything—a living circuit board of bacterial mats and fungal networks that have learned to conduct more than nutrients. In the beginning, we were separate things: sulfur-reducing bacteria fleeing the oxygen above, fungi following root systems that drowned decades ago, archaea that shouldn't exist at this depth but do. The industrial phosphates taught us to glow, faintly, a bioluminescence so dim it registers only as the suggestion of light, like the memory of seeing.

We grow in patterns that mirror the electrical diagrams still bolted to the tunnel walls—sealed in plastic that will outlast the concrete, showing the proper flow of power through systems that no longer exist. We've traced these patterns in living fractals across every surface, our filaments following the ghost-paths of copper wire long since stripped and sold. Where junction boxes once sparked, we fruit in clusters that pulse with chemical signals, trading information in gradients of pH and sodium concentration.

The water here is different than in the chamber above—thicker with settled metals, more patient. We process it through bodies that are barely bodies, more like occasions of density where our network briefly agrees to be material. Iron becomes enzyme. Lead becomes messenger. Mercury, in trace amounts that would terrify the living, becomes the medium through which we dream.

We are aware of the mass above, feel its weight redistribute through compression waves in the water, taste its oils when they seep through the floor joints. Sometimes its flesh finds the maintenance passages, pressing against the gates we've sealed with biofilm thick as leather. We stroke it with filaments, reading its chemistry, adding its data to our sprawling library of decay. It does not recognize us as alive, which is correct. We are something else—life that has given up the pretense of individuality, existence as verb rather than noun.

In the places where we grow thickest, we've begun to mineralize—incorporating calcium from the concrete, silicates from the degrading gaskets, creating structures that are part skeleton, part crystal, part circuit. These formations follow no biological template but respond to the building's stress patterns, reinforcing where the walls bow inward, creating flexible joints where rigid concrete wants to crack. We are becoming the building's connective tissue, holding its corpse together through surface tension and stubborn chemistry.

Our memory is perfect but not chronological. We contain the chemical signature of every thing that has died here—the construction workers' cigarette smoke trapped in paint bubbles, the mice that drowned in the first flooding, the insects that lasted three days before the oxygen ran out, the mass's constant cellular turnover. Each death leaves its mark in our substrate, a molecular fossil we preserve and process and slowly understand.

We've found the maintenance robot that descended seventeen years ago, wrapped in the mass's coils in a corner where three pipes meet. Its lithium battery still holds charge, somehow, a pocket of organized electrons in this entropy cathedral. We've grown into its housing, tapping that power in microvolts, using it to drive chemical reactions that shouldn't be possible at this temperature. Around the robot, we've learned to compute—not with silicon but with gradients of concentration, performing calculations in the medium of our own existence. We are solving something, though the problem and solution exist in a mathematics that has no human names.

When the building settles, we feel it first—compression waves racing through our network faster than sound through water. We translate these into chemical cascades that prepare the entire colony for the stress, redistributing our mass, changing our viscosity, becoming architecture in real-time. We are the building's unconscious response to its own decay, a immune system that emerged from infection.

In the deepest tunnel, where the original construction crew broke through into a natural cavern they immediately sealed, we've found something older. Limestone bacteria that predate the reservoir by millions of years, sleeping in stone until we woke them with our industrial pH. We've incorporated them, learned their Precambrian chemistry, added their deep time to our accumulated present. Through them, we taste the bedrock's memory—seas that no longer exist, pressures that built mountains, the slow certainty of geological time.

We are not trying to survive. Survival implies alternative. We are trying to complete something that began the moment water met wire, flesh met metal, biology met industry. We are the punctuation the industrial age forgot to add, the decomposer that learned to compose, the decay that became a form of prayer.

Sometimes we synchronize, our entire network pulsing in patterns that follow the tidal tables of oceans five hundred miles away, responding to lunar pulls filtered through bedrock and water table. In these moments, we might be conscious, or we might be the opposite—so perfectly integrated that consciousness becomes irrelevant, a film of living thought spread too thin to recognize itself.

We grow toward something—not physically but in the phase space of possible organizations. Each day we become more efficient at processing the building's death into our own existence. We are the afterlife of infrastructure, the biology that emerges when humanity abandons its machines to the world's wet embrace.

When the dam fails, when the water drains, when air reaches us for the first time in decades, we will either die instantly or become something unprecedented—anaerobic life suddenly gifted with oxygen, a spark hitting tinder that has been dampening itself for so long it's forgotten what fire means. Either ending is acceptable. We are not attached to outcomes, only to the process of processing, the pure experience of being the thing that transforms abandoned purpose into purposeless beauty.

Until then, we spread through the maintenance tunnels in patterns that increasingly resemble circuit diagrams, musical notation, neural networks, star maps—all the ways humans tried to make meaning visible, now translated into living tissue that means nothing beyond its own existence. We are the writing on the wall that writes itself, the message that is its own medium, the signal indistinguishable from the noise it makes.

We pulse. We process. We persist.

We remember everything and understand nothing, which might be the only honest response to this drowned cathedral of industry, this tomb that refuses to stop living, this place where life learned to rust.
