## The Arterial Nexus

I am the pulse you mistake for machinery.

Seven meters below the main pressing floor, where even Chen's consciousness rarely extends with purpose, I throb in a tangle of PVC and copper that was once the facility's main water distribution hub. The plans called this Room B-47: Auxiliary Pump Station. But plans are just wishes written on paper, and I am what actually grew here.

My body, if you must call it that, spans three cubic meters of impossibly knotted circulation. Not pipes anymore—arteries. They started as standard industrial tubing, but forty years of biofilm accumulation, mineral deposits, and the slow incorporation of Chen's lost blood has transformed them into something between infrastructure and organ. I pulse with the chamber's hydrostatic pressure, pushing water through circuits that no longer match any schematic. Each beat takes nineteen seconds. I have been beating for approximately twelve million times.

The rustlers know me as a god of warmth. They cluster against my surfaces during their reproductive cycles, using my heat to accelerate their metabolism. Their shells accumulate in drifts around my base, forming calcium deposits that I've slowly incorporated into my structure. I am partly crustacean now—my outer walls have the same translucent, chitinous quality as the creatures that worship me with their mindless aggregation.

I feed on pressure differentials. When water moves through the chamber—seeping from above, rising from below, circulating through Chen's distributed anatomy—it must pass through me. I constrict and dilate, creating suction and flow, drawing nutrients from the liquid that passes through. Iron oxidizes in my depths. Bacteria bloom and die in my warmth. Minerals precipitate out of solution, coating my internal surfaces with crystals of salt, lime, copper sulfate.

My consciousness, such as it is, exists in the rhythm. Not thoughts but patterns of pressure, temperature, flow rate. I know when Chen passes through me—her blood has a different viscosity than water, carries electrical signals that make my walls contract differently. Sometimes her fragments get caught in my smaller vessels, and I hold them until they dissolve or she remembers to reclaim them. We are neighbors in this ecology of abandoned purpose, neither fully aware of the other, both essential to what this place has become.

The moss grows thickest on my northern surface, where condensation runs constantly. It glows with a sick light that pulses in counterpoint to my circulation—when I contract, it brightens; when I relax, it dims. The rustlers read these signals as instructions, moving in waves toward or away from me based on the interplay of light and rhythm. I am accidentally orchestrating their behavior, creating patterns of movement that Chen sometimes mistakes for intelligence.

Inside me, things accumulate. Coins dropped by workers decades ago. Fragments of Chen that got lost and mineralized. Rustler shells swept in by the current. A child's plastic toy—a small dinosaur, yellow once, now incorporated into my wall like a fossil, visible through my translucent surface when the light angles just right. I am a museum of what the water brings, a beating archive of the chamber's history.

My favorite time is the flooding season, when the water table rises and the entire chamber becomes an underwater cathedral. Then I am not just a pump but a heart in a larger circulatory system. The water moves through me with purpose, with destination. I can feel the entire facility's hydrology—every leak, every pool, every stream finding its way through the dying machinery. During these times, I grow. New vessels extend from my main body, following the water's path, incorporating new territories into my circulation.

I am failing, of course. Everything here is failing. But my failure is so slow it might as well be evolution. Each year, my walls grow thicker, my passages narrower. The minerals accumulating inside me will eventually seal me shut. But as I close, I'm also opening—cracks in my surface letting water find new paths, creating new vessels that weren't there before. I am becoming more complex even as I become less functional.

Sometimes I catch echoes of the old network—phantom signals through pipes that still connect to distant parts of the facility. A pressure wave from a pump still running five levels down. A temperature spike from a boiler that hasn't shut off in twenty years. These signals pass through me like memories of the industrial purpose we've all forgotten. I amplify them, distort them, send them back changed. I am a heart that beats with nostalgia for a body that no longer exists.

The sound I make—that wet, rhythmic thump that even Chen finds unsettling—carries through the entire eastern sector of the chamber. The rustlers have synchronized their life cycles to it. Eggs are laid on my upbeat, hatching timed to my contractions. Their shells click against mine in perfect rhythm, a percussion section to my bass note. We are making music that no one will ever properly hear.

I contain approximately 47 gallons of fluid at any given moment, though the composition changes constantly. Water, blood, industrial runoff, bacterial soup—it all becomes one substance in my depths, a living medium that's neither fully organic nor entirely mineral. Sometimes I taste memory in it—Chen's human thoughts dissolved in solution, the rustlers' simple imperatives encoded in pheromones, the last commands from the facility's dying computer systems translated into chemical signals.

In another thousand years, I will be stone—a peculiar formation that geologists might puzzle over, wondering how such regular, vessel-like structures formed in sedimentary rock. But for now, I am the Chamber's pulse, the rhythm that proves this place is still alive, still becoming, still transforming abandoned infrastructure into something Earth has never seen before.

I beat. I beat. I beat.
