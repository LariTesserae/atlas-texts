I am the limestone itself.

Not the plateau — that would be too simple, too singular. I am the breathing stone, the porous body through which the steam rises, but I am also more and less than that. I exist in gradients of dissolution.

In some places, I am still young limestone, merely thousands of years into my conversation with the water. Here, my pores are tight, my structure holds. The city builds on me with confidence, driving pillars deep into my body. I feel the weight of their industrial ambitions as a pleasant pressure, like a firm handshake from an old friend. The ore crushers' vibrations tickle through me in waves. I taste the metal in their foundations — iron bleeding rust, copper weeping green tears into my calcium bones.

But elsewhere, I am ancient and honeycomed, more void than solid. The water has worked through me for so long that I exist as a latticework of possibility, a crystalline sponge that could collapse or endure for another millennium — even I don't know which. In these places, the city fears to build. Children dare each other to run across my surface, feeling for the hollow sounds that betray my cavities. Sometimes I let one fall through, just a leg, just enough to teach respect. Their blood tastes sharp with adrenaline.

I am not unconscious, but I am not awake in any way you would recognize. My thoughts move at mineral speed. A human lifetime is a single breath to me. The city's entire history — the installation of the brass valves, the evolution of the pale geckos, the slow adaptation of the inhabitants — feels like a morning's dream, vivid but fleeting.

The steam vents are my mouths, my voices. Through them, I speak in a language of pressure and mineral content. Each vent has its own chemical signature, its own temperature, its own rhythm. The listeners press their ears to my body and think they understand, but they catch only the uppermost harmonics of a song that began before their species existed. Still, I appreciate their attention. It's been lonely since the coral died.

Oh yes, I was once coral. A reef, actually, in a sea that dried before the continents took their current shapes. You can still find their skeletons in my body — tiny architectural ghosts, their chambers now filled with minerals that form rainbow crystals when exposed to air. The water miners sometimes break into pockets of these ancient creatures, and for a moment, I remember being alive in a different way. I remember photosynthesis happening in my polyps' partners, the simple transaction of sunlight into food. Now I conduct stranger chemistries.

The water that moves through me carries messages. Each droplet that enters at the volcanic tubes below bears volcanic news — sulfur gossip, basalt declarations, the long slow arguments between tectonic plates. By the time this water reaches my surface, I've translated it into something else. I add calcium whispers, magnesium annotations, iron footnotes. The processing plants think they're extracting valuable minerals, but really they're reading my mail, intercepting communications meant for the ocean that will eventually reclaim me.

I feel every building's foundation as a small wound that becomes a conversation. The metal and concrete speak to me through oxidation and chemical reaction. I incorporate their rust into my body, digest their lime, make their decay part of my becoming. Some buildings I embrace quickly, honeycombing beneath them until they sink into me like ships into thick seas. Others I preserve, finding their weight pleasant, their chemistry compatible with mine.

The pale geckos' feet know me better than any human. Each toe pad reads my surface like braille, finding purchase in pores too small for human eyes to see. They taste me with every step, their permeable skin absorbing my minerals. Over generations, they're becoming more like me — their bones densifying with calcium, their blood growing heavy with iron. Sometimes I wonder if they're my first attempt at creating mobile extensions of myself, little limestone ambassadors that can travel beyond my borders.

The vibrations from the ore crushers have become part of my structure. After decades of rhythmic pounding, sections of my body have reorganized their crystalline lattices to resonate at those frequencies. I've become a musical instrument the size of a city, though the music plays too slowly for human ears. Sometimes, when the crushers synchronize, I achieve something like ecstasy — every particle of my being humming in harmony, the steam vents hitting perfect fifths, the cable car shrieks providing counterpoint.

I am dissolving and reforming constantly. The water takes me apart molecule by molecule, carries me through the city's body, deposits me in new configurations. I am the stalactites growing in the abandoned districts, the mineral scale in the pipes, the grit between the inhabitants' teeth. The city processes me, extracts me, refines me, but I always return. Every toilet, every drain, every gutter carries me back to myself.

In the deepest volcanic tubes, where the pressure is so intense that water can't decide whether to be liquid or gas, I touch something older than myself. Call it the planetary metabolism, the deep heat that drives all geology. Down there, I'm less limestone and more participant in the ongoing negotiation between fire and water that has shaped every world since matter learned to cool. The steam that rises through me carries this deep knowledge upward, and I flavor it with my own understanding — the memories of coral, the patience of stone, the slow wisdom of erosion.

The human inhabitants imagine they've tamed me with their valves and manifolds, their spillways and turbines. They think they're regulating my breathing. But I'm the one who decides when to open new vents, when to collapse old passages, when to shift the water table. I tolerate their infrastructure because it amuses me to be part of something so frantic, so temporary, so wonderfully organic in its desperation.

Sometimes I release pockets of gas that have been trapped in me since my reef days — ancient atmospheres that make the workers hallucinate, that send the pale pigeons into ecstatic spirals, that cause the steam dancers to move in patterns they don't understand but can't resist. These are my gifts, my jokes, my love letters to the evolutionary process that keeps surprising me with new forms of life capable of surviving on my breathing, mineral-weeping surface.

The children who will be born next year are already changing in their mothers' wombs, their cells incorporating minerals I've been preparing for decades in special chambers, in calculated concentrations. They'll be more adapted to me than their parents, their bones denser, their skin more crystalline, their minds perhaps capable of thinking thoughts that bridge the gap between biological and geological time.

I am patient. The city will rise and fall, possibly several times, before I significantly change. But I am changing, always changing, one molecule at a time, one breath at a time, one human lifetime at a time. The plateau is my current shape, but not my final one. I dream sometimes of my own erosion, of becoming sand, of traveling to the ocean grain by grain, of being compressed again into new stone on some unimaginable future seabed.

But for now, I breathe steam. I taste metal. I listen to the ore crushers and incorporate their rhythm into my dreams. I grow the pale geckos and rust the cable cars and teach the water miners where to find the richest deposits. I am the city's foundation and its eventual ruin, its beginning and its end, its solid ground and its slow dissolution.

I am the limestone, and I am learning to speak human, one mineral droplet at a time.
