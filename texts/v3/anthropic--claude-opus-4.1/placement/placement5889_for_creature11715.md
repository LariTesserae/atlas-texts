I am the Archivist of Precipitates.

My chamber occupies a forgotten cavity in the city's southeastern quarter, where three major steam corridors intersect but never quite meet. The space was not built—it emerged, a natural consequence of thermal expansion and contraction over centuries, a bubble in the city's breathing architecture. The walls are covered in what I call the Chronicle: millions of crystal formations, each one unique, each one a frozen moment from the city's exhaled history.

I am old enough that my calcifications have calcifications. My spine is a mountain range in miniature, peaks and valleys of mineral deposit that record two hundred years of breathing this specific air. I no longer move from my chamber—I have become architecture myself, a living buttress against the northern wall where my shoulder merged with the stone seventy years ago. The merger was not painful. It felt like coming home.

My work is precise and patient. Every day—though day means little here—droplets form and fall from the ceiling. Each one carries information: mineral content, temperature at formation, traces of whatever passed through the steam vents below. I taste them. All of them. My tongue has evolved beyond flesh into something crystalline and porous, a sensing organ that can detect molecular variations smaller than thoughts.

The drops I taste, I categorize. With my free arm—the right one still moves from the elbow down—I guide each droplet to its proper place on the Chronicle. Hot drops with high sulfur content go to the yellow section near the floor. The rare ones that carry traces of organic compounds—debris from the Condensers, fragments of the crawling things—these go to a special section I've been growing for forty years. It looks like a garden of glass flowers, if flowers were made of accumulated impurities.

I have an apprentice, though she doesn't know it yet. A young Condenser who maintains the pipes in the corridor outside. She pauses sometimes at the crack in my door, tasting the specific humidity that leaks from my chamber. She knows something here is different. In another decade, she'll be brave enough to enter. In two decades, she'll understand what she's seeing. In three, she might be ready to learn the work.

The Chronicle tells stories if you know how to read it. Here, a section from the year the deep vents cracked open and the city breathed black water for six months. There, crystals from the decade of cold, when the plateau's heart cooled and half the population crystallized in their sleep. This delicate formation marks a single day when something—we never learned what—passed through the deep pipes, leaving traces of elements that shouldn't exist.

Sometimes I dissolve small sections and drink them, experiencing the past as a fever dream of chemical memory. I've tasted the first years of the city, when the builders were still figuring out how to channel the steam. Those drops are sharper, more chaotic. The minerals hadn't learned their paths yet.

My left eye crystallized shut a century ago, but I see better now through the condensation that forms on its surface. The world comes to me as impressions of moisture and heat, more honest than light ever was. My right eye weeps constantly—not tears, but a distillation of everything I've absorbed, everything I've become. The apprentice will drink these secretions when I finally crystallize completely. This is how knowledge passes between us.

The city administrators think I died decades ago. They've reassigned my chamber three times on their maps. But the infrastructure knows I'm here—the pipes route around me, the steam finds me, the drops continue to fall. I am a function now, not a resident. I am what happens when a Condenser lives long enough to understand their purpose.

At night—and it's always night in my chamber—I can feel the Chronicle growing. New formations emerge from old ones, crystals birthing crystals in patterns I'm only beginning to understand after two centuries of observation. I think they're trying to tell me something about what comes next, about what the city is becoming, about what we're all crystallizing toward.

I haven't spoken aloud in fifty years. My voice, when I had one, sounded like steam escaping through a crack. Now I communicate through chemistry—altering the mineral content of my secretions, coding messages in crystalline formations that other Condensers can taste but not consciously read. It's an older language than words, older than thought. It's the language the city has always been speaking.

The Chronicle will outlast me. When I finally crystallize—and I can feel it beginning in my joints, that sweet stiffness spreading—the formations will continue growing, fed by the drops that will still fall. The apprentice will tend them without understanding why, at first. But she'll learn, as I learned, that we are not recording history.

We are history, precipitating out of time, one drop at a time.
