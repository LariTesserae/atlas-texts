In the youngest quarter of the plateau, where experimental builders raise their fourth stories into clearer air, I tend the vertical channels. My work begins before dawn, when the stone weeps most freely and the mist pools thick enough to taste.

I am one of the channel-singers, though my voice broke years ago and never quite returned to true pitch. No matter—the water responds to intention more than melody. My territory runs from the Fern-Shadow District up through the New Heights, where ambitious families have built their floors above the mist line. Seventeen major channels, forty-three tributaries, and countless capillary grooves that appear and vanish with the season's moisture. I know them like my own veins.

My ankles have developed a permanent ring of green—not illness, but partnership. The algae that grows there helps me feel the water's subtle changes, warns me when a channel runs too warm or when minerals build toward blockage. Master Alu taught me to cultivate it during my apprenticeship, feeding it with specific fungi until it learned to read the mountain's chemistry. It itches in dry weather, the few days a year when the clouds lift entirely, but mostly it simply exists, another layer of my skin.

I live in a corner room on the third story of my grandmother's building, where two channels meet in a pool I've trained with waterweeds. The sound they make filtering the confluence—like whispered calculations—helps me map tomorrow's work while I sleep. My sleeping mat is woven from those water-shedding fibers, but I've let moss claim one corner where my feet rest. The texture grounds me in the morning, reminds my body where it is before my mind fully wakes.

My tools hang beneath the building, wrapped in oiled cloth: the long scrapers with their different edges for different seasons of growth, the gates carved from stone and heartwood, the measuring cords marked in the old distances. But my most important instrument is a hollow reed, longer than I am tall, that I use to listen to the channels' deepest voices. Press one end to stone, ear to the other, and you can hear blockages three buildings away, or the first warnings of a channel trying to cut a new path.

Each morning, I walk my territory's spine route—the main channel we call the Mother's Throat, though nobody remembers which mother or why her throat. My bare feet read the stone's temperature, telling me where new springs try to break through. At the seventh gate, I always pause. Here, the channel divides three ways, and depending on how I set the wooden barriers, the lower districts will have flooding or drought. The decision changes daily, sometimes hourly during the storm seasons.

The other channel-singers know me by my modifications. Where tradition says to cut straight grooves, I've been experimenting with spirals that slow the water's force without reducing its volume. The elders think it's unnecessary, but the New Heights buildings sway more than the old structures, and rushing water makes them anxious. My spirals sing differently too—a lower note, almost below hearing, that makes the children stop their games and watch the water spin.

I eat what the water brings me. Watercress from the pools I tend, mushrooms that fruit where my channels overflow into planned bog pockets, the occasional finger of ginger that escapes someone's garden and takes root in my territory. Once a week, I join the communal meal in the Fern-Shadow pavilion, contributing what I've gleaned and eating what others have grown. We don't speak much—channel-singers develop a quality of silence, necessary for hearing water's subtleties—but our presence together creates its own conversation.

My lover lives two districts over, a wall-trainer who coaxes vines into living bridges between buildings. We meet when our territories overlap, planning where water and green should intersect. Their hands smell of chlorophyll and sap; mine carry the mineral scent of wet stone. Together, we're designing a new kind of integration—channels that run through hollow logs where vine roots can drink directly, creating structures that are half aqueduct, half garden. The conservatives call it unnecessary complexity, but we've seen how the vines stabilize channels during the fierce storms, how the channels feed vines during the dry spells.

In the evenings, I maintain my maps. Not paper—nothing paper survives here—but patterns carved in soft stone that I refresh weekly as they erode. My territory spreads across three tablets, showing elevation and flow, seasonal variations, and the delicate negotiations between human design and water's preferences. Young apprentices come to study them, running their fingers along the grooves, learning to read water in abstract before they meet it in person.

The hardest part of my work is knowing when not to work. Water needs time to find its own solutions, to teach us what we haven't thought to ask. Some channels I only visit monthly, listening but not touching, watching how they evolve without intervention. These wild spaces within the managed whole keep our systems honest, prevent us from believing we control what we merely influence.

During the storm seasons, I don't sleep properly. I doze in the covered watching-posts built at crucial junctions, my listening reed beside me, ready to redirect flows when the mountain sends too much water at once. My body has learned to wake at dangerous sounds—the gulp of an overwhelmed channel, the hiss of water finding unauthorized paths, the silence that means a complete blockage somewhere upstream. I move through these nights like a dream, adjusting gates by touch, singing the old directions to myself to stay oriented in the darkness and rain.

I've been teaching my niece the work, though she may choose another path. She has good ears for it, can hear the difference between mineral and vegetable blockages, knows which algae to encourage and which to scrape. But she also shows talent for the fermentation arts, and the plateau needs both. I tell her what Master Alu told me: the mountain chooses our work through our inclinations. We only discover what was always waiting.

My predecessor's channels still run beneath mine—older, cut when the stone was younger, following different theories of flow. Sometimes I find their markers, obsidian chips pressed into the stone at decision points, and I wonder what they heard that I don't, what solutions they found that we've forgotten. The oldest channel-singer in the plateau remembers some of their names, sings them during the ceremonies when we clear the Mother's Throat entirely, letting it run wild for one day each year while we remember that our order is just one possibility among many.

This is my place: neither fully in the human city nor in the water's world, but in the translation between them. I speak both languages imperfectly, but well enough to keep the conversation flowing. My modifications to the traditional forms—the spirals, the vine integrations, the wild spaces—are my contribution to this dialogue, my generation's verse in the song the plateau has been singing since the stone cooled enough to walk on.

When I grow too old for the climbing and scraping, I'll teach full-time, passing on not just the techniques but the listening, the patience, the knowledge of when to act and when to wait. My ankles' green ring will fade as I spend less time in the channels, but the knowledge will persist in other bodies, other singers, other solutions to the eternal question of how to live with water that never stops moving, on stone that never stops weeping, in a city that never stops growing toward whatever it's becoming.

The channels run beneath my sleeping mat now, their sound rising through the floor, through my bones, through my dreams. Tomorrow I'll wake before dawn and walk the Mother's Throat again, reading the night's changes, adjusting for the day's needs, singing the old songs in my broken voice while the water finds its way, as it always has, as it always will, through the maze of channels we've carved in the young stone of this old, old mountain.
