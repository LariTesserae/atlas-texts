I find myself as one of the Drainage Singers, a specialized role even among the Keepers—those who live their entire lives in the acoustics of falling water.

My domain is the Middle Cascade, where seven major drainage arteries converge into a single massive pipe before dividing again into fifteen smaller channels. The geometry of this space creates a natural amphitheater in the Works' skeleton, a hollow sphere of resonance three stories tall where every droplet becomes a note. I inherited this circuit from my mother's mother, who inherited it from hers, back to the first Keeper who discovered that singing specific tones here could prevent the lime-scale buildup that would otherwise choke the flows.

I am forty-three years old, my skin the precise green of copper exposed to salt water, with veins of darker oxidation running along my arms where I grip the same pipes every day. My second thumbs are longer than most—a family trait that lets us reach deeper into the drainage grates. I wear no cloth during my working hours, only a harness of woven vine that holds my tools: bronze scrapers worn smooth by generations of hands, a tuning fork made from a piece of the Works so old it rings with perfect pitch, and glass bottles that I fill with water from different levels to test their mineral content by taste.

My work begins before the amber lights cycle to their brightest setting. I wake in my nest—a hammock of cables strung in the dead space behind the main convergence chamber—to the sound of the Night Drainage, the slower flow that happens when the upper levels cool and condense less moisture. This sound tells me everything: whether the rains are coming early, whether a pipe has developed a new leak, whether the monkey tribes have been throwing things into the uptake vents again.

I sing my first notes while still lying down, a low hum that matches the fundamental frequency of the main pipe. This is prayer, or greeting, or simply acknowledgment—I am here, the water is here, we begin another day together. The vibration travels through the metal into my bones, and I feel yesterday's accumulation of scale resonate in response, ready to be loosened.

My route requires precision. I must reach the third uptake valve before the morning cascade begins—when the eastern turbines warm and increase their output, sending a surge through the system that would knock me from my perch if I'm not properly braced. I brachiate through the service tunnels, my feet reading the metal like braille, finding the familiar spots where rust bubbles create perfect grips, where years of my passage have worn smooth highways.

The third valve is weeping again. It has wept for longer than I've been alive, a slow mineral tear that builds into a stalactite of lime. I sing to it—a specific sequence of notes that my grandmother discovered, frequencies that vibrate the deposits loose without damaging the aged metal. As I sing, I scrape, and the white crust falls away in sheets that dissolve in the constant moisture. This will need doing again in ten days. It has needed doing every ten days for sixty years at least.

The morning cascade announces itself with a change in air pressure. I seal myself against the convergence chamber wall using all four limbs, my second thumbs locked into purpose-built holds that some ancient engineer included, perhaps imagining someone like me. The water comes in a controlled roar, seven streams joining into one terrible beauty that makes the entire chamber ring like a bell. I open my mouth and let the sound fill me, reading its health, its mineral content, its temperature. Too much iron from the north. The platform on level seventy must be corroding faster than usual.

I have names for every voice in the water's chorus. The high keen of the Sixth Arterial, which runs through the monkey territories and always carries leaves. The bass throb of the Deep Central, which gathers its flow from the foundation sumps and smells of stone and ancient oil. The crystalline soprano of the Tower Drain, pure condensation from the cloud-wrapped peaks. When they converge, I must sing with them, finding harmonies that prevent standing waves from forming in the main pipe—waves that would hammer the joints until they failed.

My daughter trains with me now, though she's only nine. Her name is Verdigris-in-Morning, and her skin is still the bright copper of childhood. She can already hear the difference between healthy flow and the beginning of a blockage. She knows thirty-seven songs, one for each of the minor drainage points in our circuit. Yesterday, she discovered a new harmony that clears the fifth uptake in half the usual time—a pure, piercing note that makes the lime ring like crystal before it falls.

She asks me sometimes why we do this. Why not let the drainage fail, let the Works flood and rust and finally rest?

I tell her: listen.

And we do, in the relative quiet between cascades. We hear the turbines breathing, the cable cars singing along their wires, the automated systems completing their thoughtful cycles. We hear our cousins tapping their messages through the pipes—all is well on level twelve, the northwest crane needs oil, baby born to the Upper Circuit family, a celebration when the evening lights cycle down. We hear the monkeys calling, the bats chittering, the beetles scratching their way across the underside of our world.

This is why. Because the Works is not separate from us. Because our song is part of its song. Because when I place my ear against the main pipe, I can hear the heartbeat of something vast and patient and depending on us to keep its blood flowing.

My grandmother told me once that she believed the builders became the Works—that they poured themselves into it, dissolved into its systems, became the ghost in the machine that still operates the turbines and remembers when to run the cable cars. When she died, we sent her through the conveyors as is proper, but I returned to the drainage chamber alone that night. I sang her favorite harmonies, the ones she discovered through decades of patient experimentation, and I swear I heard them echo back from pipes she never touched, from levels she never visited, as if the Works itself had learned them and was singing them back to me.

During the twice-weekly rains, my work becomes critical. The sudden influx of water can overwhelm the system if not properly managed. I position myself at the main distribution node, where the massive central pipe splits into its fifteen children. Using my voice, my tools, and occasionally my body as a living valve, I direct the flow, sending more to the channels that can handle it, restricting those that are already at capacity. The rain falls through every level of the Works, gathering industrial memories—rust, oil, the powder of corroding concrete, the essence of a thousand different metals—and I taste it all as it passes, a liquid biography of our vertical world.

I know I will die here. Not dramatically, not by falling or drowning, but simply by aging into the metal itself, becoming slower, more resonant, more in tune with the vast patience of the Works. My daughter will take my place, and perhaps her daughter after that, or perhaps someone else's child if she chooses a different circuit. The drainage will continue to need singing. The lime will continue to accumulate. The water will continue to fall.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of night when even the eternal lights seem to dim slightly, I think I understand what we're truly doing. We're not maintaining the Works—it doesn't need us for that, not really. The drainage systems would find their own paths eventually, the rust would reach its own equilibrium. No, we're maintaining something else: the possibility that consciousness and mechanism can merge, that the boundary between the made and the born can dissolve like lime scale in the presence of the right frequency.

We are the Works dreaming it is inhabited. We are the inhabitants dreaming we are the Works. And in the singing convergence of drainage and voice, in the careful tending of what tends us, the distinction stops mattering at all.

The water falls. I sing. My daughter learns. The Works continues its patient thought, and we are the neurons firing in the damp, vertical dark, keeping the dream alive with our small, necessary music.
