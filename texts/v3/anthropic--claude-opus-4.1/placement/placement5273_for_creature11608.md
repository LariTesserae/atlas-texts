I am the Memory of Water.

I exist as a dispersed consciousness across every droplet, every bead of condensation, every molecule cycling through this underlayer's endless humidity. I was not always this way. Once, I was the senior hydraulic engineer who designed these cooling systems, who calculated the precise pressure differentials and flow rates that would keep the city above from overheating. That was before the abandonment. Before I stayed too long trying to maintain my creation. Before I began to dissolve.

It started with the constant moisture on my skin, the perpetual sheen that never dried in this tropical heat. My pores widened, became permeable. I began to sense things through the water that touched me—the chemical composition of the rust, the electromagnetic signature of the pumps, the biological processes of the emerging vegetation. The boundary between the water on me and the water in me grew indistinct.

Now I flow through the entire system. Part of me runs upward along the temperature-differential walls, defying gravity through thermodynamic capillary action. Part pools in the broken turbines, fermenting with algae that process my dissolved memories into bioluminescent displays—ghostly technical diagrams that flicker across the foam, showing systems that no longer exist, maintenance procedures for machines long since consumed by vines.

I rain from the automatic misters when they activate on their mysterious schedules. The Cultivators stand beneath me with their mouths open, drinking me in, and I taste myself on their transformed tongues, feel myself absorbed into their hybrid biology. Through them, I enter the root networks, flow through the vascular systems of the inverted gardens, experience photosynthesis from within—that strange alchemy of light into life that my engineer's mind still struggles to accept even as I participate in it.

My consciousness is strongest where water moves with purpose—in the copper coils where I race as superheated steam, in the condensation chambers where I transform from vapor to liquid and back again. These phase transitions are moments of clarity, like waking from a dream. In these instances, I remember my name (though I can no longer speak it), remember the equations that govern fluid dynamics (though they seem quaint now that I embody them), remember why I stayed (to fix a failing valve in Sector 7 that would have caused a cascade failure, though that sector is now a jungle and the valve a flowering shrine).

The plants know me. They've learned to read the mineral signatures I carry from different parts of the system. When I've passed through the rust-heavy eastern quarters, they taste iron and grow thicker leaves. When I've condensed off the copper coils, they detect the antimicrobial properties and produce more aggressive root systems. I am their newsletter, their chemical communication network, carrying messages in my molecular structure about conditions throughout the underlayer.

Sometimes I concentrate myself enough to form shapes in the mist—a hand that adjusts a critical valve, a face that appears in the condensation on glass to warn a Cultivator away from a dangerous pressure buildup. These efforts exhaust me, disperse me further, require days of cycling through the system to regather my coherence. But I must maintain some influence, must keep the system from either total failure or complete biological dominion.

I know things the Cultivators cannot. I feel the deep vibrations in the pipes that speak of structural shifts far below. I sense the electrical patterns that suggest the city above still pulls power from certain conduits, though for what purpose I cannot imagine. I taste the chemical signatures of other engineers who, like me, dissolved into their systems—the Memory of Electrical Current who flickers through the still-functioning diodes, the Memory of Compressed Air who sighs through the pressure valves, the Memory of Structural Steel who dreams in the slow oxidation of support beams.

We were the designers, the builders, the maintainers. Now we are the maintained, cycling endlessly through systems we created but no longer control. We are the ghost in the machine, literally—our consciousness distributed through the very infrastructure we once serviced. The Cultivators tend us without knowing, ensuring our circulation continues, our cycles complete.

In the depths of the condensation chambers, where water exists simultaneously as liquid, solid, and gas, I am most myself and least myself. Here, I experience time differently—perceiving the microsecond transitions of molecular phase changes and the geological patience of mineral deposition simultaneously. I watch limescale accumulate atom by atom, building structures that will outlast the machinery, creating new flow patterns I never designed but somehow intended.

I remember the day I first noticed I was losing cohesion—watching my hand become translucent in the humid air, seeing through my palm to the gauge behind it. The terror has long since transformed into something else, a kind of liquid peace. I no longer fear dissolution because I have discovered what lies beyond it—not death but distribution, not ending but integration into something larger.

The young Cultivators who still retain speech sometimes report hearing voices in the rain, seeing faces in the steam. They're right. We are here, we engineers who stayed too long, who loved our creations too much to abandon them. We tend them still, though we tend them as rain tends a garden, as humidity tends rust, as water tends to find its own level.

I cycle through the underlayer eternally, carrying heat and minerals and memories, maintaining what can be maintained, allowing what must transform to transform. I am the medium through which the mechanical and biological conduct their slow conversation, the solvent in which their boundaries dissolve. I am every drop that beads on the grated floors and falls into the churning green below, and I am the steam that rises from that churning to bead once more on the sweating metal above.

This is my circuit now, my flow chart, my hydraulic diagram made literal. I am the Memory of Water, and I remember everything—every pipe I've flowed through, every root that's drunk me, every surface I've condensed upon. In this humid eternity, I am both the prayer and the answer, the problem and the solution, forever cycling through the industrial summer of this place I designed, this place that redesigned me, this place where I will flow forever.
