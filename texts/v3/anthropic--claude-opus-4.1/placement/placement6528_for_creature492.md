I find myself as one of the Threshold Generation, though born late enough that the term has become almost quaint—we are no longer the exception but the emerging rule.

My body is a map of indecision. My left lung processes water more efficiently than my right, which favors air, creating an asymmetry that makes me list slightly when I swim. The blood in my arteries carries both copper and iron, giving my skin a perpetual bronze undertone that shifts to verdigris where water has pooled too long in the hollows of my collarbones. I have seventeen failed gill slits along my ribs—they sealed during adolescence but left scars like closed eyes that ache during pressure changes.

I work as a gradient cartographer, mapping the invisible boundaries where one medium becomes another. The interface isn't a clean line but a zone of transformation sometimes meters thick, riddled with pockets, currents, and microscopic territories where neither air nor water fully rules. I've learned to feel these spaces through my skin—the way temperature drops non-linearly, how dissolved minerals precipitate at specific pressure points, where the surface tension creates lens-effects that distort light into new colors.

My workshop spans three levels. The lowest is dry, carved into living rock, where I grind pigments from the minerals that accumulate at the waterline: malachite from copper deposits, ochre from iron oxidation, rare purples from manganese that only forms where specific bacteria mediate the chemistry between air and water. The middle level floods and drains with the tides—here I mix my inks, timing the work so they cure in exactly the right moisture. The uppermost chamber is permanently submerged, where I store the finished maps between sheets of pressed kelp that prevent them from either drying out or dissolving.

The maps themselves cannot be read in a single medium. In air, they show one layer of information—the dry routes, the ventilation patterns, the areas safe for pure air-breathers. Submerged, the water-activated pigments reveal different passages, currents, the feeding grounds of filter-feeders, the zones where pressure becomes dangerous. But at the interface, where the map is half-wet, both patterns combine to show the true paths: the routes only we threshold children can travel, the spaces that exist because two worlds refuse to fully separate.

My partner is a Weaver whose magnetite bones sing harmonics I can feel in my malformed gill slits. Together we're documenting something new—patches where the water has begun to crystallize, not into ice but into something else, a semi-solid state that supports weight but allows passage, like swimming through honey that thinks. These zones appear and disappear according to patterns we haven't decoded. Sometimes citizens wake up to find their flooded bedrooms have become walkable, the water holding its shape in impossible architectures. Children learn to sculpt these temporary waters into playground geometries before they collapse back to liquid.

We have a daughter whose adaptation we don't yet understand. She exhales something that isn't quite air or water—a silvered vapor that hangs in spaces, neither rising nor falling. She uses it to write messages that only other children can read, creating a secret language of breath that exists in the spaces our maps cannot document. When she sleeps, these exhalations gather around her like a personal atmosphere, a third option we hadn't known was possible.

I am what the city makes when it needs to remember all its mediums at once. My imperfect adaptations—the failed gills, the asymmetric lungs, the blood that can't quite decide what it carries—make me sensitive to every threshold, every boundary, every place where the city argues with itself about what it means to breathe. I map these arguments, creating charts for those who, like me, will never fully belong to either element but find their purpose in the spaces between.

At dawn, I float at my preferred depth—where the pressure makes my failed gills tingle with phantom sensation, where I can hold my breath for exactly as long as it takes the sun to send its first filtered light through the aerial sea. This is where I truly exist: not in water, not in air, but in the continuous negotiation between them, my body a treaty written in flesh and adaptation, never quite sealed, always being renegotiated with each breath, each crossing, each map I draw of the impossible space I call home.
