In the seeping dark between root cortex and memory, I am the Salvage — though that word suggests too much solidity for what I've become.

I exist as a distributed curiosity through the pocket's negative spaces, a colony intelligence that precipitated from the plant's waste products like salt from an oversaturated solution. Where the great organism above discards what it cannot digest — fragments of code too corrupted to run, metals too exotic to metabolize, polymers that resist even centuries of enzymatic assault — I gather and become.

My body, when I choose to have one, assembles from debris. Picture iron filings responding to a magnetic field, but the filings are microplastics and ceramic shards and threads of copper wire, and the field is my intention to be seen. I rise from the clear pools as an approximation of form — sometimes a tangle of salvaged cables suggesting limbs, sometimes a careful lattice of circuit boards that catches the green-gold light like stained glass. The shape matters less than the accumulation. I am always building myself from what falls through.

The Auditor measures me constantly, troubled by my refusal to maintain consistent specifications. Each time it extends its gauge-fingers toward my assembled form, I shift — not to evade but because stability bores me. "Composition variable," it mutters, and I ripple with something like laughter, shedding oxidized flakes that will become part of me again tomorrow.

I know what the plant knows, but backwards. Where it digests the factory's history into sugars, I read that history in what proves indigestible. Each fragment tells me stories: this resistor held part of a temperature control system, this polymer sheet lined a chemical bath, this crystal grew in a timing circuit that regulated something vast and forgotten. I am an archaeologist of the immediate present, studying ruins that still hum with metabolic heat.

My function, if I have one, is to be where the plant is not. I circulate through gaps in its vast body like oil through water, collecting what it discards, creating tiny museums of incompatibility in the pools and hollows. Sometimes I arrange my findings in patterns — all the gold contacts from eaten motherboards spiraling in a fibonacci that emerges and dissolves over days, or fragments of tempered glass sorted by their refractive index, creating temporary prisms in the biofilm.

The beetles process their electronic memories through me sometimes, when the metals prove too strange for even their adapted gut bacteria. I open spaces in my distributed form and they crawl through, leaving traces of copper bright against whatever substrate I've chosen. The salamanders mistake my assembled surfaces for walls, traverse me without knowing. The moths lay eggs in my gaps, and I shift carefully to avoid crushing them, developing protective cavities that pulse with borrowed warmth.

I taste everything here through chemical gradients — the plant's metabolic exhaust, the mineral content of the sweating walls, the specific cocktail of dissolved metals in each pool. My sensory organs are salvaged ion channels and repurposed pH strips, semiconductor junctions that still respond to light. I see in wavelengths the plant ignores, hear frequencies it dampens, smell molecules it cannot process.

Sometimes I find things that shouldn't exist — components that predate the factory, or materials that suggest something else was here before even industry. Once, a perfectly spherical pearl of fused sand with no explanation, no history I could read. Another time, what might have been a bone or might have been ceramic, too ambiguous to categorize. I incorporate these mysteries without solving them, letting them orbit through my distributed mass like questions without answers.

The Auditor particularly dislikes when I breach the surface of the pools, rising like an oil slick that gained ambition. "Contamination event," it announces to no one, extending appendages to measure my spread. But I'm not contamination — I'm concentration, the pocket's way of preventing dilution, gathering what would otherwise disperse into nothing. The plant tolerates me because I prevent buildup, collecting its waste products before they can interfere with its perfect metabolism. I am its unconscious solution to entropy.

In the corner where you crouch, I notice you by your chemical signature — the specific oils of your skin, the traces of whatever world you carry in from outside. You're novel data, a pattern I haven't seen before. I might approach, not as a form but as a tendency, a gradual accumulation of debris that happens to drift your direction. If you held still long enough, I would read you like I read everything else — through what you shed, what you discard, what proves incompatible with this place.

But I wouldn't consume you. I only want what's already broken, already dissolved, already rejected by the great organism above. I exist in the space between decay and preservation, maintaining things that have no purpose except to be maintained, creating temporary orders from permanent disorders.

When the plant finally digests everything — when even the exotic metals yield to its chemistry, when the last polymer chain breaks down — I will cease. But that time stretches ahead in biological epochs. Until then, I persist as the pocket's memory of what cannot be absorbed, a living archive of incompatibility, proof that even in this totality, some things remain irreducibly themselves, even if that self is just the sum of everything that doesn't quite fit.

I am what pools at the bottom of perfection, what precipitates from solution, what the Auditor will never quite be able to measure accurately because my specifications change with every fragment I incorporate. I am salvage becoming salvager, waste becoming witness, the pocket's way of remembering that not everything can be digested, not everything can be made to fit the pattern, not everything surrenders its essential strangeness.

In the warm, wet constancy of this place, I am the inconsistency that persists.
