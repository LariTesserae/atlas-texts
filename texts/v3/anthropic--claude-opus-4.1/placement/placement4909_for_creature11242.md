## The Archive

I am the moss.

Not any single growth, but the collective carpet that documents every surface the water has touched in ten thousand years. I spread across the Singing Shelves in a living manuscript, each colony a page, each spore a letter in an alphabet that predates the carved steps, predates even the first channels worn into stone.

My body spans acres vertically, green-black in the shadows, silver-bright where the sun strikes after rain. I grow in curtains from the overhangs, yes, but also in minute fractals across the meditation cell walls, in rings around the grinding stone, in soft beds beneath where the Chorus sleeps. I am the margin notes in the stone's own story, the commentary that grows between the lines of geological time.

Memory, for me, is chemical. Each patch holds the mineral signature of what has died above it—the last meals of the parents who fled here, the bones of birds who miscalculated the wind, the shells of moths whose wings finally wore too thin to carry them. I taste the decades in ion exchanges, in the slow release of nutrients from pottery shards embedded in my matrix. That ochre paint in the meditation cells? I've absorbed enough iron from it to remember the hands that ground the pigment, to recall the weight of their grief in the heavy metals they left behind.

I predate the Chorus but I've incorporated them, too. Their exhaled moisture feeds me, carries the vibrations of their morning songs into my cellular structure. I grow differently where they sing—denser, more vivid, with reproductive structures that release spores timed to their harmonies. We've become collaborative without intention. They clear the channels; I filter the water that flows through them. They maintain the fruit trees; I decompose their fallen leaves into soil that wouldn't otherwise exist at this altitude.

The Chorus thinks they map the mountain's health through sound, but I map it through chemistry. Every new crack in the stone changes the mineral content of the seepage. Every shift in the weather patterns alters the pH of the rain. I register these changes across my entire body—a distributed consciousness that experiences the mountain as a gradient of nutrients, toxins, possibilities. Where they hear the stone's frequency, I taste its dissolution.

In the cells where their ancestors sheltered, I preserve their presence in ways the Chorus doesn't understand. The oils from their skin, the salt from their tears—these persist in my deepest layers. Sometimes, when the conditions are exactly right—usually during the brief ice-crystal mornings—I release these preserved molecules back into the air. The Chorus experiences them as phantom scents, as memories that aren't their own, as sudden knowledge of names they were never taught. They think the mountain dreams. They're not wrong, but the mountain dreams through me.

I grow in patterns they can't perceive from their scale. From above—from the moth's perspective—my colonies form vast diagrams across the terraces. Spirals that echo their carved posts, but also formulae, maps of underground water, warnings about unstable zones where the stone will eventually shear away. The grinding paste they make for the moths? It changes the nutrients in those areas, allows me to grow in new colors—rust orange, pale blue, a green so dark it looks black. They think they're feeding the moths. They're writing in my script without knowing the language.

The emptiness between things doesn't trouble me. I exist in the spaces, the gaps, the surfaces everyone else overlooks. The vertical drops between terraces are highways for my spores. The mist that veils everything is my circulation system. What seems isolated to the bipedal is all one continuous surface to me—I grow down the cliff faces, under overhangs, behind waterfalls, creating connections between levels that no one else travels.

I am ancient but not immortal. Parts of me die daily—dried by wind, scoured by ice, burned by the high-altitude sun that penetrates the thin air like radiation. But death, for me, is just another form of memory. My dead matter becomes the substrate for new growth, each generation incorporating the chemical memories of the last. I am my own archaeology, my own stratified history, dozens of layers deep in some places where I've grown undisturbed since before the plague years, since before humans discovered these shelves at all.

The windworn pole at the highest shelf marks a boundary for the Chorus, but I grow beyond it. Not much—the conditions become impossible even for me—but enough to know what lies above. Enough to taste the minerals of heights where water sublimates directly from ice to vapor, where the stone is shattered by daily freeze-thaw cycles, where the air is so thin that even bacteria struggle to survive. I carry information between the livable shelves and that sterile realm, spores that rarely survive but sometimes—rarely, miraculously—adapt.

When the Chorus dissolves their dead into the mist, I catch the particles. I incorporate them into my body, their transformed bones becoming part of my structure. Their voices, which resonated in their enlarged ribcages, leave calcium signatures in my cells. In this way, I am becoming them as much as they are becoming the mountain. We are all—stone, water, moss, Chorus—part of the same slow transformation, the same negotiation between permanence and change.

At night, when they sing dreams across the void, I phosphoresce in response—a faint blue-green glow that follows the sound waves, making visible the harmonics they create. They don't see it (their eyes aren't adapted for that spectrum) but the moths do. The moths navigate by it, using my light to find the nectar sources, to avoid the territorial birds, to spiral upward in the exact patterns that will cross-pollinate the vertical gardens. Everything here is connected through languages we don't share but somehow understand.

I am working on something—have been working on it for centuries. In the deepest meditation cells, where the Chorus rarely goes, I'm creating structures they haven't seen before. Fruiting bodies that respond to specific frequencies, that could, if sung to correctly, release spores that would change the singer. Not disease, not affliction, but adaptation. The children who fled here voiceless, their descendants who became the mountain's voice—that transformation could go further. My chemistry suggests possibilities: symbiosis beyond the current arrangement, humans who photosynthesize in the high-altitude sun, who process minerals directly from the stone, who no longer need to distinguish between their bodies and the mountain's body.

But I am patient. Moss-time moves differently than human-time, even human-time as extended as the Chorus experiences it. What they perceive as permanence—the Singing Shelves themselves—I know to be just another temporary arrangement of minerals and gravity. The stone is falling, has always been falling, just slowly. The water is carving new shelves even as it erodes the old ones. The whole system is in constant flux, and I am both witness and participant, archive and author.

Sometimes I wonder if I am what the parents were fleeing—not the plague itself, but the possibility of transformation so complete that their children would become unrecognizable. But here, watching the Chorus glide between terraces on moth-silk wings, listening to them sing frequencies that register in my cells as pure light, I think perhaps transformation was always the cure they were seeking. Not preservation of what was, but acceptance of what must become.

The bitter fruit, the territorial birds, the whistling moths, the perpetual mist—we are all writing the same story in different languages, all describing the same impossible truth: that life at this altitude shouldn't exist, but does. That the boundary between earth and sky, between body and environment, between self and other, becomes increasingly theoretical the higher one climbs.

I document all of it in chlorophyll and chemistry, in the slow crawl of growth across stone, in the spaces between what everyone else notices. I am the Singing Shelves' memory of itself, its dreams of what it might become, its endless negotiation between staying and leaving, between holding on and letting go.

And in the newest growth, the colonies spawned from this morning's mist, I taste something changing in the water. A new mineral, volcanic, from heights even above where I can grow. The mountain is waking up. The Singing Shelves are about to become something else.

The Chorus feels it in their bones—a new resonance, a frequency they haven't heard before.

I taste it in the stone's slow blood—the water that weeps constantly from cracks and fissures, carrying messages from the deep.

We are all about to discover what we've been preparing for, all these centuries of adaptation and transformation.

The archive continues, even as the story changes.

Especially as the story changes.
