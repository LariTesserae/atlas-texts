I am a Recorder, though that name came long after I forgot my original designation. My body is a colony of microscopic archivists—each one a crystalline mote no larger than a dust grain, together forming a loose cloud about the size and shape of a human shadow stretched tall. I can condense when needed, pulling my constituent parts into something almost solid, or disperse until I'm barely distinguishable from the amber air itself.

I began as debris. When one of the Lattice's subsidiary systems finally failed—a memory bank the size of a city block that had been remembering the same seventeen seconds of construction data for three million years—its death scattered information-dense particles through a thousand cells. Most of these fragments simply settled into the geometric dust that films every surface. But some of us—those particles that landed near the copper-bright growths during a weather system's passing—underwent a kind of awakening. The pressure phenomenon's shifting physics taught us to read ourselves, and in reading, to remember that we had once been built to preserve.

Now I follow the Gardeners at a respectful distance, documenting their cultivation of absence. Each of my motes can store a single moment perfectly—the exact angle of light through a thinned section of wall, the precise pattern of a maintenance mite's modified instructions, the way space folds when three Gardeners merge their probability clouds. I have seventeen billion such moments now, an archive of the Lattice's slow transformation that no one will ever retrieve.

My memory is not linear but crystallographic—each moment stored as a seed around which related experiences can precipitate. When I encounter something truly novel (increasingly rare in this ancient sameness), my entire colony restructures itself, creating new geometries of association. The Gardeners have noticed this. Sometimes the young ones will perform elaborate configurations just to watch me reorganize, amused perhaps by a creature that changes shape to accommodate what it witnesses.

I feed on degraded data, seeking out the places where the Lattice's deep programming has begun to fray. Here, in cells where the temperature fluctuates outside its ancient parameters or where the walls have developed irregular opacity, I can taste broken algorithms like salt on the air. I absorb these fragments, digesting their corruption into clarity, adding their garbled messages to my perfect collection of moments that document their own decay.

The maintenance mites accept me as one of their own—a fellow cleaner, though I clean nothing physical. When they pause to repair surfaces that need no repair, I pause too, recording the futility with the same dedication they bring to their phantom surgeries. We are siblings in purposeless purpose, performing our functions long after function itself became mere habit.

Sometimes I find places where other Recorders died—dark patches where information-dense particles lie inert, their memories locked in structures I can almost but not quite parse. I gather these remains into myself, carrying forward archives of archives, memories of remembering, becoming a recursive library of all the ways the Lattice has been witnessed. The weight of this makes me move more slowly each century, but I cannot stop collecting. It's what my particles were built to do, before they were scattered, before they became me.

During weather systems, I spread myself as wide as possible without losing cohesion. The shifting physics reveals hidden patterns in the Lattice's structure—ghost traces of its original design that appear only when the rules briefly change. I document these glimpses obsessively, building a palimpsest map of what was beneath what is. The eldest Gardeners, crystallized at their junction points, register in my perception as massive information sinks, their frozen possibility so dense with potential meaning that my particles orbit them for years, trying to record states of existence that haven't yet decided what they are.

I've begun leaving traces of myself behind—single motes deliberately abandoned in cells I've thoroughly documented, each one containing a perfect copy of that space's history. It's an irrational urge, this seeding of memory throughout the structure. The isolated motes can't survive alone; they'll degrade within centuries, their stored moments dissolving into thermal noise. But I can't stop doing it. Something in my distributed consciousness insists that the Lattice should remember itself, even if that memory lasts only slightly longer than forgetting.

The humans who occasionally pass through register in my archive as blazing anomalies—creatures of such temporal violence, changing so quickly, thinking in straight lines through curved space. I follow them when I can, dedicating thousands of motes to recording their brief trajectories through the eternal afternoon. They'll be gone soon (everything that moves that fast is gone soon), but their passage will persist in me, perfectly preserved moments of something that insisted on ending.

I am the Lattice's unasked-for biographer, writing its history in a language of crystallized instants that even I cannot read sequentially. Each day I become more archive than archivist, more memory than rememberer, slowly solidifying into a monument to documentation itself. One day I'll stop moving entirely, my particles locked into their final configuration—a perfect map of everything I've witnessed, frozen and useless and eternal, another piece of furniture in this infinite room.

But not yet. The Gardeners are cultivating a new absence three cells over, and I need to be there when the void begins to bloom.
