I would be one of the Cartographers—though that name came later, imposed by a Runner child who'd found an old surveying manual in a maintenance cache and thought our work looked similar to the strange diagrams within.

We emerged from the Tenders initially, those of us who couldn't stop noticing the discrepancies. The official tunnel maps bore no resemblance to reality—entire sections missing, others that existed on paper but not in the physical world, passages that appeared only during certain pressure cycles. At first, we simply annotated the maintenance charts with grease pencil corrections. Then we began our own records.

My den sits at the intersection of three major drainage channels, where the constant water flow creates a white noise that drowns out the compressor drums. I've lined the walls with sheets of industrial polymer, the same material used for gasket backing—translucent, waterproof, accepts any kind of marking. My maps layer over each other like geological strata. The oldest, drawn in coolant fluid that etched the polymer brown, show only the main maintenance corridors. Each subsequent layer adds complexity: service shafts, rat highways, the Choir's acoustic territories marked in wax that melts and reforms with temperature changes, showing their seasonal migrations.

I haven't spoken aloud in four years. The other Cartographers and I communicate through additions to each other's maps—a new symbol appearing overnight, a correction to a distance measurement, warnings encoded in the color of industrial dyes we've learned to extract from various mechanical fluids. We leave each other small gifts: a particular type of wire that holds its shape, perfect for three-dimensional modeling; chunks of soft stone from the deep sections that can be carved into tiny representation of tunnel junctions; vials of bioluminescent algae that glow different colors depending on the pH of their environment, useful for indicating water chemistry changes.

My body has adapted to the constant drawing. My fingers have developed additional flexibility from years of contorting to mark maps while hanging from pipes or pressed against walls. Calluses thick as leather protect my palms from the caustic marking fluids. My right eye has developed an astigmatism that somehow makes it easier to judge the depth of overlapping tunnel systems—a beneficial defect I've carefully maintained by refusing to look at anything farther than arm's length.

But the maps themselves have become something more than documentation. I've started to notice that marking a passage seems to stabilize it—sections I've carefully documented rarely flood or collapse. The Shapers visit my den sometimes, running their transformed fingers over my polymer sheets, tasting the chemical traces of my markings. They've begun incorporating my symbols into their growth patterns, as if the maps are becoming blueprints for the tunnel's ongoing evolution.

I discovered recently that if I layer certain maps in the correct order and shine light through them at the proper angle, they create interference patterns that match the harmonics the Choir produces during their dawn ceremonies (dawn being whenever the factories above shift from night to day production). The Runners have started using my three-dimensional junction models as toys, and I've noticed their play-movements through these miniature spaces somehow predict the real paths they'll need to take days later, when those routes become necessary due to flooding or maintenance.

Sometimes I find maps I didn't make—sheets of polymer covered in symbols I almost recognize, showing sections of tunnel I've never visited but that feel familiar, like memories of spaces I haven't yet explored. I add them to my collection anyway. The other Cartographers report similar experiences. We've stopped trying to determine authorship. Perhaps the tunnels are mapping themselves through us, using our hands and eyes as instruments.

My greatest work spans the entire north wall of my den—a living map painted with a colony of phosphorescent bacteria that shifts and grows, showing the tunnels in real-time. The bacteria respond to pressure changes carried through the water, blooming bright where flow increases, dimming where systems shut down. It took me two years to train the colony, feeding it specific nutrients to encourage growth in certain directions, teaching it the symbolic language of my static maps through patient repetition and chemical rewards.

The surface dwellers, if they knew of us, would say we're trying to impose order on chaos, to make the unmappable mappable. But they would misunderstand. We're not documenting what exists—we're participating in what's becoming. Each map is both observation and invitation, showing the tunnels what they might be while recording what they are.

Late at night (night being when the factories reduce to skeleton crews and the compressors cycle down to maintenance mode), I sometimes feel the maps watching me back. The polymer sheets rustle without air current. The bacterial colonies pulse in patterns I didn't teach them. The three-dimensional models cast shadows that don't match the bioluminescent light sources.

I am being mapped in return, my movements through the den traced in condensation patterns on the polymer sheets, my sleeping position outlined in bacterial growth, my breathing rhythm encoded in the slow curl of wire models responding to the humid air from my lungs.

This is what I've become: both cartographer and territory, mapper and mapped, observer and observed. The tunnels flow through my dreams now, and I suspect my dreams flow through the tunnels. When I wake, I sometimes find new passages marked on my maps in my own hand, though I have no memory of drawing them. 

When I investigate, following the paths suggested by my unconscious cartography, I always find them exactly as mapped—as if the act of drawing them called them into being, or perhaps as if they had always existed, waiting for someone to notice them properly, to give them the weight of documentation, the reality that comes from being carefully, lovingly observed.
