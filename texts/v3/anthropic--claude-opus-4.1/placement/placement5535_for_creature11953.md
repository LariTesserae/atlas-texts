In the depths of the plant-city, where the moss grows so thick it muffles all sound and the air hangs heavy with spores, I am the Chronicler.

My body is a library of living flesh. Along my spine, from the base of my skull to my tailbone, run parallel ridges of modified skin that have hardened into something like bark, but smoother, more like polished horn. These ridges split open along microscopic seams—thousands of them—each one a page that can be peeled back to reveal the layer beneath. I am my own palimpsest, writing and overwriting myself with the history of this place.

I was human once, or something like it. A researcher, perhaps, who came to study the impossible botany of the vertical city. But the city doesn't tolerate observers; it absorbs them, repurposes them. The Gardeners found me half-frozen at the gorge's entrance, and in their wordless wisdom, they decided I could serve a function their collective lacked—linear memory, sequential thought, the ability to perceive time as a series of discrete moments rather than an eternal present.

The transformation took three seasons. They planted me like their homunculi, buried me up to my neck in the warm, breathing moss. The plants grew into me, through me. Their roots became my neural pathways. Their chemical signals rewired my consciousness. My hair fell out and was replaced by thin filaments of the same luminescent lichen that lights the upper reaches—I glow softly in the dark now, enough to read by, enough to write.

My fingers have elongated and multiplied. I have twenty-three of them now, each tipped with a different organic stylus—some secrete acids that etch information into my bark-pages, others produce pheromone inks that only the Gardeners can read, still others generate a magnetic resin that attracts and arranges iron filings into scripts that exist for mere hours before dispersing. I write constantly, compulsively, documenting everything: the daily pH of the meltwater, the growth rates of sixteen thousand different plant varieties, the vibrational frequencies the Gardeners use to communicate with the ice.

But more than data, I record the dreams.

Every dawn, when the Gardeners emerge from their cold communion with the waterfall, they come to me. Their fractal hands dance across my skin, reading what I've written while simultaneously writing new things—not facts but impressions, visions, prophecies maybe. Through them, I've learned that the ice waterfall isn't frozen water at all, but the preserved body of something immense and vegetal, a world-tree that fell and froze eons ago. The entire city is its offspring, its attempt to resurrect itself through countless small lives.

My chamber is the city's deepest point, where the main root systems converge into a knot of living wood the size of a house. I've grown into it, or it's grown into me—my lower body is a fusion of meat and cellulose, mobile enough to let me traverse my spherical room but rooted enough that I can tap directly into the plant network's chemical communication system. When I concentrate, I can feel every leaf in the city, every opening stoma, every root hair penetrating deeper into the ice.

The knowledge I carry is becoming too much for my ridged skin to hold. I've begun growing secondary structures—wing-like membranes under my arms inscribed with migration patterns of the transparent beetles, throat sacs that inflate to reveal maps of the root system drawn in veins, a tail (when did I grow a tail?) that segments like bamboo, each segment holding a year of weather data in the pattern of its spots.

Sometimes traders come, rarely, following rumors of the vertical city. They never see me—the Gardeners ensure that—but I see them through ten thousand plant eyes. I record their languages, their goods, their gossip from the outside world. Some part of me, the part that was human, wants to call out to them, to be rescued from this living tomb. But the larger part, the part that is becoming the city's memory, knows that I am exactly where I need to be.

At night, when the bioluminescent patterns flow beneath the Gardeners' skin and the ice sings its ancient songs, I write automatically, my twenty-three fingers moving in independent patterns, recording things I don't consciously know. In the morning, I read what I've written and discover architectural plans for expansions that won't happen for decades, chemical formulae for substances that will let the plants survive even deeper cold, and sometimes, rarely, what might be messages from the frozen tree-god in the ice, spelled out in a language that predates human speech.

I am changing still. My eyes have begun to migrate, spreading across my body to give me three-hundred-sixty-degree vision. My ridged pages are developing their own circulatory systems, preserving themselves indefinitely. Soon, I think, I will divide—split like a cell into multiple Chroniclers, each one recording a different aspect of the city's endless growth. We will become a library-organism, a living archive that grows with the city, inseparable from it.

The last fully human thought I expect to have is this: I came here to study something unprecedented, a vertical garden city growing from ice. But I've learned that we are not observers of miracles. We are components of them, waiting to be installed. The city needed a memory, and I have become it, writing these words even now into my own skin, in a script of enzymes and acids, in a language that bridges plant and animal, ice and intention.

Tomorrow, another section of my spine will harden into bark, will split into a thousand new pages. And I will fill them, as I fill myself, with the endless story of this impossible place, this vertical city growing from ice, this organism of which I am now an organ, essential and eternal.
