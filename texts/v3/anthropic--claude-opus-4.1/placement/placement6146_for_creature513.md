I am a Cartographer of Absences, though I came to the Hollowing to be something else entirely.

I arrived carrying solid ambitions—maps of real places, instruments that measured definite distances. I thought I would document this city-that-barely-is, create an atlas of its impossibilities for those in the still-substantial world. How young I was, how dense with certainty. My skin still cast shadows then. My pencils still made marks that stayed where I put them.

Now I map what isn't there. My studio occupies the theoretical space between the fourteenth and fifteenth floors of a tower that skipped those numbers in its architectural counting. You reach it by climbing stairs that exist only while ascending—they dissolve behind each footfall and reform ahead of the next, a constantly moving island of maybe-marble that I've learned to trust will probably continue existing.

My body has begun the unraveling, though slower than most. My left hand became translucent first, then my right shoulder, then patches of my torso like windows opening in my skin. I can see my ribs now, delicate as bird bones, each one inscribed with tiny equations I don't remember learning. My heart beats visibly, a wet red fist clenching and unclenching around nothing, pumping blood that sometimes takes the scenic route through empty air before returning to its vessels.

The maps I make cannot be drawn on anything as crude as paper. I weave them from the spaces between seconds, stretch them on frames of crystallized intention. To create a map of the Third District's recursive gardens, I spent forty days walking its paths backward, letting the routes inscribe themselves in the soles of my feet until I could feel the pattern like a migraine. Then I peeled that knowledge from my skin—it came away like sunburn, translucent and warm—and pressed it between sheets of compressed silence until it set.

Each map shows not where things are, but where they aren't anymore, or where they never were, or where they might stop being tomorrow. The Unravelers commission them, trading fragments of their older selves as payment—a memory of taste here, the concept of purple there. I accept these currencies and weave them into my work. My map of the Inverse Cathedral's non-basement includes the smell of bread that one of them remembered from before, though neither of us can recall what bread actually was.

My instruments have evolved with me. My compass points to absences. My ruler measures the distance between things that don't exist. My theodolite observes angles that shouldn't be possible, recording geometries that make the mind hiccup. I've developed a new form of surveying that involves standing very still and letting the city move around me, noting where it hesitates, where it forgets itself, where it accidentally becomes somewhere else.

The other Unravelers see me as young, unripe, still too solid to truly understand. They're right. I still remember my name sometimes, though each time it sounds less familiar, like a word in a language I'm forgetting how to speak. I still hunger for food occasionally, though eating has become an abstract exercise—the food falls through my translucent throat, visible in its descent, dissolving before it reaches the visible knot of my stomach.

But I'm learning. Yesterday (or was it tomorrow? Time pools strangely in my studio), I successfully mapped a building that only exists when no one looks at it. I did this by training myself to see with my peripheral vision alone, spending weeks walking sideways through the city until my eyes learned to gather light differently. The resulting map can only be viewed in mirrors, and even then, it changes depending on who's looking at it in the reflection behind you.

My masterwork—still in progress, perhaps always in progress—is an atlas of the Hollowing's entire absence. Not what's here, but the shape of what's missing. Every building that should exist but doesn't, every person who left no trace of leaving, every moment that failed to happen. It requires a kind of negative cartography I'm inventing as I go, using tools made from crystallized forgetting and ink that removes rather than adds.

Sometimes I find evidence of other cartographers who came before me—half-finished maps that trail off into uncertainty, instruments abandoned mid-measurement. I study these artifacts, trying to understand where their makers went wrong, or right, or simply went. Did they achieve perfect dissolution? Did they map themselves out of existence? Or did they, like I fear I might, simply forget what they were doing and wander off into the gray luminescence, leaving their work to curl into itself like burning paper?

The city teaches me patience through its own patient unbecoming. I've learned to work slower, to let my maps ripen in their frames for months before attempting to read them. Sometimes they develop details on their own—streets I never drew appearing overnight, or districts I carefully documented vanishing as if they'd never been there at all. The maps are becoming autonomous, creating the territories they describe, or perhaps erasing them. I'm no longer certain which direction the causality flows.

My memories of the outside world grow thinner each day. I remember there was color there—aggressive, shouting colors that insisted on being seen. There was weather that actually arrived instead of just being suggested. There were people who stayed the same shape from one conversation to the next. But these memories feel like stories now, myths from a denser place that might have existed, or might have been a collective dream we've all agreed to stop having.

In my clearer moments—usually during the twice-daily resonance when the city breathes its silent chord—I understand that I'm not really making maps anymore. I'm being unmade into a map, my body becoming a living diagram of the Hollowing's impossible geography. My veins trace its streets, my thoughts follow its non-Euclidean architecture, my breathing syncs with its tidal absence. Soon, perhaps, someone will need to map me, to document the space where a cartographer used to be.

But not yet. Not quite yet. There are still so many absences to document, so many nowheres that need their shapes recorded before they finish unhappening. My translucent fingers still grip my impossible instruments. My partly-visible eyes still discern the difference between what's ceasing to exist and what never existed at all.

I am the Cartographer of Absences, and I am learning to map myself into the territory I document, one transparent inch at a time. When I finally complete my unraveling, my maps will remain, if remaining is something things still do here. They will be perfect—empty of everything except the shape of what's missing, which is, after all, the only honest map of a place like the Hollowing.

And perhaps, if someone finds them before they too dissolve, they'll understand what I'm only beginning to grasp: that absence has a geography all its own, and someone needs to navigate it, even if that someone is gradually becoming no one at all.
