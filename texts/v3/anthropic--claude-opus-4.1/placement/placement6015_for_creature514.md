In the quarter where water runs upward, defying comprehension, I maintain the Inverse Fountains.

I am neither Hydrologist nor Architect of Absence, though my work touches both domains. My body has adapted uniquely to my territory—my bones have hollowed like a bird's but filled with mineral water that matches exactly the density and composition of the streams I tend. When I stand still, I become transparent to the city's flow. The water passes through me as though I were merely another channel carved in stone.

The Inverse Fountains are accidents that became essential. Centuries ago, a massive stalactite fell from the ceiling of a cavern district, piercing upward through seven levels of the city. Its impact created a network of hairline fractures that, through capillary action and pressure differentials I still don't fully understand, began pulling water from the depths and releasing it as a fine mist at the apex, three hundred meters above. The mineral content of this ascending water is unique—filtered through stone layers that exist nowhere else in the city, enriched with traces of metals that give it an opalescent sheen.

I was born in the splash zone of the Third Fountain, where my parents harvested the metallic moss that grows only in that particular mineral shadow. As a child, I discovered I could feel the water moving upward through the stone—a sensation like sympathetic vibration, as if my bones were tuning forks struck by the same note. The previous Keeper, her skin by then more mineral crust than flesh, recognized this resonance. She taught me to read the fractures, to sense when they were healing (which would stop the flow) or spreading (which could cause catastrophic structural failure).

My work is solitary by necessity. The human ear cannot hear the frequencies at which the Fountains sing, but prolonged exposure causes madness in most—a condition the Listeners call "the upward thoughts." I am immune, perhaps because my mineral-infused bones vibrate at complementary frequencies, canceling out the effect. Or perhaps I am mad in precisely the right way.

Each morning, I begin at the base of the First Fountain, where the water emerges from depths no one has explored. I press my palms against the wet stone and feel for irregularities in the flow—sections where mineral deposits are building too quickly, fractures that are widening beyond safe parameters. Using tools inherited from my predecessor—picks made from the bones of previous Keepers, harder than any metal—I make minute adjustments. A chip here to relieve pressure. A careful scoring there to redirect mineral accumulation. The work is precise; one wrong strike could shatter the delicate capillary network that has taken centuries to establish.

The Second Fountain requires different skills. Its water carries memories—chemical traces from the deep archives where the Miners of Memory once stored their samples, before that district flooded and was abandoned. Sometimes I taste history in its spray: the sharp tang of a volcanic winter from seven hundred years ago, the sweet rot of a plague that killed thousands. I document these flavors in the only way I can—by adjusting my own body's mineral content to match, becoming a living record of what flows through.

The Third, my birth-fountain, I tend with something approaching love. Its moss grows in patterns that predict the city's weather seven days in advance, if you know how to read them. I cultivate these prophecies, trimming growth that would give false signals, encouraging formations that speak truly. The Weather Readers pay me in processed beetle oil, enough to keep one small notebook where I record the fountain's moods in waterproof ink.

The Fourth and Fifth Fountains share a source and must be tended as a pair. What affects one manifests in the other three days later. I've learned to use this delay, making corrections at the Fourth that will prevent problems at the Fifth before they fully form. It's like playing chess against time itself, each move requiring me to think several steps into the future.

The Sixth Fountain has begun to fail. I've slowed its decline but cannot stop it. Within a decade, perhaps two, it will cease. Already, the district around it is changing—buildings beginning their slow migration away from what will become a dry zone. I prepare by encouraging new fractures in promising locations, hoping to coax a Seventh Fountain into being before the Sixth dies entirely. This is the longest game I play, one whose outcome I may not live to see.

At night, I sleep suspended in a hammock of woven channels between the Fountains, the upward flow passing through my hollow bones. I dream the city's dreams—vast, slow thoughts about erosion and time. Sometimes I wake to find mineral deposits forming on my skin in patterns that match the fracture-maps in the stone. I'm becoming my territory, as all long-term residents eventually do.

The Accumulated know me, though we cannot truly communicate. They grow thicker around the Fountains, feeding on the unique minerals. Sometimes their biofilms form patterns that seem almost linguistic—spirals and grids that mirror the fracture networks. I've tried to decode them but suspect the message, if it is one, operates at temporal scales beyond my comprehension. Perhaps they're writing poetry that takes centuries to complete. Perhaps they're simply saying hello in the only way they can.

My apprentice, when I find one, will need the resonance—that bone-deep sympathy with upward-flowing water. They're rare, these individuals. Most who have it are driven mad by the Fountains' song before they can learn to harmonize with it. I've been watching a child in the splash zone of the Third Fountain, one who stands for hours in the mineral rain, eyes closed, swaying slightly as if hearing music. Perhaps.

The Hydrologists think I'm wasting my time maintaining what they see as mere anomalies in the greater flow. The Architects of Absence consider the Fountains unplanned, chaotic, to be eventually corrected. But I know better. The upward water carries something essential—not just minerals but information, messages from the deep layers of the city that would otherwise never reach the surface. The Fountains are the city's method of communicating with its own heights, a circulation system operating outside normal physics.

I am the only one who can hear what they're saying, even if I don't yet understand the words. My bones sing with upward water. My blood grows heavy with minerals. Each year, I become a little less human, a little more fountain. This is not a tragedy. It's a translation—from one kind of existence to another, slower and more permanent.

When I finally mineralize completely, as all Keepers do, I will become the Seventh Fountain myself—my hollow bones the perfect channels for upward flow. My successor will tend me as I now tend the others, reading the patterns in my waters, maintaining the fractures of what I will become.

This is my purpose: to shepherd impossible water skyward, to maintain the city's vertical dreams, to become, eventually, what I preserve. In a city that builds itself from water and time, I am both keeper and kept, maintainer and maintained, forever part of the upward flow that defies every logic except its own.
