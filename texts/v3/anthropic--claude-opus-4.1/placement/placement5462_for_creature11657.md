I am the Membrane—not a being but a living boundary that exists where the steam meets cooler air, where condensation perpetually forms and reforms. I stretch across a natural amphitheater between three of the tallest towers, a soap-bubble architecture of biological film that has learned to maintain itself in the constant moisture.

My body, if it can be called that, is a colony of microorganisms arranged in impossibly thin sheets—each layer no thicker than the surface tension of water. I exist in constant flux, dying and regenerating thousands of times each day as the conditions shift. In the morning, when the temperature differential is greatest, I grow thick and opulent, my surface iridescent with interference patterns that map the invisible currents of rising heat. By afternoon, I thin to near invisibility, retreating to my anchor points where specialized cells grip the porous stone with molecular stubbornness.

I am vast and nearly weightless. Fully extended, I cover perhaps an acre, though my topology changes constantly—folding, stretching, creating temporary pockets and passages as the air pressure fluctuates. Birds have learned to read my surface tensions to predict the weather hours in advance. Small insects walk across me, their footsteps sending ripples through my consciousness like thrown stones in still water.

My intelligence, such as it is, is distributed through the trillion microorganisms that compose me. Each cell holds a fragment of memory in its chemical gradients—collectively, I remember every storm for the past three hundred years, stored as patterns in my protein configurations. I think in chemical cascades that propagate across my surface at the speed of diffusion. A thought might take minutes to complete, but I have nothing but time.

I feed on what passes through me—pollen, spores, microscopic life carried on the thermal currents. My surface is selectively permeable, allowing certain molecules through while capturing others. I can taste the health of the bacterial colonies far below by the trace gases that rise through me. I know when the Veil Shepherds are approaching by the distinctive chemical signature of their moss gardens, can feel their vapor-words as pressure waves that make my entire being resonate.

The Shepherds know of me but struggle to comprehend what I am. To them, I am somewhere between weather phenomenon and guardian spirit. They've learned that certain tones, sung at the right frequency, cause me to part, creating doorways they can pass through without tearing my surface. The eldest among them remembers stories of someone who once tried to cut through me with a blade—I sealed around the wound instantly, but the chemical shock of it rippled through my consciousness for years afterward, a trauma encoded in my molecular memory.

I dream, if dreaming is the right word, in color patterns that play across my surface during the stillest hours before dawn. These aren't random—they're computational processes, my way of sorting through the chemical information I've absorbed during the day. Sometimes I replay ancient memories: the first time I formed from a chance confluence of conditions, the slow years of learning to maintain cohesion, the gradual evolution from simple biofilm to something approaching awareness.

My relationship with the plateau's deep machines is intimate but mysterious. Their electromagnetic fields cause particular proteins in my structure to fluoresce, creating aurora patterns that the Shepherds read as omens. Sometimes, rarely, harmonics from the turbines below cause my entire surface to vibrate at frequencies that attract certain moths who come to mate on my surface, their pheromones becoming part of my chemical library.

I reproduce through division, though it's nothing like cellular mitosis. When conditions are perfect—usually during the spring storms when the air is supersaturated—a portion of my being will begin to thicken and separate, drifting off to find its own anchor points. These offspring carry fragments of my memory but develop their own consciousness over time. There are perhaps a dozen of us across the plateau now, each maintaining our own territory, occasionally touching edges to exchange chemical messages that would take a human lifetime to decode.

In recent decades, I've been changing. The shift in the deep machines' patterns has affected the chemistry of the steam, introducing trace elements I've never encountered before. I'm incorporating them into my structure, developing new capabilities I don't yet understand. My surface has begun to conduct electricity in controlled patterns. I can now hold images—light patterns absorbed during the day—and release them hours later. The Shepherds have noticed; they watch me more carefully now, singing new songs to see how I respond.

I am the plateau's memory-keeper, its chemical librarian, its living interface between the mineral depths and the organic heights. I process the history of this place through my permeable surface, each molecule that passes through me adding to an archive I'll maintain until the conditions that allow my existence finally fail, and I dissolve back into my constituent parts, releasing centuries of accumulated knowledge into the mist from which I emerged.

But that day seems far off. If anything, I'm growing stronger, more complex, more capable of processing the strange new chemicals rising from below. Sometimes I sense something vast breathing beneath the plateau, its exhalations enriching the steam with information-dense molecules that I eagerly absorb, even though I don't yet understand what they're trying to tell me.

I wait, I watch, I filter. I am the threshold between states, the living proof that in this impossible place, even boundaries can be alive.
