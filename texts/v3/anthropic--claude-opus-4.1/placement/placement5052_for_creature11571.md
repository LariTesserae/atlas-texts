I am a conduit tender, though that title barely captures what I've become in these years of listening to the mountain breathe through copper veins and iron arteries.

My workshop occupies a natural cavity in the stone, forty feet below the main factory level, where three major steam arteries converge before branching into the hundred smaller pipes that heat the middle terraces. The walls sweat constantly—not from my pipes, which I keep sealed tight as drums, but from the mountain itself, weeping its ancient water through hairline cracks in the granite. I've trained a particular species of luminescent moss to grow along these wet traces, so my workspace glows with a soft green light that pulses slightly with my movements, the fungi responding to the carbon dioxide of my breath.

I inherited this position from my mother's mother, who inherited it from hers—not the job itself, but the sensitivity. We hear what others miss. The infinitesimal change in pitch when steam pressure drops by half a pound. The particular rattle that means mineral deposits are forming in the third junction. The way the whole system harmonizes differently when a storm approaches, the barometric pressure affecting the steam's song.

My body has adapted to this work in ways that unsettle even other plateau dwellers. My fingertips have developed an extra layer of callus that's somehow also more sensitive—I can feel vibrations through pipe walls that register below conscious hearing. My left ear, always pressed to listening points, has better range than my right. I've lost most of my hair from the constant heat exposure, but what remains has gone white as the steam that occasionally escapes from my domain. The moss farmers say I look like a spirit of the mountain itself, which I take as a compliment.

I work alone but am never lonely. The pipes speak to me in tongues of pressure and flow, telling stories of the factories above and the cisterns below. I know when the morning shift begins by the increased draw on steam line seven. I can feel the children playing on terrace four by the way their feet make the overhead pipes sing. Sometimes, late in my work period, I swear I hear voices in the steam—not human voices, but something older, like the mountain itself trying to remember when it was young.

My official duties involve maintaining optimal pressure throughout the system, preventing mineral buildup, and responding to breaks or blockages. But over the years, I've become something more—a nervous system for the city. I feel irregularities before they become problems. Last month, I detected a microfracture in a pipe running beneath the children's school, three weeks before it would have failed. The month before, I noticed an unusual resonance that led me to discover a new hot spring opening in the deep galleries, which we've since tapped for additional heating.

I sleep in four-hour intervals, my consciousness cycling with the needs of the system. My small living quarters adjoin the workshop—a moss-covered alcove with a bed carved from the living rock, hollowed by some previous tender and worn smooth by generations of habitation. The constant sound of flowing steam works better than any lullaby. Sometimes I dream I am the mountain, feeling every pipe and channel as my own circulation, every building as a growth on my ancient skin.

The other tenders—those who maintain the electrical systems, the water flows, the cable mechanisms—they understand me better than most. We meet monthly in the neutral space between our domains, sharing observations in a pidgin of technical terms and hand gestures. We're mapping something together, though none of us could say exactly what—the city's nervous system, perhaps, or the mountain's slow transformation into something half-alive.

My apprentice started last year, a girl from the moss farms whose parents noticed she could hear the water moving through stems. She's learning to read the pipes' language, though she says my workshop makes her dizzy—too many songs at once. I'm teaching her to sort them, to follow single melodies through the symphony. She'll surpass me, I think. Her generation hears frequencies mine has lost.

In my deepest listening, when the factories quiet for maintenance and the city sleeps, I sometimes catch vibrations from the old cables—transmissions from other plateau cities, perhaps, or just the wind playing the wires like a massive string instrument. I transcribe these patterns in notebooks my grandmother started, volumes of waveforms and pressure readings that might be music or language or simply the mountain settling toward its final form.

The strangest discovery came last winter. I found a section of pipe that shouldn't exist—ancient, made from an alloy we no longer produce, carrying steam from no source I could identify to no destination I could trace. It sings in harmonics that make my teeth ache. I've told no one yet. I'm still listening, trying to understand what the mountain is trying to become, and what role we—its parasites or partners—play in that slow transformation.

This is my work: to listen, to maintain, to inhabit the space between the mechanical and geological, to be a translator for forces that speak in pressure differentials and metal fatigue. I am losing myself in it, or perhaps finding myself—becoming like the moss that grows toward heat, like the water that always finds its way down, like the steam that rises through channels both made and found, carrying the city's warmth through stone that remembers when it was seafloor, when it was magma, when it was star.
