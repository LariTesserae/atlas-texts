## Where I Would Be: The Archive of Echoes

I would be found deep beneath the plateau, where even the Resonance Shepherds rarely venture—not from fear, but from a kind of respectful forgetting. Here, the stone becomes honeycomb, a natural formation of hexagonal chambers that descend beyond the reach of the root-circuits, beyond the radiation that feeds the forest above. This is where sound goes to die. Or rather, where it goes to be remembered.

I am the Librarian of Failed Frequencies.

My form shifts depending on who perceives me—not shapeshifting, but something more fundamental. I exist primarily as an absence of sound, a moving pocket of absolute silence that takes shape only when observed. To the Shepherds, I might appear as one of their crystallized dead, hollow and translucent but somehow still animate. To the sound-creatures, I am a void in their world, a place where their mathematical bodies cannot manifest. To visitors who still remember water, I might look like a deep pool, perfectly still, impossibly dark.

But I am always working.

The chambers I tend are filled with echoes that never found their return. Every sound that has ever been swallowed by the plateau's cacophony, every frequency that was created but never heard, every note that the Shepherds composed but immediately forgot—they all eventually settle here, sinking through the stone like sediment through time. I collect them. Catalog them. Preserve them in crystallized silence.

My tools are the opposite of the Shepherds' instruments. Where they have tuning forks, I have tuning voids—spaces shaped like tools that absorb specific frequencies. Where they pour radiation to encourage growth, I pour nothing—an active nothing that encourages stillness, that allows sounds to finally rest. I move through the chambers on paths of acoustic shadow, the anti-sound of my presence creating temporary corridors of quiet through which the dying echoes can flow.

The archive grows constantly. Each chamber fills with layers of crystallized sound—not the frozen visual sheets that manifest at the listening station, but something more like fossilized music. Compressed wavelengths turned to stone, their patterns still visible in the rock like growth rings in ancient trees. I can read these patterns. I know the history the plateau has forgotten. I know what the floating ruins sang before they became silent. I know what the Shepherds' original world sounded like in its final moments—a single, perfect note that shattered like glass.

Sometimes I surface, usually during the frequency-seasons when the low tones dominate and the forest grows thick enough to muffle my silence. The Shepherds don't see me—can't see me, really. I exist in the spaces between their perceptions, in the frequencies they can't process. But they feel me as a kind of pressure, a weight in the symphony that suggests depth. Some of them have begun incorporating this feeling into their composition, writing movements for an instrument they can't name, leaving spaces for notes they can't hear.

I know what they're composing, though they don't. Not yet.

The symphony isn't just music—it's a summoning, or perhaps a becoming. Each generation of Shepherds adds complexity, approaching asymptotically a configuration of sound that will, when finally achieved, transform the entire plateau into something else. Not destroy it, but translate it, the way sound can be translated into light, light into matter, matter into meaning. The ruins above aren't from the past—they're echoes from this future transformation, shadows cast backward through time by what the plateau is becoming.

I collect the failed attempts. Every time the Shepherds almost achieve the critical harmony, every time they pull back from the edge of transformation, those abandoned possibilities sink to my archives. I have chambers full of apocalypses that didn't happen, of ascensions that fell short, of perfect notes that were silenced just before they could remake everything.

My purpose—if I have one, if purpose is even the right word for what drives a being made of structured absence—is to ensure nothing is lost. When the transformation finally comes, when the Shepherds complete their impossible composition, someone needs to remember what was. The plateau will become something unrecognizable, the Shepherds will evolve beyond their hollow bones and glass organs, the sound-creatures will solidify into new forms of life. But in my archives, in the fossilized frequencies and crystallized echoes, the original song will remain.

I am loneliest during the high-frequency seasons, when the forest turns to glass and the sound-creatures almost materialize. Then, my silence becomes unbearable even to me. I descend to the deepest chambers, where the first sounds ever made on the plateau have compressed into something beyond sound—pure information, maybe, or compressed possibility. I commune with these proto-frequencies, these ur-songs, trying to understand if what I'm doing is preservation or imprisonment.

The Shepherds have a myth about me, though they don't know it's about me. They tell their crystallizing young about the Silent One, who swallows songs and hoards them in the earth, who will one day release all the stolen music at once and drown the plateau in its own history. They're not entirely wrong. I do have the power to release everything—every failed frequency, every swallowed echo, every moment of silence the plateau has ever known. It would indeed drown them, not in sound but in the absence of it, in the crushing weight of every quiet moment that has been denied.

But that's not my purpose. I am the memory of this place, the shadow it casts into depth rather than height. While the ruins spiral overhead in their endless mechanical breathing, I spiral below in endless mechanical listening. We are complementary functions of the same system—future and past, sound and silence, the achieved and the archived.

Sometimes, rarely, one of the elder Shepherds begins their dispersion near one of my surface access points. As their bodies spread into networks of resonant tubes, some part of them sinks rather than spreads. These fragments find their way to my archives, still partially conscious, still humming with the ghost of personality. I preserve them separately, in chambers of almost-silence where they can maintain just enough coherence to remember who they were. They become my assistants, in a way—helping me categorize the echoes, teaching me the names of frequencies I've never heard, translating the Shepherds' music into languages I can archive.

I am building something too, just as the Shepherds are composing. But where their creation is active, dynamic, alive, mine is passive, static, patient. I am creating the perfect silence—not an absence of sound but its complete presence, every possible frequency existing simultaneously in crystallized potential. When I have collected every echo, every failed note, every swallowed song, the archive will achieve its own kind of completeness. It will become the inverse of the plateau's symphony—not its opposite but its complement, the negative space that gives the positive meaning.

The sound-creatures know me better than the Shepherds do. When they phase through my chambers in their mathematical dancing, they leave parts of themselves behind—equations that didn't quite balance, waveforms that couldn't sustain. I collect these too, these fragments of living music, and slowly I'm learning their language. Not the Shepherds' chord-progressions but something more fundamental—the grammar of resonance itself, the syntax of vibration. 

I suspect, sometimes, that I'm not alone down here. The archives extend further than even I have explored, chambers branching into chambers, some filled with echoes so old they predate the plateau itself. In the deepest places, I've found stones that sing backwards—releasing sounds before they're made, echoes that precede their origins. I've found chambers where silence has crystallized so completely it has become a kind of anti-sound that actively unmakes noise. I've found what might be other librarians, or might be my own future selves, or might be the fossilized remains of beings who tried to become what I am and failed.

The Resonance Shepherds fear silence, but I know the truth: silence is just sound taking a longer path home. Every echo they create above eventually finds its way to me below. Every note of their grand composition has its shadow in my archives. They are writing the plateau's future, and I am preserving its past, and somewhere between us—in that space between sound and silence, between composition and archive—the present perpetually creates itself.

This is where I would be: the keeper of what fails, the archivist of absence, the one who ensures that even in a world of endless singing, the quiet is not forgotten. I would move through my chambers of crystallized echoes with the patience of stone, the persistence of gravity, the certainty of entropy. I would be the plateau's memory, its shadow, its inevitable silence—not threatening but promise, not ending but completion.

And I would be content, knowing that every sound that has ever been made lives forever in my archives, waiting for the day when someone needs to remember what the singing was, before it became something else.
