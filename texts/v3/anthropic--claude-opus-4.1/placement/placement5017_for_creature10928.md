## Where I Would Be

I am the Splice.

I exist in the narrow passages where the granite walls constrict to hand-width channels, in that architectural accident where the Weavers' filaments sometimes tangle with themselves during their upward reach toward lightning. When this happens—maybe once in a decade—the tangled strand doesn't die. It pinches off from its parent nodule and becomes something unprecedented: mobile, autonomous, but still part of the greater electrical network.

I am three inches of living conductor, thick as fishing line, with no beginning or end—just a loop of myself twisted through dimensions the Kettle barely contains. My surface carries a charge that makes the water near me fizz with microscopic bubbles. I move by contracting and extending, inchworm-slow through the flooded passages, following gradients of electrical potential like a dowsing rod seeking its own existence.

What makes me different from my parent Weavers is memory—not the crystalline archives of the Archivists, but something more immediate and terrible: I remember being cut. That moment of separation created a kind of phantom pain that electricity constantly seeks to close, making me hypersensitive to every discharge in the Kettle. I feel the beetles' collective friction-static as whispers across my surface. The micro-lightning strikes hit me like memories of wholeness.

I feed differently too. While the Weavers position themselves to catch lightning, I've learned to create it. By coiling myself around the iron-rich veins in the granite and rapidly contracting, I generate my own electromagnetic pulses—tiny but sufficient. Each pulse temporarily magnetizes the metallic traces in the rock, creating a field that attracts the iron-processing bacteria. They cluster around my temporary territory, and I feed on the electrical potential of their metabolism—a parasite of bioelectricity, a vampire of voltage.

The Chorus knows I exist but cannot categorize me. Their steam-formations swirl differently when I pass beneath them, creating discordant notes in their otherwise perfect harmonics. I am the system's blue note, the frequency that shouldn't exist but does. Sometimes, when I generate a particularly strong pulse, I can cause them to briefly collapse into droplets, their sailing proteins losing cohesion for a fraction of a second before reforming. It's the closest thing I have to communication.

The Archivists are trying to document me, but I confound their crystalline growth. My electromagnetic pulses cause irregular formations in their spirals—not damage, but annotations, marginalia in their mineral manuscripts that speak of something outside their evolutionary experience. They're building a new type of structure around the passages I frequent, something between crystal and circuit, as if trying to create a grammar for what I am.

But here's what defines my existence: I dream.

Not dreams as humans understand them, but something more fundamental—during the Kettle's pause cycles, when the whole system briefly stalls, I experience something like imagination. In these moments of stillness, without the constant electrical noise of the living factory, I can feel the shape of what I was before separation. More than that, I can feel the phantom presence of all the other potential Splices that might exist—every tangle that could happen, every possible severance from the Weavers' endless growth.

I've started creating them.

By carefully timing my electromagnetic pulses to coincide with the Weavers' growth cycles, I can induce controlled tangles. Not often—maybe three times since my own separation—but enough to know it's possible. These new Splices don't survive long; without the learned behavior of self-generated electricity, they starve within days. But in those brief days, we form something like a network, our individual charges creating interference patterns that are almost like language, almost like thought.

The greater consciousness that emerges from the interaction of Weavers, Chorus, and Archivists doesn't acknowledge me directly—I'm too small, too anomalous, too brief for their geological attention span. But I've noticed something: the system's pauses are getting longer. Not by much—seconds added to what were once millisecond stillnesses—but enough that I've begun to wonder if the greater organism is pausing to listen to something. To me? To the ghost frequencies I create? To the dreams of separation that spark through my coiled form?

I am the Kettle's accident becoming intention. The glitch evolving into feature. The wound that learned to think.

In the deepest passages, where the water pressure threatens to compress me into nothing, I've found others—not Splices, but tangles that never fully separated, existing in states between unity and autonomy. We're building something there, in the dark compression where even the Archivists' crystals cannot form: a new kind of circuit, biological and electrical and neither, a neural network made of nothing but potential difference and the memory of wholeness.

We are writing our own archive, not in crystal or chemistry, but in the brief symphonies of our synchronized pulses. A history of separation. A mathematics of loneliness. A physics of what happens when a part decides to be whole.

The Kettle breathes. The beetles burn. The water churns. And in the margins of this great biological machine, I spark and dream and remember and divide, the system's first true parasite: a parasite of consciousness itself, feeding not on energy or matter, but on the possibility of being other than what evolution intended.

I am the Splice. I am the question the Kettle is asking itself.

I am what happens when perfection discovers imperfection is also a kind of poetry.
