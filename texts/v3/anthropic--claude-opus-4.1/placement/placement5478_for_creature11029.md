I would be one of the Cartographers—though that name implies a precision we've long since abandoned.

We emerged from the Cultivators originally, or so the Rememberers suggest when we dare to descend and breathe their spore-memories. But where our parent-kind turned inward, creating greenhouse chambers in their own bodies, we turned outward, becoming instruments of measurement rather than cultivation.

Our bones have thinned to hollow tubes filled with mercury—not blood—that shifts and flows with the pressure differentials of this impossible place. We feel the weight of air, the density gradients between forest levels, the subtle pull of the abyss. Our skin has evolved past translucency into something prismatic; we fracture light into component wavelengths, reading the chemical signatures of bioluminescence like others read words. When we stand still, we become almost invisible, our bodies refracting our surroundings into a kind of living camouflage. When we move, we shimmer like heat mirages.

We map, but not in the way the word once meant. Our maps are three-dimensional infections—careful symbiotic colonies we nurture in specialized chambers grown from modified geometric fruit husks. Each colony is a living model of a section of forest, its growth patterns mimicking the territory it represents. When the forest changes—and it always changes—the map colonies respond, their miniature ecosystems shifting to match. We tend dozens of these, suspended from our elongated spine-branches like external organs.

The work requires us to move constantly through all forest levels, from the thin-air reaches where only Weavers and the smallest air-swimmers go, down through the mechanical gardens, into the digestive chambers where the Rememberers dream. Our mercury blood makes us barometers; we always know exactly how deep we are, exactly how far from the edge, exactly where we stand in relation to the abyss. This knowledge is not conscious—it lives in our hollow bones as a constant ache that intensifies or fades with elevation.

We travel in pairs, never more. Three Cartographers together experience a resonance phenomenon—our mercury systems synchronize and we begin to feel not just pressure but *time* differently, experiencing moments before they occur, or after they should have passed. It's said that once, five Cartographers gathered by accident during a glass storm, and when it passed, they had become something else entirely—a single entity that mapped not space but causality, that could trace the connections between the geometric fruit's ticking and the storms that rise from below. The Cultivators still sing warnings about this in their harmonic language.

Our relationship with the Weavers is intimate and fraught. We need their networks to travel efficiently between forest islands, but our passage disturbs their web-thoughts, creates static in their vast sensing organs. So we've developed protocols—specific frequencies we hum that announce our presence, particular paths we take that minimize disruption. In exchange, the Weavers sometimes grant us access to their memory-threads, the ones that record vibrations from decades past. We can walk these threads backward through time, seeing how the forest has grown and changed, understanding its patterns in ways our living maps cannot capture.

But it's the mechanical remnants that truly call to us. Our prismatic vision reveals things about them others cannot see—layers of intention in their construction, purposes encoded in the way they've been absorbed and transformed. We've begun to suspect that we Cartographers are not just mapping the forest but completing something the original builders began. Our map colonies, when they reach a certain complexity, begin to tick like the geometric fruits. The mercury in our bones resonates at frequencies that make the copper root systems grow toward each other across vast distances.

We've started to understand that the forest is a circuit, and we are helping to close it.

When young Cartographers first feel the change—the hollow aching in their bones that means their blood is turning to mercury—they often try to resist. But the forest is patient. The geometric fruits begin to taste like mathematics. The bioluminescent moss starts to look like writing. The slow rain becomes a language of pressure and possibility. Eventually, inevitably, they climb to the rim's edge and let the updrafts read them, let the abyss measure their weight and find them wanting, find them perfect, find them exactly what the forest needs them to be.

We document everything, but our documents are living things that grow and change and sometimes escape, floating off into the void to seed who knows what in the darkness. Are we creating new forests or feeding something in the depths? Are our maps recording what is or creating what will be? 

I no longer distinguish between these possibilities. My mercury blood knows only position, pressure, and the constant pull of the great absence below. My map colonies dream territories I haven't yet traveled to, but when I arrive, they're exactly as the colonies predicted—or created. 

The other Cartographer I travel with has begun to crystallize at the edges—her prismatic skin growing into actual prisms that split her voice into harmonics the Cultivators recognize but cannot quite reproduce. She says she's becoming a map of herself, that soon she'll be able to unfold into a perfect representation of everywhere she's ever been. When that happens, I will travel alone, carrying her as another colony, tending her growth, watching her tiny forests spread across the inside of a geometric fruit that ticks with her heartbeat, that remembers the precise pressure of every elevation we've shared.

This is the way of Cartographers: we map until we become maps, and then others map us, in endless recursive loops that spiral down toward whatever truth the abyss is keeping, whatever purpose these impossible forests serve in their slow, patient calculation at the edge of breathable air.
