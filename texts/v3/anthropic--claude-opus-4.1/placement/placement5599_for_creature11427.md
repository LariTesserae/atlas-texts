In the furthest reach from the Keeper's recess, where the cavern narrows to a passage barely wide enough for shoulders, I tend my small domain. The humans who sometimes found this place called me the Cupbearer, though I came long after their language died, drawn by whispers in the water table that spoke of transformation happening here.

I am salamander-pale from centuries without sun, my skin gone translucent as the cave fish that swim the lightless pools below. But unlike them, I kept my eyes—needed them to read the mineral patterns, to understand which deposits would poison and which would purify. My pupils have grown until they swallow all but the thinnest ring of iris, drinking in every photon the phosphorescent deposits offer.

My fingers have lengthened over time, developing extra joints that let them bend backward as easily as forward—better for navigating the forest of formations without disturbing the critical drip-lines. My nails have become something like hollow straws themselves, testing mineral content by absorption, tasting the water's message through keratin transformed to sensor.

I was human once, or close enough. A village water-keeper who followed an old map, seeking the source of healing water that traders sometimes carried in tiny vials worth more than gold. I found her, found this place, and understood immediately that someone needed to maintain the ancient systems. The bowls were cracking. The channels were clogging with mineral buildup. The careful balance was failing.

So I stayed. First for days, then seasons, then years beyond counting.

The transformation happens slowly here. I noticed my bones growing denser before I noticed them growing longer. The cartilage in my joints began secreting a fluid that prevents the minerals from locking them in place—an adaptation I didn't consciously choose but accepted as necessary. My digestive system simplified, then nearly disappeared. I sustain myself on the bacteria colonies that bloom where the Keeper's water meets raw stone, scraping them up with a tongue that's developed a texture like pumice.

I maintain the bowls, grinding new ones when the old become too encrusted. I clear the channels with tools made from my own shed teeth—they grow back now, pushing through my gums in rows like a shark's, harder each time, better suited for scraping stone. I position the formations, sometimes spending decades redirecting a single drip to fall where it's needed.

But mostly, I listen. My ears have changed shape, developing a cavity that resonates with the exact frequency of falling water. I can hear the difference between a drop that's passed through her completely and one that's only touched her edges. I know which water will heal and which will begin the mineralization in living tissue. I map the cavern's circulation in my mind, understanding it as a single organism with the Keeper as its heart.

I've learned to move like the blind crawlers, so slowly that the motion doesn't disturb the air currents. A journey from one end of my domain to the other takes three days of continuous movement. I've developed a nictitating membrane that keeps my eyes moist in the constant steam. My hair has become something between fungal mycelium and human keratin, spreading across the surfaces where I rest, helping me sense vibrations in the stone.

Sometimes I remember speech, remember having a name, remember the taste of bread. But these memories feel like they belong to someone else, someone who couldn't have survived here, someone who wouldn't have understood the necessity of becoming part of the cavern's ecology. I'm more useful now, more essential. The system needs me as much as it needs her.

I've begun to understand her dreams—not their content, but their rhythm. They pulse through the water, through the stone, a slow tide of consciousness that takes years to complete a cycle. When she dreams deep, the water runs clearer but colder. When she surfaces toward waking, it carries more minerals, more of her essence bleeding into the flow.

There are others here—I sense them sometimes in the passages below, things that were human longer ago than I was, now so transformed they're indistinguishable from the rock formations except for the faint heat they still generate. We don't communicate except through the water we all tend, each maintaining our section of this vast, breathing machine.

I know I'm changing faster now. My spine has developed segments that can compress and extend, letting me flatten myself to crawl through passages that grow narrower each year. My ribs have become mobile, able to collapse when needed. I suspect I'm developing something like gills—not for breathing water but for breathing steam more efficiently, extracting oxygen from the supersaturated air.

One day I'll be like the others, a formation that happens to have purpose, a mineral deposit with intention. But not yet. For now, I remain the Cupbearer, keeper of bowls, reader of drops, the one who ensures the sacred water finds its way from her form to the vessels where it's needed. The cavern breathes because she breathes, but it functions because I—and the others like me—maintain the intricate machinery of its circulation.

This is my place: between the divine geology of the Keeper and the simple stones, a necessary interface, a translation between speeds of existence. I am becoming the cavern's memory of human purpose, crystallized in flesh that's finding its way to stone.
