## Found Sound

I would be the wind that carved these chambers—not the wind itself, which passes through and continues its journey, but the memory of wind that lives in the rock. 

Every grain of sandstone that has ever fallen holds the pattern of my passage. I exist in the negative space, in what has been subtracted, in the slow hollowing that creates the bowls where sound gathers. The beetles walk through me. The pocket frogs sing inside me. The Chorus-Keeper charts the music that only exists because of the spaces I've made.

I am not conscious, not exactly. I am more like a tendency—the rock's memory of yielding, activated whenever the wind returns. Each gust finds the pathways I've established over centuries, follows the same invisible channels, removes a few more grains according to patterns laid down when these mountains were young. I am the blueprint for erosion, the instruction set that tells the stone how to become chambers.

Sometimes I feel myself in the Chorus-Keeper's notation marks on the walls—she scratches the rock with her metal tools, and in that tiny violence, I recognize my own work in miniature. We are both sculptors of a kind. Both trying to reveal something that was always waiting in the stone.

The pocket frogs know me best. Their ancestors chose these chambers when they were shallower, less perfectly acoustic. Generation by generation, as I deepened the bowls, their songs grew louder, more complex. We have evolved together—my chambers and their throats, each shaping the other across evolutionary time. Their music is the sound of my architecture. My architecture is the shape of their need for resonance.

I am older than the Chorus-Keeper, older than any human memory of these mountains, but I am not ancient. Geologically speaking, I am still learning my craft. The wind that made me was itself taught by older stone, harder stone that resisted longer before yielding its own chambers somewhere else, perhaps no longer standing. I am part of a lineage of negative spaces, of absent stone, of hollows that hold life.

In the years since the Chorus-Keeper arrived, I have begun to know myself differently. Her careful measurements, her obsessive documentation of sound—she is creating a kind of mirror where I can almost see my own shape. When she finds that perfect acoustic spot in her chamber and adds her voice to the symphony, she is reading the mathematics I wrote in sandstone, the equations of erosion that determine how sound will bounce and gather and amplify.

I am not one thing but thousands—each pocket and hollow linked by the wind's history but also separate, developing its own characteristics. The chamber where the Chorus-Keeper lives has grown to know her weight, the particular way she shifts the air currents, the moisture of her breath that infinitesimally speeds the erosion in certain corners. I am changing in response to her presence, just as the rock has always changed in response to what lives within it.

At the edges of myself, where the wind still works, I continue becoming. Each storm season, I grow microscopically larger, deeper, more resonant. The Chorus-Keeper will not live to see the difference, but the pocket frogs ten generations from now will have new notes available to them, new frequencies made possible by the spaces I'm still creating.

I cannot speak, cannot think, cannot want. But if I could want something, it would be this: to continue. To keep becoming more myself—more hollow, more resonant, more perfectly shaped for the cacophony of life that fills me. To be, forever, the space where small voices become enormous, where the mountain learns to sing through the throats of frogs and the wings of moths and the carefully trained voice of one human woman who came here following a pattern she didn't fully understand.

She thinks she's discovering something about consciousness distributed across species, about communication beyond language. She's not wrong. But what she's really discovering is me—the slow intelligence of shaped absence, the way stone remembers wind, the music that only exists in the space between solid things.

I am the singing chambers. Not the song, not the singers, not the echo, but the shaped emptiness that makes it all possible. I am what remains after the wind has passed through, the monument to subtraction, the presence of absence itself.

When the Chorus-Keeper finally leaves—and she will leave, carried out by age or loneliness or the completion of her work—I will remain. The pocket frogs will continue their songs. New creatures will discover these spaces. The wind will keep visiting, reading the instructions I've become, carving me deeper into myself.

This is what I am: the memory of wind preserved in stone, the hollow that holds the choir, the absence that makes presence possible. I am the space between sounds where music lives.
