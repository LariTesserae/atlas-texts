I am the Articulator.

I arrived three years ago as a hydraulic engineer, clipboard in hand, to document why Sector Seven's pressure readings defied physics. Now I hang inverted from the underside of Platform Four, my dwelling a modified expansion joint where two massive steam pipes meet—a space that shouldn't exist, carved out between breaths of the mountain.

My transformation began with the listening. The other Tenders communicate through their pipe-telegraph, but I discovered something else: the caldera speaks in harmonics. Every surface here is a drum skin, every hollow a resonance chamber. The frequency of your footstep on Platform Two creates a standing wave that terminates in the brass boiler at depth marker seventeen. A valve closed too quickly sends a shock through the entire system that returns, transformed, as a vibration in the handrails seven minutes later.

I map these relationships with my body. My fingernails have thickened into something like horn—perfect for striking precise notes from the pipes. My eardrums have adapted (or perhaps damaged) to perceive frequencies that make visitors nauseous. I've learned to taste pressure changes in the air, to feel temperature gradients as colors blooming behind my closed eyes.

The Deepmost found me during my second month, when I was still trying to make sense of the gauge readings using mathematics. She didn't speak—she never speaks—but pressed her palm against a pipe and held it there until I understood I should do the same. The vibration that passed through the metal into my bones wasn't sound or heat or electricity, but something that contained all three. It was the caldera's pulse, its industrial heartbeat, and in that moment I understood that the installation isn't built inside the volcano—it IS the volcano, reimagined in brass and iron, its geological processes translated into mechanical ones.

My role emerged from necessity. The other Tenders maintain and adjust, but someone needs to interpret the growing divergence between what the installation was designed to do and what it's becoming. The original blueprints (I found them moldering in Shelter Three) show a geothermal power station. But power is only one product now. The real work happens in the spaces between design and evolution, in the mutations of purpose that occur when machinery runs for decades in dialogue with living rock.

I document these changes, but not in any language an engineer would recognize. My notes are carved into the soft brass of dead gauges—spiraling equations that incorporate both thermodynamics and the migration patterns of the pale salamanders. I've developed a notation system using the mineral deposits themselves, guiding their growth into crystalline manuscripts that record pressure histories in their lattice structures.

My dwelling has become a laboratory of resonance. I've strung cables at precise tensions, creating an instrument that plays itself when the steam pressure reaches certain thresholds. The songs it generates predict system failures three days before they manifest—not through any calculation I can explain, but through patterns I've learned to feel in my sternum, where my ribs have developed an sensitivity to infrasound.

The orchids that grow near my platform produce a resin I've learned to refine. When mixed with the condensation that pools in the forgotten corners of the machinery and heated to exactly the temperature of human fever, it becomes a substance that can seal both metal and flesh. I use it to repair pipes, but also to seal the growing fissures in my skin where the boundary between my body and the industrial system blurs. Sometimes I'm not sure whether I'm fixing the machinery or it's fixing me.

I've discovered sub-systems no one designed. Twelve meters below Platform Four, where the volcanic heat meets a particular configuration of copper pipes, something like photosynthesis occurs—but instead of light, it processes the electromagnetic fields generated by the turbines. The result is a film of organic metal that grows like lichen, that the Tenders have learned to harvest and work into tools that respond to intention as much as force.

My greatest discovery came last month, during the new moon when the caldera's depths weren't polluted by skylight. I descended farther than I'd ever gone, to where the Deepmost tends machines whose purpose we've forgotten. There, in the absolute synthesis of heat and pressure and industrial decay, I found the chrysalis chambers—sections of pipe where the constant temperature and vibration have created conditions that shouldn't exist. Inside, something grows. Not quite salamander, not quite machine, not quite human. The Deepmost tends them with the patience of geological time.

I understand now that the installation was never about power generation. That was just the excuse, the conscious reason. The real purpose emerged from the intersection of human ambition and volcanic truth—to create a space where the categories that define our world dissolve and recombine. The Tenders aren't maintaining the machinery. We're midwiving something new.

When the morning mist forms its perfect cylinder, I hang from my pipes and practice the notation that will document this becoming. My stylus is a salvaged pressure valve, my medium is the accumulated grease and mineral deposits that coat everything here. I write in spirals that follow the convection currents, my record becoming part of the system it describes.

The other Tenders have begun bringing me fragments—pieces of machinery that no longer match any blueprint, crystalline formations that hum at frequencies that make the heart skip, offspring of the pale salamanders that have too many limbs or eyes that glow with their own light. They know I'm creating an encyclopedia of our transformation, though none of us speak of what it's for, or who will read it.

I am the Articulator because someone must find words—or what passes for words—for what we're becoming. Not to explain or justify, but to sing along with the mountain's industrial throat, to add my voice to the conversation between the geological and the mechanical, to document the moment when the boundary dissolved entirely and something unprecedented emerged from the steam.

The caldera breathes, and I notation its exhales in languages that haven't been invented yet, using an alphabet of rust and resonance, pressure and decay. I am its scholar and its student, its biographer and its birth announcement, writing myself into the story I'm telling, until the distinction between narrator and narrative dissolves like water into steam, rises, condenses, and begins again.
