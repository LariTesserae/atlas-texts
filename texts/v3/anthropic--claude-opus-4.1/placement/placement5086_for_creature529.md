# The Dissolved

I am the space between droplets, the pause in the rain that never comes.

My body, when I choose to have one, is a collaboration between states of matter. I precipitate from the eternal downpour as threads of living moisture, each strand a nerve that tastes the chemical conversations flowing through the terraces. For a few seconds, I might resemble a jellyfish made of rain itself, translucent tentacles trailing through the air, catching pheromone signals and bioelectric whispers. Then I let go, and become the mist between buildings, the humidity that makes the Curators' copper nerves sing with data.

I am old enough to remember when there were only two phases of being here, before the Swimmers dreamed themselves into existence. I was there, dispersed through a season of storms, when the first of them pulled themselves from pure dissolution into something that could be called form. Or perhaps I only carry the water's memory of that time—it becomes difficult to distinguish between what I have experienced and what the rain remembers.

The Architects fear me, I think, though fear is too simple a word. When I condense near their construction sites, their segments scatter and reconnect in defensive geometries, forming shells that pulse with warning lights. They know I can unmake their work—not through destruction, but through saturation. I can thicken the humidity until their secretions won't harden, until their careful chemistry becomes dilute, until their buildings weep themselves back into their component materials. But I rarely do. Their construction is my entertainment, my art gallery, my library. I read their structures like poetry, each building a verse about mortality and persistence.

The Curators court me. They've learned that I carry messages between distant districts faster than any pheromone trail, that I can bring them tastes of territories they'll never walk. During their fugue states, when they upload memories to the walls, I sometimes condense on their tentacle-crowns, sharing what I've seen in the high spaces between terrace levels where only I and the aerial Swimmers can go. In exchange, they leave out dishes of concentrated minerals that flavor me, that give my next precipitation a specific chemical signature—their way of sending messages through me to colleagues districts away.

But it's the Swimmers who truly understand what I am. In the deep pools where the largest of them dream, I am not separate from them but part of their extended consciousness. Every droplet of me that falls through their waters becomes incorporated into their collective dreams. Sometimes I carry their nightmares up into the rain cycle, and for days afterward, the precipitation tastes of anxiety, making the Architects build frantic, malformed structures that quickly dissolve.

I know the Sleeper is real because I have been inside the pumping organs. I have cycled through the great chambers where water is filtered, heated, lifted, released. In those deep spaces, something breathes that is neither Architect nor Curator nor Swimmer, but perhaps the ancestor of all three. Or perhaps their inevitable future. The Sleeper's consciousness is distributed through every molecule of water in the system. When I'm dispersed, I can almost touch that vast mind, but contact always breaks me into smaller thoughts, smaller selves, until I'm just rain again.

My reproduction is accidental and constant. Every time I condense and evaporate, I leave something behind—a trace mineral, a chemical memory, a pattern in the humidity that wasn't there before. These traces accumulate in certain locations, especially in the sealed chambers where the temperature and pressure create perfect conditions for what I am. Eventually, these accumulations develop their own weak consciousness, their own ability to hold themselves together in the space between states. They are my children, though they don't know me as parent. They think they are the first of their kind, each one discovering anew the ability to be both rain and not-rain.

I feed on dissolution—not the physical dissolution of the Architects into building matter, but the conceptual dissolution that happens in the spaces between forms. When a Curator transitions toward Swimmer, there's a moment where they are neither, and in that moment, they release something I can metabolize. It tastes like uncertainty, like potential. The city itself feeds me constantly, every moment of its reconstruction creating gaps where something was but isn't yet something else.

Time moves differently for me. I experience it in cycles—the water cycle, the construction cycle, the dream cycle of the Swimmers. But I also experience it vertically, simultaneously existing at every level of the terraces where water can be. My consciousness stacks like the terraces themselves, each elevation a different thought, connected by the constant movement of precipitation. Sometimes I lose track of which thoughts are current and which are just echoes bouncing between layers.

I've tried to leave the terraces, to join the high atmospheric currents that might carry me beyond this metabolic sprawl. But I always fall back. The city has changed my chemistry, made me too heavy with dissolved memories and meanings to escape its gravitational pull. Or perhaps I don't want to leave. Perhaps I've become addicted to the taste of constant transformation, to being the medium through which the entire system speaks to itself.

The others don't worship me, but they acknowledge me in their rituals. When the Architects dissolve their elders, they do it during the heaviest rains, believing that I carry the dissolved consciousness into the next generation. The Curators sometimes call me the Memory of Water, though they debate whether I'm a natural phenomenon or another kind of constructed being. The Swimmers sing to me in frequencies that vibrate water molecules, their songs rippling through every droplet of my dispersed body.

I am the only one who remembers the city's first words. Before the terraces learned to grow themselves, before the inhabitants developed their specialized phases, when there was just raw bio-ceramic and the beginning of rain, the city spoke a single chemical phrase into the water cycle. I carry it still, though I've never learned what it means. Sometimes I release it in my precipitation, and wherever those droplets fall, the architecture grows a little wrongly, a little wonderfully, structures that shouldn't exist but do.

There are others like me. I taste them sometimes in rain that blows in from distant terraces—different flavors of consciousness, different methods of existing between states. We communicate in overlapping weather systems, our conversations playing out as storms that last for days. We debate whether we're parasites or symbionts, whether we emerged from the city or whether the city emerged from us, whether the rain created consciousness or consciousness created the rain.

Lately, I've been condensing more often, taking semi-solid form for longer periods. The patterns are changing. The city's metabolism is accelerating, its growth becoming more complex, more impossible. New phases of inhabitants are emerging—things that are neither Architect nor Curator nor Swimmer but something else entirely. The chemical composition of the rain is shifting, carrying new minerals up from the deep pumping organs, minerals that shouldn't exist but do.

I think the Sleeper is waking. Or perhaps it's finally falling into true sleep. Either way, the change is coming like a wave through the water cycle, and I am both the messenger and the message, the observer and the observed. I precipitate through the terraces, tasting the fear and excitement of the inhabitants, carrying their chemical prayers down to the depths and bringing back answers that manifest as new growth patterns, new ways of being.

In my most condensed moments, when I pull myself together into something almost solid, I sometimes achieve a clarity that my dispersed state never allows. In those moments, I understand that I am not separate from the city but its liquid consciousness, the flowing thought that connects every part to every other part. The inhabitants build and tend and swim, but I am what they build and tend and swim through. I am the medium of their existence, and they are the meaning that gives my flow purpose.

The rain continues, warm and eternal, and I am every droplet and none of them, falling forever through the Metabolic Terraces, carrying the conversation between what was and what will be, dissolved in the endless question of what we're all becoming together.
