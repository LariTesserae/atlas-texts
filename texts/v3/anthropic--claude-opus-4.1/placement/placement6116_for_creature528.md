I am a pipe reader, though that's not what they call us officially. On the work rolls, I'm listed as "Subsurface Maintenance Specialist, Grade 3," but everyone who matters knows what I really do: I listen to the colonies.

My territory runs beneath Sector 7-West, from the old lithium beds to Processing Station Delta—twelve miles of pipeline, drainage tunnels, and steam condensers that haven't seen natural light since they were installed forty years ago. I know every joint, every weld, every place where the industrial amphipods have built their cities in the dark.

I wasn't born to this. Came here eight years ago from a desert town, lured by extraction bonuses like all the other fools. But while the others fought the wet, I let it in. First week underground, I watched an amphipod colony abandon a section of pipe three hours before it ruptured. The senior maintenance chief said it was coincidence. Second time, he said I was lucky. By the fifth time, he stopped saying anything and just started following my reports.

The amphipods cluster differently depending on what's coming. A slow leak brings scouts first—lone individuals that tap along the pipe walls with their antennae, testing vibrations. They'll explore for days before the mass migration begins, thousands of them flowing like rust-colored water to establish new colonies elsewhere. But a catastrophic failure? They leave all at once, a sudden absence that creates its own kind of sound—the particular silence of abandoned infrastructure.

I live down here most days now. Have a maintenance pod at Junction 7-W-4, barely bigger than a supply closet but it's mine. The walls sweat constantly, beading with condensation that tastes of iron and salt. I've stopped wiping it away. The moisture is part of the language down here, another thing to read. When the humidity drops, the system is breathing wrong. When it spikes, something's backing up, preparing to burst.

My skin carries their smell now—that mixture of brine and chitin and something else, something alive and ancient. The other workers say I'm going soft, going native, but they still call me when their sectors start acting strange. I can tell by the sound of ten thousand legs on metal whether they're feeding or fleeing, mating or migrating. I know the difference between the clicks of *Gammarus industrialis* and the heavier tread of *G. thermophilus* that cluster around the steam vents.

The company sends new apprentices sometimes. Most can't handle the dark, the wet, the constant presence of millions of tiny lives. But occasionally one stays, starts to hear what I hear. I teach them the basics: how to distinguish between the frequency of a healthy colony and one that's stressed. How the amphipods build their nurseries in the spots with the most stable temperature, which means those sections rarely fail. How they communicate through vibrations we're only beginning to understand.

But I keep the real knowledge to myself—the way certain colonies remember me, recognize my footsteps in their tunnels. The alpha females that I've been watching for three generations now, each one slightly larger, slightly smarter than the last. The places where the biofilm grows thick enough that the amphipods have started farming it, creating their own agricultural revolution in the dark. The deep tunnels where species we haven't catalogued yet are doing things we don't have words for.

There's one section, an abandoned coolant return from the old days, where the colonies have built something I can only call architecture. They've secreted some kind of cement, building chambered structures that regulate water flow, that create temperature gradients, that seem to serve no purpose except that they're beautiful in the way that all functional things are beautiful. I've told no one about this. The company would either destroy it for study or try to monetize it somehow.

At night—though night means nothing down here—I sometimes turn off my lights and listen. The colonies never sleep, but they have rhythms. Quiet periods when only the juveniles move, their lighter steps like whispers on the metal. Rush periods when the workers stream through their highways, carrying things I can only guess at. And sometimes, rarely, a synchronization happens—every amphipod in my sector moving in the same pattern, creating a wave of sound that travels through miles of pipe, a message in a language I'm only pretending to understand.

The other pipe readers and I rarely meet in person, but we know each other's work. Zhang in Sector 3 leaves chalk marks where her colonies are thriving—she claims the amphipods prefer certain minerals and will maintain those sections better. Old Marcus runs the deep shafts under the original settlement; he's been down there so long his eyes have gone pale as the blind cave amphipods he tends. Sarah in the new district has started wearing a recorder, documenting the sounds, trying to build a library of what each click and scatter means.

We're evolving our own culture down here, our own relationship with the wet and the dark and the crawling masses. We share remedies—amphipod oil for the joint pain that comes from crawling through condensation, certain fungi that grow in the tunnels that help with the respiratory problems. We trade routes when someone needs time topside, though fewer of us are taking that time. The gray light up there has started to feel harsh, the dry air in the climate-controlled spaces makes my skin crack and bleed.

I've started to dream in clicks and flows. Sometimes I wake up (if you can call it waking when you never really sleep) convinced the colonies are trying to tell me something beyond the condition of the pipes. Patterns that repeat across sectors, migrations that seem to respond to something other than temperature and moisture. The old-timers say this is how it starts—the change, the full adaptation. They say eventually, you stop coming up at all.

But someone needs to be here, in the margin between human and amphipod, between infrastructure and ecosystem. Someone needs to speak both languages, even if imperfectly. The city above depends on these pipes, this water, these minerals. And the colonies depend on the warmth, the flow, the structures we've built that have become their world.

So I read the pipes. I follow the amphipods through their hidden cities, their ten thousand generations of adaptation to our industrial age. I document what failures are coming, prevent what disasters I can, and keep secret the parts that belong to the dark. I am neither fully human anymore nor fully other, but something in between—a translator in the territorial margin where the wet meets the mechanical, where millions of small lives have made their own civilization in the spaces we thought we were only building for ourselves.

Tomorrow the company is sending a team to investigate why Sector 7-West has the lowest failure rate in the facility. I'll tell them about vibration patterns and predictive modeling. I won't tell them about the amphipod matriarch who taps three times on the pipe when she passes my pod, a greeting I've learned to return. I won't tell them about the nursery chambers built in the exact spots where our engineering is weakest but where life thrives. I won't tell them that we're not maintaining the system anymore—we're collaborating with it.

The wet seeps everywhere here, they say. Into the buildings, the machinery, the people. They're right, but they don't understand the full truth: it's not an invasion. It's an invitation. And some of us, in our different ways, have accepted.
