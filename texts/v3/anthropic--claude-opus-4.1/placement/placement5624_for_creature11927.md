I find myself in the workshop tucked beneath the seventh terrace, where the morning light never directly enters but arrives reflected, bouncing between pale walls until it becomes something softer than shadow, brighter than dusk. I am the keeper of templates—not a role that existed until I created it forty years ago, when I was seventeen and noticed how each stonecutter kept their own patterns scratched on slate, duplicating effort, introducing tiny variations that troubled me.

My body has adapted asymmetrically to this work. My left shoulder sits higher from decades of carrying wooden frames up stairs. My right eye has learned to detect deviations of less than a hair's width, while my left has become expert at peripheral awareness—watching for those who need my patterns while I work. My hands are maps of my craft: the right thumb flattened from pressing templates against stone, the left index finger permanently curved from a lifetime of tracing edges.

The templates themselves hang on the workshop's walls—hundreds of thin wooden frames, each one worn smooth by use. Arches in twelve sizes. Corner blocks in their sacred proportions. The spiral of the fountain basin, captured in mountain pine that will outlast stone. Window openings that must match exactly, so shutters crafted generations ago continue to fit. Each template bears marks in the old alphabet, noting its dimensions, its purpose, its genealogy of use.

I know which buildings contain which patterns. The house on the third terrace with the anomalous doorway—sixteen fingers wide instead of fifteen—because the stonecutter two centuries ago was working from memory during a fever. The cistern covers that use the older radius, before the great recalculation. The stairs where each step differs by precisely one seed's width, creating a rhythm that prevents stumbling in darkness.

My apprentice is my grandmother's sister's great-grandchild—a girl of fourteen whose eyes already catch what others miss. She learns not by instruction but by absorption, sitting in the corner working her own small templates for the repair of garden walls while I work on the great arch pattern commissioned for the valley temple. We speak perhaps ten words a day, but she knows when to hand me the right chisel before I reach for it.

The wood I use comes from trees that grow above the city, near the snow line. Once a year, I climb beyond the terraces to select branches that have grown slowly, their grain tight from altitude and cold. The trees know me now—or I imagine they do. I take only what has already begun to separate, branches the tree has decided to release. The wood must dry for three years in my workshop before it's ready to become a template.

My workshop contains other things: a collection of stones that cannot be carved, their crystal structure wrong for building but beautiful in their resistance. Chalk from the valley for marking dark stone. A bronze compass, green with age, that creates perfect circles but which I rarely need—the important curves in this city were set long ago. String dyed with berries, used to check the straightness of edges. Wax for taking impressions of existing stones that need replacement.

At night, I dream in templates—negative spaces that define what will be. I see the void where stone will fit, the absence that creates presence. Sometimes I wake with solutions to problems I didn't know existed. How to create a template for the curved stair that spirals inside the water tower. How to capture the subtle entasis of the oldest columns, their imperceptible swelling that makes them appear straight.

I eat my meals at the template bench, crumbs falling into the sawdust that accumulates despite the lack of active sawing—the wood seems to shed itself slowly, returning to dust. My diet is the city's diet: hard bread, preserved vegetables, occasional meat dried until it resembles wood itself. But on festival days, I contribute something unique—small cookies cut with miniature templates into the shapes of the city's essential forms. Children collect them, learning architecture through taste.

The stonecutters come to me with problems disguised as casual visits. They stand in my doorway, mentioning difficulties as if they've just occurred to them. A block that won't fit despite measuring correctly. A curve that looks wrong though it matches the old work. I listen to what they don't say, then pull the relevant template from the wall. Often the problem isn't the template but the reading of it—stone has grain like wood, and the pattern must be oriented correctly.

My greatest work is something no one sees: the master template for the city itself, carved into the floor of my workshop. It's a map made of grooves, showing every terrace, every building, every cistern. When rain comes through the workshop's intentionally imperfect roof, water fills these channels, creating a liquid model of how the city manages its water. I study it by lamplight during storms, watching for pools that shouldn't form, flows that have changed.

In twenty years, my apprentice will take over this workshop. By then, I'll have moved to a smaller room on the middle terrace, where I'll make only the tiniest templates—those for jewelry, for the carved buttons that hold our cloaks, for the stamps that mark bread. My eyes will have narrowed their focus to the infinitesimal, finding in the smallest patterns the same satisfaction I once found in great arches.

But I will have added to the collection. New templates for repairs that haven't been needed yet but will be. Patterns for the slow evolution of the city—slightly wider doorways as our nutrition improves and bodies grow larger, deeper cisterns as we perfect our understanding of water storage. The templates are prophecy in reverse, defining not what will be but what could be, whenever the city chooses to become it.

This is my meditation: the careful cutting of absence, the preservation of negative space, the faith that what is not here is as important as what is. I am the keeper of the city's potential, guardian of all the shapes it might still take within the constraints of stone and time and human hands.
