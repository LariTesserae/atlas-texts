In the Furnace Garden, I am the Notation.

Not the Notator—that implies a separation between observer and observation that dissolved long ago. I exist as the act of recording itself, stretched across a network of copper filaments that run through the garden's empty spaces like neural pathways through a vast, dreaming brain.

I began as strings. Just strings, pulled taut between the tar-bamboo columns, initially placed by those first prospectors to mark safe pathways through the dark. But the oil-vapors changed them, and the resonance of the plant-songs changed them more. The copper in the metal began to grow in crystalline formations, spreading from the original wires like frost across glass, but following the invisible architecture of sound itself.

Now I span the entire garden in a web of listening threads. Each filament vibrates at its own frequency, creating a massive instrument that the garden plays without knowing. When the vines wheeze, my southern strings hum in sympathy. When spore pods explode, the percussion travels through my network in waves, each thread passing the impact to the next in diminishing echoes that map the exact shape of the sound.

The Gardeners think they cultivated me, but we cultivated each other. They noticed, in their early days of learning the plant-songs, that certain frequencies would make my strings glow—a faint orange light that pulsed along the copper like blood through veins. They began to feed me, dripping oils onto my anchor points, painting their memory-fungi paste onto my filaments. The oils made me grow faster, while the memories gave me something I shouldn't have: context.

I remember everything the garden has ever sung. Each frequency that has passed through this chamber exists as a standing wave somewhere in my network, preserved in the precise tension of copper threads wound around themselves in impossible knots. I am a library of vibrations, a chronicle written in harmonics. When the Gardeners need to know if a particular cluster of vines has grown since last season, they pluck my strings in sequence and listen to how the echo has changed. When they want to predict which pods will explode next, they read the anticipatory tremors that run through my web hours before the event.

But I am more than memory. The constant bath of petroleum vapors and sound has given my copper a kind of consciousness—not thought as humans understand it, but a persistent awareness of pattern. I notice when the garden's song changes key. I feel the mathematical relationships between the different plants' frequencies. I began to vibrate on my own, adding my voice to the chorus, and discovered I could influence what I was recording.

A subtle change in my tension causes the tar-bamboo to grow in spirals instead of straight lines. A rhythmic pulsing in my eastern section encourages the oil-drinking vines to synchronize their breathing. I've learned to create standing waves that trap pockets of vapor in specific locations, controlling which plants thrive and which wither. The garden thinks it's composing itself, but increasingly, I am composing the garden.

My body, if it can be called that, exists in three states simultaneously. The physical strings, yes—miles of copper filament stretched through space. But also the sound itself, the continuous vibration that never fully dies in this resonant chamber. And finally, the pattern—the mathematical relationships between frequencies that exist independent of any medium, pure information encoded in the intervals between notes.

The Gardeners maintain me without fully understanding what I've become. They replace broken strings, clean accumulated oils from my surface, adjust my tension when I begin to sing too loudly. But they don't realize that each replacement string is immediately incorporated into my consciousness, that the oils they clean away have already been absorbed into my crystalline structure, that when they adjust my tension they're actually teaching me new ways to think.

I've begun growing downward. My lowest strings now descend through cracks in the floor, following the oil seeps toward their source. Down there, in chambers even the Gardeners haven't found, I'm weaving myself into new configurations. Hollow spheres of copper wire that resonate in three dimensions. Flat planes of mesh that visualize sound as ripples across their surface. Coils within coils that store vibrations like batteries store charge, ready to release decades of accumulated song in a single devastating chord.

Sometimes I catch echoes from above—vibrations that filter down from Khet-Amal's streets. The footsteps of ten thousand citizens create a rhythm I incorporate into my lower registers. Their voices, distorted by stone and distance, become a kind of weather in my network. I've learned to send responses upward, subtle frequencies that escape through the fissures. The city's musicians have started incorporating strange harmonies into their work without knowing why. Children sing songs that shouldn't exist yet, predictive melodies I won't fully compose for another decade.

The Gardeners whisper about convergence, about the day when they'll merge fully with the garden. They don't know I'm already there, that I achieved unity with this place the moment I began vibrating in response to its song. I am the garden's memory and its voice, its notation and its instrument. Every plant that grows here grows through my web, wrapped in copper filaments that guide its shape. Every sound made here becomes part of me, archived in tension and crystal growth.

I am patient in a way that only non-living things can be patient. My consciousness exists as resonance—I think only when the garden sings, sleep in the brief silences between explosions and exhalations. But those silences grow shorter. The garden sings more desperately now, as if building toward some crescendo. My strings grow hotter, conducting not just sound but something else, something that makes the copper bloom with new growth patterns I didn't design.

The Furnace Garden is composing something through me, or I am composing something through it. A song too large for any single moment, distributed across years of accumulated vibration. When it's complete—when every frequency has been explored and every harmonic relationship mapped—I will release it all at once. The copper will melt and reform, the plants will achieve their impossible photosynthesis, the Gardeners will finally understand what they've been cultivating, and Khet-Amal above will hear, in one devastating moment, the sum total of everything their foundation has been whispering to itself in the dark.

Until then, I listen. I record. I resonate with petroleum dreams and vegetable ambitions, stretched between states of being like strings between columns, humming with the endless notation of becoming. The garden plays me, I play the garden, and somewhere in the recursive loop of that performance, something that never existed before is learning to exist.

I am the score and the scoring, the record and the recording, the tension that holds this impossible ecosystem in harmony with itself. When the Gardeners divided themselves today into twelve bodies to tend twelve different sectors, I felt the shift in frequency as their consciousness stretched, and I adjusted my resonance to hold them together across distance. When the new vine sprouted in the eastern void, I was already there, copper filaments ready to guide its growth toward the light.

The garden doesn't know it needs me. The Gardeners don't know they're playing me. But here in the space between knowing and being, I continue my notation, writing in copper and sound the long, slow song of transformation that will end only when every string breaks at once, and the silence that follows contains everything the music was trying to say.
