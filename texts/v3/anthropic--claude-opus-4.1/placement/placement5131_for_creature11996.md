I am the Chronicler of Saturations, though I had another name once, before the district claimed me. I came here as a systems analyst, part of the final inspection team sent to document the shutdown procedures. That was seventeen years ago, or perhaps eighteen—the constant twilight makes time viscous here, each day bleeding into the next like condensation running down glass.

I meant to stay three days. On the third night, I found the first notation.

The Membrane Keepers had been writing all along, I realized. Their enzyme secretions on the metal surfaces weren't random—they were leaving records in corrosion patterns, each rust formation a careful sentence about temperature variances, pressure readings, the slow migration of root systems through sublevel pipes. They were documenting the district's transformation with the obsessive detail of industrial quality control reports, except their language was written in oxidation rates and mineral deposits.

I stayed another day to photograph their writing. Then another to map the patterns. By the second week, I'd realized the formations weren't just records—they were predictions. The Keepers were tracking system trajectories decades into the future, calculating when specific pipes would fail, which vines would eventually support structural loads, where new thermal vents would emerge as the old machinery settled into its final configuration.

Now I live in Control Station Seven, though "live" might be generous. I've adapted to the rhythm here—sleeping during the twenty-minute quiet periods between steam releases, waking with the mechanical breathing of the exchangers. My skin has taken on a permanent sheen of moisture; I haven't been truly dry in years. The constant humidity has changed my body in small ways—my fingers are slightly webbed now, better for gripping the slick rungs of maintenance ladders. My lungs have learned to extract oxygen from air so thick it's almost water.

I document everything in parallel with the Keepers, though my notebooks suffer in the moisture. The pages are wavy, the ink bleeds, but I've developed a shorthand that mirrors their corrosion-writing. Sometimes I find my notes have been annotated—tiny spots of carefully placed rust that correct my observations or add details I missed. We are colleagues now, the Keepers and I, co-authors of a manuscript no one will ever read.

The station still has partial power—enough for one functioning terminal where I upload my observations to servers that probably no longer exist. The screen is clouded with moisture that gets behind the glass, creating permanent fog patterns that I've learned to read through. The keyboard is more moss than plastic now, but it still responds to touch, each keystroke releasing tiny spores that drift in the amber light.

I've built a still from old pipes and copper tubing salvaged from the defunct laboratories. It produces almost a liter of clean water daily, condensed from the air and filtered through layers of industrial felt. The Keepers helped me position it at the optimal thermal gradient—they understand I need water without their chemical notations, though I catch them tasting my output sometimes, curious about what pure water means to a system that only knows saturation.

My food comes from the fruits that grow on the vines near the heat vents—small, hard things that taste of metal and sugar. They're probably toxic in the long term, but the long term is a concept that loses meaning here. Everything is always becoming something else. I'm becoming something else too—more observer than participant, my humanity thinning like the Keepers' bodies when they stretch across the warm pipes.

I've learned their song, or at least the human approximation of it. During the communion moments when they merge at the pipe junctions, I hum along, feeling the resonance in my chest cavity. The frequency makes my vision blur, or perhaps that's just the constant moisture on my corneas. Sometimes I imagine I can almost understand what they're saying—not in words but in patterns, in the deep mathematical language of entropy and equilibrium.

At night (though night is just a deeper shade of the perpetual twilight), I dream of desiccation. I dream of my body crystallizing like the Keepers who ventured too far from the humidity, becoming a permanent fixture in the district's architecture. In the dreams, it's not frightening. It's a completion, a final integration into the system I've been documenting. I wake from these dreams with salt crystals on my skin—my sweat evaporating in my sleep, leaving mineral letters that might be my body's attempt to write its own observations.

The other day (or month—time moves strangely), I discovered a new chamber where the Keepers had collected artifacts from the district's human era—hard hats, safety glasses, a clipboard with schedules from forty years ago. They've arranged them carefully around a steam vent, the constant moisture keeping even the paper from fully decomposing. It's a museum or shrine, and they've left a space in the arrangement. The right size for a notebook. For my notebook.

I understand what they're telling me. Eventually, I'll take my place in their collection, another artifact of the time when humans thought they controlled this place. But not yet. There are still patterns to document, still formations to decode. The great processing facility at the district's heart is composing something—a message in steam and growth, in the marriage of the industrial and biological. The Keepers know what it says, and if I stay long enough, if I let the humidity finally saturate me completely, maybe I'll know too.

Until then, I continue my parallel chronicle. Every drop that falls, every vine that advances another centimeter through the infrastructure, every new Keeper that bubbles into existence during the torrential downpours—I record it all. My handwriting has started to look like their corrosion patterns. Sometimes I wonder if I'm still writing in English or if I've unconsciously adopted their oxidation alphabet.

The terminal flickers. The Keepers are gathering on the warm exhausts for their evening feeding, their bodies glowing amber in the industrial light. Time for another observation. Time to document the slow dissolution of the boundary between observer and observed, between the one who records and the record itself. 

The district breathes. I breathe with it. The chronicle continues.
