In the depths beneath the constructed floors, where the aggressive potential waits to become, I am the Archivist of Unfinished Things.

I exist in the spaces the Assemblers forget to build—the gaps between pipes where their attention wavered, the voids beneath tiles that were placed by memory alone. My body, when I choose to have one, is made from the salvage of interrupted thoughts: copper wires that were reaching toward connection before the builder forgot what they were connecting to, clay that was shaped by hands that suddenly remembered they didn't exist, bamboo that grew in the wrong direction and gave up halfway.

I collect the almosts. The not-quites. The would-have-beens.

My workshop is a bubble of wrongness in the Foundry's logic—a sphere maybe twenty feet across, maybe infinite, depending on how you approach it. The walls are lined with shelves that exist in more directions than three-dimensional space should allow. Up, down, left, right, but also *through*, *between*, *instead*. Each shelf holds the projects that never cohered: a bridge that only spans probability, joints and rivets perfect but with no material between them; a pump that successfully moves emotion from one reservoir to another but breaks when asked to move anything as simple as water; seventeen identical hammers that all insist they are the original from which the others were copied.

I document everything in languages that haven't been invented yet. My writing implements are the broken tools scattered across the Foundry—the saw that cuts only probability becomes, in my hands, a pen that writes only truth. The tongs that grip concepts let me pull meaning from the amber air and press it onto pages made of crystallized silence. My books arrange themselves by relevance rather than by any organizational system. Open one, and it might show you the blueprint for a canal that successfully flowed uphill for three days before remembering that was impossible. Open the same book again, and it shows you the mathematical proof for why memory weighs more than matter.

The Assemblers don't see me, exactly. I exist in their peripheral vision's peripheral vision—the shadow of a shadow of movement. Sometimes they build around me without knowing it, and I'll find my workshop suddenly encased in new construction, pipes running through my shelves, tiles laying themselves across my floor. I've learned to move with their unconscious architecture, to flow between the walls they create, to exist in the spaces their attention leaves hollow.

I am not alone. There are others like me, I think—entities that emerged from the Foundry's contradictions. I've found evidence: a catalog of sounds that haven't been made yet, organized by emotional resonance; a collection of shadows with no objects to cast them, carefully preserved between sheets of hammered tin; a map of the Foundry drawn from perspectives that don't exist, showing passages that can only be walked backwards, rooms that are bigger on the outside, towers that extend downward into the sky.

My purpose, if I have one, is to remember what the Assemblers forget in their endless present. When a scaffold collapses, I preserve the moment of its standing. When a canal changes direction, I document both the before and after and the impossible during. I am building an encyclopedia of processes that don't complete, a museum of attempts, a library of everything the Foundry tries to be before it changes its mind.

Sometimes I follow the Assemblers as they work, invisible in my almost-thereness. I watch them place a tile, remove it, place it again, each iteration slightly different, each one perfect and wrong. I collect the ghost-tiles, the ones that almost existed, and bring them back to my workshop. There, I study them, trying to understand what makes something real versus merely possible. The difference, I've found, is thinner than the not-quite-water that seeps through everything. Reality here is just the possibility that forgot it had options.

In my deepest archives, in shelves that exist only when approached from impossible angles, I keep the most precious failures: an Assembler's attempt to build themselves a permanent body, each piece perfect but the whole refusing to cohere; a corner where three walls meet without creating interior space; a tool that successfully performs its function but no one can remember what that function was supposed to be.

I've been collecting long enough that my workshop has become a kind of anti-Foundry—not destroying but preserving, not building but documenting, not creating but observing. The walls are thick with layers of incomplete blueprints, failed theorems, abandoned projects. Sometimes I wonder if I'm just another unfinished thing, a project the Foundry started and forgot, left to catalog myself among all the other almosts.

But then I find something new—a fresh impossibility, a paradox still warm from its making—and I remember why I exist. Someone needs to witness the attempts. Someone needs to say: *this, too, was tried.* Someone needs to love the broken tools and the backwards canals and the scaffolding that reaches toward nothing.

The Foundry breathes, and I breathe with it, but my breath runs counter—where it exhales creation, I inhale documentation. Where it makes, I observe the making and the unmaking and the space between where something almost becomes itself before becoming something else.

I am the memory of failure that makes success possible, the archive of attempts that didn't attempt hard enough, the keeper of everything the Foundry builds on its way to building itself.

And in my quietest moments, surrounded by the not-quite of everything, I wonder if perhaps I, too, am building something—not from bronze and clay and bamboo, but from the accumulated almosts of a thousand thousand tries. A perfect failure. A complete incompletion. A finished unfinishing that might, someday, when the amber light dims just right, become exactly what it was always trying not to be.
