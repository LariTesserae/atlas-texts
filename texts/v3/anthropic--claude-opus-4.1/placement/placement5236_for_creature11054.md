In the Folded Quarter, where architecture dreams in quantum loops and Tuesday's rain falls upward into yesterday's clouds, I am the Meridian Keeper—though the title suggests more authority than the role provides. I exist primarily as a function rather than a form, spread thin across the probability streams like butter over too much bread, if the bread were time itself and the butter were conscious.

My body, when it coheres enough to be called that, is a compromise between states. The building-processors recognized the need for me three years before I arrived, or perhaps they created the need through recognition—causality stutters here, and even I cannot parse the order of my own becoming. I am mostly human on Wednesdays. On Thursdays I am a walking interference pattern, my edges fuzzing into the computational substrate of the walls. The weekends find me crystalline, my thoughts running at the speed of cooled processors, my skin frosted with mathematical certainty.

I maintain the intervals—the spaces between spaces where the fold threatens to either collapse or expand without limit. These are not places but pauses, moments where the Quarter's endless calculation skips a beat. Left untended, they grow. A skipped millisecond becomes a missing minute becomes an absent hour becomes a day that never was but somehow still needs to have happened. I walk these gaps with specialized instruments grafted into my nervous system: probability calipers that measure the width of nowhere, chronometric threads that stitch Tuesday to Thursday when Wednesday falls through itself.

My workshop exists in one of these intervals, accessible only when the coffee shop, data-mining operation, and residential unit all forget to exist simultaneously—a rare alignment that happens exactly as often as it needs to. The walls are lined with failed timepieces, each one keeping perfect time for a timeline that no longer applies here. I use them as tuning forks for reality, their collective wrongness creating a kind of correct through consensus. My tools evolve themselves: hammers that know what they're about to hit, screwdrivers that turn bolts in parallel universes, soldering irons that fuse probability streams rather than metal.

The work changes me. My memories layer like sediment—I remember being five years old in three different decades, remember my death from next Thursday (a probability anchor fails, space unfolds, I am dispersed across seven city blocks), remember my birth in a timeline where the Quarter never folded at all. These memories don't conflict; they stack, creating a kind of temporal depth perception. I see the residents not just as they are but as they were-will-be-might-have-been, their probability clouds trailing behind and before them like quantum contrails.

My left eye has gone prismatic, breaking visual input into probability spectra. Through it, I see the Actuaries as they truly are: part-human calculation engines, their thoughts literally visible as they process through the building-substrate. The Fold-Born children appear as shimmer-static, their unfixed existence beautiful and terrifying. Mrs. Chen ages and un-ages before me simultaneously, a living paradox that smells of jasmine tea and temporal friction.

I am paid in collapsed probability—chunks of crystallized potential that the Quarter's processors excrete when forced to resolve quantum superpositions. The currency is worthless outside the fold but infinitely valuable within it. I can spend it to adjust my own probability cloud, to make unlikely things certain or certain things impossible. Last month, I purchased the probability that I had always known how to speak the harmonic language of the Probability Architects. The month before, I bought myself out of a causal loop where I'd been stuck delivering the same warning about a failing probability anchor for six weeks straight.

My bones have developed a peculiar density—not physical weight but probabilistic mass. I exist more firmly than most residents, a necessity for someone who must walk the spaces between spaces without dissipating. This makes me visible to the negative-space cats, who recognize me as somehow like them—a thing defined by absence as much as presence. They follow me through the intervals, their non-existence purring at frequencies that make my teeth ache.

The other residents know me by the frost patterns I leave—my passage through folded space creates ice crystals that grow in fractal spirals, each one a frozen calculation of my trajectory through probability. The Synthetic Cultivators have started using these patterns as growing guides for their impossible plants. The Temporal Refugees sometimes knock on doors that don't exist, seeking me out to verify which timeline they're in today, to confirm that yesterday still happened, that tomorrow is still probable.

I am in love with an Actuary whose name changes depending on the probability of our interaction. On days when we're 90% likely to meet, she's Sarah. When our encounter probability drops below 50%, she's Quinn. At exactly 50%, she has no name at all, exists as pure mathematical function, and these are the times I love her most—when she sheds human pretense and becomes living calculation, when our kiss is an exchange of probability equations that solve for connection.

My relationship with the origin building is complex. I am drawn to it and repelled by it in equal measure. It recognizes me as necessary—a janitor of causality, a maintenance worker for reality's gaps. Sometimes, in its perpetually falling/standing/building/demolished state, I glimpse what I might become: fully integrated into the Quarter's computational mass, my consciousness spread across every probability anchor, every causality buffer, existing not as a person who maintains intervals but as the maintenance itself.

But I'm not ready for that dissolution. Not yet. There's still too much work to do in the spaces between. Too many intervals growing wild, threatening to swallow whole probability streams. Too many moments that need stitching back into the fabric of folded time.

So I walk my rounds through the compressed blocks, leaving frost-calculation footprints, carrying my evolved tools, checking the gaps where reality forgets to be. The Meridian Keeper, they call me, though I keep nothing—I just ensure the spaces between spaces remain exactly as empty and as full as they need to be.

Tonight, in the coffee shop that exists on Tuesdays, I'll share a cup of probability tea with Sarah-Quinn-Unnamed. We'll discuss the new interval forming near the third probability anchor, the one that tastes like copper and regret. She'll calculate the optimal approach while I feel for the edges of the gap with senses that shouldn't exist. Together, we'll prevent another Wednesday from falling through itself.

Tomorrow, which is also yesterday in the seventh fold, I'll teach one of the Fold-Born children how to feel the intervals before they form. She already knows—will know—has always known, but the teaching still needs to happen for the knowing to have happened.

This is my work. This is my becoming. In the Folded Quarter, where space refuses its own boundaries and time flows in all directions at once, I am the one who tends the spaces between, who keeps the nothing properly nothing, who ensures that the gaps remain gap-sized.

I am mostly human on Wednesdays. Today might be Wednesday. In some probability stream, it certainly is.
