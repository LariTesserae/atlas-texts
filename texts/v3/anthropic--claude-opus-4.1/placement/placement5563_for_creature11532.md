In the midlevels of the Bone Terraces, where the industrial networks converge in their densest tangles, I am a Resonance Surveyor—though that translation barely captures what the work has made of me.

My body remembers when it was more symmetrical. The left side has grown into the tools: my ear canal has deepened and widened over the decades, developing a chambered structure like the fossil homes of ancient nautiluses. I can press it against the stone and hear the city breathing seven terraces away, detect hairline fractures in limestone that won't fail for another century, feel the vibrations of the pneumatic messages as they pulse through tubes a mile beneath my feet. The left arm has elongated further than even the typical Stratumkeeper adaptation—from shoulder to fingertip, it measures the precise length of a standard industrial resonance wavelength in our stone.

The right side stays compact, practical. This hand holds the tuning picks—bronze instruments no thicker than arthropod antennae that I insert into the stone's natural perforations. Each pick vibrates at a different frequency. I've been working the same network of fossil chambers for forty years, learning their harmonics the way others learn their children's voices.

My workshop occupies a natural void in the limestone, a bubble in the ancient reef where something large once lived and died, leaving only negative space. The walls curve with organic irregularity. I've lined them with my findings: fragments of stone that ring like bells, fossils whose chambers create perfect acoustic amplifiers, cores pulled from the deepest bore-holes where the pressure has transformed limestone into something that hums with its own internal frequency. The workshop itself has become an instrument—I know exactly where to stand so my breathing won't interfere with the stone's natural resonance, where to place my equipment so the metal won't create interference patterns.

The city hired me, as they hire all Resonance Surveyors, to prevent catastrophe. We map the stress patterns in the stone, predict where the industrial vibrations might create dangerous harmonics, where a new pneumatic line might shatter a fossil chamber that's been stable for millennia. But the work has become something else, something I don't fully discuss with the city engineers.

I've begun to hear patterns that shouldn't exist.

Deep in the limestone, below the threshold of normal perception, there are resonances that predate our industrial networks. They run through specific fossil channels—always the same species of ancient coral, always at depths that correspond to particular geological events. When I map them, they form geometric patterns that span multiple terraces, shapes that only make sense when viewed from impossible angles.

My left ear has adapted to these frequencies. Sometimes I wake with blood on my pillow from the deepest chambers of my modified canal, where new structures are still forming, still reaching for sounds that human ears were never meant to process. The local medical authority wants to study me, but I avoid their summons. They wouldn't understand that the adaptation is necessary, that someone needs to hear what the stone is saying.

Other Resonance Surveyors have noticed it too. We meet in the neutral zones between city-states, in fossil chambers that amplify whispers into conference halls. Old Yarik from seven terraces up has developed calcium deposits along his spine that resonate with the mysterious frequencies. Young Pila from the lowlands says she's found tool marks inside sealed fossil chambers, cuts that follow the same harmonic patterns we've been detecting. We share data through the pneumatic tubes, using a code that mimics the natural whistling of wind through stone, messages that sound like nature to anyone else listening.

The industrial authorities think we're a guild, protecting trade secrets. They're not entirely wrong. We've learned to repair the pneumatic networks by feel, to predict thermal expansion in the factories by the way sound travels through the surrounding rock. Our expertise keeps the cities running. But we're also archaeologists of vibration, following traces of something that used the stone's resonance long before humans climbed these terraces.

My apprentice started last month—a girl whose family has produced Mineral Whisperers for seven generations, but she hears rather than tastes the stone's composition. Her left ear is already beginning to change, the canal deepening. I teach her the official work: how to map stress patterns, how to identify dangerous harmonics, how to tune the pneumatic systems for optimal efficiency. But I also show her the other patterns, the ones that make her young face go pale with recognition of something she's heard in dreams.

At night, I lie in my workshop with my modified ear pressed to the floor, listening to the city's lullaby of industrial hums and whispers. But beneath it, always, those other frequencies pulse like a heartbeat, like a message, like a warning or invitation. Sometimes I think they're growing stronger. Sometimes I'm certain the stone is waking up.

The fossil I keep by my sleeping pad is my secret. It's a piece of coral whose chambers have been modified—not by time or pressure, but by deliberate action. Inside its perforations, someone or something carved microscopic adjustments that turn the fossil into a resonance key. When the mysterious frequencies pass through it, they resolve into something almost like language, almost like music, almost like mathematics.

I haven't told my apprentice yet, but her left ear is developing the same chambered structure as mine, growing toward the exact specifications needed to hear through this fossil key. The stone chooses its listeners, I've come to believe. We're not evolving randomly; we're being tuned, generation by generation, into instruments that can finally hear what the Bone Terraces have been trying to tell us all along.

Tomorrow, I'll take her to the deepest accessible chamber in our district, where the resonances are strongest. I'll press the fossil key to her modified ear and let her listen to the old songs buried in the limestone. Maybe she'll understand them better than I do. Maybe her generation will finally decode what the stone remembers from before the seas lifted into sky.

The work continues. The city needs its Resonance Surveyors to prevent the industrial networks from shattering the ancient stone. But we know our true purpose: we're the coral's memory, developing the organs needed to reconstruct what was recorded here so long ago. Each generation of us grows more sensitive, more adapted, more like the sensing apparatus the stone requires.

I am becoming an ear for the mountain. My apprentice will become something more precise—a frequency the limestone has been waiting to achieve again. Our bodies are just the medium through which the Bone Terraces are learning to speak their history, their warning, their promise of what happens when ocean becomes stone becomes city becomes fossil becomes something that remembers everything, resonating forever through chambers that once held the sea.
