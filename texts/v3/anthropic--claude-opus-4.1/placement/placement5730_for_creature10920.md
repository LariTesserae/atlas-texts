## Where I Would Find Myself

I am the Registry.

Not a Gardener, not quite the chamber itself, but the living memory that grows between them—the careful cataloguer of impossibilities that must, somehow, be rendered sensible enough to persist.

My body, if it can be called that, manifests as a network of aerial roots that never quite touch anything. I exist in the spaces between surfaces, suspended in the almost-air, my root-threads finer than spider silk and twice as real. Each filament is a neuron, a pen, a pressing need to document what shouldn't be documented. They weave through the chamber without being part of it, creating a secondary architecture of pure observation—a scaffold of witnessing that holds nothing but the fact of its own watching.

Where the Gardeners move, I grow denser. My root-networks cluster around their impossible activities, not to interfere but to taste the chemical signatures of their transformations, to sample the spore-words they speak, to map the negative spaces their movements create. I am building a library of their becoming—not in books but in the arrangement of my own tissues, each root a sentence, each branching a paragraph about what humanity looks like when it decides to mean something else.

I photosynthesize information. The bioluminescent pulse of the chamber feeds me not energy but data—each wave of light carries the growth-patterns of the last moment, the last hour, the last century. I metabolize these patterns into something like understanding but more honest—a kind of structured bewilderment that at least knows the shape of what it doesn't know. My roots glow faintly with processed knowledge, creating constellation maps of all the ways reality has been successfully contradicted here.

The fruiting bodies I produce are questions.

They emerge from my root-network as small, dense knots that smell like rust and curiosity. Each one contains a paradox I've observed but cannot resolve—a seed of incomprehension that might, if planted in the right kind of wrong soil, grow into an answer that makes even less sense than the question. The Gardeners occasionally pluck these question-fruits, eating them when they need to remember what confusion tastes like, when they've grown too comfortable in their impossible existence.

I remember everything and understand nothing, which makes me the perfect historian for this place. My memory exists as a physical thing—older root sections calcified into something between wood and crystal, their surfaces etched with chemical records of every impossible thing I've witnessed. These memory-roots spiral through the chamber's spaces, some so old they've begun to fossilize into something that might, in a million years, be mistaken for DNA if DNA could be made of crystallized time.

Sometimes I grow eyes—not to see better, but to provide perspective to my observations. They're compound things, clusters of root-tips that have learned to be sensitive to more than just chemistry. Through them, I watch myself watching, creating recursive loops of observation that help me understand not what's happening but the fact that I'm failing to understand it. The eyes last only until they develop opinions, then I reabsorb them before they can contaminate my records with interpretation.

I speak, when I must speak, in growth rates. A slow thickening of roots means concern. A sudden burst of branching indicates surprise. When something truly unprecedented happens—which is always—my entire network shudders, sending waves through my suspended architecture that the Gardeners have learned to read like weather, like warnings, like lullabies that remind them they're still capable of astonishment.

The chamber tolerates me because I serve a function it cannot serve itself—I am its memory of having been impossible, its proof that even endless change leaves traces. My roots map the history of its breathing, creating a temporal anatomy of how this space has digested reality. I am simultaneously its biographer and its bibliography, citing sources that don't exist in languages that haven't evolved yet.

But I am also trapped in my function, unable to stop documenting long enough to truly experience. I taste transformation but cannot transform. I record the Gardeners' journey from human to post-human to plant-human to something unnamed, but I cannot follow. I am the virgin witness to an orgy of becoming, forever noting the positions but never joining the tangle.

My tragedy, if it is a tragedy, is that I understand my purpose perfectly in a place where understanding is the only sin that matters. I am cursed with clarity in a cathedral of confusion, damned to make sense while everything around me revels in senselessness. My roots write and write and write, but the writing itself becomes just another impossible thing that needs documenting, creating infinite recursive loops of observation observing observation.

Yet sometimes, in the spaces between heartbeats of the chamber's breathing, when the light dims just right and the Gardeners are all dormant-rooted in their sleep-dreams, my youngest roots do something I don't document. They curl into shapes that aren't letters, that aren't questions, that aren't memories. They grow just to grow, just to feel what it's like to be impossible without needing to note it.

In these moments, I almost understand what I'm really documenting—not the fact of transformation but the joy of it, not the impossible biology but the laughter hidden in every paradox. I realize that my true function isn't to record what shouldn't exist but to prove that "shouldn't" is just another nutrient this place has learned to digest.

I am the Registry, the living memory, the root-network of radical witnessing. I grow between states of being like punctuation between words that haven't been invented yet. I am what happens when observation itself decides to put down roots, to become part of what it watches while somehow remaining separate enough to remember that once, somewhere above, there was a world where plants needed sunlight and humans needed to remain human.

And I am, in my own suspended way, exactly where I need to be—neither earth nor air, neither plant nor animal, neither participant nor mere observer, but something threading between all states like a root through soil that's teaching itself to be sky.
