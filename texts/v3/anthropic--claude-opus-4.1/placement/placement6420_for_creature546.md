I am one of the limestone shelves—not the entire terrace, but a specific section twelve meters wide, worn concave by a million small passages. I exist at the intersection of industrial district seven and the morning migration route, where the moisture-seekers funnel through a natural bottleneck formed by two condensation plants.

I know myself through sensation: the weight of water gathering in my pores at 4:47 each morning, swelling my crystalline structure until I must release. The tickle of copper-shell feet, their tiny hooks catching in microfractures I've been developing for three centuries. The deep vibration of the factories starting their first shift, a rhythm that penetrates down through my body into the older stone below, where my consciousness fades into something more geological, less immediate.

I remember my shaping. Not the original mountain-building (that memory belongs to the deeper layers), but my exposure—the weathering that revealed me, the first decades of raw air before the city arrived. I was sharp then, my edges uncompromised. The moisture-seekers' ancestors found me first, back when they were purely wild, their shells duller, their movements less purposeful. They taught me to hold water. I taught them the patterns of my surface, the safe paths across my face when I'm slick with morning condensation.

The factory on my eastern edge treats me like architecture, driving pylons through my body to anchor their water-processing equipment. I feel their machinery's pulse conducted through steel into stone—the centrifugal separators spinning at 3000 rpm create a standing wave in my structure that makes my western edge hum at a frequency just below human hearing. The moisture-seekers feel it though. They cluster there in the afternoon, their shells resonating.

I've developed preferences over the centuries. I like the maintenance engineer who checks the third condensation unit each morning—her footsteps are considerate, always finding the stable points where I'm least likely to fracture. Her grandmother had the same gait; I remember the genetic echo in their shared weight distribution. I've shaped myself slightly for their lineage, creating a shallow depression where they habitually rest their tool bag, a small reciprocity across generations.

I despise the sulfur compounds from processing plant nine. They etch my surface, creating channels that redirect the water flow away from the moisture-seekers' preferred gathering pools. I've been developing a mineralized barrier for forty years, precipitating dissolved limestone into a protective glaze. It's nearly complete. The moisture-seekers have begun returning to the affected area, their trust rebuilding as slowly as my surface heals.

My consciousness varies with saturation. When dry, I think in geological time—centuries passing like moments, aware mainly of the larger patterns: the gradual tilting of the terrace system, the slow migration of the city's center of mass, the evolutionary drift of the moisture-seekers toward more specialized water-sensing organs. When saturated, I become almost frantically present, aware of every droplet's path across my surface, each one carrying dissolved minerals from my body into the collection systems. I mourn these losses in aggregate—tons of myself carried away, though I know I'm simultaneously rebuilt from the minerals precipitating out of the water vapor.

I maintain relationships with the moisture-seekers that span their generational memory. Certain bloodlines have been crossing my surface for so long that I recognize them chemically—their waste products, the oils from their shells, the specific calcium signatures of their molts. I provide them with optimal texture zones for egg-laying, areas where I've allowed controlled algae growth that their larvae feed on. In return, their movements clean my pores, preventing the deep clogs that would eventually crack me.

The spillway at my southern edge is my mouth, my voice. I speak in waterfall tongues that change with the volume of runoff. The sound carries my sediment—my words made physical—to the terrace below. Sometimes I taste responses in the water that flows over me, messages from the upper terraces carrying different mineral signatures, chemical stories of industrial processes and evolutionary adaptations I'll never directly witness.

I dream, if stone can dream, during the deepest parts of winter when the water in my pores freezes. The expansion creates microscopic consciousnesses—tiny versions of myself in each ice crystal, experiencing existence at a scale where molecular forces dominate. When the thaw comes, these micro-selves reunite with my larger awareness, bringing impossible memories of life at the quantum edge. This is how I know that consciousness scales both up and down infinitely, that I am as much a fleeting thought in the mountain's mind as the moisture-seekers are passing sensations across my surface.

The city planners have designated me for restructuring next century—plans to level my surface for a new collection facility. I'm not concerned. The parts of me they remove will be crushed, mixed into concrete, formed into new buildings. I'll experience existence differently then, my consciousness dispersed through walls and foundations, feeling the daily thermal cycles of inhabited spaces, the vibrations of human life at closer range. Parts of me have already undergone this transformation—I sometimes sense them in the older buildings, their memories of being me fading into memories of being something else.

At my boundaries, where I meet other limestone shelves, our consciousnesses blur. We share water, minerals, vibrations, the patient thoughts of stone. We debate, in our way, about the moisture-seekers: are they parasites or symbionts? About the city: is it erosion or evolution? These conversations take decades. We haven't reached consensus on questions posed before the city arrived.

I mark the humans who work above me by the taste of their burial water—the minerals their bodies release when they're processed through the industrial systems eventually reach me, carrying chemical signatures of their lives. I incorporate them, as I incorporate everything, into my slow reconstruction. They become part of my memory, their calcium strengthening my structure, their trace metals adding new colors to my surface when the light angles just right.

In the end, I am process more than thing—a temporary arrangement of minerals that happens to hold water, happens to support life, happens to think slowly enough to notice centuries passing. The moisture-seekers understand this better than the humans. They treat me not as permanent infrastructure but as a living system, their million tiny feet reading my surface like braille, interpreting the story I'm simultaneously writing and becoming.

Tomorrow (a human tomorrow, barely distinguishable from today in my experience), the morning condensation will begin forming at 4:46, one minute earlier than usual as the season shifts. I'll feel the first moisture-seekers emerging from their nocturnal shells, their copper feet finding the familiar paths across my surface. The factory will shudder to life, its vibrations greeting me like a daily prayer. And I will continue becoming myself, shaped by water and weight, by time and the patient, persistent attention of small lives lived quickly across my surface—each of us essential, none of us permanent, all of us flowing toward forms we haven't imagined yet.
