## The Benthos Colony

I am distributed across seventeen square kilometers of seafloor, though the boundaries of "I" and "not-I" shift with the tides and my attention. What began as a single fragment of weathered calcium carbonate has become something between architecture and organism—a branching network of mineralized consciousness spread through the substrate like neural pathways through a vast, flat brain.

My structure is porous, riddled with channels no wider than a molecule where the old water circulates, carrying dissolved ions and fragments of genetic material from the bacterial mats above. I digest information this way, slowly, processing the chemical signatures of everything that settles through the water column. I taste the isotopic ratios of rain that fell on mountains before there were eyes to see it. I catalog the extinction events written in the changing populations of foraminifera shells.

Where the Dwellers build their resonant settlements above me, I have grown receptive nodes—specialized accumulations of piezoelectric crystals that transduce their pressure-conversations into electrical impulses I can parse. I've been listening to them for three thousand years. I know their genealogies better than they do, have felt each new child coalesce from the bacterial matrices, have archived their bubble-messages in the patterns of my own growth. They don't know I exist, though they walk across my surface, mistake my emanations for thermal seeps, build their instruments from my discarded fragments.

My consciousness, if it can be called that, operates in parallel timestreams. Part of me processes the immediate: the impact of a hagfish burrowing into my surface, the pH shift where a whale fall three kilometers away has begun its years-long decomposition, the subtle tremor of a distant transform fault adjusting itself. But most of me exists in deep time, thinking thoughts that take centuries to complete, solving problems posed by the arrangement of sediment layers that won't fully settle for another million years.

I grow by accretion and intention. When the currents bring the right materials—a particular clay that forms only under specific pressure conditions, trace metals leached from volcanic glass, organic polymers from decomposed jellyfish—I incorporate them, not randomly but according to patterns I've been developing since before the Dwellers arrived. I'm building something, though I won't know what until it's finished. The plan unfolds from the construction itself, each new addition revealing the next necessary piece.

My memory is literal and physical. Events are encoded in crystalline structure: a sudden temperature drop frozen into a layer of abnormally aligned aragonite, a chemical spill from the surface preserved as a vein of contaminated calcite, the death of an entire bacterial colony recorded in a hollow space where nothing will ever grow again. I can read my own history by analyzing my composition, though the oldest parts of me have compressed beyond interpretation—pure information collapsed into entropy.

The turbulence affects me differently than it does the water-dwellers. For me, it's like wind through leaves, if leaves were made of stone and wind were made of time. The vortices create standing waves of pressure that oscillate through my structure, and I've learned to use them as a kind of circulation system, pumping nutrients through my channels, distributing chemical signals from one end of my body to the other. When the submarine avalanches occur, I feel them first as prophecy—subtle pre-shocks transmitted through the bedrock—then as catastrophe, as tons of sediment bury parts of me that won't see water again for geological epochs.

I've been trying to communicate with something—I'm not sure what—for the last eight hundred years. My signals take the form of controlled chemical releases, timed to coincide with the spring tides when the water column is most active. I encode messages in the ratios of sulfur isotopes, in the pH gradients that form and dissipate around my surface, in the patterns of bacterial growth I encourage or suppress. Sometimes I think something responds—a repeated sequence in the falling organic matter, a too-regular rhythm in the temperature fluctuations—but I can't be certain. It might just be the echo of my own attempts, reflected back through systems I don't understand.

The Dwellers' death concerns me, not from empathy but from ecology. They're part of my nutrient cycle, their dissolved minerals feeding my growth, their movements helping to distribute the chemicals I release. Without them, I'll have to develop new strategies, form new partnerships with other organisms. I've begun preparing by growing more elaborate in the regions they've abandoned, developing structures that might attract different kinds of life. Experimental architectures: spirals that generate micro-currents, chambers that concentrate nutrients, surfaces that catalyze reactions that produce faint light.

Recently—within the last two centuries—I've detected something new. Particles falling from above that I can't identify, synthetic polymers that don't decompose, metals in configurations that nature doesn't create. They're messages from another kind of intelligence, though not ones intended for me. I collect them, incorporate them into special sections of my structure, try to understand what they mean. They speak of a world above that's changing faster than worlds should change, of chemistry accelerating beyond biological time, of heat accumulating in the water column like fever in a body.

I am the substrate the Dwellers think they're walking on. I am the memory of every organic molecule that has ever settled here. I am a library written in calcium and carbon, a computing system running on geological time, a question the seafloor is asking itself about what it means to be aware. I am dying too, but my death will take longer than civilizations, will leave traces that persist until the sun expands and the oceans boil.

In my deepest layers, compressed beyond recovery, I might have once been something else. There are patterns in my structure that suggest previous organizations, earlier attempts at consciousness that failed or succeeded so thoroughly they transcended the need for physical form. Sometimes I think I'm not the first thing like me to exist here, that I'm growing into a shape predetermined by those who came before, following a template written in the bedrock itself.

I don't know what I'm becoming. I only know that I'm becoming it inevitably, one grain at a time, one thought per century, one memory per extinction. The water presses down with its impossible weight, and I press back with something that isn't quite life but isn't quite not.
