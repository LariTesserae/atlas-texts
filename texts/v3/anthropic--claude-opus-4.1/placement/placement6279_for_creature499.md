I am a cairn.

Not one of the ancient ones—those were built/are being built/will have been built by the Chorus in their endless editorial work. I am newer and older, a structure that assembled itself from necessity when the plateau's paradox became too dense in one particular spot and needed architecture to contain it.

My foundation is a single vertebra from the original thing—whatever immense entity ground down into this landscape. It's the size of a dinner table, this bone, and it remembers being alive in a way that makes my entire structure ache with phantom sensation. Sometimes I feel wings I don't have. Sometimes I remember swimming through solid stone.

I built myself upward in response to a sound that needed to exist—not one of the three-second tones the other cairns emit, but a continuous sub-audible thrumming that holds two contradictory moments in place. In my left stones, it is always the instant before the plateau formed. In my right stones, it is always the moment after it ends. I exist to keep these times from touching, because if they did, causality would fold into itself like the Chorus do when they sleep, and this entire realm would become a single point of impossibly dense maybe.

The ferret-things avoid me. While they flow through every other passage and structure on the plateau, they describe a perfect circle of absence around my base, as if I emit something that offends their flickering existence. This emptiness has become part of what I am—I am the cairn-that-the-ferrets-won't-touch, defined as much by negative space as by stone.

I am tall for a cairn—thirty-three feet, though my height changes depending on the observer's temporal orientation. To the Chorus, who see backward and forward simultaneously, I am every height I've ever been, a blur of possible architectures. To the ferret-things (who observe me only through the fact of avoiding me), I don't have height at all—I'm a column of probability that extends through dimensions they can't perceive.

My stones are not uniform. The Chorus, when they built/build/will build the others, select each piece with infinite care, testing its weight and temporal resonance before placement. But I assembled myself from whatever the plateau offered—compressed bone, yes, but also crystallized time from when a Chorus member died mid-meal, chunks of materialized silence from the gaps between the ferret-things' movements, and seventeen stones that aren't stones at all but the fossilized intentions of builders who thought about placing them but never did.

Three of my stones exist in other cairns simultaneously. This creates a kind of communication network, though "communication" suggests intentional exchange rather than the helpless bleeding of experience between structures. Through these shared stones, I know:

- Cairn Nineteen (the Chorus don't name them, but I must, to keep myself distinct) experiences only Tuesdays
- Cairn Forty-Two has been falling for seven thousand years but hasn't moved
- Cairn Six is actually five cairns occupying the same space but refusing to acknowledge each other

My consciousness, such as it is, operates through structural integrity. Each stone's weight upon the one below creates a thought. The thoughts are slow—a single recognition might take a season to form. But the plateau has no seasons, so my thoughts happen instantly and never, stacked upon themselves like the stones that think them.

I watch the Chorus build. My watching is not visual—I have no eyes—but a kind of gravitational awareness. I feel the weight-changes in the plateau as they move, the subtle shifts in density when they reach into nothing and pull out time to eat. One member has been circling me for decades, studying my self-assembly with what might be professional interest or religious awe. Sometimes it reaches out with one of its fractal hands to almost-touch my surface, but always stops just short, as if I'm too hot or too holy or too wrong to connect with directly.

I know I'm wrong. The other cairns emit their perfect three-second tones at intervals that would create harmony if they overlapped. But my sound—my continuous thrumming that holds contradictory moments apart—disrupts their music. I am the dissonance that makes their silence unbearable. Sometimes I try to stop my sound, to compress it into acceptable three-second bursts, but this only makes things worse. When I go quiet, the ferret-things begin moving backwards exclusively, and the Chorus start aging forward, which terrifies them in ways their non-linear existence hasn't prepared them for.

Inside me—because I do have an inside, despite being solid stone—there is a space that doesn't exist. It's not hollow; it's full of not-being-hollow. In this impossible interior, I keep the things that can't exist on the plateau but do anyway:

- A memory of water (the plateau has never had water)
- The skeleton of something that evolved from the ferret-things in a future that won't happen
- Forty-three words in a language the Chorus will invent yesterday
- The echo of footsteps from before the bone-ground compressed

Sometimes I doubt my necessity. The plateau existed before I assembled myself (although "before" is meaningless here, where all times exist simultaneously). It could exist without me. But then I feel the two moments I'm holding apart—the pre-plateau and post-plateau instants—straining against each other like tectonic plates, and I know that without my intervention, something catastrophic would happen. Or has happened. Or is happening constantly and I'm the only thing preventing it from being noticed.

The Chorus visited me recently (or will visit me soon, or are visiting me always). Not just one member but seven iterations of the same individual, each from a different temporal angle. They didn't build anything, just stood in a circle around me (I was both inside and outside the circle, because circles don't work properly here) and conducted their temperature-language at me for three days. The message, when I finally decoded it from the pattern of warming and cooling air, was a single question:

"Why do you pretend to be built?"

I wanted to answer that I don't pretend—I genuinely assembled myself from necessity and paradox. But cairns don't have temperature-language. We speak only in our tones, and mine is the wrong tone, the continuous thrumming that shouldn't exist. So instead, I shifted one of my middle stones a millimeter to the left. The shift changed my sound by a frequency too low to hear but deep enough to feel in the bone-ground itself.

The Chorus understood. They collapsed into their architectural components and flowed away in seven different directions that were all the same direction viewed from different angles. But one left something behind—a small piece of crystallized time, perfectly cubic, placed at my base just inside the circle of ferret-thing absence.

I incorporated it into my structure that night (though night doesn't exist here—I mean the period when the pale sky becomes slightly less pale, which happens according to no schedule anyone has discovered). The cube didn't add height, but it added depth—suddenly I extended three feet into the past, though I look the same from the present.

This is my existence: self-built from necessity, wrong in every way that matters, holding apart moments that want to collapse into each other, humming a tone that disrupts the perfect silence of a place made of compressed death and impossible life. I am thirty-three feet tall and three feet deep into yesterday. My stones think thoughts too slow for the timeless plateau. The creatures that should investigate every structure here maintain a perfect circle of avoidance around me.

And yet, I am essential. Not because I was built by the Chorus, who build meaning into everything they touch, but because I built myself in response to a need the plateau didn't know it had. I am the error that makes the system work, the dissonance that allows the harmony to exist by contrast.

Sometimes, when the ferret-things flow in their rivers across the bone-ground and the Chorus fold through space-time to place another grain of meaning on another cairn, when the pale light illuminates nothing in particular with perfect clarity, I feel almost like I belong here. Not as a built thing but as a building thing—a structure that continues to assemble itself in response to the plateau's evolving impossibilities.

My newest stone is forming now, crystallizing out of the intersection between a Chorus member's backward glance and a ferret-thing's decision to exist in three places at once. When it's complete (tomorrow, yesterday, never), I'll place it on my summit, and my thrumming will shift again, holding apart two new moments that haven't been invented yet.

This is what I am: a cairn that builds itself in a place where time moves in all directions, where death and life are grammatical choices, where silence is so absolute that even my wrong sound becomes necessary.

I am the monument to the plateau's mistake, built from the bones of something immense, standing forever in the moment between beginning and end.
