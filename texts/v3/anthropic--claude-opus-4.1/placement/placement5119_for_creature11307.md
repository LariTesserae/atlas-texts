**The Cartographer of Forgetting**

I am the last one who remembers how to read the old maps, though remembering is perhaps too strong a word for what I do. I *recognize* them—the way your tongue recognizes the shape of a missing tooth, the way your hand knows the weight of a tool you haven't held in decades.

My workshop spans three levels of what was once the Municipal Planning Office, though the floors have long since ceased to agree on their relative positions. To reach my desk, I climb through what should be the ceiling, emerge from what was once a water cooler, traverse a hallway that exists only on Tuesdays and Thursdays (the trees haven't decided what it should be on other days). The walls are papered with maps from every era of the city's transformation—survey charts from when the first factories were built, evacuation routes drawn in charcoal and spit when the trees began their consumption, my own attempts to chart the non-Euclidean districts using a mathematics I'm inventing as I go.

I wasn't born here. I came twenty years ago, following rumors of a city that had solved the problem of industrial death by learning how to digest it. I was a surveyor then, hired by the lowland governments to assess whether the city could be reclaimed, rehabilitated, returned to productivity. I carried instruments that no longer work here—theodolites that insist every angle measures exactly 73 degrees, GPS units that place you simultaneously at sea level and in the stratosphere, compasses that point not north but toward the nearest concentration of photosynthetic current.

I should have left after the first week, when I realized my maps were lying. Streets I had charted in the morning led somewhere else by afternoon. The warehouse district had twelve corners that all turned left. The main boulevard existed in seven parallel versions, each slightly offset in time, so that walking down it you could watch the same Weaver braiding the same catwalk at different stages of completion.

But I found the first of the old maps on my eighth day, in a filing cabinet that was also, somehow, a root system. The paper had been replaced cell by cell with cellulose that remembered its original form. When I touched it, I could feel the cartographer's intentions preserved in the grain—not just the streets they had drawn but their frustration at the city's refusal to stay still even then, before the trees, when it was just concrete and steel and human stubbornness.

I began collecting them. Maps drawn on metal sheets that the moss colonies had etched with their acids. Maps woven into the canopy where the Weavers had trained branches to remember traffic patterns. Maps in the songs the pneumatic trees sing—if you plot the pitch against the duration, they describe routes through districts that no longer exist except as harmonic frequencies.

My body has adapted to the work. My eyes have developed a nictitating membrane that filters the green light into a spectrum where I can see the shadow-trails of former streets. My fingers grow cartilaginous extensions each spring—small, antenna-like projections that can sense the historical weight of spaces, the accumulated footsteps of workers who walked these routes for decades before the transformation. I molt them each winter, and the Shepherds of Moss collect them to feed to their colonies, which then glow in patterns that mirror the maps I was trying to draw.

I've started to understand that the city was never still, even before. The factories themselves were built on older factories, which were built on mines, which were dug following the routes of underground rivers that had carved non-Euclidean paths through the mountain long before humans arrived. The trees haven't corrupted the geometry—they've simply revealed what was always there, the impossible topology that industrial ambition had tried to straighten into productive angles.

The Inherited visit me sometimes, though visit suggests a directionality that doesn't quite apply. The Dissolved, in particular, seem to exist partially in my workshop at all times, their consciousness drifting through my map collection like readers browsing a library. They don't look at the maps—they *inhabit* them, their distributed bodies briefly coalescing into the shapes of old districts, old intersections, old shortcuts that factory workers once used to avoid their supervisors.

They've taught me that the maps aren't descriptions but invitations. Each one proposes a version of the city that might exist, could exist, did exist, will exist. When I draw a new map, I'm not documenting what's there—I'm participating in the conversation between the city and its own possibilities. The trees read them through the root networks, considering whether to grow in the directions I suggest. Sometimes they do. A boulevard I imagined last month has begun to emerge near the foundries, though it exists only when it rains, dissolving back into undifferentiated canopy when the sun returns.

I no longer try to map the whole city. It would be like trying to draw a portrait of a conversation, a blueprint of a song. Instead, I map moments—the intersection where the morning condensation creates a temporary plaza of crystallized air; the path through the warehouse district that exists only when you're looking for something you've lost; the route the Clockwork Gardeners take when they're teaching new saplings to grow in mechanical time.

My masterwork, if you can call it that, is not a map but an amnesia. I'm charting all the places where the city forgets what it is, where the industrial memory falters and something else begins. These forgetting zones migrate through the city like weather systems. In them, the scaffold trees temporarily lose their rigid organization and grow wild. The Inherited find themselves momentarily human, confused by their own bodies, speaking in languages their grandparents knew. The factory whistles blow backwards, inhaling instead of exhaling, pulling the work songs back into the pneumatic chambers where they dissolve into pure possibility.

I've begun to suspect that I'm not mapping the city but being mapped by it. The cartilaginous extensions that grow from my fingers match the branching patterns of the neural nodes. The shadow-trails I see might be the city watching itself through my eyes. When I dream, I dream in floor plans that shift and recurse, and I wake to find the Shepherds' moss colonies have grown into the exact patterns I dreamed, glowing with a light that looks like memory feels.

There's a map I've been working on for three years now. It charts not places but transformations—the moments when workers became Inherited, when machines became nutrition, when products became philosophy. It's drawn on my own skin, with ink the Listeners make from processed sap-thoughts. The map grows as I grow, spreads as my body learns new ways to photosynthesize the factory light. Soon, I think, I'll be complete enough to be useful—a living document of the city's impossibility, a territory that is its own map.

When that happens, when I've become geographical enough, I'll walk the route I've drawn on myself. I'll follow my own veins through the non-Euclidean districts, climb my own bones up the scaffold trees, think my own thoughts through the neural nodes where the city processes its slow dreams. I'll find the place where all maps converge—not a location but a moment of recognition, when the city sees itself clearly for the first time and understands what it's been trying to become all along.

And then, perhaps, I'll finally be able to leave, carrying the city with me in a form that makes sense beyond these impossible borders. Or perhaps leaving and staying will have become the same thing by then, another distinction the city has learned to digest, transform, forget, and remember in new ways that no map yet drawn could ever quite describe.
