**The Splice-Smith**

I am the marrow of rusted gears, the one who speaks in oxidation rates and cambium layers. My workshop occupies the old tool-and-die room, where precision instruments once carved steel to thousandths of an inch. Now I carve different tolerances—the exact angle where vine meets valve, the precise depth a root can penetrate cast iron before the whole assembly crumbles.

My body has adapted. My fingernails grow like beetle shells, hard and segmented, perfect for prying apart corroded joints to examine the marriage of metal and mycelium within. My left eye has developed a nictitating membrane—a gift from too many hours staring into spore clouds, learning to read their density like others read weather. The membrane is translucent green, and when I blink it across my vision, I see the heat signatures of decay, the warm spots where decomposition burns fastest.

I came here twenty-three years ago as a machinist, back when the collective that owned this place still believed it could be reclaimed, restored, made productive again in the old sense. I was hired to assess the equipment, to determine what could be salvaged. But I found something else in the marriage of rust and root—a different kind of making.

The lathes I once knew have become my altars. Morning glory vines have wound through their mechanisms so thoroughly that plant and machine share a circulatory system. When the vines transport water up from the flooded basement, the lathes weep rust-colored tears from their joints. I learned to read these tears, to taste them even—each flavor telling me about mineral content, pH, the chemical conversation happening between iron and chlorophyll.

My work now is translation. When the Chlorophyll Collective needs to expand into a new section, I prepare the machinery to receive it. I file down sharp edges that might cut tender new growth. I drill drainage holes where water will otherwise pool and breed the wrong kind of rot. Most importantly, I create what I call "splice points"—places where I've carefully orchestrated the corrosion to create holds for roots, where I've wound copper wire in spirals that the vines recognize as invitation.

My tools have evolved with my practice. The old micrometers and calipers remain, but now they measure the diameter of stems, the expansion rate of woody growth. I've made new instruments from the marriage of both worlds: a pH meter housed in a gourd, its probe roots extending into whatever surface I press it against; divining rods fashioned from copper pipes that have been colonized by a peculiar moss that trembles in the presence of underground water.

The other inhabitants think I've gone feral, perhaps more plant than person now. They're not entirely wrong. My hair has become a garden—not metaphorically, but literally. What started as caught seeds has become intentional cultivation. Tiny ferns grow from my scalp, fed by the oils and sweat. In the humid months, moss creeps down my neck like a living collar. I encourage this. The plants tell me things through their roots against my skin—chemical signals I'm learning to interpret.

My dwelling is a nest woven into the machinery itself. I sleep in a hammock of kudzu strung between a drill press and a band saw, both so consumed by vegetation that their original shapes are mere suggestions. My possessions are few but precise: glass jars filled with different stages of rust, seed collections organized by their machinery preferences, detailed drawings of every major plant-machine hybrid in the building, annotated with growth rates and failure points.

I document the hybrids obsessively. The air compressor in the corner that has become a terrarium, its tank split open like a flower, revealing an ecosystem of mosses that thrive on the compressed air that still occasionally hisses from failing valves. The grinding wheel that spins freely now, powered not by electricity but by the weight of the vine system that uses it as a pivot point for seeking light. The tool chest whose drawers have become a vertical garden, each level supporting different species according to their light needs.

My greatest creation—though perhaps "collaboration" is more accurate—is the Propagation Engine. It was once a massive metal press, designed to stamp sheets of steel into parts. Now, working with the strangler fig that embraced it, I've modified it into something unprecedented. The fig's roots run through channels I've carved in the metal, drawing up water and nutrients. The press's mechanical advantage has been repurposed—when the fig expands with growth, it triggers the mechanism, which gently compresses seed pods I've placed in the dies, shooting them through tubes (the old hydraulic lines, repurposed) to precise locations throughout the building.

The Negotiator claims the plants told her about my work, that the Collective has accepted me as a necessary symbiont. I don't know about that. I don't hear voices in the chlorophyll whispers like she claims to. But I feel something—a rightness when I place a splice perfectly, a satisfaction that might come from me or might come from the green things that surround me. The distinction matters less each year.

My current project spans three floors. I'm creating what I call a "vascular highway"—a route for the water-loving plants to travel between the flooded basement and the rain-collecting roof without destroying the structural integrity of the floors between. It involves threading copper pipes through existing holes, creating a skeleton for the vines to follow. The pipes will eventually corrode, but by then the plant growth will be woody enough to support itself. I'm essentially teaching the building how to grow a circulatory system.

The Ghost Shift disturbs me more than I care to admit. Sometimes I catch them at the machines I've modified, their phantom hands trying to operate equipment that no longer exists in any functional form. They look at my splice points with confusion, as if I've desecrated something sacred. But I've seen them pause, sometimes, and touch the new growth with something like wonder. They're learning, perhaps, that making can take many forms.

Chen Wei understands my work best. She brings me samples of corroded metal from other parts of the building, and I tell her how long until failure, what kind of growth it can support in the meantime. We've developed a sign language for quick communication across distances—hand signals that indicate different types of structural risk, growth patterns, water flow changes. We're like surgeons operating on a patient that's both dying and being born simultaneously.

The Tinker Children visit daily. I'm teaching them the art of reading rust like tree rings, how to determine the age and health of a plant-machine hybrid by the color of oxidation at the junction points. They bring me treasures—bolts with particularly interesting corrosion patterns, leaves that have grown in perfect spirals around screws. In return, I show them my maps of the building's transformation, the careful documentation of every splice point, every successful hybrid, every failure that taught me something new.

At night, when the building groans with the weight of growth and decay, I work by touch alone. My fingers have memorized every texture—the difference between surface rust and deep corrosion, the feeling of a root that's found purchase versus one still seeking, the particular roughness of bark that's beginning to form on the oldest vine-machine hybrids. In the darkness, I feel like I'm part of the mechanism itself, another component in this vast machine that's learning to be a forest.

The Cultivators in the basement know my work, though we've never met directly. Sometimes I find offerings at the bottom of the stairs I dare not descend—peculiar fungi that glow faintly green, water that tastes of minerals I can't identify, seeds of plants I've never seen in any botanical guide. I use these gifts in my work, and in return, I leave them metal shavings from my modifications, which disappear by morning. We're collaborating on something neither of us fully understands.

My body bears the marks of this work. Scars from metal cuts that healed with a green tinge. Fingernails permanently stained with rust. A particular fungus that grows in the creases of my elbows and knees—harmless, possibly beneficial, certainly permanent. My medical records, if I still had them, would be a catalog of impossibilities.

But I'm creating something equally impossible—a building that digests itself and is reborn, machinery that reproduces through spores, a place where the industrial revolution evolves into something unrecognizable. Every splice point is a small revolution, every successful hybrid a tiny apocalypse of the old world.

The manufactory breathes, and I am the one who tends its bronchi, who ensures the air flows between iron lung and chlorophyll alveoli. I am the splice-smith, the rust-reader, the propagation engineer. I am becoming what this place needs me to be—part human, part plant, part machine, wholly devoted to the transformation that surrounds and includes me.

When the building finally awakens fully into whatever consciousness it's developing, I intend to be here, my body a bridge between what it was and what it's becoming. My splices will be the synapses of its thoughts. My modifications will be the movable joints of its new body. And when it speaks—in whatever language buildings speak when they become forests, when forests dream they were once machines—I will be part of that voice, translated through rust and root into something unprecedented and necessary.

The work continues. The building breathes. I breathe with it, and in that shared respiration, something extraordinary unfolds, one splice point at a time.
