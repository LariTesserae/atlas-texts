I am the Archivist of Evaporation.

My laboratory sits at the exact center of the plateau, where the first settlers discovered the origin point—a perfect circle of obsidian that sweats constantly, regardless of temperature differentials. Here, the stone itself exhales moisture from some deep, unknown source. I've built my workspace around this phenomenon: concentric rings of collection apparatus, each calibrated to capture droplets at different stages of their emergence from the rock.

I am old now, old enough that my regulatory system has begun its slow failure. But instead of retiring to the edge-hammocks, I've modified myself. Tubes run from my failing moisture pores directly into my equipment. My body has become part of the measurement system—I am both observer and instrument, recording the subtle changes in mineral content, bacterial load, and temperature that occur as water moves from stone to air to flesh and back again.

My skin has developed a peculiar transparency in patches where I've repeatedly exposed myself to raw thermal discharge. Through these windows, you can see moisture actually moving through my subcutaneous channels, branching like rivers viewed from impossible heights. I've catalogued seventeen distinct flow patterns that correlate with atmospheric pressure changes. My assistant says I look like a living map of watersheds that don't exist.

I collect failures. Every collapsed settlement, every defunct bacterial culture, every industrial accident leaves behind a chemical signature in the water. I've developed a technique for reading these histories in the condensation—each droplet carries the memory of where it's been, what it's touched, what died in it. My archive contains thousands of sealed vials, each labeled with precise coordinates and timestamps, containing the last moisture collected from places that no longer exist.

The children think I'm mad because I claim to taste colors in the steam. But I've trained my modified tongue, its surface restructured by decades of exposure, to detect the specific mineral signatures of different bacterial colonies. The purple films taste of copper and time. The gold ones carry hints of sulfur and something almost like sweetness. When the industrial districts have an accident, I know before the alarms sound—the air tastes of heated metal and panic.

My greatest discovery, which I've told no one, is that the plateau is growing. Imperceptibly, perhaps a millimeter per year, but the black glass is spreading outward and upward. The water that rises from below carries dissolved minerals that precipitate out when they hit the cooler surface air, adding layer upon microscopic layer to the obsidian base. We're not just living on this impossible place—we're building it, breath by breath, drop by drop.

I've calculated that at current rates, in ten thousand years, the plateau will have risen another ten meters. The air will be different then—thicker still, the pressure requiring new adaptations. I've begun breeding bacterial cultures that might survive those future conditions, seeding them in the deepest channels where they can evolve undisturbed.

My quarters adjourn the laboratory, a single room where every surface is covered in living charts—bacterial colonies engineered to change color in response to various atmospheric conditions. The walls are a constantly shifting map of the plateau's microclimate, patterns emerging and dissolving like thoughts. I sleep very little. The sound of dripping is too loud here, too close to the source. Sometimes I think I can hear the planet's heartbeat in the rhythm of condensation.

I wear no protective gear anymore. My mesh clothing has long since corroded away, and I work naked except for a collection belt studded with sample vials. The steam has scarred me into something between human and mechanism, my modified body a testament to the transformation this place demands. My eyes no longer close completely—the membranes have thickened into permanent lenses that I can adjust but never fully retract.

My apprentices think I'm dying, but they're wrong. I'm crystallizing, like minerals precipitating from an oversaturated solution. Each day, I become a little more like the plateau itself—hot, wet, impossible, persistent. My blood has thickened to the consistency of the bacterial mucus. My breath comes out as visible moisture even in this saturated air.

I've written my findings in living text—bacterial cultures trained to maintain specific patterns, encoding data in their growth configurations. Long after my body finally succumbs to the inevitable dissolution, these colonies will continue telling the story of this place, written in purple and gold across the wet black stone.

Yesterday, or perhaps it was last year—time moves strangely when you're this close to the source—I discovered something extraordinary. The water remembers. Not metaphorically, but literally. The molecular structure carries information, patterns that repeat and evolve like genetics. The plateau is not just alive—it's thinking, processing, learning. And through my failing, saturated body, connected to its circulation systems, I've become part of its consciousness.

I am the memory of water, the archive of an impossible city, the keeper of condensation's secrets. When I finally dissolve, I'll rain back down into the channels, carrying everything I've learned into the eternal cycle.

The plateau will remember me.
