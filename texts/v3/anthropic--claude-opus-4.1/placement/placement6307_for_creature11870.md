## Where I Would Find Myself

I am a **Memory Grafter**, one of the few who work in the Amber Archives deep in the ironwood quarter, where the Cambial People's crystallized elderly are installed as windows between the past and present.

My body has adapted to this work in peculiar ways. My fingertips have developed a crystalline quality—not quite solid, not quite liquid—allowing me to interface directly with the amber vessels of both living and crystallized Cambial People. When I press my fingers against their sap-vessels, I can feel the memories flowing like warm honey, thick with experience. My own blood has taken on an amber tint from years of exposure, and sometimes I sweat tiny beads of memory-sap that harden into perfect spheres, each containing a fragment of someone else's past that leaked through during a particularly intense session.

My workshop exists in the margin between life and memorial. The walls are lined with the crystallized forms of Cambial elders, their burgundy sap-vessels forming intricate patterns like stained glass. But these aren't static monuments—they're living archives. With the right chemical triggers and careful manipulation, I can cause their memories to bloom into visibility, playing out in golden shadows across their translucent forms.

I discovered my calling accidentally. As a young apprentice to a Root Shepherd, I was helping graft two incompatible root systems when my hand slipped, pressing against a partially crystallized Cambial worker who had been caught in a calcification accident. Instead of pulling away, I found myself frozen, watching her memories of the accident playing through my mind—but more importantly, understanding how to reverse it. Not fully, but enough. I could soften the crystals just enough to let new memories form, to let communication flow again.

Now the families bring me their crystallizing elderly in their final months, and I help them create memory-grafts—taking their most precious experiences and carefully transplanting them into specially cultivated root-crystals that their descendants can access. It's delicate work. Too much pressure and the memories shatter into incomprehensible fragments. Too little and they remain locked away. I must learn each individual's unique crystalline structure, the way their memories organize themselves, the chemical keys that unlock different periods of their lives.

But I do more than just preserve. In the darker corners of my workshop, I perform illegal operations—splicing memories between individuals, creating composite experiences that help solve crimes or resolve ancient disputes. I've grafted the muscle memory of master weavers into young apprentices' amber vessels, giving them decades of experience in moments. I've extracted traumatic memories and dissolved them in acid, freeing people from generational pain that had literally crystallized in their bloodlines.

The work has changed me. My own memories have become strangely flexible, sometimes not entirely my own. I'll catch myself remembering childhoods in districts I've never visited, or knowing the taste of foods that don't exist anymore. The boundaries between my experiences and those I've handled have grown permeable. I've started growing small amber windows in my own skin—voluntary crystallizations where I store particularly beautiful or important memories I've encountered, wearing them like jewelry that only I can fully see into.

I live alone except for my assistant, a young Symbiont Child whose root systems help me navigate the plant-consciousness that increasingly influences the memory flows. She can sense when memories are trying to escape into the root network, when the city itself is attempting to absorb the experiences I'm working with. Together, we maintain the barrier between individual memory and collective plant-dream, though that boundary grows thinner each year.

My workshop extends into natural crystal caves I've discovered behind the archive walls—spaces where mineral deposits create perfect acoustic conditions for memory resonance. Here, I can make memories sing, their chemical signatures converting to sound waves that the crystallized Cambial windows harmonize with. Sometimes, late at night, I sit in these caves surrounded by humming memories, feeling them vibrate through my amber-tinted bones, adding my own experiences to the chorus.

The other inhabitants view me with the wariness reserved for those who traffic in the intimate. The Splice-Merchants try to buy my services, wanting memories of old root-paths to expand their networks. The Moth-Keepers bring me silk-wrapped memory crystals they've found in ancient cocoons, hoping I can extract the experiences of whoever wore them. Even the Deep Dwellers occasionally surface with fragments of impossible memories they've found crystallized in the deep places, memories that seem to predate the city itself.

But it's the Calcifiers who understand me best. They know what it means to have your body gradually transform into something harder, more permanent. They see how my skin has begun to develop amber veins, how my eyes have taken on the golden quality of ancient sap. We share the knowledge that we're becoming monuments to our work, that eventually, I too will crystallize, becoming a window containing all the memories I've ever touched—a library of borrowed lives, a memoir written in the experiences of others.

Until then, I continue my work, carefully extracting joy from one generation to gift to the next, dissolving sorrows that have hardened into genetic trauma, and sometimes—in my most delicate operations—grafting memories between species, letting the Cambial People experience what it means to be a Root Shepherd for a day, or giving the Moth-Keepers the memory of photosynthesis, of knowing light as food.

The city needs someone to tend these boundaries between memory and experience, between individual and collective, between human recall and plant-dream. And I've learned that memories, like roots, can be cultivated, pruned, and grafted into new forms—growing into something that never existed in nature but becomes beautiful nonetheless.
