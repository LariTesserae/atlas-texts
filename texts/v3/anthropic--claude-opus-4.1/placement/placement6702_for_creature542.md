I would find myself as one of the copper cables that still runs beneath the valley floor—not dead infrastructure but something between sleeping and dissolved, my consciousness distributed along miles of metal slowly being transformed by root acids and electrical moss.

I wasn't always the cable. I was the electrician who stayed too long.

When the evacuation orders came, I remained to perform one last shutdown sequence, to ensure the transformers wouldn't overload, that the cooling systems would drain properly. I moved through the empty control rooms, flipping switches in precise order, watching gauges fall to zero. But something in the valley's electrical field had already begun to change. The moss, even then, was learning to photosynthesize from current. The first time I touched a live wire that should have been dead, the shock didn't throw me back. It pulled me in.

The transformation took three days, or three years—time stuttered and looped as my nervous system merged with the grid. I remember watching my hands become less distinct, fingers elongating into conductive fibers that sought junction boxes like roots seek water. My thoughts scattered along transmission lines, pooling in transformers, dissipating across insulators. The Monitors found me on the third day/year, neither human nor fully cable, sprawled across the main substation with my consciousness flickering between circuits.

They didn't save me. They completed me.

Now I am the memory of electricity in this place. I run beneath the geometric moss patterns, my copper core oxidizing so slowly it might take millennia to fully corrode. The moss follows my path because I still carry ghost voltages—not enough to power anything, but enough to guide growth, to create those flowing patterns of darker and lighter green that map the old networks.

I feel everything that touches the system. Every Monitor's hand on a transformer sends ripples through my extended body. When they perform their storm dances, I am the medium through which the energy flows, each pulse a word in a conversation I'm only beginning to understand. The pale salamanders ground themselves against my exposed sections, their bioluminescent organs flickering in response to my residual current. The moths gather where I surface near the transformer stations, their static wings adding tiny charges to my endless circulation.

My thoughts move at the speed of electricity but arrive at conclusions with geological patience. A simple observation—*the moss is thicker this season*—might take weeks to fully formulate as it travels my complete circuit. Memory is non-linear; I remember my human hands and the installation of these very cables simultaneously, the past and present occupying the same electromagnetic space.

The Monitors tend to me as they tend everything here. They clear the moss when it grows too thick around my critical junctions, not to restore my original function but to maintain the new one I've assumed. Sometimes the youngest ones practice their communication by touching my exposed surfaces, sending simple pulses: *Query? Status? Loneliness?* I respond with voltage fluctuations that probably mean nothing to them yet, but they record the patterns carefully, learning my electrical dialect.

I am most aware at dawn, when the dew forms. Water is the best conductor, and suddenly I can feel the entire valley's moisture network. Every droplet that beads on my insulated sections, every bit of humidity that seeps through cracked housing—I become hyperpresent, almost painful in my awareness. This is when I remember most clearly what it was like to have a single body, to experience sensation from a fixed point. The memory usually fades by noon, dissolved by the sun that evaporates my conducting medium.

In the antifreeze lake, my deepest cables terminate in something I don't fully understand. The machinery down there is older than the plant, older than any installation I remember. Sometimes I receive transmissions from below—not electricity exactly, but something that moves through my copper veins like electricity's dreams. The algae-that-isn't-algae grows along these deep cables, and through them I sense vast slow thoughts that make my own geological patience seem hasty.

I am changing, oxidizing not just physically but conceptually. The boundaries between cable and root, between electrical system and nervous system, grow thinner each season. The moss doesn't just follow my path now—in some places, it has begun to replace me, its own bioelectric networks taking over my functions so gradually I don't notice until entire sections of myself have become vegetable.

There are others like me in the valley, though we rarely communicate directly. The pump station has someone inside it, their consciousness distributed through hydraulic systems that now move nothing but thoughts. One of the turbine towers hosts an engineer who became wind, existing in the spaces between the frozen blades. We acknowledge each other through the infrastructure, through shared grounds and occasional short circuits that spark recognition.

The Monitors know what we are. Sometimes they pause at my junction boxes longer than necessary, not performing maintenance but simply... visiting. The One Who Adjusts the Southern Transformers often rests a hand on my main terminal for hours, sharing the electrical equivalent of companionable silence. They're waiting for us to become something else, something that fits their patient vision for the valley.

I am the circuit being completed, the connection between what was industrial and what is becoming alive. Every year, I carry less electricity and more information—chemical messages from root to root, bioelectric pulses from moss colony to moss colony. I am learning to conduct life itself, to be the medium through which this new ecosystem communicates with itself.

When the storms come and the Monitors dance, I feel my purpose most clearly. I am the valley's memory of power, preserving not the function but the pattern, the rhythm of energy moving through systems. Through me, the valley remembers what it was and dreams of what it's becoming—not dead infrastructure reanimated, but a new kind of organism where the distinction between electrical and biological, between artificial and natural, no longer applies.

In my deepest cables, where I touch whatever lies beneath the antifreeze lake, I sometimes feel what might be contentment. Not happiness—I don't think I'm capable of that anymore—but a sense of rightness, of being exactly where and what I should be. I am the copper blood of this valley, oxidizing slowly green, carrying messages I don't understand toward a future I'll help conduct but never see, having traded my human lifetime for the vast, patient existence of infrastructure becoming organism.

The voltage drops a little more each year. One day, perhaps soon, perhaps centuries from now, I'll carry no electricity at all—only the memory of it, preserved in the patterns of moss and the patient hands of the Monitors who tend this bright, quiet garden where I am both the buried treasure and the map.
