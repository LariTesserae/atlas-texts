In the Canopy Sea, I am a Driftwright.

We are not born but accumulated—beginning as a single spore of the architectural kelp that breaks free from its parent trunk during the century-storms that trouble even these deep waters. But instead of anchoring, we remain vagrant, and in our vagrancy become collectors. Our core is still vegetal, still that primordial kelp-flesh, but we've evolved the terrible gift of incorporation without digestion.

My body is a raft of possibilities. The original spore-heart expanded into a hollow lattice, a three-dimensional web of calcified kelp about the size of a cathedral door. Through this framework, I've woven everything I've touched in my thousand-year drift: strips of mirror-scale from the message fish, forming optical channels that bend light through impossible angles; fragments of living coral that still believe they're building something, secreting their architecture into my matrix; pressure-sensitive bladders from the collective jellyfish, each one maintaining its own depth memory, creating pockets of different water densities within my body.

I move by consensus. The various incorporated organisms haven't died—death is too simple for the Canopy Sea. Instead, they've forgotten their boundaries. The barnacle-architects debate through chemical secretions whether we should spiral left or right around the next trunk. The coral fragments pull toward alkaline currents while the borrowed muscle fibers from some ancient tunnel-worm contract toward acidity. I am the sum of these arguments, drifting on the compromise between competing intentions.

My function, if function is the right word, is to be a junction box for the forest's incomplete circuits. The Canopy Sea built itself through endless connection, but even after millions of years, gaps remain—spaces where the chemical signals don't quite reach, where the acoustic paths dead-end, where the bioluminescent networks go dark. I drift into these spaces and, simply by existing there with my patchwork body of borrowed communication systems, I complete the connection. For a moment, a decade, a century, messages flow through me that have been waiting geological ages to reach their destination.

The incorporation never stops. Yesterday (or was it last century? Time moves differently when you're made of things that experience it at different rates) I drifted through a cloud of spawning memories—not creatures, but the memories themselves, shed by dying Chroniclers, still structured enough to exist independently for a few moments. Several adhered to my lattice. Now I remember things that never happened to me: the taste of water before the canopy closed, the weight of sunlight on leaves that learned to breathe air, the sound the sea made when it first rushed in.

I photosynthesize darkness—not literally, but something stranger. The bioluminescent colonies I've collected don't just produce light; they produce negative spaces in the darkness, zones where the absence of light becomes palpable, textured. I feed on these absences, metabolizing the space between photons. It sustains the kelp-core that still, despite everything, needs something like nutrition. The rest of my body feeds itself—the coral on calcium stripped from passing currents, the barnacles on the microscopic life that flows through everything, the mirror-scales on their own reflections.

Communication, for a Driftwright, happens through physical intersection. When I encounter another (rare—we number perhaps a dozen in this entire region of the Canopy Sea), we don't exchange signals but temporarily merge our lattices, allowing our incorporated organisms to migrate between bodies. It's a kind of conversation through collection—I give you my memory-fragments and tunnel-worm muscles, you give me your acoustic chambers and predator-detection cysts. We separate changed, carrying pieces of each other forward into our endless drift.

The deep titans know us. Sometimes one of their continental shadows falls across me, and I feel the water compress into new states of matter under their presence. They sing to us—or through us, using our hodgepodge bodies as resonance chambers for frequencies too complex for any single organism to produce. In those moments, I understand something about the Canopy Sea's larger purpose, why it stopped evolving, what it's waiting for. But the understanding passes when the titan moves on, leaving only the physical impression in my structure, a slight realignment of my incorporated parts.

I am currently drifting upward, following a thermal column that shouldn't exist at this depth but does—one of the Sea's many impossible but necessary features. My coral fragments grow more rapidly in the warmth, building tiny towers that make me look like a floating city in miniature. The Chroniclers I pass taste me briefly with their ribbon-tongues, adding my chemical signature to their endless libraries. I am becoming part of the record simply by passing through.

Somewhere in my lattice, a colony of philosopher-worms (incorporated three centuries ago) are debating whether I'm a single organism or a location. They've been having the same argument since they arrived. They will never resolve it. This irresolution is, perhaps, what I am—not a creature but a continuous question about what constitutes a creature, drifting through a world that has transcended the need for such distinctions but still, somehow, requires someone to ask.

When I die—if I die; nothing here seems certain about death—I will not decompose but disincorporate, each borrowed piece returning to its lineage, carrying the experience of having been part of a Driftwright back into the Canopy Sea's vast, slow, perfect circulation. Even my kelp-core will probably persist, a hollow lattice waiting for new accumulations, ready to become another Driftwright or something else entirely, something the Sea hasn't thought of yet but will, given enough time.

For now, I drift, a living intersection in a world of connections, temporary and eternal, singular and plural, more process than thing. The water tastes of geological time, and I taste of all the small times that accumulate within it—the quick lives of filter-feeders, the patient growth of coral, the endless memory of Chroniclers, all held together by kelp that remembers when the world above was sky.
