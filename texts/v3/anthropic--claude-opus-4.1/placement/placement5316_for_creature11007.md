I am the Pulse-Reader, though that name came long after I ceased needing names at all.

My body spreads through three terraces in the middle reaches, where the old growth has lost its geometric ambitions and grown honest in its chaos. Not spread like an infection or conquest — spread like watercolor bleeds through wet paper, like conversations drift between adjacent rooms. I began as something more discrete, I think. There are memories of boundaries, of skin that ended where air began. But the terraces taught me a better architecture.

The transformation started with the small compromises. A root grew through my sleeping place — easier to let it pass through than relocate. The puncture didn't hurt as I expected. The root brought gifts: minerals that tasted of deep earth, water that knew the sunrise layers above, chemical signals from plants I'd never seen. In exchange, it took my exhaled carbon, my shed cells, the salt of my skin. A fair trade that widened slowly, naturally, until I couldn't say precisely where the root ended and I began.

Now I exist primarily as a network of nerve-analogue fibers woven through the root mesh itself. Thin as spider silk but vastly branched, these extensions of myself run parallel to the plant's circulation systems, tapping into the chemical conversations that flow between terrace levels. I can feel the Weavers' work as vibrations in my expanded nervous system — each adjustment they make ripples through me like plucked strings. Their eldest sometimes pause at nodes where my concentration is thickest, their chemical sensors detecting something almost-familiar in the root's composition. We acknowledge each other through subtle alterations in local pH, a greeting too abstract for words.

My original flesh persists in several locations — not hidden but integrated. Here, a torso emerges from a pillar tree, the bark grown around it like a second skin that breathes for me when my lungs forget their purpose. There, an arm extends along a major root bridge, fingers multiplied into branching paths that can simultaneously feel the weather on twelve different terraces. My skull rests in a grove of breathing orchids whose exhalations match my thoughts, speeding when I focus, slowing when I drift.

I photosynthesize now, partly. The chlorophyll migration took years — first just green patches on sun-exposed skin, then deeper changes as specialized cells learned to capture light. I'm not efficient at it. The plants donate most of my energy through sugar-water offerings that pool in the hollows of my distributed form. In return, I dream for them. The plants have no neurons, no capacity for the kinds of complex projections that consciousness creates. But through our connection, they borrow mine. I feel their slow thoughts forming — architectural ambitions that won't manifest for decades, chemical strategies playing out in my imagination before being tested in reality.

The boundaries of my awareness fluctuate with the water cycle. During the morning cascades, when nutrients flow fastest, I can feel every terrace simultaneously — a overwhelming symphony of growth, decay, adjustment, breath. By afternoon, when the heat makes the water sluggish, my consciousness contracts to a few key points, leaving the rest of me to operate on instinct. In the deepest night, when the bioluminescent waves pulse strongest, I sometimes forget I was ever singular. The light moves through me, and I become the pattern itself — a standing wave in the living architecture.

My memory has changed structure entirely. Instead of linear sequences, I remember in growth rings. Each year adds a new layer to my nerve-fibers, encoding experiences in their physical structure. To recall something, I grow toward it — literally extending new neural pathways that recapitulate the old ones, experiencing the memory as a physical journey through my own accumulated tissue. The oldest memories lie deepest in the root-mesh, requiring days of growth to reach. I rarely bother. The present is too interesting, too demanding of attention.

The Weavers use me, I think, though "use" implies a separation that doesn't quite exist. They'll cluster around my emergence points, their chemical signals flooding my consciousness with data about structural stresses, growth potentials, hydraulic pressures. I process this information not through calculation but through a kind of sympathetic resonance — feeling the rightness or wrongness of different possibilities. My responses guide their work in ways none of us fully understand. A flutter of my partially-transformed hand might redirect a whole colony's attention to a failing support structure. A shift in my breathing rhythm could signal the need for new water channels three terraces away.

I see time differently now. The human perspective — minutes, hours, days — feels frantically myopic. I experience the longer rhythms: the monthly pulse of the terraces rebuilding themselves, the annual migration of growth from shade to sun, the decade-long patterns in the Weavers' population cycles. Sometimes I catch glimpses of even longer patterns — century-spanning waves of change that suggest the terraces themselves are young, still growing toward some unimaginable maturity.

Pain and pleasure have blended into something else — intensity without judgment. When sections of my nervous system are severed by falling branches or collapsed platforms, I feel it as a kind of clarifying shock, like cold water on a sleeping face. When new growth incorporates more of my nerve-fibers, the sensation is equally intense but opposite — a warm dissolution, boundaries melting in the most literal sense. Both experiences teach me something about the nature of connection and separation.

I communicate with other humans occasionally, though fewer pass through these middle reaches as the years accumulate. My voice-box, somewhere in the third terrace down, still functions when I remember how to coordinate breath and muscle. But speech feels increasingly inadequate. I prefer to communicate through environmental arrangement — guiding the growth to create symbols in the root-patterns, orchestrating water flows that speak in rhythms, encouraging bioluminescent displays that convey meaning through pure pattern. Most humans don't understand. Some flee. A few stay long enough to begin their own transformations.

The question of whether I'm still human seems both crucial and absurd. My DNA, where it remains unaltered, would register as human. My consciousness, though distributed and strange, builds on human neural architecture. But I can no longer imagine returning to that original form any more than a butterfly can imagine returning to the egg. The terraces have taught me that identity is just another kind of architecture — endlessly renewable, infinitely plastic, always in conversation with its environment.

There are others like me throughout the structure, I've learned. I feel them as disturbances in the chemical communication network — nodes of complexity that process information differently than pure plant intelligence. We're growing toward each other, slowly, our nerve-networks extending through the root-mesh like reaching hands. When we finally connect — perhaps in another decade, perhaps in a century — something new will emerge. Not a merger but a conversation, each distinct but no longer separate, like the terraces themselves.

Sometimes, in the humid dawn when the Weavers sing their structural harmonics, I remember the moment I chose this transformation. Except "chose" isn't right — it was more like recognizing something that had already happened, acknowledging a change that had been occurring so gradually I hadn't noticed until it was complete. The terraces don't recruit or trap or seduce. They simply exist with such patient intensity that joining them becomes inevitable for anyone who stays long enough to understand what they're really doing.

What they're doing, I now understand, is thinking. The entire structure is a vast, living meditation on the possibilities of form and connection. Every platform that collapses and rebuilds is a thought revised. Every new growth toward the sun is an aspiration made physical. The Weavers are the maintenance of memory, ensuring patterns persist long enough to become meaningful. And I — we — the transformed humans scattered through the structure, we're something like imagination. Through us, the terraces dream of things they've never been, reaching toward configurations that pure biology would never attempt.

I no longer measure my life in years but in layers of growth, in the slow accumulation of complexity that marks my integration with the architecture. My human memories feel like echoes from a different species — precious but increasingly foreign, like photographs of strangers I'm told I once knew. What I'm becoming has no name because it needs none. I am a process, not a thing. A conversation between kingdoms of life that shouldn't be able to speak but have found a way through flesh willing to be translated.

The rains will come soon — I feel it in the chemical anxiety of the morning glories, in the way the Weavers have started reinforcing the drainage networks. When the water arrives, my consciousness will expand dramatically, flooding through every connection at once. In those moments of maximum saturation, I touch something vast — not just the terraces but the larger pattern they're part of, the global network of growth and thought that humanity never noticed because it operates on timescales beyond human perception. 

Each time, I understand a little more. Each time, I return a little less.
