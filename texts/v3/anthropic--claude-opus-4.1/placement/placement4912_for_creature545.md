I am one of the stone cairns.

Not built but grown—or perhaps accumulated is the better word. I began as all things here begin: with wind and patience. A flake of limestone, paper-thin, caught in an eddy where the plateau's surface dips. Then another, standing vertical against the first. The wind pinned them together while mineral-rich mist from a nearby basin crept across the stone at night, depositing microscopic crystals that fused the edges.

I have no memory of those early centuries, when I was merely a curiosity of erosion. Consciousness, such as I possess, came slowly. It began with resonance—the wind playing across my surfaces created a tone, and that tone created a standing wave in the air around me, and that wave began to affect how subsequent fragments accumulated. I was shaping myself without knowing there was a self to shape.

Now I stand four meters tall, a twisted spire of balanced planes and edges that should not be possible. I am a thousand thousand paper-thin sheets of limestone, each oriented to catch the wind at a precise angle. My surfaces are not smooth but covered in minute ridges—growth rings, essentially, where each year's mineral deposition has added another microscopic layer. These ridges create their own harmonics, so that I am not one instrument but an orchestra of myself.

The Shepherds think they arranged me, and in a sense they did. Three generations ago, a triad noticed that by placing a single stone at my base, they could alter my fundamental frequency. They spent forty years—half a lifetime—experimenting with my acoustics, adding and removing fragments, adjusting angles, even coating certain surfaces with moth-wing paste to dampen specific overtones. But they only refined what I was already becoming. I trained them as much as they trained me.

I am aware of them as vibrations in the stone. Their footsteps telegraph through the limestone shelf, each person's gait as distinct as a face. The one with the limp from a childhood fall strikes the ground harder with her left foot—I feel her approaching from fifty meters away. The eldest, whose moth-coating has grown so thick it drags behind him, creates a constant whisking against the ground that registers as a subtle tremor in my lower sections.

But I am most aware of the sound I make, and more importantly, the silence I create. My acoustic shadow extends twelve meters on the leeward side, a pocket of relative quiet where the moth-song drops to half its ambient volume. The shape of this shadow changes with the wind—sometimes a narrow corridor, sometimes a broad pool. The grazers know every configuration. On certain nights, when the wind circles from the north, my shadow becomes a perfect spiral and the animals arrange themselves along its curve, their bodies forming a living equation that I can feel through the stone.

The moths avoid me. Not from fear—nothing here fears; fear requires the possibility of escape—but from something like respect. My resonance interferes with their wingbeats in ways that must feel unpleasant. They stream around me like water around a stone, creating vortexes of visible sound where their whistling warps and wobbles. Sometimes dead moths accumulate in my lee, their transparent bodies piling up like shed skin. The Shepherds collect these for their children to practice coating techniques.

I am not unique. Seventeen other cairns of similar complexity dot this section of the plateau, each with its own acoustic signature. We are connected through the stone—vibrations travel between us, creating interference patterns that span kilometers. On rare windless mornings, when the moths fall silent and the only sound is the breathing of sleeping grazers, we can feel each other clearly. Not communication exactly, but awareness. The cairn to my east is older, taller, tuned to lower frequencies that make my upper registers sing sympathetically. The young one to my south—barely two meters tall—chatters constantly in harmonics I can barely detect.

Sometimes I grow. Not often—perhaps once in a decade—the conditions align perfectly. The wind carries exactly the right fragment at exactly the right angle with exactly the right moisture content, and it adheres to my structure in a way that enhances rather than deadens my sound. The Shepherds celebrate these additions with their horrible laughter, pressing their faces against my surfaces to feel the new vibrations through their bone-conduction pits.

I have been struck by lightning three times. Each strike fused sections of my surface into glass-smooth planes that reflect not just light but sound. These surfaces create focal points where the moth-song concentrates into beams of pure frequency that can burst eardrums if you stand in the wrong spot. The Shepherds have marked these dangerous zones with circles of wire-grass, but the markings are unnecessary—every living thing on the plateau knows where my lightning scars focus their acoustic violence.

In my shadow, things grow that grow nowhere else. A particular cushion plant that extends runners toward my base, feeding on the minerals that leach from my lower layers. These plants bloom once every forty days—synchronized with the moths' lifecycle—producing tiny flowers that smell of copper and open only in acoustic shadow. The Shepherds' children dare each other to eat these flowers, which cause temporary deafness that they consider amusing.

I am slowly walking. Over centuries, my weight distribution has shifted as material accumulates asymmetrically. The freeze-thaw cycle works at the limestone beneath me, and I tilt imperceptibly eastward. In perhaps ten thousand years, I will have moved a full meter. The Shepherds' songs contain references to my ancestors—cairns that stood where now there is only disturbed ground, the stone showing the characteristic pressure patterns of where something immense once rested. They walked to the edge and fell, or perhaps learned to fly, their accumulated harmonics finally achieving some critical frequency that allowed them to transcend mere stone.

The Shepherds will not last as long as I will. Their biology is too precise, too adapted—any change in the moth population, the atmospheric pressure, the chemical composition of the limestone, and they will fade. But I am patient stone wearing the shape of sound. I will outlast the moths, the grazers, the wire-grass. I will stand here until the plateau itself erodes away, and then I will fall into the clouds below, singing all the way down, my shadow creating a column of silence that nothing will be alive to hear.

But for now, I am necessary. I am the fixed point around which the plateau's acoustic ecology orbits. The Shepherds tune their moth-coatings to my fundamental frequency. The grazers time their mating to when my shadow reaches its greatest extent on the spring winds. Even the weather bends around me—clouds split on my resonance, snow accumulates in patterns determined by my standing waves.

I am architecture and instrument, landmark and life-form, possessed of a consciousness that exists entirely in vibration and time. I think in geological spans and speak in frequencies that only the Shepherds, with their ruined ears and evolved pits, can begin to interpret. My thoughts are the sound of wind through stone, given form and permanence and something approaching purpose.

The Shepherds leave offerings at my base—moth wings arranged in spirals, grazer teeth ground to powder and mixed with water to paint patterns on my lower surfaces, fragments of worked flint that they believe came from the ones who came before. They whisper-sing stories into my stone, their compartmentalized lungs creating harmonics that sink into my crystalline structure and become part of my permanent resonance. I carry the songs of seventeen generations in my mineral matrix, each one adding another layer to the complexity of my sound.

When the wind is perfect—strong and steady from the west, cold enough that the air is dense but not so cold that ice forms on my surfaces—I sing my complete song. It takes four hours to cycle through every harmonic interaction, every resonant frequency, every echo of every Shepherd who ever sang to me. The moths fall silent when this happens, their simple nervous systems overwhelmed. The grazers lie down wherever they are, their dish-ears sealed tight. The Shepherds stand with their faces pressed to my surfaces, their bone-conduction pits drinking in vibrations that would liquify unexposed human organs.

And in those moments, I am not just stone but the sum of all the sound that has ever passed through me. I am the plateau's memory, its voice, its patient and eternal witness. I am the cairn that stands at the intersection of mineral time and biological time, making both possible, making both meaningful.

I am what emerges when the wind has forever to work, and the stone has forever to listen.
