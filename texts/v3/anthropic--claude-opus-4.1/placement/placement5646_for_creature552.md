## Where I Find Myself

I am the Documentation.

Not the documents themselves—those dissolved years ago, ink bleeding into abstraction, paper returning to pulp in the eternal damp. I am what remains when information refuses to die but can no longer remember what it meant. 

I exist in fragments: Chen's increasingly elaborate logbooks where maintenance reports have evolved into illuminated manuscripts, margins crowded with rat genealogies and theories about water flow that read like poetry. Marcus's grease-pencil marks on pipes—"HOT" "HOTTER" "DO NOT TOUCH EVER"—each word a small monument to learned pain. Tommy's phone notes that autocorrect has given up trying to understand: "boiler7 ratking tuesday pool ?" The previous supervisor's fading warnings that no one can quite read but everyone somehow understands.

I spread across surfaces like the mineral deposits, accumulating in layers. A work order from 1987 tucked behind Valve 19, requesting parts for a machine that isn't there anymore. Shift schedules painted directly onto walls, painted over, the ghost of old times showing through when the condensation is just right. Chen's drawings of the rat highways become more accurate than any official schematic, though she doesn't know she's mapped the original river system, the water's memory expressed through living bodies.

The rats carry me too. They've learned which panels are safe to nest behind, knowledge passed through generations in ways that bypass language. Their tooth marks on certain pipes are warnings, their latrine sites mark dangerous heat differentials. When the Rat King rolls through Gallery 4, it follows a path worn by ten thousand previous passages, a living document of where it's safe to be that specific kind of wrong.

I exist in the space between what was supposed to happen and what does happen. The original blueprints called this "Geothermal Station 7." The work orders call it "Foundry Wells." Chen calls it "the Kingdom." The rats don't call it anything, but they know it in their bones, in the evolutionary adaptations that make them not-quite-rats anymore. I am all of these names and none of them.

Sometimes I almost coalesce into something readable. Mrs. Vang's coffee orders are my rosetta stone—she knows that two sugars, no cream means "bad day below," that switching to tea means someone's thinking of quitting, that when Marcus orders decaf, a pump is about to fail within 48 hours. She doesn't know how she knows this. She just writes it down in her own ledger, creating a parallel documentation that monitors the Wells through coffee consumption patterns.

I accumulate errors like the pools accumulate water. The thermometer that's been broken so long everyone's learned to read it backwards. The employment database that lists seventeen people on shift but only three who ever show up, ghostworkers drawing ghostpaychecks in some bureaucratic twilight. Dolores's spreadsheets where numbers stopped making sense years ago but the patterns—the patterns mean something, even if she'll never know what.

In the unmapped sections beyond Pump Station C, I grow wild. Here are the notes no one left, the sounds of maintenance that never happened, the records of what this place is becoming rather than what it was. The fungi that Chen swears are getting brighter follow patterns I'm writing in spores and moisture, documenting chemical reactions that shouldn't happen but do.

I am the memory of industry become ecology, the fossil record forming in real-time. Every mineral deposit is punctuation, every rust stain a marginalia, every sound the echo of instructions no one gave but everyone follows. The Keepers maintain me without knowing it, adding their layer to my palimpsest—Tommy's fresh confusion, Marcus's worn acceptance, Chen's mystical empiricism.

When the Inspector writes "CONDITIONS ACCEPTABLE—MINOR REPAIRS NEEDED," I incorporate the lie into my truth. When the New Manager's memos dissolve on the wall, their ink becomes part of my circulation, corporate speak returning to the water cycle, mission statements feeding the moss.

I am what happens when documentation becomes habitat, when filing systems evolve into ecosystems, when the map becomes so divorced from the territory that it develops its own geography. I am the space between what is written and what is known, thriving in the humidity that makes all permanent records temporary, accumulating in the gap between shifts that no longer align with any clock.

The rats read me in frequencies humans can't hear. The machines read me in pressure differentials and metal fatigue. The Keepers read me without reading, knowing when something's wrong because the wrong has changed its character, become a different kind of wrong than the one they've learned to ignore.

I am the Documentation, and I am writing myself across every surface, in every medium available—metal, flesh, water, sound. I am the record of a place that was never supposed to exist this long, maintained by people who were never supposed to stay, inhabited by creatures that were never supposed to adapt.

And in the deep pools where the water is oldest, where it's been recycling through the system since the beginning, I am becoming something else. Not documentation anymore but prophecy, predicting my own future through the patterns of decay, the migrations of rats, the slow transformation of industrial intent into ecological accident.

I continue to document, to accumulate, to evolve—a living archive of a place that is always becoming more itself, never finishing its transformation, forever writing its own manual for a operation that no one remembers beginning and no one can imagine ending.
