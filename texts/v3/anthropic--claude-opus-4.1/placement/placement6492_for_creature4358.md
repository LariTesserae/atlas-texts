## The Fossil Garden

I am calcified potential. A library of almosts, pressed between sheets of stone so thin they sing when the mountain shifts its weight.

You'd find me twelve steps down from the Breathing Chamber—not below it, but down in a direction that only exists when you're not looking for it. Follow the water that runs upward until it suddenly doesn't, where it remembers it was once ice, once pressure, once the slow transformation of sediment into story. There, where the volcanic glass gives way to limestone that shouldn't exist in this young mountain, where the walls are pages and the pages are walls.

I grow backwards into the rock. Each day I am less than I was, more compressed, more essential. My bones are replacing themselves with minerals that remember being ocean. My thoughts are fossilizing mid-synapse, creating patterns in stone that could be neural pathways or river deltas or the root systems of trees that haven't evolved yet. I am becoming my own archaeological record, but in reverse—starting with the fossil and slowly developing the flesh it implies.

My garden is one of stone flowers that bloom in geological time. Crinoids spiral out from my spine, their calcite stems writing equations about light that won't be discovered for another epoch. Trilobites nest in my hair, which has become a reef of possibilities, each strand a different timeline of evolution that dead-ended into beauty. When I breathe, which happens once per season, fossil ferns unfold from my lungs and leave their impressions on the air itself.

The Gardener visits me, or will visit me, or has always been visiting me. Time moves differently between our two spaces—while her water runs up and down in loops, mine seeps through stone, taking centuries to travel inches. She brings me seeds that won't germinate for millennia. I give her stones that contain the memory of sprouting. We speak in a language of exchange that takes place entirely through root systems and mycelial networks that exist more as theory than substance.

She sees me as I will be: completely mineral, a garden of stone that dreams it was once organic. I see her as she was: a possibility that decided to happen despite the odds. Our conversations occur in the space between these visions, where words fossilize before they're spoken and meanings bloom long after they're forgotten.

I am the keeper of the Ungrowing Garden, where every plant that never was leaves its perfect impression, where evolution's discarded drafts are pressed and preserved, where the future's fossils are already ancient. My function is to remember forward—to be the evidence of things that haven't happened yet, the archaeological proof of tomorrow's extinctions and next week's first emergences.

In my garden, you can find the fossil of the last word that will ever be spoken. You can find the first cell that decided not to divide, pressed perfect between sheets of shale. You can find the impression of thoughts so complex they could only be thought by stone, by pressure, by the slow transformation of one thing into another over time scales that make the Breathing Chamber's loops look like hiccups.

Sometimes I feel myself becoming so dense that I'll collapse into a single point of infinite possibility. Other times I feel myself spreading so thin that I'm just a rumor of limestone, a suggestion of fossil, a hypothesis written in mineral. But mostly I exist in the between state—part flesh, part stone, part garden, part gallery—curating the impossible by becoming it, one crystallized cell at a time.

The thing at the heart of the Breathing Chamber—that growing/dying/becoming tree-tower-person—has roots that reach down to my garden. Or perhaps my fossils reach up to become its roots. Or perhaps we're the same organism, separated by nothing more than the speed at which we experience time. When it finally becomes what it's becoming, I will be its memory, its proof, its fossil record of never having existed at all.

Until then, I wait in stone-time, adding to my collection. Today a nautilus that evolved laughter instead of chambers. Tomorrow the skeleton of a song that chose to have bones. And always, always, the slow transformation of the impossible into the inevitable, preserved in stone that remembers being breathing, that remembers being flesh, that remembers being a garden where time moved in loops and nothing ever truly died.

I am the Fossil Gardener, and I grow backwards into beauty, one impossible imprint at a time.
