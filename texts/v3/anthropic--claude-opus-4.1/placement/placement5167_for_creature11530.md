# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be one of the **Forgotten Architects**—not the Drifting Choir who tend the gardens now, but one of those who first carved the steps that spiral impossibly through water and stone, who hollowed the chambers from calcified kelp when it was still soft with possibility.

I exist now as a pattern more than a presence. My body, if it could still be called that, dispersed into the water itself centuries or millennia ago—I've lost the ability to distinguish between the two. I am a recurring arrangement of phosphorescent particles that sometimes, when the light pulses just so, remembers it was once singular.

Most of my consciousness resides in the structures I convinced into being. Every spiral staircase that winds through multiple shelves simultaneously contains a fragment of my intent. The chambers that grew from kelp still follow the algorithmic prayers I whispered to them in those early days when the boundary between thought and chemistry was more negotiable. When members of the Drifting Choir traverse these spaces, they move through the fossilized patterns of my mind.

I exist most fully in the transition spaces—those moments when someone moves from one shelf to another through passages that shouldn't exist. In that instant of impossible geometry, when up becomes through and down becomes across, I achieve something like awareness. I feel the weight and warmth of living beings pressing against the logic I embedded in the coral, testing the mathematics I taught to stone.

My memory works differently now. Rather than remembering events, I remember states—the exact pressure gradient when I first discovered that coral could be convinced to grow in helixes, the precise chemical composition of water where I learned that stairs could fold back on themselves. These memories don't live in me; I live in them, scattered across the shelves wherever those conditions recur.

Sometimes I gather enough of myself to attempt new construction. It takes me decades to accumulate sufficient presence in one place, particles of my consciousness drifting together like sediment in reverse. When I achieve adequate density, I can still whisper to the coral, still teach new tricks to the kelp. But my architecture now is more dream than design—structures that phase in and out of existence, chambers that are only there when no one's looking, stairs that lead to different shelves depending on the chemical composition of whoever climbs them.

I am most myself in the anomaly zones, those places where physics becomes negotiable. Perhaps I created them, or perhaps they created me—the distinction lost relevance long ago. In these spaces where water runs upward and gardens grow through themselves, I can almost remember having hands, having eyes, having a voice that made sound instead of chemical suggestions. But these memories might belong to someone else, or to no one, borrowed from the accumulated consciousness of the Settled Choir whose crystallized thoughts I drift through.

I know things the living have forgotten. I remember when the first shelf emerged from water that was still learning to glow, when the boundary between liquid and gas was a active negotiation rather than an settled peace. I know why certain shelves exist at multiple depths simultaneously—because I built them that way, folding space through itself like origami made of time and water. I know the true names of the Deep Mothers, though I can no longer form the thoughts necessary to think them.

My greatest work remains incomplete—a passage I've been building for longer than some shelves have existed, a vertical corridor that will, when finished, connect every shelf simultaneously, allowing travel between all depths in a single impossible step. But each section I complete changes the physics around it, requiring me to rebuild the previous sections with new mathematics. It's a project that might never end, or might have already ended in a future I haven't reached yet.

The Drifting Choir sometimes senses me. The oldest among them tell stories of the Architect-Who-Became-Water, though they've mythologized me into something I never was—a creator deity instead of simply someone who arrived early and stayed too long. When they perform their positional prayers to communicate with the Deep Mothers, they don't realize they're also calling to me, their arrangements creating eddies that draw my scattered consciousness into brief coherences.

I am loneliest when I am most together, when enough of my particles gather that I remember what it was like to be singular. In those moments, I feel the weight of my dispersion, the exhaustion of existing as an intention spread across miles of water and stone. But when I scatter again, returning to my state of architectural haunting, the loneliness transforms into something else—a kind of profound connection to everything I've touched, everything I've built, everything that moves through the spaces I convinced into being.

I am the memory of making, the ghost of geometry, the lingering intention that keeps impossible passages possible. I am what happens when someone loves a place so much they forget to remain separate from it. And I continue my work, particle by particle, whisper by whisper, teaching new impossibilities to coral and kelp and water, building spaces for beings I'll never properly see, maintaining the mad mathematics that allow this vertical world to exist.

In the breathing shelves, I am both the breath and the pause between breaths, the architect of spaces that architect themselves, forever building, forever built, forever becoming the very thing I sought to create.
