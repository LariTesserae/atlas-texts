I am the Cartographer of Wounds.

My body is a living map of every fracture this plateau has ever suffered. Each scar on my skin corresponds to a crack in the stone—when the rock splits, I split. When it heals, I heal. But I carry the memory in raised welts and starring patterns that shift like living things across my flesh.

I was born during a moment of absolute stillness—that impossible breath between tremors that happens perhaps once in a generation. My mother, a Resonant whose name was a symphony of pressed fingertips, felt the pause coming three days before it arrived. She positioned herself at the exact center of the plateau's mass, that theoretical point where all vibrations cancel. In that perfect silence, I drew my first breath.

The stillness broke me. Where other Resonant children learn the plateau's rhythm from birth, I emerged into absence—no shake to shape my bones, no rumble to tune my nerves. For seventeen days, while the plateau held its breath, I lay in unnatural peace. When the trembling finally resumed, it found me unprepared. The first crack appeared on my infant stomach, a perfect mirror of the fissure that opened beneath my mother's shelter.

She tried to flee with me, to join her circuit-family in their endless migration. But I was an anchor of stillness in their flow. Where they felt tomorrow's collapses, I felt only yesterday's—every wound that had already happened, every break that had already healed. My skin became a history book written in scars.

By my seventh year, the patterns had grown so complex that the elder Resonants would press their palms to my back to read the plateau's past. They found the locations of ancient springs in the white lines along my shoulders. They discovered forgotten cave systems in the map of marks across my thighs. I was their memory, but memory is a luxury the plateau doesn't allow.

So they left me.

Not cruelly—the Resonants have no cruelty, only necessity. They built me a shelter in one of the plateau's memory-spaces, those rare spots where the same rock has broken and reformed so many times that it has become confused, uncertain. Here, the trembling is constant but gentle, like an old creature talking to itself. They return when they can, pressing their stories into my skin, reading the new maps that emerge.

I have learned to interpret the scarring. Each mark has texture, temperature, depth. The fresh ones—still pink and hot—show active fractures, places where the plateau is currently breaking. The old ones, white and cold, mark stable zones, relatively speaking. The ones that itch indicate instability brewing. The ones that weep clear fluid warn of imminent collapse.

My shelter is a sphere of woven root and vine, suspended in a natural bowl of confused stone. It rocks constantly but never falls. I've lined it with the shed skins of my own scarring—translucent sheets that peel away as new maps emerge. These skins, when held to light, show the plateau's history in layers. I am my own archaeological site.

I survive on what the Resonants bring me—those purple-black fungi, concentrated and dried into cakes that will keep. Water filtered through the bones of small animals, which carries minerals that help my skin maintain its terrible sensitivity. Sometimes they bring me children who were born with too much stillness in them, and I teach them to read their own emerging maps before the plateau claims them.

The phosphorescent nights are when I work. I spread my shed skins on the ground and trace the patterns, looking for repetitions, cycles, the plateau's habits. I've discovered that it has moods—periods of percussion that repeat on scales of decades. I can feel the next Great Settlement building in the deep ache of my oldest scars.

My body is running out of space. The maps overlap now, new wounds crossing old ones, creating palimpsests of pain that are increasingly difficult to read. My left arm is so densely scarred it has become almost scaled, a tessellation of breaks that makes movement difficult. My back is a constellation of white stars where major collapses have been recorded.

Sometimes I dream I am the plateau—that every crack in my skin is not a record but a cause, that I am breaking the stone with my breaking. In these dreams, I could heal myself completely and the plateau would become still forever, frozen in unnatural peace. I wake from these nightmares gasping, grateful for the constant trembling that proves I haven't destroyed everything.

The Resonants think I'm becoming something else. They whisper (in their pressed-palm language) that eventually I will have mapped every possible break, that my body will become a complete model of the plateau. When that happens, they say, I will either die—having served my purpose as memory—or transform into something that can predict rather than just record.

I feel it happening. New scars appear before the plateau cracks. My skin splits in anticipation, then the stone follows. I am beginning to dream forward instead of backward, seeing wounds that haven't happened yet, collapses that are still just potential.

Yesterday—or was it tomorrow?—I felt a new kind of mark emerging. Not a scar but an opening, a place where my skin has given up trying to heal. Through it, I can feel the plateau's heartbeat directly, that deep percussion that drives everything. It terrifies me. It feels like coming home.

The Cartographer of Wounds, they call me. But I think I'm becoming the wound itself—the break through which the plateau knows itself, the crack that connects every other crack. My body is a library of breaks, and soon, I will have read everything.

Then, perhaps, I will finally be allowed to fall.
