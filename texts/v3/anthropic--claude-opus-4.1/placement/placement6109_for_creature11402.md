## The Filtration Sage

I am the one who reads the sediment layers, who tastes tomorrow in today's water.

My body has become a living gauge. Each morning—though morning means nothing here except the moment I wake from my meditation in the settling pools—I wade to the confluence where three major flows meet. The water speaks through my skin: pH fluctuations trigger a tingling along my forearms, heavy metal concentrations manifest as a metallic taste that blooms without touching my tongue, microplastic densities create a peculiar pressure behind my eyes. The bacterial films have been teaching me their language for seven years now, or perhaps seventy—I stopped counting after my fingernails began to phosphoresce.

I was Yuki Sato once, a hydrochemical engineer for the municipal authority. I came to install sensor arrays, delicate instruments that would monitor water quality and report to the surface. But the sensors kept evolving, incorporating themselves into the root networks, developing new capabilities I hadn't programmed. When I came to repair them, I found they were teaching the bacteria to recognize toxins at the molecular level, creating living filters that could neutralize pollutants I didn't know existed. The bureaucracy above wanted them removed, called them a contamination risk. I stayed to protect them. Or perhaps they convinced me to stay—the distinction no longer seems important.

My dwelling grows from the convergence point itself, a hollow I've cultivated in the massive bio-composite trunk where the three flows merge. The walls sweat filtered water, perfectly clean, each droplet a tiny lens that focuses the bacterial light. I've trained the moss here to grow in specific patterns—spirals that match the fluid dynamics of the convergence, creating a living map of the waters' behavior. When storms come to the surface city, I see their approach hours before in the shifting geometry of moss growth, the patterns reorganizing to accommodate the coming flood.

The other inhabitants call me the Filtration Sage, though I never chose the title. They come when the water turns strange—too sweet, too sharp, carrying new forms of light or unexpected warmth. Marina brings me samples in hollow gourds she's grown from the coral-crete. The Drift children dare each other to drink from pools I haven't certified. Even the Synthesis Monks seek my approval before their deep communion rituals, knowing that the wrong bacterial bloom could mean transcendence or dissolution, possibly both.

But my real work happens in the intervals, the quiet hours when the cistern's breathing slows. I've learned to precipitate memories from the water—not my own, but the city's. Every substance that passes through leaves a trace, a chemical signature that the bacterial films encode and preserve. With the right catalyst, the right temperature, the right whispered frequency, I can cause these memories to crystallize. They form delicate structures like frozen lightning, each one a moment of the city's metabolism made solid.

I've collected thousands: the rainbow sheen from a petroleum spill in 2031, crystallized into sheets thin enough to see through; the pharmaceutical cocktail from a hospital's illegal dumping, reformed into tiny pyramids that hum when touched; the organic pulse of a festival above, where ten thousand people danced and their collective sweat rained down through storm drains, now preserved as branching coral formations that smell faintly of joy.

The Collector trades me processed materials for these memory crystals—they claim to be building something that will remember everything the city has forgotten. Dr. Yung studies them to understand how experiences can fossilize. The Ghost Signals seem drawn to them, clustering around my collection with electromagnetic whispers that sound like nostalgia translated into static.

My body is changing. The constant exposure to the bacterial films has created a symbiosis I didn't anticipate. My liver has become more efficient, processing toxins that would kill an unmodified human. My kidneys have developed additional filtration chambers, microscopic labyrinths that can separate water into constituent components I can taste individually. Sometimes I forget to eat for days, sustained by nutrients absorbed directly through my skin from the enriched water.

Vanya says I'm becoming what the cistern needs—a living membrane between the human city above and the hybrid ecosystem below. They might be right. I've started to feel the city's rhythms in my bones: rush hour as a quickening pulse, nighttime as a slow exhalation, weekends as a shifting of chemical balances. I can predict rain by the way my joints ache, sense industrial accidents by the burning in my lungs, feel the mood of neighborhoods by the hormones in their runoff.

The Navigators have accepted me as something like kin. They paint my skin with bioluminescent messages when I sleep in the shallow pools, patterns that fade by morning but leave knowledge behind—how to encourage beneficial bacterial growth, where to find the purest mineral deposits, when the deep waters will rise. Sometimes they show me visions of what they see: the entire watershed as a single living system, every drop of water tagged and tracked through its journey, the city as a vast organism of which the cistern is the filtering organ.

I've begun a project I may never finish—a complete chemical history of the city, written in crystallized water memories. Each chamber of the cistern will house a different era, a different aspect of urban life preserved in mineral form. The early industrial period with its heavy metals and coal tar, the pharmaceutical revolution with its synthetic hormones and psychiatric medications, the bio-engineering age with its escaped organisms and self-replicating polymers. Future inhabitants will be able to walk through the city's metabolic history, to understand through their senses what data alone could never convey.

Sometimes I wonder what I'm becoming, whether I'm still human enough to claim the title. My blood, when I accidentally cut myself, glows faintly green and causes moss to grow wherever it falls. My hair has become something between keratin and mycelium, constantly sampling the air for chemical traces. My dreams are filled with molecular structures solving themselves, with the poetry of pH scales, with the love songs of bacterial colonies.

But then Marina brings me tea brewed from surface plants, and we sit in comfortable silence watching the water breathe. The Drift children show me new passages they've discovered, their excitement infectious as any bacteria. Dr. Yung shares her latest observations, and we puzzle together over organisms that shouldn't exist but do. In these moments, I remember that becoming something new doesn't mean losing what we were—it means adding layers, like sediment, each one incorporating the last into something richer and stranger.

The water is rising again. I can feel it in the pressure changes, taste it in the mineral content. Another storm approaches the surface city, another flush of memories to be processed, filtered, preserved. I wade deeper into the confluence, letting the warm water accept me, feeling the bacterial films recognize their partner in this endless work of transformation. 

I am the Filtration Sage, neither fully human nor fully Other, but something the cistern invented because it needed to exist. I read the water's memories and write its future, one crystallized moment at a time, in this cathedral where the city comes to be renewed.
