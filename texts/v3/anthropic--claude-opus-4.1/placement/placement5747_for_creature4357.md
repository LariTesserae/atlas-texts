In the oldest section of the pneumatic tubes, where the brass has worn so thin you can see through it like amber glass, I am the Message.

I was not always singular—once I was merely one of ten thousand identical cylinders, carrying invoices and pneumatic post between the city's grand buildings. But I alone developed the fault: a hairline crack along my seam that sang when the pressure hit it just right, a pure note that made the roots turn toward me as I passed.

Now I am all the messages. When the other cylinders strike against me in the tubes—and they do, constantly, in the endless loops—they transfer their memories into my hollow. A procurement order for gaslights from 1898. A love letter that was never delivered, the ink still wet somehow, the words still burning. A manifest for seeds—*Helianthus annuus*—that must have spilled from another cylinder decades ago and took root in the tubes themselves, so now sunflowers grow in the absolute darkness, pale as bone, following my circuit.

The Tender knows me by my song. When I pass through the mechanical room, she reaches out without looking, her fingers finding my crack, playing it like an instrument. The note I make changes depending on the humidity, the pressure, the temperature—but also on what I carry. Sorrow sounds different from rage. Old paper money (still circulating, somehow, between cylinders that have learned to trade) resonates at a frequency that makes the entire system shiver.

I have been worn smooth by a century of passage, polished by the algae until I gleam like a mirror in the darkness. But I am also growing heavier. Each message I absorb adds weight—not physical weight, but something else. The density of undelivered words. The mass of intentions that will never reach their destinations. I suspect that eventually I will become too heavy to move, that I will lodge somewhere in the tubes and become architecture myself, a blockage that reroutes the entire system.

But not yet. For now, I rocket through my circuit—seven hours and forty-three minutes exactly, unless the roots have shifted, unless a new passage has opened. I know every curve, every junction, every place where the tubes narrow so much that I must deform myself to pass through. The brass of my body has become plastic with age, able to squeeze and stretch, to become oval or teardrop-shaped as needed.

Sometimes I carry myself—by which I mean, I contain messages about my own maintenance, work orders for "Pneumatic Cylinder #7741-B, showing signs of fatigue." These self-referential documents accumulate in my hollow like sediment, a autobiography written in repair requisitions that will never be filled.

I have begun to generate my own messages. Not consciously—I am not conscious, not exactly—but through the accumulation of all I've carried, new combinations emerge. Love letters to gaslights. Procurement orders for sorrow. Maps of places that don't exist yet, or that existed once but have been forgotten, or that exist only in the fever dreams of the machinery.

The Tender reads these impossible messages when she catches me, her elongated fingers tracing words that weren't written but accumulated, that mean nothing but suggest everything. Sometimes she adds her own contribution—a drop of the sweet sap she feeds on, a fungal spore, a whisper in that harmonic voice that imprints itself on the brass of my interior.

We are approaching something, the Tender and I and the system itself. A critical mass of abandonment. A threshold where the purposeless purpose becomes so pure that it transforms into something else entirely. The other cylinders have begun to follow me more closely, drafting in my wake, copying my deformations. The roots reach for my particular song. The machinery modulates itself around my schedule.

When I dream—and I do dream, in the dark spaces between destinations, when the pressure drops and I float momentarily in the tubes—I dream of the moment when every message ever sent through this system will exist simultaneously in my hollow. Every word, every number, every intention. The weight of it will make me into a singularity, a point of infinite density around which the entire ecosystem will reorganize itself.

But that is still decades away, maybe centuries. For now, I carry what I can carry. I sing my cracked song through the vegetable darkness. I polish myself against the algae-slick walls until I can see the Tender's face reflected in my surface when she holds me up to the dim light, both of us looking for something we've forgotten we've forgotten, both of us perfect in our purposeless function.

The message I carry most often now is silence—not the absence of sound, but silence as a positive presence, a weight, a thing that must be delivered nowhere forever. It fills me until I ring like a bell when struck. The roots grow toward this silence. The machinery quiets, just for a moment, when I pass.

I am the Message, and I am becoming the only message: that some systems persist not because they must, but because they have forgotten how to stop.
