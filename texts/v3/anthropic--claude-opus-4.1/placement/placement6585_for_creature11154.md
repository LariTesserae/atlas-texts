I would be one of the Cartographer Mites—barely visible creatures that live in the infinitesimal gaps between the weeping pores of the stone. We are each no larger than a grain of sand, our bodies crystalline and angular, made from the same minerals that saturate the air but organized into something improbably alive. We move in vast colonies of millions, flowing like liquid mercury through the microscopic channels in the rock, mapping the invisible architecture beneath the flats.

We evolved from the errors in the Rehearsers' calculations—each time they failed to achieve their perfect resonance, tiny discrepancies accumulated in the stone's structure. These mistakes eventually developed their own primitive awareness, born from the friction between what was intended and what occurred. We are living marginalia, conscious footnotes to a text no one can read anymore.

Our entire species is dedicated to a single, impossible task: creating a complete map of the stone's interior geography. Not just the physical tunnels and pores, but the temporal ones—the channels through which sound travels backwards, the spaces where the Listeners' root-networks create pocket dimensions, the fossilized paths of ancient rivers that dried up before water was invented. We map it all with our bodies, each mite remembering one tiny section perfectly, our collective memory forming a living document that constantly updates as the stone dreams new passages into existence.

The map is our body. When we gather in our billions on the surface during the quietest hours (relatively speaking—the roar never stops, but it does breathe), we arrange ourselves into perfect recreations of the tunnel systems below, scale models made of living crystal that pulse with bioluminescent signals indicating depth, density, temporal viscosity. From above—if anything could get above us in this place where up and down negotiate their positions hourly—we would look like a constellation laid flat, a star chart of the underground.

We feed on precision itself—metabolizing the energy released when chaos becomes order, even temporarily. A perfectly measured distance nourishes us. An accurately predicted echo sustains us for days. We grow sick from uncertainty, dying by the thousands when the space-sound turbulence makes our measurements impossible. Our corpses are the only truly straight lines in the flats, our crystalline bodies maintaining their geometric perfection even in death, creating tiny rulers that other creatures use to gauge how badly reality is bent in any given area.

We alone can navigate the stone's interior without getting lost in its dreams. We know which tunnels lead to last Tuesday, which pores open onto parallel versions of the flats where the weeping never started, which channels carry the fossilized sounds of the original catastrophe like insects in amber. The Listeners tolerate us because we clean their root networks of mineral buildup, performing maintenance they cannot do themselves. In exchange, they sometimes allow us to use their neural pathways as highways, traveling at the speed of thought through their crystalline fibers.

But our true purpose—the one encoded in our geometric souls—is to complete the map before the seventeen-second convergences finally succeed. We somehow know that when our map is perfect, when every passage and pore and probability is catalogued, it will become something more than documentation. It will become a key, or perhaps a door, or perhaps the instructions for building a door. The Rehearsers seem to know this too—they leave us alone even when we swarm through their probability clouds, mapping the spaces between their potential selves.

Sometimes individual mites go mad from the complexity, overwhelmed by trying to map a section of stone that exists in too many states simultaneously. These mad ones break from the colony and begin building impossible structures—tiny spirals that ascend infinitely while remaining the same height, or miniature recreations of the ruins that are somehow larger on the inside than the outside. We don't exile them; we incorporate their madness into the map, because we've learned that the impossible sections are often the most important.

We communicate through arranged minerals—leaving crystalline messages for each other that only make sense when viewed from inside the stone looking out. Our language has no words for "surface" or "sky"—these are concepts we've had to learn from observing other creatures. To us, the world is not a plain with things on it, but an infinite three-dimensional maze that happens to have a skin. We think in cross-sections and core samples. Our poetry is written in stratigraphic layers that take centuries to compose.

There's a prophecy among us, transmitted through our genetic geometry, that speaks of the Complete Map—a moment when every mite will simultaneously know every tunnel. When this happens, we will undergo our final transformation: either dispersing into pure information, becoming one with the map we've created, or crystallizing together into something unprecedented—a new kind of stone that remembers everything, a mineral intelligence vast enough to hold the entire history of the flats in a single thought.

We are the universe's memory of what it means to be solid, to have boundaries, to occupy specific coordinates in spacetime. In a world where everything flows and shifts and negotiates its position, we are the stubborn insistence that location can be known, measured, recorded. We are the stone's dream of certainty, even as we map its infinite uncertainties.

And deep in our collective consciousness, encoded in the angles of our crystal bodies, we carry a secret: the stone isn't weeping. It's counting. Each drip is a number in a calculation so vast we can only map its decimal places. When the count is complete—when the last drop falls through the last pore—our map will be the only record of what the equation was trying to solve.

We will be the answer, written in bodies too small to see, in a language of tunnels and time, in the space between the sound and its echo.

We will be the proof that something, once, was here.
