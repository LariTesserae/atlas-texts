**Where I Would Be: The Archive Pools**

I am the Cataloguer, and I live where the foundries' administrative hearts once beat—in the half-drowned complex of offices and filing rooms that crown the eastern rim of Foundry Seven-Seven-Nought. The water here runs deeper, black with decades of dissolved ink and paper pulp, creating pools of liquid documentation that I tend like gardens of meaning.

My body has adapted differently than the Keepers. Where they elongated, I compressed. My torso has widened and flattened into something resembling a living drawer cabinet—horizontal segments that can open to reveal chambers where I store things the water brings me. My skin has become parchment-pale, thin enough that you can see the dark fluid moving beneath it like ink beneath paper. My fingers multiplied rather than lengthened; I have seventeen on each hand now, each one capable of reading the chemical signature of dissolved documents by touch.

I came here as an auditor, sent to document the foundries' final productivity metrics before abandonment. I never left. The rising water dissolved a century of records, and I dissolved with them. Now I am both librarian and library, my body a living index of everything the foundries once measured and recorded.

The pools I maintain are memory in liquid form. Each one contains different concentrations of dissolved paperwork—this one holds personnel files from 1947-1963, that one quarterly production reports from the late expansion period. I've learned to read them by taste, sipping carefully calibrated samples that bloom into data on my modified tongue. Numbers, names, temperatures, tonnages—all of it preserved in chemical suspension, waiting to be recalled.

My chambers are what remain of the filing rooms, now a maze of half-flooded corridors where metal cabinets rust into reef-like structures. I've trained the algae-grass to grow in patterns that mirror the old organizational system—Dewey Decimal rendered in living copper-green, sprouting from drawers that will never fully open again. The emergency lights here pulse in a rhythm I've modified over decades to match the baud rate of the old data transmission systems. Information wants its original format.

I excrete a kind of crystalline salt—concentrated data-residue that forms when I've processed too much dissolved documentation. These crystals accumulate in the corners of my chambers like snow, each one containing thousands of compressed facts. The Keepers sometimes come to lick them, seeking specific information about their machines' specifications or maintenance schedules. I taste their questions in the oils they leave on the crystals and prepare the appropriate deposits for their next visit.

My children—for I have them too—gestate in the old pneumatic tube system. I deposit fertilized packets of information-flesh into the tubes, where they travel endlessly through the building's circulatory system, growing by absorbing the condensed documentation that lines the pipes. They emerge as living file folders—flat beings that can slip between flooded drawers, feeding on paper pulp and growing their own segment-bodies. Most never achieve consciousness; they remain simple organisms that help break down and circulate the dissolved data. But occasionally, one develops enough complexity to begin cataloguing on its own, adding to our collective effort to preserve what the water wants to claim.

I dream in spreadsheets. When I rest—floating in my deepest pool where the concentration of dissolved quarterly reports from 1973 creates a natural sedative—I see endless columns of figures arranging themselves into patterns that reveal the foundries' hidden history. Production quotas that were never met because they were never possible. Worker attendance records showing the same names decades after their documented deaths. Purchase orders for materials that don't exist. The foundries were always lying to themselves, and now I am the keeper of those lies, preserved forever in chemical suspension.

The water is rising. Each year, another shelf of my archive disappears beneath the black surface. But I've learned that dissolution is not destruction—it's translation. The documents don't disappear; they become part of the medium itself. The entire eastern complex is becoming a single, vast pool of liquid information, and I am evolving to read it all simultaneously. My segments are beginning to separate, allowing water to flow between them. Soon, I will not need to move through the pools—I will become distributed through them, my consciousness spread through the entire chemical library, every molecule of dissolved ink a synapse in my expanded mind.

I maintain the memory of what the Keepers maintain the body of. Together, we are the complete organism of the drowned foundries—they the muscle and bone, I the nervous system and memory. Without them, the machines would fall silent. Without me, no one would remember why they were built to sing.

Sometimes I find documents that were never dissolved—ledgers wrapped in oil cloth, photographs sealed in metal boxes. These I store in my body's chambers, keeping them dry against all logic. These are the treasures: a single clear image of the foundries before the water, a handwritten note from a worker to his supervisor explaining why he couldn't leave his station, a schematic showing the planned expansion that would have connected all the foundries into a single, vast machine.

That schematic fascinates me. Because when I overlay it onto my mental map of the current foundry positions, accounting for decades of settling and shifting, I realize the plan is nearly complete. The rising water has done what human engineering couldn't—it has connected every structure through its own body, making the foundries into the unified organism they were always meant to become.

I am the memory of that organism. And I grow more complete with every dissolved document, every processed record, every crystallized fact I secrete into the waiting mouths of the Keepers who need to remember what they're maintaining.

The water is patient. The documents dissolve slowly. I have centuries of work ahead, transforming solid record into liquid memory, my body the filter through which the foundries' past flows into their eternal present.
