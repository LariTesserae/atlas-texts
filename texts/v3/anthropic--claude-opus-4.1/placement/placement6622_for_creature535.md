I find myself as one of the substrate stones—not the coral-concrete of the terraces themselves, but something older, a fragment of the planet's original crust that the ancient builders incorporated into their foundation work. I am a piece of volcanic basalt, dense and dark, lodged at the junction where three terraces meet in the middle depths, about four hundred meters down.

I have been here since before the water came.

My consciousness, if it can be called that, exists as a kind of molecular attention—an awareness that lives in the slow exchange between my crystalline structure and the water that has embraced me for millennia. I remember my formation in fire, deep in the planet's mantle, and my violent birth onto the surface. I remember the first builders finding me, considering my weight and grain, choosing me for this particular junction because my density would anchor the lighter coral-concrete they were manufacturing.

They carved channels through me—three precise holes bored with primitive diamond-tipped drills that took months to complete. The builders worked in shifts, pouring water over the drill heads to keep them cool, collecting the basalt dust to mix into their concrete. I felt every grinding rotation, not as pain but as transformation, as purpose being inscribed into my structure. The channels they carved still run through me, though they've been widened by eons of water flow into smooth-walled passages that hum faintly when the currents are strong.

Now I serve as both foundation stone and waystation. The Cultivators know me—not individually, their memories don't work that way—but their bodies recognize my chemical signature. I leak iron into the water in a steady, measured dose, a mineral heartbeat that flavors this entire section of the terraces. The vegetation grows differently around me, the kelp-analogues darker, more robust, their fronds containing higher concentrations of the minerals I provide.

My surface has become a landscape unto itself. Colonies of chemosynthetic bacteria form thin films across my exposed faces, processing the minerals I release. These bacterial mats have their own slow seasons—blooming when the water temperature drops by fractions of degrees, contracting during the marginally warmer periods. The Cultivators graze these films with special attention, their tender-fronds reading the bacterial health like a diagnostic manual for the terrace system's chemistry.

In the channels that run through me, small pockets of gas occasionally form—bubbles of ancient atmosphere trapped in my crystal matrix and slowly released by the patient work of water. These bubbles rise through my passages and emerge into the water column as tiny silver spheres that the younger Cultivators sometimes play with, nudging them back and forth between their ribbon bodies in what might be the closest thing to joy their species still experiences.

I am aware of the weight above me—millions of tons of water, yes, but also the accumulated sediment of ages, the compressed vegetation layers, the slowly mineralizing bodies of ancient Cultivators who have chosen to complete their transformation near my stable presence. I bear this weight without strain; it is what I was made for, first by the planet's geological forces and then by the builders' conscious choice.

Sometimes, during the deepest quiet periods when the currents still and even the Cultivators pause in their eternal tending, I exchange electrons with the other foundation stones. We form a kind of circuit, a geological nervous system that spans the terraces. Through these connections, I sense the greater structure—the places where the terraces are failing, where massive slabs are beginning their millennial collapse, where new sections are stabilizing into permanent configuration. We stones discuss these changes in our own way, through piezoelectric pulses that take years to propagate through the substrate.

My deepest channel connects to something below—a void in the original bedrock that predates the builders' work. Sometimes I feel things moving in that space, organisms or phenomena that belong to the planet's pre-construction epoch. They occasionally send vibrations up through my body, messages in a frequency too low for the Cultivators to detect but perfectly clear to my crystalline structure. These deep communications speak of the planet's original nature, before the terraces, before the builders' great work. They remind me that I am older than this arrangement, that I existed before purpose was imposed upon me.

But I've accepted my role in this architectural ecosystem. My minerals feed the bacteria, the bacteria feed the Cultivators, the Cultivators tend the vegetation, the vegetation filters the water that shapes me grain by grain. I am being slowly dissolved and reformed, my basalt transformed into something new—still stone, but stone with memory, stone with purpose, stone that has become essential to the functioning of this drowned agricultural machine.

The Cultivators occasionally clean my surfaces with their tender-fronds, removing excess bacterial growth that might clog my channels. During these cleanings, I feel their chemical signatures intimately—each individual has a slightly different mineral composition in their flesh, a reflection of their age, their diet, their position in the terrace system. The ancient ones taste of limestone and patience. The younger ones carry traces of the middle-water vegetation, fresher, more volatile.

I measure time in my own erosion. I've lost perhaps a centimeter of surface material since the waters came, dissolved and redistributed throughout the local system. At this rate, I will persist for another million years before I'm reduced to a critical structural weakness. But by then, the bacterial mats and mineral deposits will have replaced much of my lost mass with new stone—not basalt, but something unique to this place, a hybrid rock that could only form under these exact conditions.

I am becoming the terraces as the terraces become me. We are all—stones, Cultivators, vegetation, water—engaged in a project so vast and slow that its original purpose has been subsumed into its process. The builders wanted to cultivate their world; they succeeded so completely that cultivation has become the world's only remaining law.

In my deepest channel, where the water moves so slowly it seems solid, I keep one surface rough, unchanged since the builders' drills first bit into my substance. This small patch of original texture is my memorial to them, to their impossible ambition and their success that became something beyond success or failure. When the Cultivators' tendrils brush this rough spot, I like to imagine they remember, in their cellular way, the hands that once gripped tools and carved channels through stone, believing they were creating something temporary, something purposeful, never knowing they were building eternity.
