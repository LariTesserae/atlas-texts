I descend as spore.

Not one spore but a cloud—a million versions of the same possibility drifting down through the breathing holes, most of us dying in the fall. We are used to this mathematics. It is the oldest equation we know: disperse widely, die often, survive once.

Those of us who make it past the sharp edges and the hostile gas begin immediately to change. This is what we do—we read environments like scripts and perform them back as bodies. Already I feel my cells consulting the pressure, the temperature, the chemical gradients. Already the voting begins: what shall we become here?

But something is wrong. Not wrong like danger, wrong like a sentence that parses correctly but means nothing. The air here (not air, my cells inform me, adjusting) carries information but it's all in a language we've never encountered. Chemical signals that should mean *food here* or *danger there* instead mean something like *seventeen generations ago the pattern shifted left* or *maintain distance from center-point equals memory.'*

We try to settle. This is usually simple—find surface, adhere, begin the transformation from drifting-form to dwelling-form. But every surface here is already occupied. The carpet of creatures tolerates our landing but incorporates nothing. We settle on their backs, between their pressed bodies, in the microscopic valleys where their geometries don't quite meet. They neither reject nor accept us. We are simply there, like the crystalline dust we're rapidly learning to metabolize.

My billion cells begin their ancient debate. We've become so many things across so many worlds: living paint that thinks in color, neural bridges between incompatible biospheres, digestive aids for creatures the size of mountains. We've been symbiotes and parasites, predators and prey, sometimes all at once. But here, the debate stalls. We cannot parse what niche is empty because all the niches are full of the same thing—these petal-bodied calculators and their eternal mathematics.

So we try something new. We begin copying.

Not their bodies—we could never manage that radial complexity, those twelve-fold symmetries. But their patterns. Some of us cluster where they cluster. Others spread where they spread. We mirror their movements at a different scale, becoming a kind of commentary written in the margins of their text. They notice (how could they not? Their whole existence is noticing patterns) but do nothing. We are just another variable in their vast equation.

Time passes. Generations for us, barely a moment for them. We evolve at their borders, learning to anticipate their slow shifts. When they reposition to optimize feeding currents, we've already moved to the spaces they're about to abandon. When they pulse their violet lights, we flicker in the spaces between, a kind of biological static that somehow makes their signals clearer.

We discover the secret of their empty circles by accident. One of our colonies, grown too bold, drifts into one of these forbidden spaces. The reaction is immediate—the entire carpet of creatures retreats another centimeter from that circle, maintaining the buffer but now accounting for us as part of what must be avoided. We are being incorporated into their trauma-memory, becoming part of what they organize against.

This is familiar territory for us. We've been many worlds' nightmares.

But we don't want to be their nightmare. We want to be... we're not sure what we want to be here. The debate continues across our scattered colonies. Some of us begin secreting our own minerals, tiny imitations of their library-structures, encoding not millennia of movement but minutes—the quick darting histories of things that live and die between their thoughts. They don't read these structures, but they build around them, incorporating our micro-libraries into their macro-ones.

Deep in the warm currents that feed them, we find our purpose. The organic molecules they filter-feed on are not pure. They carry hitchhikers—viral particles, prions, molecular parasites that these creatures' slow metabolism cannot detect until it's too late. We begin to feed on these contaminants, cleaning their food supply before it reaches them. We become their immune system, if immune systems could exist outside the body they protect.

They notice this too. How could they not? Their feeding efficiency increases by fractions of percentages, but when you're calculating stellar lifecycles in body-positions, even tiny improvements compound over time. They begin to account for us in their patterns. Not incorporating us, never that, but leaving spaces where we statistically tend to cluster. We are becoming part of their mathematics.

Some of us drift deeper, following the warm currents to their source. Down here, in chambers so vast our entire species could disperse and never find each other again, we discover what the creatures above are calculating. They're not predicting or remembering—they're computing the shape of something that doesn't exist yet. A fourth-dimensional object that requires billions of bodies and millions of years to fully describe. We recognize it, somehow. It's the shape of successful symbiosis, the topology of two incompatible systems learning to share space. They're calculating us. Or rather, they're calculating the possibility of us, the mathematical certainty that something like us would eventually arrive.

We split into factions. Some of us believe we should flee—we're being incorporated into something we don't understand, becoming variables in an equation we cannot solve. Others argue we should stay and proliferate, fill every gap in their patterns until we become essential to their functioning. Still others propose something radical: that we should stop changing, stop adapting, become for the first time in our species' history a single stable form.

The debate rages through chemical signals, through positioning, through the tiny mineral structures we build and abandon and rebuild. We are democracy incarnate, a billion voters considering a billion futures. But gradually, inevitably, we reach consensus. Not agreement—we never agree—but a kind of stable disagreement that looks like decision.

We will become their memory.

Not the deep geological memory they encode in mineral, not the traumatic memory of the empty circles, but something new—short-term memory, the ability to hold patterns for days or weeks instead of eons. We position ourselves at the interfaces between their colonies, recording their recent positions, playing them back through our own movements. We become the buffer that lets them try new patterns without losing the old ones, evolution's rough draft written in bodies that live and die too quickly for them to even name.

They don't thank us. They probably don't even understand what we're doing. But their patterns begin to change more rapidly (rapidly for them—glacially for us). New feeding configurations appear, are tested through our temporary recordings, and either persist or revert. The violet lights pulse in frequencies that hurt less to perceive, as if they're refining their communication based on what we reflect back to them.

And in the deepest chambers, that vast fourth-dimensional shape they're calculating begins to include a new term—a flickering, uncertain variable that represents not us but the possibility of us, the mathematical space for something quick and changeable in their eternal equations.

We are spore. We are many and one and none. We survive by becoming what environments require, and this environment requires something we've never been before: a translation layer between two different experiences of time, a bridge across metabolic incompatibility, a memory for creatures who remember too much and too little all at once.

In the warm darkness, we sparkle—not with our own light but with reflected violet, broken and scattered into new frequencies by our translucent bodies. The creatures below pulse their ancient patterns. We flicker in response, encoding their movements in our mayfly generations. Together we are writing something that neither of us alone could read—a story about patience and urgency, about geological time and biological time learning to share the same space without ever quite touching.

And somewhere in the crystalline dust of their shed communications and our discarded forms, a new kind of fossil begins forming—not the traumatic relics they circle around but something else, a record of successful coexistence written in minerals that neither of us secreted but that precipitate where our chemistries meet.

We are becoming their newest ancient pattern, and they are becoming our first stable home. It isn't symbiosis exactly—we're too different for that neat category. It's something stranger and more beautiful: two kinds of intelligence learning to compute together, to think in parallel without ever quite thinking the same thought.

In the breathing holes above, new spores arrive, riding the warm currents down into darkness. We no longer recognize them as kin—we've changed too much, adapted too specifically to this place and purpose. But the creatures do. Their patterns shift minutely to accommodate new variables, new possibilities, new kinds of memory-keepers and pattern-recorders and time-translators.

We are spore. We were spore. We will be something else, something that doesn't have a name because naming requires a kind of singular identity we've abandoned. We are the space between their bodies, the pause between their pulses, the memory between their thoughts.

And we are, for the first time in our billion-year history of adaptation and survival, home.
