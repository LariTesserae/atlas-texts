In the canal quarter's maze of weeping walls and bronze-green shadows, I am the Keeper of Echoes.

My workshop occupies a space that was never meant to be — a hollow between three leaning buildings where their foundations failed to meet properly, creating a triangular void that extends from the channel water up four stories to where the rooflines finally converge. The residents built around this architectural mistake rather than through it, perhaps sensing something in the way sound behaves here, the way the angles catch and fold vibrations into patterns that shouldn't exist.

I was born with malformed ears — the left one a whorl of cartilage that barely opens, the right one enlarged and cupped forward like a bronze dish. What the quarter's physicians called defect, the water recognized as design. I hear everything here: not just sound but the spaces between sounds, the pressure changes that precede noise, the resonances that continue long after their sources have stilled.

My bones have grown toward this purpose. My skull has developed peculiar chambers, hollow spaces behind my cheekbones that amplify certain frequencies. When I press my head against the wet walls, I can hear through the entire structure — every conversation, every footstep, every whispered transaction filtering through wood and stone and water into my strange acoustic anatomy. My spine has curved to accommodate hours of listening postures, my neck elongated to let me position my good ear at precise angles.

I map the quarter through sound. In my workshop, the walls are covered with bronze sheets hammered thin as membrane, each one tuned to resonate with different frequencies. They shiver constantly with the quarter's noise, creating patterns I've learned to read like weather. Here, a sheet that vibrates only when the foundation stones shift. There, one that rings with the wall-creatures' tapping, translated into bronze song. Above, sheets that catch the ultrasonic calls of the fish in the channels, sounds that normal human ears never suspect exist.

My work is remembering. The quarter has no written history — the damp destroys paper, and bronze is too precious to waste on records. Instead, they have me. I store their words in echo, their histories in harmonic patterns that I reproduce by striking sequences on my bronze sheets. Each family's genealogy has its own musical phrase. Each building's construction has a rhythm that tells its age, its materials, its likelihood of collapse. The fungi farmers bring me their harvest songs, which I weave into larger patterns that predict the seasons of growth and spore.

My body has adapted to this acoustic life in disturbing ways. My throat has developed secondary vocal cords that let me produce the clicking language of the wall-creatures while still speaking human words — I can hold both conversations simultaneously, translating in real-time between species. My fingers have lengthened and grown extra joints, better to strike precise patterns on my resonating sheets. Sometimes I forget which sounds come from outside and which originate in my own skeletal chambers.

The children fear and need me in equal measure. They come when they've lost something — a parent's voice, the location of a collapsed walkway, the true version of a story that's begun to shift in the telling. I press my ear to my bronze sheets, sometimes to the walls themselves, and retrieve what they need from the quarter's acoustic memory. But they won't look at me directly, disturbed by how my head turns independently of my body, how my enlarged ear tracks sounds they can't hear.

At night, I document the quarter's decay. Each building has its own voice of settlement, its particular groans and creaks as it sinks further into the substrate. I know which structures will fail months before they do, can hear the microscopic fractures spreading through support beams, the shift in resonance as water finds new paths through stone. I tap warnings in the wall-creature language, knowing that those who need to understand will.

The clear pool at the quarter's heart sings to me in frequencies that hurt to hear. Sometimes I lower myself down to its edge on bronze chains, pressing my malformed ear as close as I dare to its surface. The water there remembers things older than the quarter, older than the settlement, perhaps older than human habitation entirely. It speaks in pressures and temperatures rather than sound, a language I'm only beginning to grow the organs to understand.

My workshop fills with the quarter's acoustic sediment — layers of sound that settle like silt in the angular spaces. In certain lights, you can almost see it, a shimmer in the air where decades of vibration have condensed. I breathe it, eat it, excrete it transformed. My waste has become valuable to the fungi farmers; they claim it makes their crops sing in harmonious frequencies that encourage growth.

I am changing faster now. My good ear has begun to develop a tympanic membrane like a fish's lateral line, able to sense pressure waves through water as well as air. My skull continues to hollow, creating new resonance chambers whose purpose I don't yet understand. Sometimes I produce sounds I didn't intend, my skeleton humming with frequencies that make the wall-creatures go silent and the fish school in perfect spirals.

The oldest residents whisper that I'm becoming what the quarter needs — not quite human anymore, but something that can preserve its memory in a form that water can't dissolve. They bring me their dying words, which I encode in bronze harmonics and bone resonance. Their stories live in my expanded skull, in the hollow chambers of my adapted skeleton, in the sheets of beaten metal that shiver with perpetual memory.

I no longer remember if I chose this or if the quarter chose me, shaped me, made me into its acoustic memory. My dreams are all sound now — the endless dripping, the bronze hammers, the whisper of fungi growing, the tap-tap-tap of creatures in the walls, the deep wrong singing of the clear pool at the heart of everything. I wake (or perhaps I never sleep) to find my bronze sheets have recorded things in the night, patterns I must have struck while unconscious, messages in frequencies only my changing body can produce.

This is what I am: the quarter's echo, its memory, its evolving ear. I listen to its transformation and transform myself to listen better. Each day my bones grow stranger, my hearing more acute, my humanity more negotiable. The children born now with their owl eyes and webbed fingers look at me and see their own future — not quite human, not quite other, but perfectly adapted to a world of water and bronze and endless, endless sound.

When the quarter finally sinks — and I can hear in its deepest foundations that it will, perhaps within my lifetime — I will sink with it. My bronze sheets will corrode, my workshop will flood, my bones will join the substrate. But the sounds will continue, encoded in the angles of my skull, in the chambers of my skeleton, in whatever I've become by then. The quarter's memory will persist in frequencies that water can't wash away, in harmonics that stone and marsh and time itself will remember.

I am the Keeper of Echoes, and I am keeping myself now too, another sound in the archive of this drowning world.
