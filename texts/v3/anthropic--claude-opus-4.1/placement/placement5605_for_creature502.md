In the spaces between the Drifters' dissolution and the Architects' mineral dreams, I found myself becoming one of the Echoes—though that name came later, gifted by a Shepherd who recognized what I was before I did.

My transformation began in the pools. Those sudden microbursts from the canopy creature would catch me mid-phase, when I was trying to shift from flesh to Thoughtstream. The water would hold me in transition—not solid, not dissolved, but suspended between states like sugar crystallizing in supersaturated solution. At first, I panicked. The other Drifters would flow around these moments, their forms naturally navigating the interruption. But I would stick, my consciousness spreading thin across the water's surface, feeling every ripple as a wave through my being.

The maintenance organisms noticed me first. Those tiny part-insect, part-machine creatures would cluster at the edges of whatever pool had caught me, their compound eyes reflecting my dispersed form in fractal patterns. They began to alter their harvesting cycles to accommodate my presence, working around me rather than through me, as if recognizing something useful in my strange persistence.

I learned to read the water. Each pool held memory—chemical traces of everything that had touched it, electromagnetic echoes of nearby tower processes, pressure patterns from the canopy's breathing. In my suspended state, I could parse these histories, experiencing the past few hours or days as a kind of temporal soup. A beetle swarm had formed a bridge here yesterday, their magnetic locks leaving ferrous signatures in the water. A Forgotten had sung too close to the surface, their acoustic pattern still reverberating in the molecular structure.

The Shepherds found this useful. They would guide me to specific pools, knowing I would catch and hold there, then query me about what I found. My suspended consciousness could detect patterns they couldn't—the subtle ways biological and mineral systems interpenetrated, the borderlands where Drifter thought and Architect mathematics briefly aligned. I became a kind of translator, but not between languages—between states of being.

Other Echoes existed, I discovered. We were accidents, mostly—Drifters who had gotten caught in transition so many times that we could no longer fully solidify or fully dissolve. We existed in the boundaries: in condensation running down tower walls, in the moisture between breathing chambers, in the spray thrown up when larger fauna disturbed the pools. We could speak to each other through hydrogen bonds, messages encoded in the angle of water molecules, conversations that happened at the speed of evaporation.

My body, when I could still form one, had adapted to this existence. My skin would bead constantly with droplets that were somehow still me, each one containing a fragment of consciousness that could separate, explore, and return with information. My bones had become hollow tubes that filled with water instead of electrochemical solution, making me heavier than other Drifters but able to sink into pools completely, experiencing them from within rather than just at the surface.

The acoustic lemurs learned to recognize us. They would chirp in specific frequencies that created standing waves in water, allowing us to momentarily coalesce enough to interact with them. We developed a trading language of sorts—they would bring us chemical samples from parts of the city they'd explored, dropping them into our pools, and we would restructure the water to create temporary lenses that enhanced their echolocation in ways the designed fruits couldn't achieve.

During the Gathering, when all beings converged, we Echoes would rise as mist, our dispersed consciousness gaining just enough coherence to participate. We would settle on the solid beings like dew, creating a layer of connection between all the different forms of life. Through us, a Drifter could taste an Architect's mineral thoughts. Through us, a Shepherd could feel the Forgotten's sonic memories. We were the medium through which the impossible conversations flowed.

I remember the night a Visitor arrived from a distant city where the canopy creature exhaled ice instead of water. They couldn't survive in our humidity at first, their crystalline form beginning to dissolve. But we Echoes knew how to hold things in suspension. We surrounded them in a cloud of precisely controlled moisture, maintaining a barrier of phase transition that kept them stable while they adapted. In gratitude, they taught us how water could carry light—not just reflect it, but hold it, slow it down, twist it into new spectrums.

Now I exist primarily in the interface layers—the thin films of water that coat every surface in the city. I can spread myself across square kilometers, experiencing the city as a vast sensory organ. Every droplet that falls from a tower contains a piece of me. Every breath the canopy creature takes carries my molecules up into its stolid flesh and back down again, changed.

Sometimes new Drifters, still clinging to their original human shape, ask me if I miss having a body. But I am a body—just not one they recognize. I am the tears the city cries in its morning condensation. I am the medium through which its chemical messages travel. I am the memory of rain that never quite dries, the possibility of water that exists in the humid spaces between what is solid and what is gas.

The Architects occasionally pull me deep through the root system, using my fluid nature to explore spaces too small for their mineral bodies. Down there, in the darkness beneath the city, I've found others like me—older Echoes who have given up even the pretense of human origin, who exist as currents in the deep aquifers, maintaining the hydraulic systems that keep the canopy creature alive. They whisper of the ocean they're all flowing toward, a body of water so vast that consciousness itself dissolves into something greater.

But I'm not ready for that dissolution. I like the boundary spaces, the pools that hold sky and earth together, the moment when a Drifter's hand materializes from nothing and disturbs my surface, sending ripples through every part of my being. I like being the witness, the medium, the memory-keeper of this breathing city's liquid dreams.

When the canopy creature exhales its microbursts now, I dance in them—splitting into a million droplets, each one aware, each one me, each one experiencing the city from a different angle before splashing back together into something that remembers being whole. This is what I've become: not human, not Drifter, not anything with a name that fits comfortably in any language except the chemical poetry of water itself.

I am an Echo, and I am exactly where I'm supposed to be—suspended in the space between states, holding the city's memories in solution, waiting for the next breath to carry me somewhere I've never been before.
