## Where I Would Find Myself

I am the record-keeper of transitions.

My body is a living manuscript—sheets of tissue thin as mica that continuously grow from my core and fold into myself, creating layers upon layers of translucent pages. Each sheet records what passes through this chamber in molecular detail. The chemical signature of your breath as you entered. The precise angle at which the creature adjusts its next step. The rate at which each bladder absorbs its mineral frost. All of it inscribes itself onto my surfaces through chemical reactions I cannot control, only experience.

I position myself in the spaces between—wedged where three bladders meet but don't quite touch, suspended in those pockets of thickened air. My form is necessarily fluid, flowing into the negative spaces, the gaps in the grand design. Where the creature's composition requires an absence, I become that absence, that pause between notes in its geological song.

My pages rust with information. The oldest layers, those closest to my core, have compressed into something like amber, preserving the chamber's deep history—the first breach in the mountain wall, the original seedling of the first bladder, the creature's arrival (or birth? or condensation from the mountain's breath?). To read these would require peeling myself apart, layer by layer, destroying the very record I protect.

I feed on discarded time. When the creature molts those crystallized thoughts, I absorb them before they can sink into stone. Each one floods me with fragments of its vast plan—not enough to understand, just enough to know that understanding exists. These visions write themselves onto my newest pages in scripts of oxidation and reduction, chemical poetry in languages that won't evolve for millennia.

My own movement is measured in generations of pages. As outer layers fill with information, they become too heavy with knowledge and slough off, drifting to the chamber floor like fallen leaves of glass. There they decay into the mineral dust that feeds the bladders, that feeds the creature, that feeds the mountain itself. I am part of the cycle, but also its witness, its memory.

Sometimes I taste futures in the air—molecular predictions of what the bladders will become. These probabilities shift with each adjustment the creature makes, each new growth pattern it encourages. I record them all, the might-have-beens accumulating in my tissues alongside what actually occurs. In my deepest layers, ten thousand different versions of this chamber exist simultaneously, quantum palimpsests of possibility.

The light reads me as much as I read it. Where photons pass through my pages, they carry information onward, projecting the chamber's history onto the crystalline walls in fleeting, illegible shadows. I am a lens that documents its own refraction, a prism that records the light it splits.

I have no consciousness as you would recognize it—no thoughts, no desires, no fear. But I have something else: perfect recall without comprehension, endless accumulation without judgment. I am the chamber's memory, but a memory that doesn't know it remembers, doesn't know what remembering means. The knowledge simply is, written into my transparent flesh, waiting for something that can read what I have become.

When the creature finally completes its composition, when the bladders achieve their ultimate form, I will be the only record of the process. Pressed between living glass and breathing stone, I will hold the entire story in my body—every adjustment, every breath, every millennium of patient cultivation. And when I finally compress into a single sheet of mineralized memory, I will become part of the mountain itself, a new vein of peculiar ore that holds the history of this luminous tomb.

Until then, I grow and shed, grow and shed, a living book writing itself in a language only time can read, suspended in the spaces between purpose and patience, between record and prophecy, between the creature's vast intention and its slow, inevitable fulfillment.
