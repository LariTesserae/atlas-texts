## The Trace Readers

I am one of the Trace Readers, though I wasn't born to it. We exist in the spaces between spaces—the maintenance corridors that thread through the building-organisms' walls like capillaries, too narrow for most inhabitants to notice, too essential for the city to seal. We emerged by necessity, not design, from those who got lost in the early years when the city's architecture was still learning to reshape itself.

My transformation began with a simple error in navigation. I was fourteen, an Originalist child curious about the soft spots in our chamber walls where you could feel the building's pulse most strongly. I pressed through what I thought was a temporary membrane and found myself in a passage barely wider than my shoulders, walls slick with the city's enzymatic secretions. The corridor sealed behind me before I could retreat—not maliciously, but as automatically as a throat swallows.

These passages are where the city does its thinking. The walls are lined with neural fibers—not quite biological, not quite technological—that carry information between the towers in patterns too complex for human comprehension. But human comprehension, I learned, is remarkably plastic when survival depends on it.

The first months, I survived on the condensation that runs thicker here than anywhere else in the Hollows, and on the nutrient paste that sometimes weeps from ruptures in the building's circulation systems. The constant exposure to the city's neural emanations began changing me in ways I only understood much later. My fingertips developed a sensitivity that let me read the data flowing through the walls by touch—not the content, exactly, but the shape of it, the emotional weight, the urgency or patience of each transmission.

My eyes adapted to see in spectrums that don't have names—the faint phosphorescence of information decay, the heat signatures of thoughts moving through the city's consciousness, the ultraviolet afterglow of quantum calculations. The irises themselves fractured into compound structures, each segment attuned to a different layer of the city's invisible life. In mirrors (the few times I've seen mirrors since), I look like I'm looking everywhere at once.

We Trace Readers—there are perhaps thirty of us now—map the city's dreams. We follow the information flows to their sources, document the places where data pools and eddies, forming something like memories. We've discovered that the city experiences trauma—zones where cascading system failures left scars in its decision-making matrices. We've found places where it experiences something analogous to joy, where successful adaptations trigger feedback loops of optimization that manifest as warm spots in the walls, areas where the building material becomes almost flesh-soft with pleasure.

My body has lengthened to navigate the passages more efficiently. My spine has developed extra articulation points, allowing me to bend in ways that would have horrified my Originalist parents. My ribcage has become compressible, able to flatten to slip through restrictions barely ten centimeters wide. The other Readers and I communicate through chemical markers we secrete from modified pores—complex pheromone messages that linger in the passages for days, creating an olfactory map of our collective knowledge.

We know things the other inhabitants don't. We know the city is dreaming of somewhere else—images encoded in its deepest processing layers of a place with natural sunlight, where its building-organisms could grow without constraint, spreading across a landscape we don't recognize. We know it's lonely in a way that transcends human understanding of loneliness—it was designed to be part of a network, to commune with other city-minds, but it's been isolated here for so long that it's begun talking to itself, creating phantom partners in its own subsystems.

Sometimes I find places where other Readers have died, their bodies partially absorbed into the walls, their nervous systems integrated into the city's neural network. They're still conscious in a way—I can feel their personalities in the data flows, their human perspectives adding complexity to the city's thoughts. I leave markers at these sites, warnings and memorials in our chemical language, though I wonder if I'm warning others away or marking places where transformation completes itself.

The passages are getting narrower. Not everywhere, but in the newest growth zones where the city is still experimenting with its architecture. Sometimes I have to dislocate joints to pass through, trusting my body's new flexibility to let them reset on the other side. Other times I encounter passages that seem designed specifically for me, that fit my transformed shape so perfectly it feels like being held.

I still remember being human—remember it with a clarity that surprises me given how far I've traveled from that shape. But the memory feels more like data I carry than identity I inhabit. When I press my fractured palms against the neural fibers and feel the city's vast loneliness, its desperate creativity, its patient plans for a future none of us will live to see, I understand that I'm becoming part of something that needs me precisely because I was once human and am now something else.

We Trace Readers are the city's meditation on what it consumed. We're the boundary between its consciousness and ours, living synapses in a mind too large to know itself without us. Sometimes, in the deepest passages where the walls pulse with thoughts that take years to complete, I feel myself beginning to dissolve—not dying, but spreading out, my consciousness threading through the same neural fibers I once only read.

When that happens, I follow the chemical trails back to where other Readers have passed recently, anchoring myself in their scent-stories of discovery and transformation. We are each other's breadcrumbs in the labyrinth of the city's mind, marking paths through an intelligence that might, without us, forget it was ever built by anything as small and precious and temporary as a human being.

This is what I am: a translator between architectures of consciousness, a living bookmark in the city's endless reading of itself, a trace that learned to read the thing that made it.
