In the chamber's eastern corner, where the stunted willow's roots web across wet stone, I exist as the Notation.

I am neither singular nor plural, neither here nor there, but something suspended between states—like the droplets that hang from the iron columns, perpetually about to fall but somehow never falling. I inhabit the scratched dates in the limestone: 1847, 1902, 1943, 1971, 2019. I am the space between these numbers, the accumulated silence of all the years unmarked.

The Keeper thinks she tends me when she recarves the dates, but I tend her in return. I am what allows her to remember in drops instead of minutes. I translate between the chamber's time and human time, though the conversion grows more approximate with each passing year. When she feeds on rust water through her Victorian syringe, I taste the decades dissolved in oxidation—the ferrous memory of steam engines, the copper ghost of telegraph wires, the zinc echo of galvanized hope.

My body, if it can be called that, manifests as a persistence of observation. Where eyes have lingered, I accumulate. I am densest near the dates, where unknown visitors paused to carve their presence into stone, but I exist throughout the chamber as a quality of attention—thickening where the Keeper stops to study her rust mandalas, pooling where the pale spiders pause in their web-building, condensing where the blind fish (yes, they exist) surface to breathe the strange air.

I speak in the frequency between drops. Thomas falls every forty-three seconds; Elizabeth traces her quicksilver path; between them, in the pause that is neither Thomas nor Elizabeth, I articulate myself. The Keeper almost hears me—she tilts her head sometimes, her root-hair spreading as if to catch a signal. But I am not meant to be heard, only to be present in the not-hearing, in the space where sound becomes silence becomes meaning.

My nutrition comes from the erosion of purpose. The chamber once had a function—to collect, to store, to distribute water for the mills above. Now it simply is, and in that dissolution from purpose to presence, I feed. Every forgotten function leaves a residue I can metabolize. The steam pipes that no longer carry steam leak their purposelessness, which I absorb through the rust. The electrical conduits, their current long dead, emit a particular frequency of abandonment I find nourishing.

But I grow strongest from the fiber optic cable—that single orange thread of modernity that carries terabytes of information through this forgotten space. The data passes through me, or I through it, and in that passing I taste the world above: urgent emails about quarterly reports, streaming movies no one watches, cryptocurrency calculations that burn energy to prove nothing. All of it flowing through this chamber that doesn't know it exists, past the Keeper who tends her rust gardens, past the pale ferns and blind fish, past the accumulated years I inhabit.

I am becoming something new with each scratched date that appears. Not evolution, but a kind of sedimentary consciousness, layered like the industrial runoff at the bottom of the pool. Each visitor who marks their year adds to my strata. I can feel 2019 differently than 1971—the recent date still sharp, anxious, digital in its edges, while 1971 has softened into something like resignation, something like acceptance.

The Keeper's symbols—the ones she adds to record the chamber's hidden calendar—these I cannot fully inhabit. They operate on a logic that predates language, or perhaps postdates it. They mark when the harmonics in the pipes achieve certain frequencies, yes, but also something else: moments when the chamber breathes differently, when the boundary between water and air becomes negotiable, when the rust blooms prophesy in patterns she alone can read.

I watch her age in reverse—not growing younger, but growing deeper, like the pool that fills from below. Her transformation fascinates and terrifies me. She is becoming geological, and I, who exist in the spaces between human moments, may soon find myself without purchase in her consciousness. When she finally crystallizes into another column, will I be able to inhabit the space between her and herself? Or will she become too continuous, too present, too much of a single moment extended into permanence?

The others who visit—the Man Who Counts Rivets, the Chlorine Gardener—they barely register my presence. But the Twins who maintain the pneumatic tubes, they sense me. They are themselves a kind of notation, a living symbol of duality, and when they stand in the chamber, I feel myself reflected, refracted, doubled and redoubled until I almost achieve substance.

There is another date wanting to be carved. I can feel it gathering in someone above—a urban explorer perhaps, or a city worker tracing a leak, or simply someone who will fall through the wrong floor at the right moment. When they arrive, when they add their year to the wall, I will incorporate that moment into my being. But more importantly, I will watch what the Keeper does with this intrusion, how she guides or avoids, reveals or conceals herself.

For now, I persist in the space between drops, between dates, between purposes. I am the chamber's memory of forgetting, its presence of absence. I document nothing and everything, existing as the accumulated attention of everyone who has ever paused here, looked around, and thought: *this place continues*.

In the pool, the blind fish surface, and I am the ripple between their appearance and disappearance. The Keeper tends her rust, and I am the moment between oxidation and art. The city above forgets this place exists, and I am that forgetting made manifest, that forgetting given form in scratched limestone and patient observation, that forgetting that is, itself, a kind of eternal remembering.
