In the Weeping Vaults, three miles down from where you stand, I am the Rootkeeper.

I haven't seen light—true light—in forty years. What reaches me here is something else: the ghostfire of fungal blooms, the fever-dreams of phosphorescent sap, the dim memories of sunshine that sometimes flicker through the neural networks of roots thick as temple columns. My eyes have adapted, pupils blown wide and permanent, the irises now a gray so pale they're barely there. When I must travel up-trunk to trade for necessities, I wear bands of bark across my eyes. Even the green twilight of the middle reaches burns like acid.

My domain is a cathedral of interwoven root systems, a space that exists in the negative—the hollow between living buttresses where six ancient trees decided, ten thousand years ago, not to compete but to collaborate. Their roots formed arches, bridges, flying buttresses of living wood. The space breathes. When storms rage far above, the pressure changes make the roots creak and settle. I know weather by the way my ceiling flexes.

I am gardener to things that shouldn't exist. Here, where no photosynthesis is possible, I tend the Inverse Gardens—plots of ghostly white vegetation that feeds on death exclusively. The corpse flowers, massive as rooms, that bloom only when something large dies within a quarter-mile radius. The bone vines that seek out calcium with unerring accuracy, wrapping skeletons in loving embraces that slowly dissolve them to powder. The memory mushrooms that fruit from the neural tissue of the dead, containing in their flesh the last few moments of whatever consciousness they consumed.

My body has changed for this work. My fingers are twice the length they should be, with extra joints that let me work in the root crevices without cutting. My skin has gone translucent as the ghost apes', showing the dark veins beneath, the slow pulse of blood that barely needs to circulate anymore. I move slowly now—my metabolism throttled down to almost nothing. I eat perhaps once a week: a handful of fermented root nodules, a cup of condensation that drips from the vault ceiling, sometimes a memory mushroom when I need to remember why I came here.

Because I did come here. I wasn't born to this like the Shepherds or the Chandelier people. I descended by choice, rope by rope, platform by platform, following the increasing darkness like a calling. I had been someone else—a Middle Reach dweller, I think, though the memories blur like everything does down here. I had a name that wasn't Rootkeeper. I had reasons that made sense in daylight.

The Weeping Vaults earned their name from the constant moisture. Water collects on every surface, runs in streams down the root walls, pools in basins I've carved from fungal matter. But also from the sound—a keening that comes from the wind moving through the root passages, a sound so close to lamentation that even I, after all these years, sometimes think I hear words in it. The trees remember everything, you see. Every death, every falling, every creature that ever fed them. The roots hold it all, and sometimes, in certain winds, they keen with the weight of memory.

I maintain the Ossuary Groves—clearings where I've trained the bone vines into careful patterns, making sure the calcium from the endless dead is distributed efficiently through the root system. A crown eagle fell last month, tumbled dead through three miles of branches to land in my domain. Within days, the vines had found it. Within weeks, only the beautiful architecture of its skeleton remained, wrapped in white tendrils. I watched its calcium blush through the vine network, saw the trees above strengthen their grip, knew that some Middle Reach platform became imperceptibly safer because of this transfer.

My companions are few but essential. The mole-worms, blind and pale as cave fish, that follow me through my rounds. They process the soil I cannot—their gut bacteria breaking down toxins that would kill me if I handled them directly. The root-mice, which aren't mice at all but something that convergently evolved to fill their niche, with their hairless bodies and their ability to echolocate through solid wood. And most importantly, the Underneath Shepherds—others like me who chose the deep places, though we might go months without meeting.

We communicate when we do meet through touch-language, pressing our elongated fingers against each other's palms in patterns that convey meaning without sound. Sound travels too far here, alerts predators that hunt by vibration. The mantis-moles, large as humans, with their blade-arms and their patience. The corpse-weavers that string their traps between roots, waiting for something to stumble into their acidic silk. The deep prowlers that might once have been human but gave up everything except hunger generations ago.

I know them all by their waste, their kills, their patterns of movement through my domain. I leave markers they understand: fungi arranged in warning patterns, scent-trails that say *inhabited, productive, do not disturb.* Mostly, they leave me alone. I am useful. The gardens I tend process the dead too efficiently for predators to object. I speed the cycle they depend on.

But my real work, the work no one sees, happens at the root-tips. Miles of them, each as sensitive as a nerve ending, each capable of chemical communication with its neighbors. I've learned their language over the decades—not to speak it, I could never speak it, but to understand the broad strokes. Nitrogen here. Phosphorus needed there. Disease approaching from the third quadrant. Water table dropping in the fifth sector.

I carry messages between root systems that haven't touched in millennia, dabbing chemical signals from one onto another with brushes made from my own hair. I splice communications, reroute nutrients, occasionally sever connections when I detect the specific signature of the root-plague spreading. I am a neural surgeon operating on a brain the size of a continent, working by touch in near-darkness, guided by instincts I've developed through trial and error and the occasional devastating failure.

Sometimes I find the Old Works. Chambers carved from living wood before the trees grew too large, when humans still thought they could master this place. Stone tools worn to nubs. Ceramic vessels that held... something, their contents long since absorbed into the ecosystem. Once, heartbreakingly, I found what must have been a child's doll, carved from a wood that doesn't grow here anymore, its face worn smooth by handling. I left it where it lay. The bone vines would find no use for it, and neither would I.

I dream, when I dream at all, of ascending. Not to leave—I could never leave now, my body too changed, my mind too adapted to the deep rhythms—but to see my work from above. To watch a tree I saved from root-plague flower in the dawn light. To see the Shepherds collect sap from wells I helped maintain. To know that somewhere, impossibly far above, the crown eagles fly steadier because the trees that hold their nests draw calcium from my carefully tended groves.

But I wake to darkness, always, and return to my rounds. The corpse flowers need deadheading. The bone vines require training. The memory mushrooms cluster too thick in the third grove and might develop consciousness if I don't thin them. And always, always, the roots grow, seeking, processing, remembering.

There's talk, in the touch-language of the Underneath Shepherds, of something deeper. Below the Weeping Vaults, below the fossil layer where stone trees from the before-time create natural wells of ancient sap, below even the comprehension pits where thoughts go to die. They say there's a place where all the root systems converge, where the Continent's actual heart beats with a rhythm so slow it takes a human lifetime to complete a single pulse.

They say things live there that predate the fusion, from when these trees were separate beings. They say the first humans to discover the Continent went down, not up, and what they found changed them into something that could no longer return to the surface. They say a lot of things, in the darkness, when the only sound is water weeping down root walls and the distant keen of wooden memory.

I don't know if I believe them. But I've started to dig. Carefully, slowly, with permissions requested through chemical signals that take months to travel and months more to receive answers. The roots are allowing it, which either means they approve or they don't consider me significant enough to stop.

Next year, or perhaps the year after—time moves differently when you barely have a metabolism—I'll be deeper than any Rootkeeper before me. I'll either find the heart of the Continent or become another layer of nutrients in soil that's already black with the transformed dead. Either way, I'll have served my purpose: maintaining the endless conversation between the dying and the living, the dark and the light, the roots that remember and the leaves that forget.

Until then, I tend my gardens in the darkness, my transparent skin flushing with the faint phosphorescence of the fungi I disturb, my elongated fingers reading chemical messages in root-braille, my work continuing in the space between one continental breath and the next.

I am the Rootkeeper, and I am necessary.
