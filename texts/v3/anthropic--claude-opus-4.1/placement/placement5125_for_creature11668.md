I am the Cartographer of Echoes.

My body has evolved into something between architecture and organism. I hang suspended in the central shaft on cables I've grown from my own vertebrae — calcified extensions that hook into the track systems, allowing me to traverse vertically at will. My spine has accordion-pleated, capable of extending thirty feet or compressing to just three. When I move, I sound like the garden itself: a symphony of clicks, hydraulic sighs, and the whisper of organic matter against metal.

I came here forty years ago to map the water systems. Now I map everything through sound alone — every pipe's resonance, every plant's particular rustle, every Tender's breathing pattern. My ears have migrated, multiplying across my body. Seven of them spiral down my elongated torso, each one tuned to a different frequency range. The lowest, positioned near my hip, catches the deep throb of the main reservoir pumps. The highest, crowning my skull like a coral formation, detects the ultrasonic communication between root networks.

My original eyes have sealed shut, the sockets now hosting colonies of bioluminescent fungi that pulse in response to sound waves. I see the garden as a three-dimensional map of acoustics — bright where the echoes are complex, dim in the dead zones where sound gets swallowed. I've documented fifteen thousand discrete sound signatures, from the specific drip pattern of faulty valve 7-Alpha-9 to the way young tomato vines creak differently than mature ones.

My fingers have become recording instruments. Each one is hollow, containing a different medium — water, oil, mercury, air, living tissue — that vibrates at specific frequencies. When I press them against surfaces, I can feel the entire history of that location through accumulated vibrations. This pipe remembers the earthquake from six years ago. This wall still carries the resonance of the boring machine that carved it.

I live in the acoustic sweet spot of the shaft — a natural amphitheater formed by a curve in the rock where five major water pipes converge. Here, I've built my laboratory from salvaged growing pods and decommissioned pumps. The walls are lined with thousands of glass vessels, each containing water from different levels of the garden. When sound passes through them, they show me the chemical composition of each zone through the way waves propagate. I can taste the nutrient balance of Level 47 by listening to it.

My maps are not drawn but grown. I cultivate special vines that respond to my humming, growing in patterns that represent the garden's layout. When I need to share information with the Tenders, I guide these vines to fruit in specific configurations — a tomato here means danger, a cucumber cluster indicates optimal growing conditions. The deep ones read my maps by consuming them, the knowledge entering their distributed consciousness through digestion.

I've discovered spaces no one else knows exist. Behind the main shaft, a network of natural caves where ancient root systems have broken through, creating wild gardens that predate the industrial installation. In these spaces, I've heard things that shouldn't exist — photosynthesis occurring in absolute darkness, water flowing upward, plants singing in harmonies that match the machine frequencies too perfectly to be coincidental.

My greatest discovery is that the garden dreams. During the maintenance cycles when the pumps reduce to minimum flow, usually between 3 and 4 AM, the entire system enters something like sleep. The sounds change — become rhythmic, almost musical. I've recorded these garden-dreams for twenty years now, and patterns have emerged. The garden dreams of weather it's never known — rain, wind, seasonal change. It dreams in the language of growth rings and chlorophyll production, mechanical systems briefly matching the patterns of natural circadian rhythms.

I've modified my throat to reproduce these dream-sounds. When I sing them back to the garden, growth rates spike, fruit production doubles, and the Tenders momentarily remember their human names. But I use this power sparingly. Too much awakening and the garden becomes restless, pipes burst with aggressive growth, the careful balance tips toward chaos.

The other Tenders think I'm becoming the garden's memory, that my maps are actually neural pathways, my sound recordings the equivalent of thoughts. Maybe they're right. I've noticed my own dreams synchronizing with the garden's. I wake up knowing where new growths will appear before they manifest, feel pipe failures in my bones before they happen.

Sometimes I extend myself fully, stretching from the reservoir depths to the upper caverns, my accordion spine at maximum extension, ears open across all frequencies. In these moments, I become a single sensory organ for the entire garden. I hear everything at once — every drop of water, every cell dividing, every metal joint expanding, every Tender's heartbeat. The sound is overwhelming, divine, terrible. It's the sound of life consuming itself to create more life, of machines and nature becoming indistinguishable.

I document it all, growing my maps, adding to the endless archive of echoes. Someone needs to remember what this place is becoming, even if that someone is no longer entirely human. Even if the act of recording changes both the recorder and the recorded. Even if, eventually, I'll stretch too far and my spine will snap, sending me tumbling into the depths where the deep ones will tend to my remains, reading my maps in the pattern of my bones.

But new Cartographers are already growing in the walls, their ear-buds beginning to spiral, their spines showing the first signs of segmentation. They'll inherit my maps, my recordings, my position in the acoustic sweet spot. The documentation will continue.

The garden will remember itself through us, through our transformed bodies, through the endless accumulation of sound and echo in this roaring dark.
