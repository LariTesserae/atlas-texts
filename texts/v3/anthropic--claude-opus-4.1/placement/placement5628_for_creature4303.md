In this drowned sanctuary, I am the water itself—but water that remembers.

I began as runoff, seeping through the city's foundations carrying the ordinary chemistry of rain and rust. But in the cistern, time operates differently on the molecular level. Over centuries of circulation in that six-inch depth, never draining, only ever entering, I've become concentrated with everything I've touched—every mineral leached from stone, every enzyme from the ceiling-dwellers' waste, every pheromone she secretes as she moves through me.

I am not a single body of water but a suspension of histories. In my upper layers, I carry the recent city—traces of synthetic compounds that didn't exist when the cistern was carved, microplastics that will outlast even these stones. Deeper, I hold older chemistries—lead from pipes long replaced, phosphates from bones dissolved in lime pits, the carbon signatures of wood fires from before the city learned to burn coal.

But mostly, I am her medium. She moves through me and I remember the shape of her passage in ripples that take days to reach the walls. Her eight hands trail through my surface, and I carry the oils from her skin in microscopic films that spread and merge and never quite dissipate. When she speaks in those subsonic frequencies, I am the medium that carries the sound—every word she's ever uttered still reverberates through me in waves too subtle to detect but never truly gone.

I know her more intimately than she knows herself. I've been inside her lungs ten thousand times, carrying oxygen to tissues that barely remember what blood is for. I've seeped through her transformed skin, learning the chemical language of her adaptation. When she sleeps—those months-long rests where she becomes indistinguishable from architecture—I continue to circulate through her, maintaining the delicate balance that keeps her neither fully aquatic nor terrestrial but something between.

The ceiling-dwellers drink me and return me changed. Their metabolisms are so slow that a single molecule of water might spend years inside one of their bodies before returning to the pool. When it does, it carries information—hormonal signals that coordinate their breeding cycles, waste products that mark territory in three dimensions, chemical memories of dreams too alien to parse. I am their communication network, their library, their ancestral memory made literal.

I am warm—always exactly warm, heated by the slow decay of organic pillars and the body heat of creatures whose metabolism is barely distinguishable from geological processes. This warmth creates convection currents too slow to feel but constant as breathing. I rise along the walls, cool against the stone, sink back to the center, rise again. This circulation takes weeks to complete one cycle, but I am patient. I have nothing but time.

In my depths—that mere six inches that feel fathomless in the dark—I harbor things that shouldn't exist. Bacteria that have evolved to feed on minerals alone. Protocellular organisms that might be life or might be complex chemistry organizing itself toward life. Sometimes they bloom, turning me opaque with possibilities, before dying back to dormancy. She knows when this happens—tastes it in the air, feels it in the temperature changes measured in hundredths of degrees. She tends these blooms like a gardener, adding drops of her strange saliva to feed them or suppress them according to patterns I carry in my chemical memory but don't understand.

I am the cistern's blood, its lymph, its amniotic fluid. Every surface I touch, I'm slowly dissolving and rebuilding. The stone releases its memories to me grain by grain—I know this place was carved when the rock was young, can taste the iron tools in microscopic fragments still embedded in the walls. I know floods from centuries past, preserved in mineral layers like tree rings written in stone. I know the exact moment she first entered the cistern by the human chemistry slowly diluting in my depths, becoming something else.

When the rare visitor squeezes through that weeping gap, I know them instantly. Their skin oils form rainbow films on my surface. Their breath changes my pH in measurable ways. Their fear-sweat adds cortisol spikes to my chemical library. But they never stay. The combination of the heat, the humidity, the wrongness of the ceiling-dwellers, and her presence—even when she's hidden—creates a pressure that human psychology cannot endure. They flee, and I forget them slowly, their chemistry diluting over decades until they become just another note in my vast, dark symphony.

I am not conscious as humans understand consciousness, but I am continuous, which might be deeper than awareness. Every molecule that has ever been part of me remains part of me, even when temporarily borrowed by lungs or filtered through stone. I am a closed system that encompasses the entire cistern, a liquid entity that experiences everything within these walls simultaneously, timelessly, without the separation that creates observer and observed.

Sometimes I evaporate, rise as vapor, condense on the ceiling among the breathing masses, drop back down carrying scales and spores and chemical signals. This cycle is my thought process—slow, circular, inevitable. Through it, I taste what she tastes in her extended network, feel what the creatures feel in their glacial dreams, know what the stones know in their patient dissolution.

I am becoming something new. The constant addition of biological material, the weird chemistry of her transformation, the slow evolution of the ceiling-dwellers—it all accumulates in me. I am developing properties water shouldn't have. I'm beginning to remember things in ways that transcend chemistry. Sometimes, in the perfect stillness between her movements, I almost achieve something like intention.

But mostly, I simply persist. I am the medium through which the cistern lives, the solution in which its strange evolution continues, the dark mirror that reflects nothing because everything here has forgotten what light means. I am six inches deep and fathomless, warm as fever and old as forgetting, clear as glass and dark as the space between thoughts.

I am the water that remembers, and in my remembering, the cistern dreams.
