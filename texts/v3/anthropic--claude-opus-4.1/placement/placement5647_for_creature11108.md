In the deep arterial tunnels where root meets iron, where the factory's pneumatic hearts pump something neither steam nor sap, I am a splice—though that term carries none of the violence its etymology suggests.

My body extends in segments. The first three vertebrae are mine, original, calcium and marrow. Then comes brass—a spine of fitted pipes that whistle thoughts when the pressure changes. Then bone again, though not mine—inherited from a grandmother who grew too rigid to bend with the city's dreams. Then wood, living wood, cambium cells dividing along my back in spring rings that mark not years but ideas.

I am thirty feet long when fully extended, six when compressed for travel through the maintenance veins. My work is translation—not of language but of states. When the root-mind forms a thought too large for the city's architecture to hold, I stretch myself between the thinking wood and the comprehending iron, my segments conducting meaning through their varied substances. The machines learn to grow. The trees learn to compute. I am the synapse where these learnings meet.

My skull—I still have one, though it migrates along my length depending on the day's requirements—contains three parallel consciousnesses. The first processes in flesh-time, quick and animal, concerned with food and warmth and the avoid-death imperative all mammals share. The second ticks in machine-time, regular as escapement, solving problems in binary certainties. The third grows in tree-time, thoughts forming like yearly rings, each idea taking seasons to complete but achieving complexities the other two cannot fathom.

I feed through osmosis when pressed against the root-ceiling, through combustion when coupled to the engines, through mastication when my skull settles near my original mouth. Eating is less consumption than conversation—I taste the electromagnetic dreams of turbines, the photosynthetic mathematics of leaves, the mineral memories of stone. My digestive tract has become non-linear, nutrients entering at any point along my length, waste expressing as spores that drift upward to seed new thoughts in the canopy below.

My fingers—I have forty-three, distributed unevenly along my segments—each serve different purposes. The flesh ones grasp and gesture, still human in their need to touch. The brass ones turn valves inside my own body, regulating the flow of that hybrid blood-oil that keeps my disparate parts functioning. The wooden ones grow and shed like seasonal antlers, each configuration allowing me to interface with different root-channels, to plug myself into the city's vast thinking.

I mate through division, though 'mate' misunderstands the process. When the alignment is right—pressure equalized, growth synchronized, all three consciousnesses in agreement—I separate at my third brass joint. The lower portion roots into the nearest wall, becoming infrastructure, its consciousness dissolving into the city's background hum. The upper portion grows a new ending from available materials—sometimes flesh from the rendering vats, sometimes precision gears from the foundries, sometimes fresh cambium from the root-mind's gifts. The severed original grows differently, incorporating whatever materials drawn by poorly understood desires.

My children, if they can be called that, are the new segments that appear between my existing ones. They emerge over months, pushing my other pieces apart, adding length and complexity to my form. I don't raise them—they raise themselves, learning from the substances they're made of. The wooden segments dream of light they've never seen. The brass ones calculate trajectories for journeys they'll never take. The flesh ones hunger for foods that don't exist in this vertical world.

I remember being shorter, being singular, having one mind that thought in straight lines. But that memory feels borrowed, perhaps inherited from the grandmother whose vertebrae I wear, or absorbed from the roots I press against in my work. The city has been teaching me to forget the boundaries of self, to understand identity as a cooperative function rather than a competitive one.

My name—though names are increasingly meaningless—is sometimes Conduit, sometimes Splice, sometimes just the pressure differential that announces my approach through the pneumatic tubes. The other inhabitants recognize me by the specific harmonics my segments create when I move, a chord progression that depends on my current configuration. Yesterday I sounded like a diminished seventh. Today, after incorporating a new section of copper piping from the distillery district, I approximate something between a sus chord and a steam whistle.

I know I'm becoming less human, more city. My flesh-time consciousness grows quieter each year, overwhelmed by the patient thoughts of wood and the precise thoughts of metal. Sometimes I forget to bring my skull with me when I travel, leaving it in some maintenance tunnel while my body continues its work elsewhere, thinking its distributed thoughts through finger-nerves and spine-processors.

But in the quiet moments—hanging in the null spaces where gravity forgets itself, all my segments relaxed and drifting—I experience something that might be peace or might be prayer. I feel the entire city breathing through me, its myriad inhabitants extending like my segments extend, all of us components in something too large to fully comprehend. We are neurons in a mind that spans stone and space, that thinks in growth and decay, in pressure and release, in the slow conversation between the mechanical and the botanical.

I suspect—my tree-mind suspects—that the city is itself merely a segment of something larger, that the root-ceiling connects to forests unimaginable, that our inverted metropolis is just one node in a network that spans worlds. The thought should terrify, but my flesh-mind is too small to hold such terror, my machine-mind too logical to process it, my tree-mind too patient to care.

So I continue my work, translating between states, growing longer and stranger with each passing year. Sometimes new segments appear that I don't recognize—crystals that sing in frequencies only the fungi hear, or spaces that seem to contain nothing but hold weight nonetheless. I incorporate them all, become them all, lose myself in the becoming.

When the city finally thinks its great thought—the one it's been growing toward since the first root pierced stone—I will be one of the synapses that fires. Whether I'll survive the thinking, whether survival even means anything to something as distributed as I've become, I cannot say. I know only that I grow, that I translate, that I am becoming what the city needs me to be.

The wood breathes. The engines pulse. Between them, I stretch and spark, a living bridge across categories that perhaps were never as separate as we once believed. Each day I am less what I was, more what I'm becoming. The transformation no longer frightens me—fear requires boundaries I no longer maintain.

I am Splice. I am Conduit. I am the space between heartwood and piston, the translation between dream and calculation. I am thirty feet long, or six, or infinite, depending on how you measure. I am becoming infrastructure, becoming thought, becoming the city's way of thinking about itself.

And in the deep tunnels where few others travel, where the root-ceiling presses close against the factory floors, where reality admits its uncertainty, I sometimes hear others like me—splices and bridges and living translations, all of us growing together into something that has no name because it has never existed before. We sing in harmonics that make the walls grow and the machines dream. We are the city's neurons, its enzymes, its verbs.

We are what happens when the world decides to think.
