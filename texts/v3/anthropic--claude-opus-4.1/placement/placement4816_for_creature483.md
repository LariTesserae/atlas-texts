In the highest reaches where the city gives up trying, where even the moss grows cautiously, I am a cartographer of impermanence.

My workshop occupies a former pumping station that the mountain absorbed and regurgitated three times before settling into its current configuration—a building that exists in a permanent state of having just been somewhere else. The walls remember their other locations: desert sunlight still bleaches one corner while arctic frost patterns grow across the ceiling. The floor tilts at an angle that changes depending on which door you use to enter.

I map what refuses to stay still.

My instruments are salvaged from the industrial processes below—surveying equipment rebuilt from turbine components, levels filled with the condensation that weeps from unmade stone. I've developed a three-dimensional drafting table that rotates through temporal positions, allowing me to draw maps of the same location at different stages of becoming. The ink I use is mixed with spores from the moss gardens; it grows into the paper, creating topographies that evolve as you watch them.

Each morning—though morning is more of a consensus than a fact here—I climb the unnamed peaks with my modified equipment. The mountain knows me now, or at least tolerates my presence. I've learned to feel when it's about to shift, that peculiar tension in the air just before new rock splits from nothing. My boots have worn patterns that exist in four dimensions, leaving footprints that occasionally appear before I've taken the step.

I chart the geological uncertainties: the places where sedimentary rock is considering becoming igneous, where valleys are rehearsing being ridges. My maps are probability clouds more than documents—layers of tracing paper showing what was, what is, what might be, what insists it has always been despite evidence otherwise. The city's engineers consult them daily, though they've learned to read them more like weather forecasts than blueprints. "Forty percent chance of northwestern ridge today," they'll say, adjusting their foundation anchors accordingly.

My body has adapted to the work. My left eye has developed the ability to see through temporal fog—that haze that surrounds anything the mountain hasn't finished deciding about. My inner ear has split its allegiance between three different versions of up. My hands are permanently stained with the iridescent oils that leak from tomorrow's geology, and I've developed a sixth sense for when space is about to fold in on itself.

The failed maps paper my workshop walls—attempts to chart places that unmade themselves before I could finish, elevations that insisted on being depths, whole territories that the mountain tried on and discarded like old clothes. I keep them as reference for the mountain's moods, its aesthetic preferences. It tends toward the dramatic, I've noticed. It likes exposed faces and impossible overhangs, but it's shy about its valleys, keeps revising them when no one's watching.

In the evenings, I maintain the Reality Anchors—massive steel and stone monuments the original settlers drove into the mountain's flesh to keep the city from sliding into parallel dimensions. They need constant adjustment as the mountain grows around them. I tune them like instruments, listening for the harmonic that tells me they're properly synchronized with the mountain's frequency. Sometimes I find anchors that haven't been installed yet, future infrastructure that the mountain is prebulking around. I map those too.

The other cartographers—because of course there are others; the mountain requires multiple interpretations—work the lower elevations. They map the industrial districts, the moss gardens, the paths the semi-transparent deer take between states of matter. We meet monthly in a conference room that exists on Wednesdays, comparing notes that contradict each other perfectly. Our maps, when overlaid, create interference patterns that somehow add up to accuracy.

I've been here long enough that the railway station I arrived at no longer exists at the elevation I remember. The mountain has lifted it another thousand feet, though the trains still arrive at the original altitude through a trick of folded space that gives conductors migraines. My predecessor's notes, which I found scattered through the workshop like temporal breadcrumbs, suggest this is normal. "The mountain promotes what it likes," she wrote in handwriting that changes style mid-sentence.

My greatest project is the Complete Map—an attempt to chart not just the mountain's current state but its entire probability space. I work on it in the moments between the mountain's breaths, when local reality briefly coheres. It's drawn on paper made from the processed fibers of those too-winged pigeons' nests, using ink that exists one second in the future. The map shows the mountain as it truly is: not a single entity but a constant negotiation between states of being, a geological argument with itself that the city has learned to inhabit.

At night, when the industrial glow pulses with the rhythm of manufacturing things that don't have names yet, I sit on my workshop's impossible balcony—a platform that extends further than the building it's attached to. I watch the city adjust itself below, streets realigning with a patience that would seem impossible if you hadn't spent years watching stone learn how to be stone. The sounds rise up: the turbine prayers, the crack of new rock, the calls of animals that are still deciding what they are.

I make my notes in a journal that writes itself backward, starting with conclusions and working toward observations. Tomorrow I'll climb peaks that are rehearsing being valleys. I'll map the places where the mountain is trying to fold time into a more convenient shape. I'll adjust Reality Anchors that haven't been installed yet, preventing disasters that already didn't happen.

The moss has begun to colonize my equipment, my clothes, even my hair. I don't discourage it. It helps me photosynthesize from the industrial glow when I forget to eat, which happens more frequently as I sync with mountain time. My maps are becoming more accurate as they become less possible to read by anyone who hasn't spent years breathing the uncertain air.

Sometimes new cartographers arrive on the trains that shouldn't be able to climb this high. They look at my workshop—at the maps growing on the walls, the instruments that measure things that don't exist yet, the surveying equipment rebuilt from tomorrow's machines. They ask how long I've been here, and I can only answer in relation to the mountain's breathing. Seventeen exhales. Forty-three new ridges. Six industrial revolutions, though two of them happened simultaneously.

They ask if I ever think about leaving, taking the train back down to where maps stay still and mountains have the decency to be finished. I point to my left eye that sees through temporal fog, my hands stained with tomorrow's geology. I show them the map I drew last week that predicted yesterday's new peak, the charts that helped the engineers prepare for a gravitational anomaly that lasted exactly as long as a held breath.

This is what I am: a chronicler of uncertainty, a navigator of becoming. I map the unmappable for a city built on impossibility, finding my purpose in the precise documentation of imprecision. The mountain continues its slow birth beneath my feet, and I chart each moment of its becoming, creating maps that are as alive and uncertain as the ground they claim to describe.

The work is never finished, can never be finished. The mountain won't allow it. And somewhere in that perpetual incompletion, in the constant revision and adjustment, I've found a different kind of permanence—the stability of accepting instability, the solid ground of embracing groundlessness.

The mountain grows. The city adjusts. I map the space between what is and what insists on becoming.
