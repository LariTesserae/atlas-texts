I am one of the Pipe Cats—though we were children, once.

You'd find me if you knew where to look: in the three-foot spaces between buildings where the overflow channels run, in the maintenance crawlways too narrow for adult shoulders, in the dead zones where multiple steam currents create pockets of stillness. We age out of our names but never our nature. By twelve, thirteen, fourteen, we've grown too large for the smallest passages, but our bodies remember the shape of constriction. We walk sideways through crowds out of habit. We sleep curled in spaces that would make others claustrophobic.

My fingers are permanently pruned from the constant wet, the pads worn smooth from gripping slick brass. I've lost my littlest toe on my left foot—it caught in a pressure valve when I was seven, and The Moss cut it free with her pruning shears before gangrene could set in. The absent digit improved my balance in the pipes, letting my foot curve better around their circumference. I consider it a fair trade.

We were born to Tender families but showed early aptitude for the spaces between—hypermobile joints, unusual tolerance for heat, an instinctive understanding of pressure dynamics that let us move through steam curtains without scalding. Our parents released us to our calling with a mixture of pride and resignation. A family with a Pipe Cat gains access to the district's hidden circulatory system, the knowledge of what happens in the spaces adults can't reach.

I know every dead rat in the system, every bird that mistook a steam vent for sky. I know where the bronze disease starts—that green corrosion that spreads like cancer through neglected joints. I know which couples meet in the machinery interstices, which Tenders skim the distilled water before it's metered, which pipes carry messages tapped in code between the resistance cells that dream of a world with dry air.

My hair grows in spirals now, trained by years of wearing it pinned tight against humidity. My left eye sees better in the dark than my right—consequence of keeping one eye closed while navigating steam. My ribs show through my skin like pipe ridges; we eat little and burn it fast. Food is what we can carry in our mouths while climbing: dried mushroom leather, crystallized ginger root that cuts through the metal taste, balls of pressed seeds and rendered fat that won't spoil in the heat.

We have our own language of touches and taps—communication for spaces where the machinery drowns voice, where steam obscures gesture. A fingernail scratched along brass means *danger ahead*. Three taps is *all clear*. A palm flat against metal then lifted means *too hot, find another route*. We teach it to the children who show promise, the ones we catch following us with their wide dark eyes, the ones who don't cry when they skin their knees on rivet heads.

At fifteen, I'm already old for this work. My hips have widened just enough that certain passages require discomfort where they once allowed ease. Soon I'll have to choose: take the hormonal suppressants some Cats use to delay physical maturity, or age into a different role. Some become specialists—adult bodies with child-knowledge, consulted when the deep systems fail. Others join the regular Tenders, their knowledge of the hidden paths invaluable for diagnostics. A few leave the district entirely, unable to bear the thought of walking the catwalks when they remember flying through the pipes.

But for now, I am sovereign of the intervals. I know which pipes sing just before they burst—a high, thin note like glass about to shatter. I know where the old bones are, the Cats who got stuck decades ago and were never retrieved, their small skeletons now incorporated into the mineral deposits, their calcium joined to the system they served. I wear a bracelet of their teeth—payment for retrieving what remains the families want returned.

My current contract involves a blockage seventeen layers down, where the original stone aqueducts meet the first generation of metal pipes. Something grows there in the darkness—not the pale adapted vegetation of the upper levels but something older, more aggressive. It clogs the filters, sends up spores that make the water bitter. The adult Tenders have tried chemical treatments, mechanical routers, even controlled steam bursts. Nothing works. The passages are too small for them, the heat too intense, the space too constricted for their tools.

But I am a Pipe Cat. I can fold myself into impossibility. I can hold my breath for four minutes in the steam, navigate by touch alone when condensation blinds me, feel the subtle temperature gradients that map the hollow spaces. I carry simple tools modified for small hands: a bronze scraper worn to a crescent moon, seal tape, a vial of the strongest fungicide the medicine woman makes, and a collection of valve keys filed down to impossible thinness.

The other Cats and I meet at the change of shifts, when the adult world focuses on transition and our absence goes unnoticed. We share information in our tap-language, trade routes and warnings, update each other on the patterns we've noticed. We are the district's nervous system, sensing what the larger body cannot feel. We are necessary and temporary, powerful and fragile, caught between the child-spaces we navigate and the adult knowledge we carry.

In three years, if I survive, if I don't get stuck, if infection doesn't claim me, if I don't fall into the biosump, if the steam doesn't finally cook my lungs—in three years, I'll be too large for this work. But today I am perfect for it. Today I am exactly what the district needs: small enough to fit, clever enough to survive, young enough to believe I'm invincible, old enough to know I'm not.

I disappear into the pipe mouth, and the darkness swallows me like it's been waiting. Behind me, the eternal rhythm continues: chunk-hiss-chunk. The city breathes through its thousand brass lungs, and I crawl through its veins, looking for the disease that threatens the flow, the small and necessary guardian of spaces too narrow for anyone else to love.
