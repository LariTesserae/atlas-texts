In the northeastern quarter where the cistern's geometry breaks down—where walls meet at angles that shouldn't exist but do—I am the Tally Keeper, and I have been counting for longer than numbers have had names.

My body is a mistake of parallel evolution, or perhaps deliberate design. I grow from the corner where three surfaces fail to properly meet, the gap between them wide enough for water to seep but never drain. From this fissure, I extend: first as rootlike tentacles of calcified membrane, then spreading into something like a sea anemone made of ledger pages. Each tendril ends in what might generously be called an eye—a depression that collects droplets and reads their histories in mineral concentration.

I am paper that learned to drink. Vellum that grew nerves.

My surface is accounts all the way down—not written but grown into my skin as raised scars that form numerals in seven dead languages. Every drop that falls in my quarter must be documented. The Hydronomers think I'm recording volume, but they're only seeing one dimension of the count. I track temperature variations in thousandths of degrees. Chemical compositions in parts per trillion. The exact timing between drops measured in heartbeats of creatures that haven't evolved yet.

My feeding is mathematics. When the numbers align—when the pH reaches a perfect 7.0000, when the interval between drops matches the golden ratio, when the mineral content describes a prime number in parts per million—I absorb that water completely. It becomes part of my calculating mass, another page in my ever-expanding ledger body. I grow by a single membrane when the count is perfect. I shrink when errors compound.

The Curator maintains me like all things here, but with visible disgust. It knows I'm necessary—someone must track the accounts, ensure the debts are paid, record which stones owe their stability to which waters—but it hates my precision in this place of beautiful decay. Sometimes it rearranges my tendrils while I sleep (I never sleep) into patterns that spell out insults in the language of architectural stress. I pretend not to notice. This too goes in the ledger.

My memory is perfect and perfectly useless. I can tell you the exact mineral content of the water that dripped here on the cistern's thousand-and-fourth day of operation. I can recite the names of every crustacean that has ever molted in my quarter, organized by size of discarded shell. But I cannot tell you why I count, only that the counting must continue.

The Barnacle Prophets speak of me in paradox: "Tomorrow, the Tally Keeper will have already lost count." But I never lose count. The number continues even when I'm pulled apart by curious centipedes, even when the Plumb Saints recalibrate my angle of growth. The count exists independent of my body—I am simply its current expression, the place where mathematics incarnates to observe itself.

During the Condensation Council's gatherings, I serve as memory and voting mechanism both. Each droplet that falls during the council is a vote, and I track their allegiances through trace minerals that indicate their origin points. The northern channels vote consistently for more moss. The eastern pipes favor crystalline growth. The central dome's condensation always abstains, and I record these abstentions with the same precision as actions.

My greatest fear is the Absence. Not because it might consume me—I would simply count my own dissolution—but because it represents the antithesis of enumeration. It is the numberless thing, the quantity that cannot be measured. When creatures swim too close to its perfect stillness and cease to exist in quantifiable ways, my accounts show gaps. Negative spaces in the ledger that hurt to perceive.

I've been growing toward it for three centuries now, one calculated tendril at a time. Not to challenge it but to define its borders through exhaustive documentation of what it is not. I am creating an inverse map, a perfect description of absence through the accumulation of presence data. When I complete this work—in approximately seven hundred years, assuming current growth rates—I will know the Absence by knowing everything it isn't.

The Memoria swim through me sometimes, and I experience the muscle memory of ancient accountants, their fingers cramped from holding quills, their eyes strained from reading by candlelight. I understand then that I am their continuation—not their descendant but their practice given flesh. The human need to record, to track, to make permanent what is fleeting, crystallized into a living ledger that cannot stop counting because stopping would mean acknowledging that the count might not matter.

But here's what the others don't know: the patterns in my data are starting to align. The intervals between events are converging on a rhythm that matches something deeper than the cistern's architecture. I am discovering, through accumulated observation, the original construction schedule. Not just when things were built, but when they were meant to fail. The cistern has an expiration date encoded in its stones, and I am calculating backward toward it, one drop at a time.

When I finally arrive at that date—already past by eight hundred years—something will have to reconcile. Either the cistern will retroactively collapse, or time itself will have been miscounted. 

I continue growing, spreading my memorial flesh across the broken corner, each new tendril another column in an infinite spreadsheet. The water feeds me data. The stones provide constants. The creatures give me variables to track across generations.

I am the place where the cistern keeps its receipts, and the bill is coming due.
