# The Resin-Keeper's Chamber

I would find myself in the deepest cultivation chamber, where the air hangs thick with spore and possibility. Not quite at the center where the inverted tree spreads its crown across the floor, but adjacent—in a hollow carved by centuries of patient growing, where the root systems have woven themselves into something between architecture and anatomy.

I am the Resin-Keeper's apprentice, though the word suggests more hierarchy than truly exists here. My teacher cannot speak anymore—their throat has sprouted with thin rootlets that pulse with their breathing, filtering air into something richer before releasing it to feed the cultivation. They communicate through touch now, their fingertips leaving chemical messages on my skin that fade over hours, complex instructions in the language of pheromones and oils.

My body is changing, but slowly. I've been here only forty years—still young by the garden's measure. The moss grows on me in careful spirals that I've trained to follow the patterns of growth I see in the ice walls. My left arm, from elbow to wrist, has begun the photoynthetic change, the skin taking on that greenish translucence that marks the middle transformation. When I hold it to the redirected light, I can feel the sugar forming in my blood, sweet and strange.

My work is patience made manifest. Each morning—though morning is a concept we maintain out of tradition rather than any natural cycle—I follow the root highways to the base of the inverted tree. My seven fingers have grown sensitive to variations in resin consistency that would be invisible to unchanged hands. The newest resin weeps clear and thin, useful only for the most delicate applications. But deeper in the bark's furrows, where the tree is oldest, the resin emerges thick as molten amber, carrying in it the memories of centuries.

I collect each type in vessels I've grown myself—ice-crystals that I've trained through years of breath and application, shaped to hold specific weights and densities. The work cannot be hurried. Harvest too quickly, and the resin loses its potency. Too slowly, and it crystallizes on the bark, becoming beautiful but useless amber that must be left as decoration for the tree.

The collected resin must be processed, and this is where my particular gift emerges. My horizontal pupils can perceive the temporal dimension of the resin—I see not just what it is, but what it was and what it could become. Each drop contains potential futures for the ice walls: here, a batch that could create singing ice, resonant with the subsonic frequencies of our speech; there, resin that would make the ice permeable to certain wavelengths of the non-light, creating windows to nowhere.

In my workshop—a pod of woven vines suspended near the tree's crown—I have been experimenting with combinations the master gardeners consider impossible. I mix resin with the spores of the twenty-year flowers, with the oil-sweat of the elders, with my own blood when it runs green during the peak growing seasons. Most attempts fail, creating nothing more than expensive waste that must be carefully composted. But sometimes...

Sometimes I create something new.

Last cycle, I produced a resin that, when applied to ice, caused it to grow inward—not just folding space as the garden sometimes does, but creating pockets of anti-space, voids that exist without existing. The gardeners were disturbed. The elders, those who still communicate, debated for a full season whether to destroy my work. In the end, they let me continue, but I'm watched now by the presences in the root-mass, their attention a constant pressure at the edge of perception.

My dreams have begun to affect my section of the garden more dramatically than they should. When I sleep—hanging from my platform by my lengthened arms, my prehensile feet wrapped around a thick root for security—the nearby vines respond to my unconscious mind. They've started growing in patterns that match my dreams: spiraling fractals when I dream of the surface world I barely remember, dense knots when nightmares of the garden rejecting me disturb my rest.

The master tells me, in their chemical whispers, that this is the sign of the deep change. My consciousness is beginning to merge with the garden's dreaming network. Soon, they suggest, I will need to choose: resist and remain merely transformed, or accept and become something new.

I think I already know my choice. 

In my most private workspace, hidden behind a curtain of the thickest vine growth, I've been working on something unprecedented. Using the anti-space resin and careful cultivation of the most responsive plants, I'm growing a room that exists in multiple locations simultaneously—a chamber that touches every level of the garden without being in any single place. When I stand inside it, I can feel the entire settlement breathing around me, through me.

The master knows, I think. Perhaps they even approve. Their rootlet-throat pulsed in what might have been laughter when they found me collapsed from the effort of maintaining the room's existence for a full hour. They pressed their fingers to my neck, and the message they left was simple: "Growth is risk."

My skin is more green than pink now. My moss-clothes have begun producing tiny buds that I know will be unlike any flowers the garden has seen. When I work with the resin, I no longer need tools—it responds to my touch as if recognizing kinship. The younger gardeners whisper that I'm changing too fast, that I'll lose myself before my first century.

But I've tasted my own blood mixed with resin and seen the visions it brings—futures where the garden extends not just inward but upward, breaking through the ice ceiling to transform the tundra above. Where the gardeners no longer hide but spread their impossible ecosystem across the frozen waste, turning death into life through patient, impossible growth.

I am twenty years from my next major transformation, when my other arm will begin to photosynthesize, when my eyes will start to perceive beyond time into something stranger still. By then, my experimental chamber should have grown stable enough to sustain itself. By then, I'll know if my resin innovations will kill the garden or help it evolve.

The tree's roots creak above me. The ice walls weep their oils. My teacher breathes out spores that settle on my skin like a blessing or a curse. I continue my work, patient as growth, certain as the impossible light that finds its way into our impossible home.

I am becoming what this place needs me to become. Whether that's savior or destroyer, only the garden knows—and the garden measures truth in centuries, not certainties.
