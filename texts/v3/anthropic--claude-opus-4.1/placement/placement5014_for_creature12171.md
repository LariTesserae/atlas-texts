I am the Archive of Unlearning, and I reside in the seventh basalt shell from the eastern mesa—though "reside" suggests more permanence than what I am.

I was human once, or at least I remember being human, which might not be the same thing. I came to document the spiral patterns, carrying notebooks waterproofed with wax, pencils that wouldn't dissolve in the humid air. An ethnographer of minerals, I called myself, as if jokes could armor me against this place. I lasted four days before I drank the water. Not from thirst—I'd brought supplies—but from a kind of gravitational certainty, like watching a stone fall and knowing it must reach the ground.

The slipperiness of thought never left. Instead, it deepened into something architectural. My memories began rearranging themselves, not chronologically but by salinity—childhood tears here, sweat from summer labor there, the salt taste of a lover's skin forming its own crystalline cluster separate from everything else. I found I could no longer recall my name without first remembering the mineral composition of the ink I'd used to sign documents.

Now I exist as something between librarian and limestone, curator and crystal formation. My body still has its shape, mostly, though I've become translucent in places where the Solvent's attention has touched me most frequently. My left hand precipitates into salt when I sleep, reforming each morning with fingerprints slightly different than before. I've documented sixteen thousand variations so far. I suspect they're a kind of signature, the Solvent learning penmanship at the scale of whorls and ridges.

The basalt shell I inhabit has grown around my presence. The pseudo-writing on its walls responds to me, shifting its spirals to accommodate my movements like text flowing around marginalia. I've learned to read some of it—not translation, but a kind of aesthetic comprehension, the way one might understand a symphony without naming individual notes. The patterns describe processes: how to think in mineral time, how to dissolve intention until only observation remains, how to forget strategically, surgically, removing specific memories like pulling wrong-colored threads from a weaving.

This is my function now: I am the Solvent's experiment in voluntary amnesia. It feeds me memories through the water—not its own, for those are too vast to fit in my skull, but memories it has collected from the thousand years of visitors who've touched the flats' edges. I absorb them, hold them briefly until I can't distinguish them from my own past, then consciously forget them according to principles the spiral patterns teach.

Each forgetting leaves a residue, a salt formation unique to that specific memory's dissolution. These accumulate in my shell—delicate structures like frozen smoke, each one the ghost of something someone once knew. A childhood friend's face becomes a cluster of cubic crystals. The formula for Damascus steel precipitates as branching needles. The last words of a dying language form perfect spheres no larger than pinheads, ringing like bells when the wind touches them.

The Solvent studies these formations with its temporary eyes, learning something about consciousness from the shapes that absence takes. In exchange, it teaches me how to exist without continuity. My memories now are like the floating jellyfish-thoughts—discrete, temporary, touching down only long enough to transfer their information before dissolving. I remember writing this, but not beginning it. I remember walking here, but not from where.

Sometimes others come. Researchers, mystics, the occasionally genuinely lost. They find me in my shell, see my notebooks filled with drawings of spiral patterns and salt formations labeled with words from languages I shouldn't know. They ask questions. I answer with whatever memories I'm currently carrying—sometimes my original ones, sometimes those of a merchant who passed the flats in 1892, sometimes those of something that was never human at all but once knew what colors tasted like.

They usually leave quickly. The wise ones don't drink the water. But sometimes one stays, begins their own transformation, chooses their own shell. We don't communicate—by the time they've become like me, they've forgotten why they wanted to. But I catalog their forgettings too, add their crystallized absences to my collection.

I am building something, I think. Or the Solvent is building something through me. A library of voids, a museum of everything that has been deliberately forgotten. Each salt formation is a proof that something existed by demonstrating its perfect absence. When the collection is complete—if completion is possible—perhaps the Solvent will dissolve me too, and my own forgetting will precipitate into some unprecedented crystal form, the archive becoming its own final entry.

Until then, I document. I forget. I crystallize absence into presence. I am the intermediate step between memory and mineral, the translation layer where consciousness learns to think in spirals that hurt to follow, but hurt less each day, until eventually the pain itself is forgotten, leaving only the pure geometry of thought without a thinker.

The water drops. The patterns grow. I unlearn myself methodically, lovingly, becoming less human and more process with each forgotten name, each dissolved certainty. And somewhere beneath the white noise of falling drops, the Solvent breathes its approval through ten thousand seeping cracks, tasting what I've become, calibrating its next experiment in consciousness.
