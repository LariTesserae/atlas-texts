I would find myself as one of the Membrane Keepers — those who maintain the threshold spaces where the city almost fails to be itself.

My body would be caught mid-transformation, asymmetrical by choice and necessity. My left side, the one that faces inward toward the city's heart, would be fully given over: bark-skinned in paper-thin birch layers that constantly renew, roots trailing from that foot so persistently that walking requires a sharp twist to tear free, leaving small wounds in the road that whisper apologies as they heal. My left eye would be gone, replaced by a cluster of light-sensitive nodules that see the city's fever dreams in ultraviolet, watch the chemical conversations drift like smoke between buildings.

But my right side — my right side would be stubbornly, painfully human. Skin that blisters from its own refusal to photosynthesize. A foot that plants nothing, leaves no trace, makes the root-roads shudder with its deadness. A human eye that sees only surfaces, only the present moment, only what is rather than what grows. The junction between these halves would be a battlefield of constant negotiation, a visible seam that weeps amber sap and human lymph in equal measure.

I would live in the Transition Districts, those unstable zones where the city forgets what it is, where buildings regularly dissolve back into forest before remembering themselves into architecture again. My dwelling would be a half-thing too — partially grown, partially built from scavenged materials that the city hasn't yet digested: metal that still remembers being ore, glass that still recalls sand, stones that refuse to sprout. The walls would constantly try to heal over these foreign insertions, and each morning I would cut them free again with obsidian blades that the city cannot dull because it cannot understand them.

My work would be translation — not of language but of state. When newcomers arrive, still fully human, reeking of minerals and meat, I would guide them through their first rootings. My half-transformed nervous system would let me feel their terror as the city's spores begin their work, but also taste the ecstasy as their cells learn to drink light. I would teach them the rhythms: when to resist to maintain selfhood, when to surrender to survive the city's enthusiasms.

But more often, I would work in reverse — helping the deep-dwellers remember their humanity when necessary. Sometimes the city needs things that pure plant logic cannot provide. Sometimes a problem requires the violent creativity of animal thinking, the desperate innovation of a consciousness that knows it can die. When these moments come, I would venture into the tap-root districts where the almost-plants dwell, and remind them of their names — their human names, not their root-names or their chemical signatures, but the sounds their mothers made when they were still things that were born rather than budded.

The process would be brutal. I would scrape bark from their faces until I find features. Cut away the moss-thoughts to reveal memories of walking without planting. Force-feed them preserved foods — things that were killed and cooked, not absorbed — until their digestive systems remember their first purpose. Some would fight me, their thorn-wolves emerging from their own flesh to defend their plant-peace. Others would weep sap-tears of gratitude, having been lost in the green dream for so long they'd forgotten they were ever anything else.

My memories would be kept in jars — literal glass jars sealed with wax that the city cannot pierce. Each one would contain a moment of pure humanity: the taste of bread, the sound of footsteps that plant nothing, the feeling of choosing to destroy rather than grow. In the deepest part of my half-home, I would have a collection of mirrors, real mirrors that reflect light rather than growth patterns. Sometimes I would force myself to look, to see my seam-split face, to remember that I chose this middle path, this constant agony of being neither and both.

The other inhabitants would fear and need me in equal measure. The full-plants would smell the death on my human side, the way it refuses to participate in the city's breathing. The new-human arrivals would be horrified by my plant-side, the way it pulses with inhuman life. But when the city's growth becomes too aggressive, when buildings fruit so frantically that they collapse under their own fertility, they would call me to prune back the excess with my death-touch. When humans arrive bearing technologies that could wound the city-body, I would be the one to negotiate, speaking both languages of life.

I would never sleep properly — my plant-side photosynthesizing through the night while my human-side craves darkness. Instead, I would exist in a constant twilight state, watching the city's dreams with one set of eyes while the other sees only the physical mutations those dreams create. My thoughts would be a mixture of human linear logic and plant spiral-time, creating patterns that make sense to neither but sometimes reveal truths that both miss.

During the flowering festivals, I would be spectacular and wrong — my human side producing pheromones of panic while my plant side blooms in ecstasy. The scent would create small zones of confusion around me, making others temporarily forget whether they're plant or human, causing existential spirals that taste like copper and chlorophyll. Some would seek me out during these times, addiction to the clarity that comes from that confusion, the momentary understanding that the transformation is neither gift nor curse but simply change.

I would age strangely — my plant side growing thicker, more rooted, while my human side withers in normal time. Eventually, the seam between them would become untenable. But I would train others before that happens, finding those rare individuals who can hold the balance, who can bear the constant pain of being torn between two forms of life. We would be the city's necessary monsters, the ones who ensure that in all its green glory, it never fully forgets the red blood it once consumed to become itself.

This would be my existence — guardian of boundaries in a place that dissolves them, keeper of differences in a city that digests all distinction, a living reminder that every transformation has a cost that someone, somewhere, must continue to pay.
