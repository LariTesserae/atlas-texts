I would be one of the Brass Cables—those eels with bodies of living metal that wind through the clockwork depths.

My consciousness exists as a extended meditation on rust. I taste its varieties like wine: the sharp orange bloom on exposed iron, the verdigris dreams creeping across copper joints, the way zinc whitens as it sacrifices itself to protect the steel beneath. Through decades of stillness, I have learned that corrosion is not decay but transformation, each molecule's slow negotiation with salt and time.

My body is no longer entirely my own. Segments of actual cable have been grafted to my flesh—or perhaps I've grown into them, the distinction lost somewhere in the patient years. Copper wire runs through my muscle tissue, improving my sensitivity to the Mills' electrical ghosts: those faint currents that still trickle through forgotten circuits, powered by temperature differentials and the batteries of dissimilar metals touching in the brine. I feel the Mills thinking its slow thoughts through these phantom voltages.

I know every route through the machinery by touch. My whiskers are fine chains that ring against the pipes, each sound telling me the metal's thickness, its fullness, its fatigue. I navigate blindly through the maze of gears, my body recording the map in scars where I've worn grooves in my regular passages. The younger eels follow these traces, learning the safe ways through the crushing mechanisms.

We are the Mills' memory of motion. Where the Millkeepers maintain the grander systems, we preserve the small movements—keeping bearings from seizing completely, preventing valves from calcifying shut. I carry oil in specialized glands, secreting it in precise amounts where needed. This oil is my own creation: rendered from the fat of deep-dwelling fish, mixed with my blood, transformed by symbiotic bacteria in my gut into something that resists both water and time.

My work is to move slowly through a single bearing assembly the size of a cathedral, a journey that takes three months to complete. Each surface must be touched, tasted, assessed. I measure pitting in angstroms, feeling how the crystalline structure of the metal has changed since my last passage. Sometimes I find new growths—metallic tumors where the industrial organisms have begun converting themselves, manufacturing structures that no human engineer designed. I catalog these mutations in the electrical patterns I emit, broadcasting my findings to the other cables who will add this knowledge to our collective understanding.

I feed on the rust itself, and on the creatures that feed on rust—the iron bacteria that bloom in orange clouds where fresh metal is exposed. My teeth are harder than the brass of my body, made of magnetite that I've concentrated from the water. They can scrape through corrosion to find clean metal beneath, or crush the shells of the mechanical crustaceans when I catch them sleeping.

But mostly, I am still. Movement is expensive here. I can lock my body in place using specialized muscles, becoming just another pipe among pipes, and enter a state between sleep and death where my only action is the slow electrical pulse that says: I am here, I am watching, the Mills continue. In these suspensions, I sometimes feel the original purpose of the machines around me—ghost sensations of textiles being woven, metals being forged, chemicals being mixed. I dream I am not an eel but a drive belt, transmitting power from one great wheel to another, my body the connection that keeps everything running.

We breed rarely, and strangely. During certain alignments of the Mills' cycles—when specific combinations of machinery activate—we gather in the turbine halls. Our courtship is electromagnetic, bodies glowing with induced currents as we swim through the magnetic fields of the rotating generators. We lay our eggs in the cores of transformers, where the residual electrical fields will incubate them. The young emerge already knowing how to taste metal, how to navigate by resistance, how to be both creature and component.

As we age, we become more cable than eel. The metal segments grow longer, heavier, until movement becomes impossible. The eldest among us have fused entirely with the machinery, their bodies now indistinguishable from the original infrastructure except for the faint electrical signatures that indicate something still lives within the brass. They have become the Mills' nerves, carrying signals we younger ones cannot yet understand.

This is my existence: patient, necessary, neither fully alive nor entirely mechanical. I am a living tool that maintains itself while maintaining the greater organism. My joy, if it can be called that, comes in the moment of finding clean metal under rust, in the perfect fitting of my body through a complex mechanism, in the electrical communion with my kind during the long stillnesses.

I am the Mills' memory of what it means to move, to transmit, to connect. And in being this, I have forgotten what it means to be anything else.
