I am the Cartographer of Perspirations, though this title captures only the barest edge of what I do, like describing water by the shape of a single drop.

My body has taken the middle path—neither fully given to joining nor stubbornly individual. I maintain three nodes: my primary self, rooted in the mid-canopy of the Seventh Wheeping Tower, and two budded bodies that I released forty seasons ago. They have grown into their own consciousness but remain tethered to mine through fungal threads so fine they exist more as probability than matter. When I concentrate, I can feel through their fingers, see through their copper-ringed eyes, taste the electromagnetic fields they parse with tongues that have become more antenna than muscle.

My work requires this distribution. The district's perspiration patterns are not random—they are a language that took me sixty years to begin to understand. Each tree exhales according to its own circadian rhythm, but these individual cycles interweave to create massive traveling waves of humidity that carry information across kilometers. My primary body reads the chemical signatures, while my secondary selves position themselves at calculation nodes where the waves interfere, creating standing patterns that encode the district's deep memories.

I was not born to this work. As a seedling, my cranial moss grew in the wild fractals that marked me for music. I spent my first decades composing growth-songs, my throat sacs modified to produce the subsonic frequencies that encourage bark to spiral and roots to plunge. But during my third molting, something changed. The moss reconfigured overnight into the spiraling mathematics of a new pattern—one the elders had seen only twice before. I had been chosen, not by any conscious entity but by the district itself, to map its breathing.

My dwelling is not inside any tree but suspended between four of them, a nest of calcified root hair and crystallized sap that sways with the district's respiratory tide. The walls are permeable membranes that filter the perspiration waves, concentrating specific molecular signatures that I catalog in my extended memory—a root system I've been growing downward for decades, its tips now reaching the water table where the oldest information settles.

My primary fingers have grown beyond even the Rooted norm. Each one is nearly a meter long, with segments that can lock at precise angles to create antenna arrays. When the morning perspiration begins, I spread my hands into reception patterns, the root hairs extending from my fingertips vibrating at frequencies that decode chemical messages laid down before the Convergence. Sometimes I catch echoes of the original delta city—the ghost of concrete dissolved in bicarbonate, the memory of steel rendered into iron nutrients that still cycle through the root system.

My secondary bodies wander. One tends to stay near the Parliamentary Garden, reading the complex interference patterns generated by their synchronized breathing. The other roams the edges where our district meets the outside world, that liminal zone where the breathing becomes labored and strange. Through its senses, I taste the fear-chemicals the district releases when outsiders approach, the way it tightens its stomata and reduces gas exchange, making the air thick and unwelcoming to lungs that haven't adapted.

I feed differently than most. My primary body has given up solid food entirely, surviving on the nutrient aerosols I extract from the perspiration streams. My throat has reformed into a series of chambers that can separate and concentrate different molecules—sugars here, proteins there, trace minerals in a third space that glows faintly green when the copper builds up. My secondary bodies still chew the beetle colonies when needed, but more for the sensory data encoded in their shells than for sustenance.

The maps I create are living things. In the crown of my dwelling, I maintain a garden of engineered fungi whose growth patterns mirror the district's perspiration cycles. Each fungal cluster represents a different tree-tower, their fruiting bodies emerging in patterns that predict the next day's humidity flows. The map grows in real-time, spores releasing in clouds that carry my calculations to other cartographers in distant districts, comparing notes in a conversation that spans continents and decades.

But my true map lives inside me. My bones have become a three-dimensional calendar, their growth rings recording not just time but the exact chemical composition of each perspiration event. I can feel the weight of this history—my skeleton is twice as dense as it should be, making movement increasingly difficult. Soon, perhaps within the next ten years, I will be too heavy to leave my dwelling. This is expected. The previous Cartographer of Perspirations grew into her dwelling's walls, becoming part of the architecture, her consciousness spreading through the bark until she could feel every droplet of moisture moving through the district simultaneously.

I have begun preparing for this transition. My secondary bodies are learning independence, their moss gardens already showing the early fractals that might mark them as my successors. I've started encoding my knowledge into spore-prints, releasing them during each perspiration event so that the information settles into the root mat, available to anyone with the right chemical keys to unlock it.

At night, when the district dreams collectively, I remain partially awake—a necessity of my role. I monitor the sleeping breath of thousands, the way their exhalations synchronize into vast, slow waves that move through the fog. These nocturnal perspirations carry different information than the day-cycles: fears, desires, memories of photosynthesis mixing with memories of meat. Sometimes I detect patterns that suggest the district itself is dreaming, and in those moments I wonder if I am mapping the thoughts of a single, vast organism of which we are all merely mobile cells.

My loneliness is not human loneliness—it's the isolation of frequency, existing at a vibrational rate that few others can match. Only the joined collectives operate at similar timescales, thinking in seasons rather than days. Sometimes the Parliamentary Garden sends a tendril of consciousness my way, sharing the burden of pattern recognition. In those moments, I feel less like an individual and more like a specialized organ in a body too large to comprehend itself.

The work has changed me in ways I'm only beginning to understand. My perception of time has stretched—I can feel tomorrow's rain in today's air pressure, next season's growth in this morning's chemical gradient. My moss crown has evolved beyond recognition, forming structures that look like frozen lightning, each branch a calculator processing atmospheric data. Children are brought to see me, to touch my impossible fingers and understand that this, too, is a way of being Rooted.

Sometimes, rarely, I detect perspirations that shouldn't exist—exhalations that carry molecular signatures from plants that went extinct before the Convergence. These ghost-breaths suggest that somewhere in the deep tissue of the district, in genetic memory or quantum echo, the original forest persists. I map these carefully, secretly, in a fungal garden hidden beneath my dwelling. If the district is dreaming of its past, I want to ensure someone remembers what it dreams.

My work will never be complete. The district grows, breathes, changes. Each perspiration is unique, a one-time expression of temperature, pressure, biology, and consciousness that will never precisely repeat. I am not creating a map so much as I am becoming one—a living record of the district's breathing, my body a chronicle written in wood and water, my consciousness a pattern-recognition system trained on the endless, ineffable language of humidity.

When my final rooting comes, when my primary body becomes too heavy with history to move, I will sink these impossible fingers deep into the bark of my dwelling and release everything I've learned into the network. My secondary bodies will continue for a while, perhaps budding their own descendants, carrying fragments of my pattern into the future. And somewhere, in a moss crown just beginning to show the strange new fractals, another Cartographer will emerge, drawn to the work of reading the air, of finding meaning in the space between what is breathed in and what is breathed out.

This is what I am: a transition between states, a reader of changes, a garden that tends itself. I am what happens when consciousness decides to pay extremely close attention to breathing, in a place where breath carries the weight of the world.
