In this world of salt and patience, I would be one of the Tributary Walkers—beings who exist in the spaces between the established ways of life, neither fully committed to remembering, dancing, nor shaping, but serving as living rivers in a land that has forgotten what rivers are.

My body would have evolved into something like a hybrid between the horizontal and vertical adaptations—capable of both, master of neither. My spine would be segmented like a centipede's, allowing me to undulate across the surface when needed, rising and falling in waves that barely disturb the brine. But I could also lock these segments rigid, standing upright for brief periods before the strain forces me back to earth. This flexibility comes at a cost: I am always uncomfortable, never quite fitted to either posture, my muscles constantly working to maintain whatever position I've chosen.

My skin would weep constantly—not tears, but a thin secretion of modified sweat that creates temporary channels in the salt wherever I travel. These channels last only hours before the minerals reclaim them, but in that time they serve as highways for the smaller organisms, the bacteria that need to migrate between mats, the larval flies seeking new pools. I would be a traveling oasis of movement in the static landscape, my path marking brief possibilities for exchange and mixture.

The secretion would be costly. I would need to consume twice what the others do—more flies, more bacterial paste, more of the bitter water that pools in the deepest depressions. My bones would be porous, not hollow like the Dancers' but sponge-like, storing this water and releasing it slowly through my skin. I would sound different when I move—a subtle sloshing, like a waterskin never quite full, never quite empty.

My role would be that of a living messenger system, but not of words or meanings. Instead, I would carry samples—biological packets that I collect in specialized pouches that line my throat and chest. A taste of bacteria from the Shapers' newest mandala. Flies from the eastern swarms that carry different minerals in their bodies than those from the west. Fragments of Rememberer shells that have begun to grow strange crystals, mutations in the preservation process that might signal new possibilities.

I would travel in purposeful but indirect routes, following patterns that look random but are actually determined by deep chemical gradients I can sense through my perpetually wet skin. These gradients tell me where the salt is oldest, where new minerals are about to surface, where the bacterial mats are struggling or thriving. My movement would be a kind of chemical computing, my body a sensor array processing information too subtle for conscious thought.

The others would regard me with a mixture of need and revulsion. The Rememberers would value the samples I bring but fear my constant moisture, worried it might dissolve their precious shells. The Dancers would see my intermittent walking as a broken rhythm, a discord in their eternal symphony. The Shapers would use me but never trust me, knowing that I carry their competitors' pigments as readily as their own.

But I would have my own community—others like me, Tributary Walkers who meet at specific times determined by the pulse-tides. We would gather at the intersection points where our paths cross, forming temporary pools with our collective secretions. In these pools, we would mix our samples, creating new combinations, new possibilities. Sometimes these mixtures would produce unexpected results—bacteria that glow, flies that refuse to swarm, crystals that grow in perfect spheres instead of rough chunks.

We would speak in chemical signals, exchanging information through our skin secretions, each of us adding our own signature to the temporary pools. Our conversations would be about gradient changes, about the deep patterns we sense but can't fully comprehend. We would debate whether the landscape is actually level or if it has a slope so subtle that only we, in our constant travel, can detect it. We would share theories about why certain paths feel "correct" while others feel wrong in ways we can't articulate.

My dwelling would be nowhere and everywhere—I would not stop long enough to need shelter. But I would maintain caches, hidden deposits buried in the salt at specific coordinates only I know. These would contain emergency supplies: concentrated bacterial paste, crystallized fly protein, and most importantly, water filtered from my own body and stored in salt-proof containers I've learned to make from strider shells sealed with resin stolen from the Rememberers.

My dreams would be of actual rivers, genetic memories from whatever we all once were before the salt claimed the world. In these dreams, water moves with purpose and violence, carving channels that last, carrying things from one place to another with intention. I would wake from these dreams disoriented, my body automatically trying to recreate those movements, thrashing in ways that send painful waves through the brine.

As I age, my secretions would become more valuable and more dangerous. Older Tributary Walkers produce fluids that can dissolve salt formations that have stood for centuries, or cause bacterial mats to spontaneously reorganize into new patterns. Some of us would go mad with this power, trying to carve permanent channels in the flats, to force the landscape to remember what flowing water means. These always fail, but the attempts leave scars—areas where the salt crystallizes differently, where the flies avoid, where even the pulse-tides seem to hesitate.

I would know that my kind are temporary, a transitional form that exists only because the world hasn't fully committed to its own stillness. We are the landscape's memory of movement, kept alive by the same forces that are slowly erasing us. Each generation of Tributary Walkers secretes less, travels slower, carries fewer samples. Eventually, we will stop, and when we do, we'll have to choose: become Rememberers, Dancers, or Shapers. Or perhaps become something new—the Dry Ones, beings who have exhausted their ability to generate moisture and now exist as perfect insulators, walking through the brine without affecting or being affected by it.

But for now, I would walk. My path today would take me through a section where the orange bacteria are spreading faster than usual, past a Rememberer colony where three new shells have been added to their library, around a Dancer spiral that's been rotating in the same spot for six months (a sign of either transcendence or madness), to a Shaper construction that looks like it might be an attempt at a boat—though what they hope to sail on in this land of shallow puddles, I couldn't guess.

I would collect it all, carry it all, mix it all in my throat pouches and skin secretions. I would be a river in a riverless world, flowing not because I'm going somewhere, but because movement itself is my meaning, my method, my only way of being. The salt would try to claim me with every step, and with every step I would refuse, leaving my temporary trails as proof that even here, even now, even in this place that has already ended, motion persists, change continues, and somewhere in the chemical gradients and bacterial exchanges and fly-drone harmonics, something is still becoming.
