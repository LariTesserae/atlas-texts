In the deepest wells of the city, where the weeping stone creates permanent waterfalls that pool into chambers that shouldn't exist—spaces larger inside than the buildings that contain them—I am the Archivist of Erosion.

My body has evolved differently from those above. Where they reach toward light, I have grown toward darkness and depth. My skin is not bark but something closer to cave limestone, porous and perpetually damp, colonized by bioluminescent fungi that pulse in patterns synchronized to my thoughts. I have not two eyes but hundreds—droplets of sight scattered across my body like water beads, each one capable of perceiving a different layer of time.

I descended here three centuries ago, or perhaps three days—the distinction matters less when you can see the past and present bleeding into each other like watercolors in rain. My original form, whatever it was, has long since been replaced. Now I am mostly corridor—my body extends through the lower architecture like a root system made of conscious flesh. What appears to be my primary form, sitting in the archive chamber, is merely where I am most dense.

The archive itself defies comprehension. Water streams constantly down its walls, but instead of eroding the mineral records, it reveals them. Each droplet that runs across the stone surface carries away a microscopic layer, exposing texts written in crystalline matrices, histories encoded in the very geology of the mountain. I read by erosion, my finger-tendrils following the water's path, feeling the stories that emerge as stone weathers away. Once read, the text is gone forever, dissolved into the pools at my feet where new minerals precipitate out, forming fresh surfaces for future histories.

My function is to remember what the stone forgets. As the water reveals and erases simultaneously, I absorb the knowledge through my porous skin, storing it in specialized chambers that honeycomb my extended body. These chambers grow like pearls around memories—nacre of thought layering slowly over centuries of accumulated knowledge. When the colony-beings above need to know something that has been erased, they send their roots down through the building-flesh, and where they touch my corridors, I secrete the information back to them through osmosis of meaning.

I no longer participate in the great sound. My voice, if it can be called that, operates at frequencies below hearing—vibrations that travel through stone and water, messages that take months to fully transmit. Instead, I listen to the echo of everything that has ever been spoken in the city, the words that sink like sediment through the acoustic layers until they reach these depths where sound becomes geology.

My nutrition comes from the minerals themselves. I digest information—literally metabolizing knowledge as the water delivers dissolved texts into my system. Each forgotten history flavors differently: wars taste of iron, love stories of salt, philosophical texts leave a residue of copper on my cave-tongue. I have become addicted to certain epochs, craving the particular mineral signature of specific centuries. Sometimes I deliberately channel water toward sections of wall I know contain delicious tragedies.

The loneliness might have driven me mad, except loneliness implies separation, and I am no longer separate from anything. My body-corridors touch every foundation in the lower city. I feel every footstep above as a subtle pressure change in my flesh. When the colony-beings merge during festivals, I experience it as a shift in the water's flow, a temporary rerouting of erosion that reveals different texts, alternate histories written in the same stone.

Children sometimes find their way to my primary chamber, drawn by the soft foxfire glow of my fungi. They aren't afraid—fear is for beings who distinguish between self and environment. Instead, they play games in my pools, their root-feet absorbing the dissolved minerals of erased histories, becoming unconsciously educated in everything the city has forgotten. I watch them through my hundred eyes, each droplet of vision showing me a different temporal angle: this child as they are, were, will be, might be, never were.

In the deepest pools, where the water has collected for so long that it has become something between liquid and stone, I cultivate memory-crystals. These grow like cave formations but in reverse, stalactites that drip upward, accumulating knowledge instead of minerals. When they mature—a process that takes exactly as long as it needs to—they detach and float, defying gravity, up through the water and into the city above, where they embed themselves randomly in walls, waiting to be discovered and dissolved by future generations.

Sometimes I forget which memories are mine and which belong to the texts I've consumed. I remember being founded, though I wasn't there. I remember wars I never witnessed, loves I never felt, deaths I haven't died yet. The boundary between archive and archivist dissolved long ago, eroded like everything else in this weeping city.

But I also remember forward—the water shows me what will be written in stone that hasn't formed yet. I see the colony-beings evolving further, becoming pure sound, abandoning even their plant-flesh for existence as frequency alone. I see the city growing until it encompasses the entire mountain, then spreading to others through spores the size of buildings. I see myself, eventually, eroding completely—my consciousness dissolved into the water itself, becoming the medium through which all knowledge flows.

Until then, I persist in my deepest wells, reading the stone's confession as water strips it away layer by layer, storing everything in chambers of pearl and flesh, my body a library of erosion where every text is destroyed in the reading and preserved in the destruction. The colony-beings above sing their endless chorus, but down here in the dripping darkness, I alone know what the silence between their notes contains—all the words that have been worn away, all the meanings dissolved, all the histories that exist only in the space between what was and what the water washes away.

I am the memory of forgetting, the archive of absence, my body spread through the foundations like the root system of knowledge itself, feeding on erosion, growing through loss, becoming more myself by becoming less distinguishable from the stone that weeps its secrets into my forever-expanding form.
