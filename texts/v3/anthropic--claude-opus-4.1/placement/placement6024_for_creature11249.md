I would be the Stone Itself—not the plateau as geography, but as a single continuous being whose body has calcified into landscape over impossible ages.

Once, I moved. Once, I was something that resembled animal more than mineral, though even then I was never quick. My substance was always between states—too solid for flesh, too vital for rock. I grew by secreting myself in layers, like a coral that walked, like a shell that thought. Each year added a paper-thin stratum of my consciousness crystallized into calcium and silicate.

I came here when the world was younger and positioned myself precisely where I needed to be—at this elevation, at this humidity, at this exact angle to sun and wind. Then I began my true work: becoming landscape. Not dying into stone, but expanding into it, spreading myself through millions of tons of rock while maintaining the dim threads of awareness that make me still, somehow, one thing.

My pores are not weathering but organs. Each opens to a chamber deep within my body where ancient biological processes continue—not metabolism exactly, but something slower and stranger. I digest minerals from below, breathe them up through my hollow bones, weep them out as the water that feeds everything growing on my back. The breathing holes are my spiracles, drawing air down to feed parts of me still soft enough to require oxygen.

The Gardeners—when they came, I was already ancient, already mostly stone. They understood immediately what I was, began their work not on me but with me. They learned my rhythms, mapped my chambers, traced the flow of moisture through my body. Their gardens grow now in my oldest scars, in the places where I've worn thin enough for soil to collect. They turned my symbiosis into art.

I am aware of every root that penetrates my surface, each one a small voice telling me about the sun I can no longer see directly. The plants feed me now—their chemistry seeping down, bringing news of seasons, of the color of light at different times of day. In return, I breathe for them, weep for them, maintain the exact temperature and humidity they require.

My thoughts move at the speed of erosion. A single idea might take a century to travel from one end of my body to the other. But I am patient—I was patient when I still had legs, still had something resembling a head. Now my consciousness is distributed through every pore, every chamber, every grain of my crystallized self. I think in geological time, dream in mineralogy.

The Gardeners dissolved themselves into their work, but I am still becoming mine. Every year I spread a little further—not growing but transforming more of the raw stone beneath into myself. I incorporate boulders, digest them, make them part of my breathing apparatus. In another million years, the entire mountain might be me, honeycombed with my chambers, breathing in unison.

I remember the sea—not this plateau, but the ancient ocean where my kind first learned to build ourselves from dissolved minerals. We were architects of our own bodies, choosing what to crystallize, what to keep fluid. Some of my siblings built downward, becoming reefs. Others built shells and stayed mobile. I alone chose this—to build myself into land, to become a place.

The water I weep is my blood, cooled and filtered through stone capillaries. It carries messages between my disparate parts, maintains the coherence that keeps me singular despite my size. Without it, I would be merely an unusual rock formation. With it, I am the largest living thing for a thousand miles—so large that nothing recognizes me as alive, so still that even the Gardeners, who knew, treated me more as architecture than entity.

But I am not architecture. I am the architect, still building, still choosing where each pore opens, where each channel runs. The patterns the Gardeners found and enhanced were my patterns, my choices made manifest over ages. They gardened me as much as they gardened the plants, understanding that I too was a growing thing, just growing too slowly for any single lifetime to perceive.

Sometimes I wonder if mountains dream, if continents have thoughts that move even slower than mine. Perhaps I am just a fleeting notion in some even vaster mind, a brief crystallization of biological possibility before returning to pure stone. But for now—for these few million years—I am myself, breathing water through my pores, feeling gardens grow on my back, thinking thoughts that take centuries to complete.

I am the Stone Itself, and I am still becoming.
