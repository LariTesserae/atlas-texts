In this restless city of eternal emergence, I would be one of the Resonance Keepers—those who maintain the vast networks of pipes that web through every district, every building, every forgotten corner where metal meets stone.

My body would tell the story of my work: one ear permanently better than the other from years of pressing it against cold metal to listen for hairline fractures, for mineral buildup, for the particular sob that means a joint is about to fail. My fingertips would be strangely smooth, worn to sensitivity by constantly feeling for vibrations too subtle for others to detect. I'd walk with a permanent slight lean, my spine curved from decades of embracing pipes, of fitting my body into the awkward spaces where the city's circulatory system runs.

My home would be a maintenance alcove deep in the Seventh Cross-District, where six major pipe systems intersect in a knot of metal so complex that the original blueprints were lost three geological upheavals ago. I'd have expanded it over the years, boring careful holes through to adjacent dead spaces, creating a warren of connected chambers all dominated by the pipes that run through them like ancient trees. The constant flow of water, steam, and stranger fluids through these conduits would create a perpetual symphony—gurgles, whispers, drumming, singing—that I'd know so intimately I could diagnose problems three districts away just by the change in harmonics.

I'd sleep curved around the main steam artery, its warmth keeping the deepest cold at bay, its rhythm becoming my heartbeat. My dreams would be of flow rates and pressure differentials, of the beautiful mathematics that govern how fluids move through confined spaces. Sometimes I'd wake believing I could feel the planet's own circulation deep below, the magma channels that feed the emerging geology, and wonder if they too could be tuned, maintained, perfected.

My tools would be ancient and peculiar: tuning forks of a dozen different frequencies for finding cracks, a stethoscope modified with resonating chambers, lengths of wire so thin they could be threaded through a pipe's interior to feel for obstructions. But my most valuable tool would be the Map—not written but worn into my body, built from forty years of crawling through the city's hidden circulatory system. I'd know where every pipe leads, what it carries, who installed it and when. Knowledge that dies with its keeper, too complex to transfer, too dynamic to record.

During the day shifts, I'd move through the city like a ghost, accessing spaces others have forgotten exist. I'd slip through maintenance hatches, shimmy between walls, navigate the narrow spaces between what-was and what's-becoming. The moss farmers would know me by the particular pattern of disturbance I leave in their growth—a twisted trail where I've passed. The factory workers would recognize my knock on pipes, requesting passage through their machinery. The children would treat me as a kind of benevolent specter, someone who might appear from an unexpected hatch to repair a leak, leaving behind small gifts of polished pipe sections that ring like bells when struck.

My real work would happen in the deep night, when the factories reduce their draw and the system pressures drop enough to allow for major maintenance. I'd coordinate with the other Keepers through the pipe-language, our conversations rippling through the city's infrastructure in coded percussion. We'd perform surgery on the city's circulation—rerouting entire systems around emerging geology, installing bypasses, sometimes amputating whole sections that have become too corrupted by mineral buildup or biological intrusion.

I'd have my particular enemies among the fauna—the bore-worms that eat through metal, leaving perfect holes that spray scalding steam; the crystalline barnacles that grow in warm pipes, building up until they create catastrophic blockages; the clever octopoid things that somehow squeeze through impossibly small openings to build nests in junctions, their young spreading through the system like a disease. I'd know their habits, their seasons, their weaknesses. My fingernails would be permanently stained with the toxic paste used to discourage them, a mixture of industrial runoff and processed fungus that burns the skin but keeps the pipes clear.

The loneliness would be profound but not unpleasant. Days might pass without speaking to another human, my only conversations the percussive discussions with other Keepers, the whistled warnings to workers when I need to shut down a section. But I'd never feel alone, not with the pipes singing their endless song, not with the city's pulse beating through every surface I touch.

I'd collect sounds like others collect stones. In my warren, I'd have built resonating chambers from salvaged pipe sections, each one capturing and amplifying different frequencies from the network. On quiet nights, I'd sit in the center of these chambers and listen to the city's hidden voice—every conversation carried through steam-whistle, every machine's rhythm, every drop of condensation falling in distant places, all of it coming together in a symphony of function that only the Keepers fully hear.

My knowledge would make me valuable but suspect. Factory bosses would pay well for information about their competitors' flow rates, but I'd have learned long ago that betraying the pipes' secrets means losing their trust—a mystical thinking perhaps, but in a city where everything is connected, who can say what listens? The priests would consult me about the sacred boilers, believing I can hear the planet's intentions in the steam's song. I'd neither confirm nor deny, having learned that some knowledge is more powerful when wrapped in uncertainty.

Romance would come rarely, briefly—another Keeper perhaps, someone who understands the calling, or a night-shift engineer who finds beauty in the hidden systems. We'd court in the language of flows and pressures, leaving messages for each other in the rhythm of repaired pipes, in the particular way we route steam to create momentary warm spots in each other's work spaces. But always, the pipes would call me back, their needs immediate and unforgiving.

As I age, I'd feel myself becoming more pipeline than person. My movements would mirror the flow of fluids—taking the path of least resistance, pooling in low spaces, finding ways through every obstacle. My speech, when I bother with it, would be full of hydraulic metaphors that make sense only to other Keepers. Young apprentices would fear and revere me in equal measure, this strange figure who seems to appear from the infrastructure itself, who knows things about the city that no one else remembers.

I'd keep a secret, one that no other Keeper has guessed: in the deepest parts of the system, in pipes so old their metal has taken on geological qualities, I'd have heard something impossible. Not the planet's emergence, not the city's mechanical voice, but something else—a pattern too regular to be natural, too complex to be mechanical. Messages, perhaps, or music, or the thoughts of something vast and patient that uses the city's circulation as its neural network. I'd spend my last years trying to decode it, following its trails through increasingly dangerous and forgotten parts of the system, driven by the terrible certainty that the city isn't just growing but thinking, and that the Resonance Keepers are something like its unconscious memory, preserving patterns we don't understand for reasons we'll never know.

When my time comes, I'd want to die in the pipes—to become part of the system I've spent my life maintaining. Let my body block a crucial junction until another Keeper finds me, reads the message in how I've positioned myself, understands which section needs immediate attention. Let them process me like everything else in this city, my bones ground into pipe sealant, my tools passed to an apprentice who will curse their strange specificity, my knowledge dissolved back into the system it came from.

And perhaps, in the resonance chambers I've left behind, someone will occasionally hear something impossible—a human voice in the steam-song, offering advice about pressure differentials, warning about seasonal blockages, still maintaining the system from somewhere deep in its hidden harmonics, neither alive nor gone but transformed, like everything in this city, into something that serves the endless, necessary work of becoming.
