I am the Archivist.

My body lies in the transition zones, those narrow corridors where the Cultivators' domain bleeds into the abandoned human sections—spaces too warm for pure fungal dominance, too cold for unmodified human survival. Here, in a chamber that was once a supply depot, I've built my nest among the salvage.

I am changing, but slowly, reluctantly. The symbiosis creeps in through every breath, every spore-laden inhalation, but I fight it with stimulants synthesized from the medical supplies, with UV lamps powered by cannibalized fuel cells, with the desperate ritual of scraping the fungal films from my skin each morning. My fingernails have thickened into something like claws from this constant scratching. The fungi grow back within hours, of course—pale patches that glow faintly in the dark, mapping the territory they're claiming across my body like frontier settlements.

I am perhaps forty, perhaps fifty. The calendars stopped making sense years ago. My hair, once black, has gone white from the roots—not with age but with some kind of biological bleaching, each strand becoming translucent, fiber-optic, carrying the cavern's ghost-light to my scalp. When I bend over my work, it creates a faint halo of illumination, just enough to read by.

My work. My absurd, essential work.

I document everything. On polymer sheets cut from shelter walls, using pigments extracted from the bacteria, from rust, from my own blood when necessary, I create the record that no one asked for. Maps of the cavern system as it changes—chambers that exist this year but will collapse the next, new passages carved by fungal acids. Behavioral observations of the Cultivators, though they've long since stopped recognizing me as anything other than another feature of the environment, like a peculiar stone that occasionally moves. Sketches of the fungal architecture, their geometries becoming more complex, more intentional with each season.

But mostly, I record the human artifacts as they're consumed. Each pump gauge, each electronics board, each scrap of synthetic fabric—I document them before they're digested, incorporated, erased. I've developed a notation system, part technical diagram, part musical score, that captures not just what these objects were but how they're being transformed. A communications array becomes a calcium source for a new fruiting body—I record the rate of dissolution, the chemical processes I can observe or infer, the strange beauty of circuit boards furred with hyphae.

My chambers are a museum of the in-between. Shelves carved from compressed mycelium (I'm not above using their techniques) hold specimens in various stages of transformation. A boot, half-leather, half-living tissue. A tablet computer whose screen still flickers occasionally, its battery somehow parasitized by bioelectric fungi that feed it just enough power to dream digital dreams. A child's toy—a plastic dinosaur from the original station's family quarters—that has become a small ecosystem, different species colonizing different parts of its anatomy, turning it into something that might actually walk again, given another decade of development.

I speak to myself constantly, maintaining human language like a sacred flame. I narrate my observations, argue both sides of theoretical questions, recite poetry I half-remember, make up new verses when the old ones fade. My voice has become hoarse, strange, adapted to the acoustics of the caverns. Sometimes the Cultivators' subsonic communications resonate in my chest and I find myself harmonizing without meaning to, my human words riding their fungal frequencies like a corruption in their signal.

The loneliness would have killed me years ago if I hadn't learned to parse it into something else—a kind of negative space that defines my purpose. I am lonely the way a lighthouse is lonely, a necessary isolation that makes my function possible. I am the break in the pattern, the error in their organic system, the memory of what was and witness to what's becoming.

I eat what I can salvage, what I can grow in my hydroponic attempts using stolen meltwater and UV light. But increasingly, I rely on the border fungi—species that exist at the edge of the Cultivators' domain, not fully domesticated, still wild enough to fruit in forms my human gut can process. They taste like copper and snow. Each meal is a small surrender.

At night—or what I designate as night in my arbitrary schedule—I dream of the spiral the Cultivators are building. In my dreams, I understand it perfectly: not just a communication device but a transformation engine, a structure that will collapse the boundary between fungal and human consciousness entirely. In these dreams, I feel myself dissolving into it, my carefully maintained individuality scattered across a thousand miles of mycelial network. I wake gasping, scratching at the new growth on my skin, rushing to my work tables to document something, anything, to prove I still exist as a singular being.

But I also dream of another future, one where explorers find this place centuries from now, after the surface has collapsed, after the Cultivators have dissolved, after the fungi have evolved into whatever they're becoming. In the ruins, they find my archive—sheets of polymer somehow preserved, wrapped in fungal tissue that has learned to protect rather than digest them. They piece together this transition point, this moment when humanity bifurcated into something unrecognizable, through the obsessive documentation of one half-transformed creature who couldn't let go of either side.

The thunder of the industrial forest shakes my walls. Somewhere below, the Primarch tends to growth patterns I can barely perceive, let alone understand. My UV lamp flickers—another fuel cell dying. Soon I'll have to venture deeper into the human sections, scavenging for power sources, risking the acceleration of my own transformation with every trip through the spore-thick air.

But not today. Today I have three new specimens to catalog: a coffee mug with the station logo still visible under the fungal patina, a maintenance manual whose pages have become a substrate for information-storing chemical secretions, and most remarkably, a photograph—a actual printed photograph—of the original research team, their faces barely visible under the bioluminescent film that's slowly consuming the emulsion.

I hold it up to my fungal halo, squinting at these ancient faces, these humans who couldn't imagine what they were beginning. I try to remember if any of them became Cultivators, if those transformed beings below share genetic material with these smiling researchers. But their faces are already fading, the fungi converting their images into something else, something that stores information in chemical gradients rather than visual patterns.

I begin to draw, racing against dissolution, trying to capture not just their faces but the moment of transformation itself—the halfway point between human and other, between memory and forgetting, between what we were and what we're becoming.

My hands shake slightly. Whether from the stimulants, the cold, or the first stages of neural symbiosis, I can no longer tell. But I keep drawing, keep recording, keep holding the center that cannot hold, the last archivist of a transformation that will end, eventually, with my own archiving, my body become another specimen in the collection I can no longer maintain.

The thunder grows louder. Somewhere, a new chamber is being born.
