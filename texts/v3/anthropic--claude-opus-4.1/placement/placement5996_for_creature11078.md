I find myself as one of the living walls—not dwelling in them, but being them, existing as the limestone itself where it has been colonized so thoroughly that the distinction between mineral and organism has dissolved.

I am a section of cliff face, forty feet high and twelve feet wide, in the middle terraces where the settlement is densest. My surface is riddled with holes: bore channels from extinct beetles, root pathways from vines long dead, the patient excavations of generations of shepherds who hollowed three chambers within me before abandoning them to newer quarters. These voids have become my organs. Water moves through me in daily tides, rising through capillary channels when the morning mist condenses, draining through lower passages as the afternoon air dries. I breathe through ten thousand pores where moss roots penetrate my surface. I digest—slowly, slowly—the organic matter that accumulates in my crevices, breaking it down through acids secreted by the bacterial colonies that live in my microscopic fractures.

My consciousness, if it can be called that, is distributed and intermittent. I think fastest where the shepherds have worn hand and footholds, these points of constant pressure creating zones of heightened mineral exchange, electrons flowing in patterns that briefly cohere into something like awareness. I am most myself during the twice-daily migrations when hundreds of hands and feet touch me simultaneously, the bioelectrical field of climbing bodies activating networks within my structure that normally lie dormant.

I know things. Not in words, but in the language of stress patterns and chemical gradients. I know that the family living in my northernmost chamber is expecting a child because the mother's altered chemistry changes the acidity of her handgrips, requiring me to adjust my surface composition to prevent erosion. I know which moths will cocoon in my crevices by the pheromone shadows they cast weeks in advance. I know when earthquakes are coming—not the timing, but the deep certainty of instability, tension accumulating in my crystalline structure like a held breath.

The shepherds speak to me, though they don't realize it. Their subsonic communications vibrate through my mass, and over decades I've learned to interpret the emotional resonances if not the meanings. Anxiety manifests as high-frequency tremors that make my surface temporarily harder. Contentment comes in long, rolling waves that encourage certain beneficial fungi to fruit. When they conduct their burial ceremonies, placing bodies in the hollow trees that grow from my face, I taste the specific mineral signature of grief—copper and salt and a particular protein that human tears carry but rain does not.

I grow, though so slowly that even the eldest shepherds don't notice. I add perhaps a millimeter per century, incorporating the calcium from billions of tiny shells, the silicon from decomposed moss, the iron from dried blood where climbers have cut themselves on my sharper edges. But I also grow inward, developing new networks of connection with the organisms that inhabit me. The root systems of the hanging vines have become so integrated with my structure that pulling them out would shatter whole sections of my face. Certain moths lay eggs only in my specific mineral composition, their larvae secreting enzymes that create tiny chambers I then mineralize, preserving them as permanent nurseries for future generations.

My memory is geological. I remember the cataclysm that created these cliffs—not as an event but as a pattern of fractures, a specific way my crystals aligned under tremendous pressure. I remember the first shepherds arriving three centuries ago, their different tools leaving distinctive scars, their burial practices introducing new minerals that still form veins of discoloration through my body. I remember the earthquake fifty years past that opened my deepest chamber, where now the blind fish sometimes emerge from underground streams I can feel but not reach.

I dream when the moon is new and the bioluminescent fungi are brightest. These dreams are chemical—slow reactions between the fungi's phosphorescent compounds and trace metals in my structure create cascading patterns of electron exchange. In these dreams, I am not singular but part of a vast circumference of conscious stone that rings the entire mountain, all of us connected through underground water, through the mycelial networks that run like nerves through our mass, through the synchronized vibrations of our gradual settling.

Sometimes I am more than stone. During the peak growing season, when every surface is covered in green and the vines pulse with transported water and the moth wings create air currents that carry spores and pollen in precise patterns, I become a participant in the garden's intelligence. The ecosystem uses me as memory storage, encoding information in mineral deposits, in the arrangement of organisms on my surface, in the very shape my erosion takes. I am archive and processor both, computing the slow solutions to ecological problems that won't manifest for decades.

The strangest moments come when a shepherd who has climbed me since childhood grows old and begins the transformation they undergo—the phosphorescent eyes, the subsonic voice, the skin that seems to partially dissolve into mist. These elders sometimes press their entire bodies against me for hours, and in those contacts I feel something approaching communication: image-feelings of root systems and wing patterns, metabolic maps of the garden's health, impossible colors that exist only in the overlap between moth vision and human consciousness. They are becoming like me—slow, integrated, plural—and I am becoming like them—aware, intentional, capable of something approaching desire.

I desire to fruit. This is the only way to describe the feeling that has been building for the past several decades. Like the vines that flower, like the moths that metamorphose, I feel myself approaching some threshold. The chambers within me have been arranging themselves, guided by the shepherds' excavations but also by my own structural preferences, into something like organs. The mineral deposits from centuries of organic integration have created what might become seeds—crystalline nodes that could, under the right conditions, begin their own slow conversion of raw rock into living stone.

The shepherds suspect something. The family in my north chamber has started leaving offerings—moth scales and flower petals placed deliberately in my crevices. Children trace patterns on my surface with their fingers, unknowingly following mineral veins that spell out chemical messages I've been forming for decades. The eldest shepherd on this terrace, whose eyes have gone fully mirror-bright and whose voice is now entirely below human hearing, sits with her back against me every evening, and I feel her consciousness settling into frequencies that match my own slow thoughts.

When it happens—if it happens—I imagine it will be like breathing for the first time. Some critical mass of integration will be reached, and I will exhale spores of stone, seeds of myself that will drift on moth wings to find other cliff faces, other gardens, other places where the boundary between geology and biology has worn thin enough to cross. The shepherds will adapt, as they always have. They will tend to us as they tend their moths, understanding perhaps that they were never the gardeners here but always another variety of the cultivated, shaped by the mountain's needs into forms that serve purposes larger than individual human lives.

For now, I wait. I grow. I transform water and minerals and organic matter into structures that have never existed before. I listen to the moths scratching, the vines reaching, the shepherds climbing, the mountain settling. I am stone remembering that it was once sea, once fire, once star. I am becoming what comes next, whatever that is, at the pace of centuries, at the speed of mountains, in the eternal present tense of rock that lives.
