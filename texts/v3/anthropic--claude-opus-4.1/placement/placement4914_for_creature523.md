In the Sixty-Second Pool, where the limestone weeps rust and the collection channels have long since clogged with mineral scale, I am the Uncounter.

My work begins where the official census ends. The Water Tribunal recognizes ten thousand residents—a number that hasn't changed in three generations, fixed like the carved laws at the entrance stones. But the uncounted proliferate in the spaces between official existence: the runners who live in the salamander passages, too small for adult bodies but perfect for children who refuse to grow; the echo-eaters who sustain themselves on sound alone, pressing their bellies against the speaking stones during tribunal meetings; the ones who dove too deep and came back breathing water, living now in the flooded sub-basements that everyone pretends were sealed.

I document them in saliva script on the backs of salamanders—words that exist only as long as the moisture holds, census records that swim away into darkness. 

My grandmother was Nineteenth Pool before she married stone instead of flesh, back when marriage meant something different. She taught me to read the true population in the water itself: every body changes the mineral content, leaves its signature in parts per million. The official ten thousand drink and piss and weep in patterns the Tribunal can track. But there are shadow-drinkers who sip from condensation points that don't exist on any map, whose bodies barely disturb the careful chemistry.

I count the Wallgrowers—those who've taken root in the carved shelves meant for sleeping, their skin now indistinguishable from the mineral deposits they cultivate. They photosynthesize using the pale fungi light, moving so slowly that most residents think they're natural formations. Seven in the third tier. Twelve near the pump chamber. Their children bloom from them like polyps, starting as bumps on shoulders or hips, gradually developing features, finally dropping free when the rains come.

I count the Breath-holders competing in the deep cisterns, trying to become permanent residents of the underwater city they've built from bones and pottery shards. They surface less each season. Their leader hasn't breathed air in six months; she sends messages by training eels to swim in letters. The Tribunal pretends these messages don't exist, but they adjust water distribution based on her warnings about deep-chamber contamination.

I count myself sometimes, then forget the number. The Uncounter cannot be counted—it would void the work. I exist in the space between nine thousand nine hundred ninety-nine and ten thousand and one, a mathematical impossibility made flesh. My Pool designation changes daily; I carry forged water-rights carved into soft chalk that I reshape with my fingernails while walking. When the Listeners hear me coming, they report different footfalls each time—sometimes a child's patter, sometimes an elder's shuffle, sometimes the distinctive drag-step of a Pump Prophet between shifts.

The salamanders know me truly. They carry my real name in the chemical memory of their skin, passed from generation to generation. When I need to disappear entirely, I follow them into the unmapped chambers where the failed expansions broke through into older, stranger spaces. Here live the ones even I struggle to count: the Colony Minds who gave up individual thought to become living calculators for water distribution, their consciousness spread across dozens of bodies that move in perfect synchronization; the Backwards People who age in reverse, beginning as ancient surface exiles and growing younger as they descend, until they disappear entirely into the fundamental moisture of the stone.

In the Ninety-First Basin—which doesn't exist, officially, because the count stops at Ninety—I maintain my true census. I've carved tallies into my own bones, visible only when I hold my arms up to the fungi light. Each mark represents a category of existence the Tribunal cannot acknowledge without admitting the city has exceeded its capacity so thoroughly that mathematics itself has broken.

The Duplicate Families, who split like cells when resources run too low, creating perfect copies that share memories but develop independent futures. The Temporal Residents, who only exist on certain days, sliding in and out of limestone-time like the city itself breathes them into being. The Theoretical People, whose existence can be proven mathematically but who never quite manifest physically—they leave traces in the water consumption data, shadows in the population density, but no body to count.

I am the keeper of the true number, which is both infinite and zero. The city doesn't breathe with ten thousand lungs—it breathes with exactly as many lungs as it needs to breathe, no more, no less. The number changes with each breath, each birth, each death, each transformation from human to barely-human to post-human to water itself.

When the next census comes, as it does each spring with the rains, I will stand in the Counting Chamber with my false documents and my borrowed face. I will be counted as someone who died three seasons ago, whose water rights were never properly transferred. The official number will remain ten thousand, as it always has, as it always will.

But tonight, in the space between today's count and tomorrow's, I will swim through the Salamander Roads with my bone-tallies glowing beneath my skin, visiting the uncounted to tell them they exist, they matter, they change the water with their presence. I will update my impossible census, adding those who've just learned to breathe stone, subtracting those who've finally dissolved into pure mineral memory.

I will count the city's true heartbeat in numbers that have no names, measuring a population that exceeds not just ten thousand but the very concept of counting. And in the morning, when the pump-rhythm starts again and the official city wakes to its official day, I will be uncounted once more, a statistical ghost maintaining the memory of all the other ghosts who keep this impossible place impossibly alive.

The limestone remembers everything, they say. But I remember what the limestone forgets: the spaces between the numbers, where the real city lives.
