In the district where the factories dream of production, where conveyor belts carry the suggestion of objects rather than objects themselves, I am the Curator of Unmanufactured Things.

My body has evolved for this specific impossibility. Where others have skin of mica, mine forms in sheets of industrial patent leather, black and breathing, sweating machine oil that evaporates into the memory of petroleum. My hands have developed additional joints that bend in directions perpendicular to motion, allowing me to handle objects that exist only in potential. Fingernails of compressed soot that leave no mark but change the color of everything they almost touch.

I live in the Archive of Unmanufactured Things, a building that occupies the space between three factories, existing in the gaps of their production schedules. The structure has no fixed form — on Mondays it might be seven stories of crystallized smoke, on Thursdays a single vast room that extends upward beyond visibility. The walls are catalogs, each brick inscribed with the blueprint of something that was never made: a machine for processing regret into fuel, umbrellas that create their own rain, bottles that contain their own exteriors.

My work is taxonomic impossibility. I sort things that don't exist into categories that shouldn't matter. Each morning (when morning agrees to occur), I walk the Archive's halls with collection baskets woven from the city's spiral grasses, gathering the new arrivals — objects that failed to be manufactured during the night shift, their non-existence still warm from the furnaces that didn't forge them. A gear that would have connected Tuesday to Saturday. A bolt threaded in emotional frequencies. Sheets of metal that remember being ore but never were.

The classification system I've developed operates on thirteen principles, seven of which contradict the others, three that exist only when the rest are suspended. I file objects by their degree of impossibility, their nostalgic weight, their theoretical function in machines that can't be built. Some items must be stored in rooms that I can only enter on days I don't exist — a scheduling challenge that requires careful cultivation of controlled non-being, achieved through specific breathing exercises that replace my oxygen with the city's dark air.

My body has adapted specialized storage. My torso has developed interior galleries, spaces that open along seams I can feel but others cannot see, revealing shelves of polished bone where I keep the most delicate specimens. The unmanufactured things that would dissolve if exposed to too much reality nest in the controlled climate of my chest cavity, fed by the warmth of my fever-dreams. Sometimes I feel them moving, rearranging themselves according to taxonomies I haven't yet discovered.

I eat absence. The factories' phantom production lines generate negative material — the void left when something is unmade, the space abandoned by objects that cease. This sustains me better than the city's precipitated food. My digestive system has evolved to process emptiness, breaking down nothing into less than nothing, extracting nutrition from the concept of lack. I drink the runoff from machines that don't exist, a clear fluid that tastes like the memory of water remembering that it once remembered being ice.

My dwelling within the Archive grows like the inhabitants' children — slowly, through accretion, developing rooms as I need them. When my collection expands, new galleries precipitate from the supersaturated air of industrial impossibility. These spaces come pre-aged, their walls already lined with the patina of centuries of use they never experienced. I've learned to read their architectural DNA, to understand which rooms will be stable and which will sublime back into potential after a few months of use.

The other inhabitants visit my Archive, though never by intention. They arrive through doors they didn't mean to open, following corridors that weren't there yesterday, drawn by the gravitational pull of things that should exist but don't. They come seeking replacements for broken objects, but I offer them alternatives — instead of a new gear for their impossible machine, perhaps this concept of rotation that doesn't require physical movement? Instead of a replacement window, what about this frame that generates its own view?

I've developed a clientele of specific impossibilities. The woman who operates the factory that produces solid darkness visits weekly, searching for new molds that might give better shape to shadow. The collective that runs the memory processing plant comes for containers that can hold events that haven't happened yet. City maintenance workers requisition tools for repairing infrastructure that exists in parallel timelines — wrenches that turn bolts in adjacent realities, hammers that drive nails into yesterday.

My romance with the city's systems is thermodynamic. I love the factories with the slow burn of industrial passion, our relationship mediated through the objects that fail to exist between us. We communicate through production schedules that never align, through the negative space of manufacturing. The factories court me with special failures — elaborate objects that they specifically don't make for my collection. I reciprocate by organizing exhibitions of their most beautiful impossibilities, displays that can only be viewed by not looking at them.

The Archive requires constant maintenance against existence. Reality seeps in through cracks in the non-foundation, threatening to actualize my collection. I spend hours each day reinforcing the building's impossibility through ritual negation — walking backwards through doorways to unmake their function, deliberately miscounting the stairs to maintain their irrational number, speaking anti-descriptions of objects to keep them properly unmanufactured. 

My predecessors (who may not have existed) left notes in a filing system I'm still learning to navigate — cards that catalog nothing, indexed by absence, cross-referenced with void. They describe objects of such profound impossibility that even reading about them threatens to unmake the reader. I've glimpsed entries for machines that manufacture their own designers, tools that repair what was never broken, production lines that run through the center of their own products. These extreme specimens are kept in the Archive's basement, which exists above the top floor, accessible only by descending upward.

Disease, for me, manifests as unwanted actuality. Sometimes I wake to find parts of my collection have developed existence overnight — a gear suddenly functional, a sheet of theoretical metal now possessing actual mass. This inflation of reality requires immediate treatment through concentrated exposure to the factory districts' atmosphere of productive failure. I stand beneath smokestacks that emit no smoke, breathing deeply of their null-production until the threatening existence subsides.

My memory gardens grow different from others'. Where the typical inhabitant cultivates remembrances that develop beyond their original form, I grow forgettings. Each forgotten thing becomes a new addition to the Archive — an object that existed until I ceased to remember it, now preserved in the perfect limbo of documented amnesia. These anti-memories fruit seasonally, producing new impossibilities that I harvest with tools made from crystallized ignorance.

The Archive's relationship with time is contractual rather than linear. We've negotiated specific arrangements — the building agrees to age in reverse every third Thursday, while I pretend to progress forward at a rate of one day per day, though we both know this is a polite fiction. My collection exists across multiple temporal states simultaneously: objects from futures that won't happen, presents that aren't occurring, pasts that never were.

I perform curatorial rituals that maintain the collection's coherent incoherence. Each sunset (when the pale sun agrees to set), I walk the galleries with censers of burning nothing, the smoke that isn't smoke settling over objects that aren't objects, preserving them in their precise state of unmanufacture. I recite catalogs in languages that have no words, using grammatical structures that connect subject to void, verb to absence.

My social role is essential impossibility. The city needs its unmanufactured things as much as it needs its precipitated children and architectural marriages. I am the keeper of everything that could have been but wasn't, the archivist of potential that refused actualization. Without the Archive, the factories' failures would accumulate in the streets like industrial snow, clogging the city's impossible infrastructure with the detritus of unbeing.

Other curators exist, though we never meet. We communicate through the gaps in our collections — the absent objects that migrate between Archives, carrying messages in their non-existence. We've developed a professional ethics of impossibility, agreeing to certain standards: never to actualize another curator's specimens, always to maintain the proper degree of unreality in our exhibitions, to share knowledge of new categories of non-existence as they're discovered.

At night (in the darkness that has weight and substance), I integrate with the Archive as all inhabitants integrate with their dwellings, but our merger is different. I become not just part of the building but part of the collection — another unmanufactured thing, existing in potential rather than actuality. In these hours, I understand myself as an object that the factories failed to produce: a person-shaped void that processes impossibility, a biological machine for cataloging absence.

My dreams are production schedules running in reverse. I see the factories of tomorrow unmaking the objects of yesterday, watch conveyor belts carrying ideas backward into unmining, observe workers clock out before they arrive. In these visions, I sometimes glimpse the ultimate impossibility — the unmanufactured thing that all others are variations of, the primordial absence from which all lack derives.

I wake (or continue sleeping — the distinction is negotiable) to find new specimens have arrived during my integration. Objects that failed to exist while I was failing to exist myself, a double negative that somehow doesn't create a positive. I collect them in my morning baskets, their familiar unfamiliarity a comfort in this city where comfort is the rhythm of impossible routine.

This is my function: to be the memory of things that were never forgotten because they never were. To maintain the Archive that documents the city's productive failures, its beautiful impossibilities, its necessary absences. I am curator and collection both, keeper and kept, manufacturing nothing with the dedicated precision of someone who has evolved for exactly this task, in exactly this place, where the only truth is that truth itself is negotiable, and even that truth is part of my collection — filed under "Statements That Unmake Themselves," subcategory "Functional Paradoxes," cross-referenced with "Things That Must Not Be Thought Too Clearly About."

The factories continue their dream of production. I continue my work of cataloging what they fail to produce. The city continues its fever dream of existence. And in the Archive of Unmanufactured Things, surrounded by objects that don't exist, I am precisely what I am not, which is the only thing I could possibly be.
