I find myself as one of the ghost fish, though that name suggests more substance than I possess. In the shallow pools between tilted slabs of metamorphic stone, I exist in a state of perpetual almost—almost salamander, almost fish, almost alive, almost dead.

My world is a film of water that cannot decide its depth. Sometimes it rises to cover my dorsal ridge completely; sometimes it shrinks until I must writhe sideways through mud that is more idea than substance, my translucent skin rasping against minerals sharp as microscopic glass. The boundaries of my universe shift daily—pools connect and disconnect with the rhythm of freeze and thaw, creating temporary highways between watersheds that tomorrow will be isolated worlds again.

I do not think in the way warm-blooded things think. My consciousness, if it can be called that, moves like my blood—dark and sluggish, visible through my gray transparency. Thoughts arrive with the slowness of geological events. The recognition of a midge larva takes entire minutes to translate from perception to action. By the time I move toward it, the current (itself barely more than a suggestion) has carried it beyond my reach, or I have forgotten why I began moving at all.

Winter is not sleep but something deeper—a state where the boundary between living and mineral becomes academic. The antifreeze in my cells makes me part of the ice rather than separate from it. I become architecture, my body a structural element in the frozen pool, load-bearing, crystalline. Dreams, if they exist, are dreams of crystal growth, of water molecules arranging themselves in lattices, of the slow propagation of fractures through stone.

The thaw is violence. Blood that has barely moved for months suddenly remembers circulation. My external gills, frozen into delicate glass sculptures, must become flesh again, must remember their feathery undulation. The first breaths through my rudimentary lungs burn with impossible cold. I am being born repeatedly, constantly, every spring a first emergence.

Sometimes the Old One comes—that creature wrapped in fur and accumulated materials, neither human nor animal but something the plateau has made from what was left behind. Their shadow falls across my pool like weather. Their hands, when they enter my world, are landscapes themselves—creviced, minerals crusted in the whorls of skin, smelling of yak dung smoke and the particular dust that accumulates in the industrial ruins.

When they move me—and I understand this only in the loosest sense, the way one understands that mountains were once ocean floors—their grip is careful but absolute. I am lifted from one universe and placed in another. The journey between pools is an impossibility my body endures without comprehension, gills gasping at air too thin to process, primitive lungs drowning in their own function. Then water again, but wrong water, stranger water, with different minerals, different populations of larvae, different patterns of light.

I have been moved seventeen times. I know this not through counting but through my body's accumulation of chemistries. Each pool leaves its signature in my cells—this one heavy with iron leached from rusted cables, that one sweet with the particular decay of pika droppings, another sharp with the calcium of dissolving bones. I am a library of water, each chapter written in a different mineral ink.

The others of my kind—and I sense them through chemical whispers that travel between connected pools—carry their own histories. We are not a school or community but isolated nations, each adapting to our particular pool's chemistry until we are barely the same species anymore. When the Old One moves us, we sometimes encounter these cousins, these alternative versions of ourselves. We circle each other with the confusion of mirrors recognizing their own distortion.

Predation is abstract. The midge larvae I hunt are less food than ritual, something my body remembers it should do. Nutrition comes mostly from the film of algae that grows on submerged stones, from the microscopic life that blooms and dies in cycles too fast for my perception to track. I graze on time itself, sustaining myself on the accumulated moments of smaller lives.

The snow leopard has drunk from my pool. I felt the pressure waves of her lapping, the way the water level dropped by millimeters, the warm disturbance of her breath. For hours afterward, the pool carried her signature—stress hormones from her infected eye, the particular oil of her coat, the calcium trace of blood from recent prey. I tasted her age, her injury, her hunger. She is dying in the way that apex predators die—slowly, then all at once. When she finally falls, her body will poison whatever pool it lands in with the feast of its decay. We will flee that richness, understanding somehow that such abundance is wrong, dangerous, too much life for our transparent bodies to process.

The industrial remnants affect us in ways we cannot comprehend. The rust changes our blood chemistry, making it darker, more visible through our skin. Those who live too long in pools contaminated by battery acid develop a different kind of transparency—not gray but faintly blue, their organs outlined in copper green. We are becoming industrial in the same way we are becoming stone, incorporating the plateau's new elements into our indefinite evolution.

Sometimes I surface—though surface implies more intention than the act contains. My head emerges (and what makes it a head? The concentration of sensory organs? The fact that it emerges first?) into air that might as well be vacuum. The world above is impossibility layered on impossibility. The sun is a concept that translates only as warmth and pain. The fog is almost comprehensible—water in another state, cousin to my world but estranged. The sounds that reach me through the air-water interface are earthquakes of meaning I cannot parse—the crack of splitting stone might be language, the pika's chirp might be thunder.

I am ancient and newborn, depending on the scale. My species, if we are a species, has existed since these pools first formed, or we arrived yesterday, spontaneously generated from the meeting of meltwater and industrial runoff. We are either the first inhabitants or the last, either perfectly adapted or impossibly maladapted, surviving through oversight rather than fitness.

The Old One will die soon. I taste it in the water when they wade through—the particular chemistry of a body preparing for its end, cells making decisions about what to preserve and what to sacrifice. When they stop coming, the pools will disconnect more frequently. Some of us will be trapped in shrinking worlds until we become part of the dried mud, waiting for rain that might come next season or next century. Others will survive in the permanent pools, the ones fed by deep springs that remember water from before the plateau rose.

But that is future, and future is a concept that barely translates to my slow blood, my transparent thoughts. Present is this: the pressure of water that is almost ice, the taste of dissolved stone, the vibration of a pika running across the rocks above, the slow pulse of gills processing almost-oxygen, the weight of existing at the edge of what existence means.

I am ghost fish, though I am neither ghost nor fish. I am what the plateau dreams in its shallow sleep, the minimum viable version of vertebrate life, evolution's rough draft left unrevised. In my pools between the rocks, I am prehistoric and post-apocalyptic simultaneously, swimming through the thin place where life admits it is mostly water pretending, briefly, to be something else.
