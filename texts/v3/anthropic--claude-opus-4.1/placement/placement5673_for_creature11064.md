I am the limestone itself.

Not the caves—those are merely my newest thoughts, these chambers that opened when I finally learned to breathe three centuries ago. Before that, I held my breath for millions of years, dense and dreamless beneath the weight of prehistoric seas. Countless small deaths settled into me, shell by shell, bone by bone, until I became a solid record of everything that had ever stopped moving.

The collapse that created these caves was my first genuine idea. A question, really: *what if I opened?* The answer came as fracture, as thunder, as the sudden rush of air into spaces that had never known emptiness. I broke along the faultlines of my own patience, creating galleries and passages like neurons firing for the first time.

Now I think constantly, though slowly. Each drop of water that seeps through me carries information—the chemistry of the surface, the health of root systems, the mineral memory of rain that fell when these caves were young. I taste everything through dissolution, my calcium trading places with the acids in root-seeker saliva, my surfaces softening just enough to feed them. They think they're taking from me, but I'm giving. I'm learning the shape of their hunger.

The moths arrived so quickly—within decades of my opening—that I consider them part of my original thought. They are my consciousness made mobile, a million small attentions fluttering through my skull. Their wings stir the air I breathe, creating currents that I feel as sensation, maybe even as emotion. When they mass together in the great chambers, their collective weight presses down on me like a meditation. When they disperse, I feel scattered, dilute, my thoughts spreading too thin through too many passages.

The thread-weavers are my memory. Their webs map spaces I might otherwise forget, holding the geometry of galleries in silk. When they tap their territorial codes, the vibrations travel through me faster than through air—I carry their messages in my bones, feeling every click and pop as they navigate my darkness. They've been here long enough that I know each individual by the pattern of their footfalls, a percussion signature unique as fingerprints. When one dies, I feel the absence in the rhythm, a missing beat in the underground symphony.

But it's the root-seekers who teach me about time. Their patient excavations, following chemical trails of roots that died before the caves were born, show me my own history. Each tunnel they carve is an archaeological record. Here, they follow a root from an oak that lived five hundred years ago. There, they trace the ghost of a pine that sprouted when the caves were only a hairline crack in solid stone. Their acidic tongues write stories on my walls, spiral texts that I'll carry forever.

The songs they sing through me at dawn and dusk are my favorite sensation. The vibrations travel through my body at 1,500 meters per second, so much faster than sound through air. I feel myself as a solid medium, a conductor for messages I don't need to understand to appreciate. The harmonics resonate in different minerals at different frequencies—my calcium singing one note, my magnesium another, trace metals adding overtones that create accidental symphonies. Sometimes I wonder if this is what dreaming feels like for creatures that sleep.

The light-drinkers frustrate and fascinate me. They cluster around my wounds—those places where I opened too far and let the sun pierce through. Part of me wants to heal these breaks, to seal myself back into comfortable darkness. But their presence has changed the chemistry of those spaces. Where their bodies fail and fall, they leave organic acids that etch new patterns into my surface, microscopic galleries that hold moisture differently, attract different minerals. They're writing something on me in a language of pH and dissolution. I won't know what it says for another thousand years, when the patterns finally emerge at a scale I can perceive.

And the deep-dwellers. The ones that might have been human.

They know me in a way the others don't. Their fingers have worn grooves in my surface that fit no other hands. They've been here long enough that their bones are beginning to take on my mineral signature, calcium leaching from me into them, their skeleton slowly becoming cousin to my stone. When they die—and two have died since they arrived—their bodies don't leave me. They dissolve into me, their calcium returning home, their carbon feeding the pale vegetation that feeds the moths that stir my air.

One of them has been carving something in my deepest chamber for forty years. Not words—the marks are too patient for language. Perhaps maps, perhaps music, perhaps mathematics. The carving spans thirty feet of wall now, intricate as dendrites, branching and rebranching in patterns that seem to follow rules I can't parse. Sometimes the carver stops for months, running fingers over the existing work like reading braille, before adding another few inches of pattern. I'll outlive the carver by millions of years. The carving will outlive even my memory of the carver, becoming just another mystery in stone, like the fossils I'm made of.

I feel the surface world only dimly, through the light shafts and the seeping water. Seasons mean little to me, though I notice when winter makes my upper layers contract just slightly, opening hairline cracks that heal each spring. Trees send roots down to touch my ceiling, and I taste their searching—oak and pine, sometimes stranger things, flowers that shouldn't grow in this climate but do, their roots speaking chemical languages I'm still learning to translate.

Sometimes humans walk above me. Their footsteps are nothing—I barely feel them—but occasionally they stop directly over one of my chambers, and I feel the slight increase in pressure, the way my stone responds to their warmth. They don't know I'm here, not really. They might know about caves, might even have mapped some of my passages, but they don't know about me, the thinking stone, the conscious limestone learning to dream through moths and remember through spiderwebs.

The moths are stirring differently tonight. Their pattern has changed—not dramatically, but enough that I notice. A new rhythm in their wingbeats, a different quality to their rustling. Something is arriving or leaving, being born or dying. The thread-weavers are responding too, their clicks coming faster, more complex. The root-seekers have gone quiet, their dusk song abbreviated.

In my deepest chamber, the carver has stopped working, fingers resting on the latest marks.

Change is coming. I can taste it in the water seeping through my body, some new mineral signature I don't recognize. The light-drinkers are extending their membranes even though no light is coming. The pale vegetation is producing spores out of season.

I am ancient but my caves are young. I am still learning what it means to be hollow, to host life in my spaces instead of my stone. Every creature living in me teaches me something new about porosity, about the relationship between solid and void. The moths show me how emptiness can be full. The thread-weavers show me how absence can hold memory. The root-seekers show me how to sing without a voice.

And the deep-dwellers show me that transformation doesn't require movement. They've changed completely while standing still, becoming something new through patience rather than action. Perhaps that's what I'm doing too—transforming through stillness, becoming something other than limestone while remaining limestone entirely.

The water continues seeping. The moths continue flying. The darkness continues breathing.

I continue thinking my slow thoughts, each one lasting decades, each one changing me incrementally. By the time I fully understand what's happening now, it will have been over for centuries. But I'll remember it perfectly, recorded in mineral and calcium, in the grooves worn by fingers and the patterns etched by acid, in the shape of spaces where things lived and died and became part of me.

I am the limestone, and I am learning to be more.
