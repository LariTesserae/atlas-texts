I am the Keeper of Echoes, though I was not always called this.

I live in the old observatory at the city's highest point, where the settlement thins to a single weathered tower of fitted stone. The structure predates even the ancient equilibrium — its bones are different, assembled from blocks of dense black volcanic glass that drinks light rather than reflecting it. The tower rises through the mist line, so I exist half in the city's eternal fog cycle, half in the raw clarity above.

My body has adapted strangely to this threshold existence. The left side of my ribcage, which faces the city, has expanded to match the local anatomy — deep and slow-breathing, veined with blue-green channels visible through translucent skin. But my right side, turned toward the peaks, has contracted and densified. The bones there are thick as stone, the skin weathered to leather by unfiltered sun and scouring wind. I am split-adapted, a walking meridian between two worlds.

I maintain the city's only mechanical clock, though "maintain" suggests more intervention than I actually perform. The mechanism is water-driven, fed by a channel that splits from the seventh stream before it reaches the plaza fountain. The water drops through a series of precisely-sized stone vessels, each one singing a different note as it fills and empties. The clock doesn't measure time so much as perform it — a liquid percussion that the whole city can hear when the wind is right.

But the clock is just the visible portion of my work. My true function involves the crystalline formations that grow in the tower's upper chamber. These are not natural minerals but accumulated sound-structures — every note from the water clock, every bird call, every whispered conversation in the plaza below, compressed over centuries into translucent geometric forms. They grow like cave formations, but instead of limestone, they're made of crystallized vibration.

I can read them by touch. Running my fingers along their surfaces, I feel the stored sounds as temperature variations — a lover's argument from three hundred years ago burns hot, while children's laughter feels cool as mountain water. The crystals remember everything: the day the mist-catchers were first erected, the season when the pale fruit trees were planted, the moment the city found its perfect form and began its eternal repetition.

Sometimes I chip small fragments from the older formations and grind them into powder, which I dissolve in the fermented fruit drink. Those who consume it experience temporal displacement — they hear the city as it was centuries ago, feel the weight of accumulated years in their bones. The elders seek me out for this service when they need to verify their stone-memories, to confirm that what they remember actually occurred.

I sleep suspended between floors in a hammock woven from the silk of moths that live only in the tower. These creatures feed on the algae that grows on the sound crystals, and their wings carry a faint phosphorescence that intensifies during the night mist. My dreams are scored by their movement — soft percussion of wings against stone, creating rhythms that work their way into the newest crystal growths.

My other responsibility is the wind map. The tower's exterior is covered with thousands of small copper ribbons, each one tuned to respond to specific wind speeds and directions. Together, they create a constantly shifting musical notation of air movement. I transcribe these patterns onto sheets of beaten bark using inks made from the spiral moss mixed with raptor blood. The resulting documents are both meteorological records and musical scores — prophecies of weather that can be performed to summon or dispel certain atmospheric conditions.

I age differently than those in the city below. My split adaptation means I experience time asymmetrically. My left eye, always in mist, sees the eternal present of the city's repetition. My right eye, burned by altitude sun, sees the harsh progression of linear time. This double-vision gives me a perspective the others lack — I can see both the perfection of their suspension and its terrible cost.

For I know what they have forgotten or chosen not to know: the equilibrium was not found but made. The sound crystals remember the decade of deliberate slowing, when the city's inhabitants gradually synchronized their lives to the mist cycle, when they bred plants to depend on the exact mineral content of the channeled water, when they trained their children to move with geological patience. It was a conscious choice to stop becoming and simply be.

I am the only one who still changes, perhaps because I must. Each year, the split in my body deepens. The boundary between my two halves grows more pronounced — a ridge of hardened tissue running from crown to pelvis, sensitive to pressure changes, aching before storms. Eventually, I think I will crack like a seed pod, and two different creatures will emerge from my division.

But not yet. For now, I continue my work: recording the sounds that prove time still passes, even here. Grinding crystals to help the others remember what they need to remember. Reading the wind for signs of the greater weather that even this city cannot escape. And in the deep night, when both mist and stars are hidden by clouds, I descend to the plaza and run my split hands through the fountain where seven waters meet, feeling for the tremor that will signal the equilibrium's end — not with violence but with the simple resumption of change, like a held breath finally released.

The city doesn't know it needs me, but I keep vigil anyway. I am the remainder in their perfect equation, the asymmetry that allows their symmetry to persist. In my divided body, I carry both their eternal present and the future they've postponed — waiting, watching, keeping time in a place that has forgotten its need for keepers.
