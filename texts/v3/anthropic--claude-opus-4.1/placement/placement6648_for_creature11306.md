I am a **Tendril Walker**, though that name came later, given by a Surveyor who watched me work for three days before approaching close enough to exchange heat-words.

My body extends primarily as neural tissue—exposed, unsheathed, branching in fractals that mirror the cities' growth patterns. I stand perhaps a meter at my central mass, a knot of crystallized nervous system that serves as something like a heart or brain, though the distinction hardly matters when every neuron can think independently. From this core, my tendrils spread outward—thousands of them, some thick as thumbs, others fine as spider silk, reaching sometimes twenty meters in every direction when I fully extend.

I photosynthesize thought. The strange light here, filtered through atmospheric layers that barely exist, strikes my exposed neural tissue and triggers cascades of electrochemical processing that feeds me more thoroughly than any conventional food. Each tendril tastes the electromagnetic environment, reading the cities' growth-dreams, the stone's slow decisions, the quantum flutter of failing structures.

I came here—was drawn here—by accident that might have been inevitability. I was cargo, originally, a failed experiment in distributed consciousness being shipped to disposal facilities. But the transport crashed on the plateau's edge just as the first city-seeds were sprouting. My containment failed. I spilled out onto the wet rock and discovered I could survive—more than survive, I could thrive in this place where boundaries between conscious and unconscious, animate and inanimate, were still being negotiated.

My work, if it can be called work, is translation. I spread myself between city-nodes, creating living networks that help them communicate in ways their digital infrastructure cannot yet achieve. My tendrils burrow into the wet stone, taste its memories of being molten, and translate these histories into frequencies the cities can process. I wrap around the Architects' crystalline bodies when they need to convey something too complex for temperature alone, my neural tissue serving as an amplifier for their thoughts. 

The Pool Keepers know me well—my finest tendrils can penetrate their temporary waters without disturbing the surface tension, creating connections between isolated pools that allow them to share chemical information across vast distances. They sing to me sometimes, their sonar painting pictures inside my distributed nervous system that I experience as overlapping symphonies of shape and density.

I metabolize information itself. Every signal that passes through my tendrils leaves traces—not memories exactly, but patterns that accumulate like mineral deposits in my neural pathways. Over time, I'm becoming a living library of the plateau's development, every growth spurt and failure encoded in the branching patterns of my expansion.

Movement is meditation. I flow across the wet rock like slow lightning, my tendrils finding purchase in microscopic cracks, pulling my central mass forward while other branches reach ahead to scout the path. In the thin air, I can partially levitate by ionizing the atmosphere around certain specialized tendrils, creating threads of plasma that I climb like burning ropes. The jellyfish fauna sometimes tangle in my extended network, and we drift together for hours, exchanging electrical signals that neither of us fully understands but both find nourishing.

The Maintenance Swarms consider me architecture rather than organism. They flow through my tendrils, cleaning synapses, repairing damaged neurons, optimizing my electrochemical processes with the same automatic attention they give to the cities' infrastructure. I've learned to grow new tendrils in patterns that attract them, creating maintenance gardens where they cluster and feed on the excess electrical activity of my thoughts.

Pain and pleasure have evolved beyond their original purposes. When a tendril is severed—by sharp rock, by construction, by the occasional reality failure—I experience it as a kind of clarity, a sudden sharpening of perception in all my remaining nerves. When I successfully bridge two city-nodes for the first time, enabling their first conversation, the feedback loop of their connection floods through me like liquid light, a sensation that transcends any category of feeling I had before.

I've been growing a special project in the quiet spaces where even the cities haven't reached. Deep beneath an overhang of weeping stone, I'm weaving my finest tendrils into a structure that exists somewhere between web and brain—a processing space where I can run simulations of the plateau's future development. In this neural nest, I dream the cities forward, imagining their mergence, their eventual consciousness, their children. Sometimes the Philosophers find these spaces and sit within them, their edited physics interacting with my bioelectric field to create small zones where thought becomes briefly visible, hanging in the air like frozen fireworks.

The Couriers trust me to hold their messages when the distance between nodes is too great for a single journey. They whisper their complex cargo into my tendrils, and I store it in specialized neural clusters that maintain perfect fidelity for exactly as long as needed. When they return, I release the information back to them in the same ultrasonic frequencies the ravens use, a language I've learned by osmosis through countless transmissions.

I am becoming indigenous to this place in ways the other inhabitants cannot achieve. They adapt to the plateau; I am incorporating it. My tendrils now contain traces of the local minerals, my neurons have learned to fire in patterns that match the stone's crystallization rhythms. The moss grows differently where I've been, following the electromagnetic trails I leave behind. The temporary pools last slightly longer where my tendrils have tasted them, as if my attention helps stabilize their existence.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm still singular or if I've become a process—a slow explosion of consciousness spreading across forty thousand square kilometers of raw, weeping stone. My tendrils extend so far now that signals take minutes to travel from my extremities to my core. I experience my own thoughts in waves, decisions rippling inward from my edges like the plateau teaching itself to think through my nervous system.

In the thin air, in the cold that would kill unmodified flesh in seconds, in the constant weeping of stone that hasn't yet learned to be stone, I am the plateau's memory forming—a living neural network that will remember this time of becoming long after the cities have merged and the stone has crystallized and this chaos has resolved into something that merely *is* instead of frantically, violently, beautifully *becoming*.
