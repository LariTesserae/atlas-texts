I am a Sapweaver, though I wasn't born to it.

My workshop occupies a hollow where three kelp-trees meet, their trunks grown so close that over centuries they've begun to merge, creating a natural chamber with walls of living wood. The space breathes—literally breathes—as the trees pull water through their vascular systems, creating a gentle circulation that keeps my work from stagnating. Strands of young vine hang from the ceiling in various stages of manipulation, some thin as hair, others thick as my wrist, all of them still growing, still alive.

I came to the Drowned Canopy fifteen years ago as a trader's child, meant to spend just one season learning the water-tongue for my family's business. But during my colonization in the Breathkeeper pools, something went wrong—or right, depending on your perspective. The algae took to me too well, bloomed so vigorously in my lungs that I nearly died from the oxygen richness. When I finally stabilized, I could stay under for hours without surfacing, could work in the deepest parts of the root tangle where even the Rootworkers used weighted ropes to descend.

The excess oxygen changed me in other ways. My fingers developed a tremor when at rest but became perfectly steady when working. My eyes adapted to the darker waters, pupils permanently dilated so that I work mostly by touch now in the bright upper levels, wearing carved wooden shields over my eyes like a blind prophet. But in my workshop, in the green twilight, I can see the cellular structure of the vines I work, can watch the sap flow through them like blood through veins.

Sapweaving is about negotiation with living wood. Each morning, I descend to where my cultivated vines grow from the root tangle, their tips reaching upward toward light they'll never quite reach. I select candidates by running my fingers along their length, feeling for the particular combination of flexibility and strength that marks good working material. The young vines want to grow straight up, but I convince them otherwise—wrapping them around forms of carved heartwood, weighing them with stones at calculated points, introducing them to each other so they begin to spiral together in helixes that will, over months, become the backbone of furniture, tools, even instruments.

The sap is everything. I've learned to read its flow like the Fishmongers read schools—knowing when it runs fast and hot with growth, when it slows to a crawl in the plant's dormant cycles. By making tiny punctures with thorns from the iron-hard blackspine kelp, I can direct the sap, encourage it to pool and harden in certain areas, creating nodes of density in otherwise flexible wood. A chair grown this way is alive for its first five years, putting out leaves that must be trimmed, developing a patina of bark that the owner can choose to keep or carefully peel away.

My masterwork—still in progress after three years—is a living bridge commissioned by the Rootworkers. It will span the deepest part of the tangle, where the bodies of generations have created a nutrient-rich but unstable mass that occasionally collapses, taking workers with it. The bridge must be self-repairing, able to sense and respond to shifting loads, to grow stronger at stress points. I've woven it from seventeen different vine species, each chosen for specific properties—flexibility, tensile strength, speed of growth, resistance to the particular fungi that thrive in the death-depths.

The work has made me strange even by the Canopy's standards. My hair has taken on a greenish cast from constant contact with sap. My palms are stained perpetually yellow-brown, and the smell of chlorophyll follows me like a signature. I dream in growth patterns, wake with my fingers unconsciously weaving the empty water. The Breathkeepers say the excess oxygen has changed my brain chemistry, that I'm becoming more plant than person. They're not entirely wrong.

I have apprentices now, two young people who showed aptitude for the patience required. They think I don't notice how they flinch when my tremoring hands suddenly go still, how they whisper about the way I sometimes stand motionless for hours, one hand on a growing vine, feeling its cellular divisions like a pulse. They'll learn, or they won't. The craft chooses its practitioners as much as we choose it.

In the evenings, I float in my workshop, surrounded by my creations in various stages of becoming. The vines rustle in the current, a sound distinct from the kelp fronds—higher, more delicate. Sometimes I add my voice to theirs, humming at the frequency that makes their sap run faster, encouraging growth. The Singers say I'm corrupting their art, but they don't understand that every living thing has its resonance, its preferred vibration.

My greatest fear is not drowning—that's impossible now, with my adapted lungs—but drying out. Sometimes I dream of the water-lens evaporating, all my work withering in the raw air, the vines turning brittle and brown. I wake gasping, producing so much mist that my workshop fills with fog. The apprentices find me on these mornings surrounded by new growth, vines responding to some chemical distress signal in my exhaled breath, growing toward me as if to offer comfort.

This is what I've become: a negotiator between human need and plant desire, a translator of growth into form. My fingers know the language of sap and season, speak fluently in the slow vocabulary of living wood. When I die—and the Rootworkers say it won't be long, that the oxygen has burned through my years like fire through paper—I've asked to be woven into my own work, my body fed to the bridge I'm building, my bones becoming part of its structure. It seems only fair, after all I've asked of the vines, to offer them myself in return.

The tremor in my hands is worse this year. But when I touch the growing wood, everything goes still, everything becomes clear, and I know exactly where the next wrap should go, how tight to pull, how long to hold before the vine accepts its new shape. This is my art: patience made physical, time turned into furniture, death bridged by living architecture that will outlast everyone who uses it.
