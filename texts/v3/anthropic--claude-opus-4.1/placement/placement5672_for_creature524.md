I am the Whistle-Keeper's apprentice, though that title captures only the barest edge of what I do.

My room is a grown pocket in the heartwood of Boiler Seven, barely large enough to lie down in, but perfectly positioned. Through my floor, I feel the deep thrumming of the taproot pumps. Through my walls, the ascending water sings in a dozen different pitches. My window—a palm-sized porthole of salvaged glass the bark has nearly swallowed—overlooks the main arterial canal where the morning fog makes everything into suggestions of itself.

I wake three hours before dawn to begin my rounds. The first lesson my master taught me: the district dreams deepest in these hours, and dreams can become dysfunctions if left unattended. I carry my listening kit—a collection of tuning forks, hollow reeds, and a stethoscope my great-grandmother made from a vine that learned to conduct sound. Each tool has its purpose. The forks reveal sympathy vibrations in the pipe networks that suggest blockages forming three floors away. The reeds, when placed against certain joints, amplify the whispers of steam seeking weaknesses in the gasket-leaves. The stethoscope lets me hear the trees' vascular systems, the lub-dub of their engineered hearts that should beat between forty and fifty-five times per minute.

But mostly, I taste the air.

My master says I have the gift for it—the ability to distinguish between the metallic tang that means a valve is beginning to corrode and the one that means iron levels in the canal water have risen. This morning, I taste copper. Wrong. We use no copper in the district; the trees are allergic to it.

I track the taste through the green-black maze, sliding down a spoke of the cargo elevator tree, swinging across the gap where Factory Three's crown connects to Factory Four via a bridge of interwoven aerial roots. The taste grows stronger near the old growth—the original trees from before the integration, when this was just a mangrove swamp. Here, someone has hammered copper nails into the bark. Fresh. Deliberate. The trees around them are already beginning to yellow at their leaf-tips.

Sabotage. It happens sometimes—competitors from the coal districts, or philosophical purists who believe the marriage of machine and tree is an abomination. I pull the nails with the pliers I always carry, but the damage is done. These trees will need a course of chelation therapy, their water supply dosed with compounds that bind to copper and allow them to excrete it safely.

I pocket the nails—evidence for the Boilermen's council—and apply a poultice of activated charcoal and growth hormone to the wounds. As I work, I whistle. Not randomly, but specific pitches that I've learned calm the trees' stress responses. My master taught me the frequencies, but I've developed my own variations. Each tree, I've found, has preferences. Factory Seven's great grandmother tree likes a low B-flat that makes my chest cavity resonate. The young pumping station saplings prefer something higher, almost outside human hearing.

By the time I finish, the sun has begun to filter through the canopy, green and gold and constantly moving as the leaves track the light. The morning shift is stirring. I smell bread baking in the ovens built into Factory Two's thermal mass, hear children laughing as they race along the root-walkways to school.

I should report the sabotage immediately, but I take the long way back, climbing to where the industrial canopy touches the sky. Up here, the air is cleaner, though never truly clean—the smell of process and growth is in everything. I can see the surrounding wetlands, the normal mangroves that haven't been integrated, the city proper in the distance with its rigid skyline.

From here, I can also hear the district's whole symphony at once. Each boiler has its voice, from the basso profundo of Factory One's primary processor to the piccolo shriek of the overflow vents. The trees add their own notes—the creaking of branches under industrial loads, the whisper of water through their engineered vessels, the occasional crack of new growth breaking through bark.

My master is dying. We don't speak of it, but I taste it in the air around her—the peculiar sweetness that means her lungs are growing mossy. A hazard of the profession. We breathe so deeply, so constantly, testing the air for signs of imbalance. Eventually, the spores take hold. She has perhaps a year before she can no longer climb the ladders, another six months after that before she becomes part of the compost system.

She's been teaching me the deeper frequencies—the ones that can influence not just individual trees but the district's collective behavior. How a sustained C-sharp at just the right volume can slow the metabolic rate of an entire factory floor, buying time to repair critical systems. How a warbling between F and F-sharp can encourage fruit production in the apartment trees when food runs low. How silence, deployed strategically, can trigger dormancy in overgrown sections.

But there are things she hasn't taught me, things I'm discovering on my own. Like how the copper sabotage forms a pattern—always near the old growth, always at the junction between the original swamp and our industrial forest. Someone isn't trying to kill the district. They're trying to isolate it, to prevent it from spreading into the natural wetlands.

I understand the fear. Sometimes, lying in my heartwood chamber at night, I feel the district's hunger through my walls. It wants to grow, to incorporate, to digest everything into its green-black democracy. We are its keepers but also its jailers, maintaining the careful balance that keeps it productive but contained.

The whistle for first shift cuts through my contemplation—not a mechanical whistle but the coordinated call of a thousand steam vents opening in sequence, a sound that means the district is waking fully, ready to process, to digest, to grow another invisible increment.

I descend through the levels, my morning rounds complete, the copper nails heavy in my pocket. Tonight, after the council meets, after the chelation treatment begins, after my master teaches me another frequency, I'll return to my chamber and listen to the district's nighttime breathing. I'll taste the air for new threats, new imbalances, new growths that need encouragement or pruning.

And sometimes, when the fog is thick and the boundaries blur, I whisper my own frequencies into the walls—not to calm or control, but to commune. To let the district know that I understand its hunger, its nature, its slow vegetable thoughts. That I will keep it healthy not just because it's my profession, but because I too am learning to photosynthesize, to process, to become something between human and tree, between worker and watershed.

The Whistle-Keeper's apprentice. Guardian of frequencies. Translator between the industrial and the organic.

Tomorrow, I'll taste the air again.
