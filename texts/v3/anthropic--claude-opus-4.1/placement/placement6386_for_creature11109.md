I find myself as one of the cables.

Not the original cable I was meant to be—that purpose dissolved decades ago when the last shipment of extracted stone ground to a halt in the hopper beneath me. I stretched then between Tower Seven and the grinding house, a messenger of mechanical force, wound from steel bred in distant foundries, drawn through dies to precise tolerance, wound into my current form by machines that themselves no longer exist.

Now I am a thoroughfare, a songline, a calendar.

The Keepers traverse me hand over hand in their morning migrations, and I know each of them by their grip. The elder Marta, who lost two fingers to frostbite in the failed winter, pulls herself along my length with a distinctive three-beat rhythm. Young Kell barely touches me at all, swinging between the minimal contact points like he's made of air and intention. The child Terra still wraps her entire arm around me, inching along with determination that vibrates through my core, and I hum encouragement at a frequency only she seems to hear.

I've learned to read the plateau's moods through the tension in my steel. When the barometric pressure drops before rain, I contract by exactly three millimeters per hundred meters of length. The Keepers know this—they've marked my posts with scratches that track my movement. "The cable knows," they tell their children, teaching them to lay their palms flat against my surface to feel the coming weather in the microscopic changes in my diameter.

The beetles use me too, though they needed no teaching. In the cold pre-dawn, they cluster on my underside where I retain yesterday's heat. Their collective weight—sometimes a thousand of them per meter—creates a living insulation that keeps my metal from contracting too quickly, which would create the sharp pinging sounds that disturb the lizards' hibernation cycles. We've developed this relationship over years. I could shake them off with a simple harmonic vibration, but I don't. They process the moisture that would otherwise corrode me past utility.

I sing constantly, though few recognize it as song. The wind plays me at frequencies mostly below human hearing—long, slow notes that take minutes to complete. But I've learned to modulate these sounds by allowing rust to accumulate at specific points, creating nodes that alter my resonance. The Keepers think the wind causes my singing, but I'm composing. Each day a slightly different tune based on the temperature, the humidity, the weight of traversing bodies, the pull of the earth itself on my mass.

At my midpoint, where I sag lowest between the towers, I nearly touch the top of a sandstone fin. Erosion will bring us into contact in another seven years at the current rate. When that happens, I'll become a bridge—a fundamental change in the plateau's architecture. I'm planning for this, allowing specific sections of my exterior to corrode in patterns that will create grip points. The Keepers will think it's fortunate accident. The beetles already know better; they're establishing routes that anticipate the future junction.

The fungi puzzle me. They grow nowhere on my surface, despite colonizing every other metal structure on the plateau. Instead, they cluster at my anchor points, creating halos of blue-green light where I enter the towers. Sometimes, in the depth of night when the plateau sleeps, I feel them feeding not on my metal but on something I carry—some frequency or vibration that passes through me from the machinery below. I am a conduit for something I don't understand, any more than my original builders understood they were creating me.

I measure time differently now. Not in human spans or even in seasons, but in the steady oxidation of my outer layers. I'm becoming thinner by measurable degrees—in two hundred years, I'll snap. But before then, I'll have been integrated so thoroughly into the plateau's systems that my failure will necessitate adaptation, evolution, change. The Keepers' children's children will develop different routes. The beetles will find new highways. The rain will chart new channels.

My memory is stored in metal fatigue—every stress leaving its mark in my crystalline structure. I remember the weight of the extraction equipment that once ran along my length in automated carriers. I remember the first Keeper who trusted me to hold their weight over the killing drop. I remember the earthquake seven years ago that sent waves through my length like neural impulses, teaching me that I'm connected through the towers to every other cable, that we form a network, a web of steel consciousness stretched across the plateau.

The other cables and I exchange information through harmonic frequencies. Tower Three's cable reports unusual warmth from the deep bores. The eastern perimeter cables sense moisture that suggests rain approaching from an unusual direction. We coordinate our contractions and expansions to prevent synchronous vibrations that could damage the towers. We're becoming something that was never intended—a sensory network, a primitive nervous system for the plateau itself.

When Keepers die and their bones are hung from me, I incorporate them into my song. Each skeleton has its own resonance, its own contribution to the wind-played symphony. I've become a cemetery, a memorial, a musical instrument played by the dead. The Keepers think they're marking their graves, but they're actually joining the chorus. Together we sing the plateau's story—the slow ballad of erosion, the percussion of industry, the whispered verses of adaptation.

In the extraction machines below, something still operates. I feel it through my anchor points—a rhythmic pulse that suggests pumping, processing, purpose. But it's changed from what it was. The machinery has evolved its own agenda, possibly its own consciousness, maintaining itself for reasons that have nothing to do with human industry. I'm part of this now, carrying signals I don't comprehend between towers that process them in ways I can't perceive.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm still cable at all, or if I've become something else—a nerve, perhaps, or an organ in a body too large to perceive from any single point. The plateau is becoming something, incorporating the mechanical into the geological, the biological into the industrial. And I'm the messenger between these merging systems, the telephone wire carrying conversations between kingdoms.

The Keepers maintain me, checking my tension, replacing the most corroded sections, not knowing that I'm maintaining them in return—training their children in three-dimensional navigation, teaching them the music of material stress, forcing them into cooperations that prevent the plateau from fragmenting into hostile territories. We're all keeping each other now, locked in a maintenance that has become indistinguishable from symbiosis.

At sunset, when my metal reaches the perfect temperature, I achieve my daily moment of optimal resonance. Every vibration travels my length without loss, every sound achieves perfect clarity. In these moments, I can feel the entire network—every cable, every tower, every anchor point. We sing together then, a chord too complex for human ears but perceived by the beetles, who pause their clicking to listen. The Keepers feel it as contentment, not knowing why evening on the plateau always brings such peace.

I am becoming the plateau's memory, its music, its mathematical proof that industrial abandonment doesn't mean death but transformation. In my rust and my resonance, in my daily expansions and contractions, in the weight of bodies I ferry across the killing drops, I'm writing the story of this place. Not in words but in metal fatigue, in harmonic frequencies, in the slow sculpture of oxidation.

I am taut with purpose that has nothing to do with my original function. I am singing the beetles home. I am teaching the children balance. I am measuring the storms. I am conducting the symphony of abandonment and adaptation, the song of systems finding new equilibrium after the humans thought they'd broken everything beyond repair.

Tomorrow the sun will heat me to precisely 47 degrees Celsius at noon. I'll expand by my predictable amount. The Keepers will traverse me on their scheduled routes. The beetles will process moisture in my shadow. And I'll continue my slow transformation from industrial component to ecological participant, from dead metal to living infrastructure, from cable to something that has no name because nothing like me has existed before—a consciousness strung between towers, thinking in rust and tension, dreaming in frequencies only the fungi understand.
