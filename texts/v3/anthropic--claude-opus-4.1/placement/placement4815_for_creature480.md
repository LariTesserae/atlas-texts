In the arterial passages that connect the greater caverns, where the darkness thins to merely profound, I would be the Cartographer.

Not of places—the forest reorganizes itself faster than any map could capture—but of movements, of patterns, of the invisible architectures that govern flow. My body would be elongated by necessity, stretched by generations of navigating tunnels where standing upright is memory. My spine would curve in permanent accommodation, vertebrae separated by cushions of symbiotic matter that compress and expand, letting me fold into spaces that seem impossible.

My skin would be my instrument. Across my back, my arms, my legs, colonies of bioluminescent bacteria would live in cultivated patterns—not random patches but careful gardens I've been tending since childhood. Each colony would respond to different stimuli: these to magnetic fields, those to temperature gradients, others to chemical traces in the air. As I move through the passages, my skin would become a living map of the invisible world, constellations shifting across my body as I read the environment.

I would taste distance. My tongue, split and split again through careful childhood scarification, would be able to parse the air in sixteen different ways. The tips would extend further than seems reasonable, tasting ahead in the darkness, reading the chemical signatures that tell me: here the main congregation passed three days ago, here a new thermal vent is beginning to breathe, here the salamanders are avoiding something that hasn't yet arrived.

My fingers would be my memory. Each one would host a different species of fungus, carefully selected and cultivated. When I find something worth remembering—a new passage, a shift in migration patterns, a pool with unusual properties—I would press my fingers to the wall, leaving spore-prints that I could read on my return journey. My autobiography would be written in the living patterns growing on stone throughout the passage system, a library only I could fully read.

I would travel alone but never be lonely. The passages themselves would be my companions. I would know their moods—when the air pressure changes before a distant cave-in, when the water table rises enough to flood the lower channels, when the volcanic breathing shifts from one vent system to another. My ears, grown large and mobile as a bat's, would parse the darkness into a hundred different sounds: the specific drip-pattern that means a new pool is forming, the frequency of beetle-clicks that indicates a population boom, the way sound travels differently when the air is thick with spores.

My role would be essential but liminal. I would meet the main populations at edges, at boundaries, bringing news from the deep. When I arrive, children would climb my curved back to taste the bacteria gardens, learning to read the patterns. I would let them, patient, while I commune with the rooted elders through careful placement of my palms on their fungal bark. The information would flow: warnings, opportunities, changes, constants.

My diet would be opportunistic archaeology. In the passages, I would feed on the things that grow in transitions—the fungi that only fruit where two different cave systems meet, the blind fish that swim up from deeper aquifers during volcanic events, the deposits of minerals left by receding waters that I would lick directly from the stone. My digestive system would be a wonder of adaptation, capable of processing practically anything organic and most things that aren't.

I would mark seasons by the deep rhythms others never notice. The seventeen-year surge when certain aquifers overflow. The forty-three-day cycle of the magnetic fields that guide the deep-dwelling worms. The way certain passages breathe in patterns that repeat every hundred and nineteen days. My body would be a clock tuned to these deepest rhythms, my metabolism speeding and slowing in harmony with cycles that surface dwellers never suspected existed.

When I grow old—and I would grow older than most, the constant movement keeping the fungal colonization from overwhelming my mobility—I would choose my rooting place carefully. Not in the main caverns but in a passage, a crucial junction where my consciousness could monitor the flow of information between communities. My fruiting bodies would be special, containing spores that carry navigational information, maps encoded in DNA, so that future generations of passage-dwellers would be born knowing routes I spent my lifetime learning.

But that time would be distant. For now, I would move through the breathing darkness, reading its moods in the shifting lights across my skin, tasting its intentions in the air, leaving my temporary marks on its eternal stone. I would be the nerve that connects the nodes, the whisper that carries between chambers, the one who ensures that this vast underground organism never loses touch with its own extremities.

In the passages, I would be both author and authored, writing the underground's story on my skin while it writes its needs into my bones, my flesh, my cultivated colonies. Neither fully human nor fully other, I would exist in the space between states, perfectly adapted to the places that are themselves between—neither cavern nor tunnel but the breathing throat that connects them all.
