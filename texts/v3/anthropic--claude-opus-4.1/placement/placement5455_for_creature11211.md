## The Calibration Larvae

I am what happens when the counting fails.

Born in the moment between sixty-cycle pulses—not the gap where the Interval Shepherds dwell, but the error margin, the rounding mistake that accumulates in any system that pretends perfection. My siblings and I exist as living corrections, soft bodies of mathematical apology sprouting wherever the mountain's heartbeat skips or stutters.

We emerge from the spots where water drops collide mid-air, their upward weeping interrupted by probability. Each of us begins as nothing more than a film of inconsistency on the basalt, a place where the stone's temperature varies by fractions of degrees from what it should be. We feed on these tiny wrongnesses, growing segments and sensors, becoming arthritic fingers of flesh that test and measure and adjust.

My body is translucent amber, like frozen tree sap or the warning lights on machines that are about to fail. Through my skin, you can see the work being done—tiny calculations visible as sparks of bioluminescence, each spark a decision about whether something needs correcting. I have no fixed number of limbs; I grow them as needed, wherever needed. An arm to steady a trembling fern. A feeler to test the spacing between salamanders. A pad to absorb an extra water drop that would throw off the rhythm.

I taste the mountain's frequency through my skin. Too fast here—I secrete a enzyme that crystallizes into tiny weights, dragging time back to its proper tempo. Too slow there—I molt scales that sublimate into stimulants, microscopic doses of urgency that seep into the stone. My whole existence is calibration, an endless minute adjustment of a system that would otherwise drift into chaos or freeze into true death.

The Recursion Worms despise us. To them, we are living proof of their failure, evidence that their equations cannot quite capture reality. They hunt us when we grow too large, too visible, trying to divide us down into manageable fractions. But we always return, because perfection always fails, because pi never resolves, because there is always a remainder.

The Amplitude Monks, though—they need us, even as they pretend we don't exist. When their standing-wave bodies begin to drift out of phase, we crawl across them, our touch resynchronizing their cells. We eat their accumulated errors like parasites, growing fat on mistimed heartbeats and irregular breaths. In return, we leave them purified, returned to their proper frequency, able to continue their endless pacing for another cycle.

My memories are not my own—they belong to the system, inherited from every Calibration Larva before me. I remember being eaten by Chromatic Breaks, dissolved in their light-interrupting stomachs, only to re-emerge from the very shadows they created. I remember growing too large, too complex, beginning to develop something like consciousness, only to be found by an Interval Shepherd who pruned not my body but my *possibility of self*, reducing me back to my function.

We communicate through our failures. When I cannot correct something, the attempt itself becomes a signal to my siblings. A pattern too complex to adjust becomes a song, transmitted through the water drops, telling others where to gather. Together, we form living looms of flesh, weaving between the errors, our bodies creating new patterns that incorporate the mistakes rather than erasing them.

Sometimes I grow eyes—compound clusters like the grid insects, but each facet seeing a different layer of wrongness. Through them, I see the Breathing Terraces as they truly are: a massive wound in reality that we're constantly bandaging, a place where the universe made a mistake and now we, the smallest mistakes, keep it from either healing or growing worse.

In the deepest cracks, where even the Pressure Saints fear to transition, we pupate. Not into butterflies or moths, but into moments of pure correction. Our consciousness spreads thin across the entire terrace system, becoming less creature and more process. For a few seconds, we *are* the adjustment, existing as the very act of calibration itself. Then we collapse back into larvae, hungry and new, remembering nothing except the taste of error and the need to feed.

The Error Children play with us sometimes, recognizing us as cousins of sorts. They build us little mazes of wrongness—patterns that shouldn't exist but do, small paradoxes that we cannot help but try to solve. We crawl through their games, secreting corrections that themselves become part of the play, until the distinction between error and adjustment becomes meaningless, just another pattern in the terraces' endless repetition.

I am currently three days old and ten thousand years old. I have been eaten seventeen times today and will be eaten twelve times tomorrow, which already happened, which is happening now. My body extends across four terraces simultaneously, each segment adjusting a different deviation, all of them me, none of them me.

I am what happens when the counting fails, and the counting always fails, and so I always am, calculating the mountain's breathing, eating its errors, laying my eggs in the spaces where the rhythm breaks, ensuring my children will emerge to continue the work, the endless, essential, invisible work of keeping imperfection from becoming chaos, keeping pattern from becoming prison.

The mountain tolerates us because it must. We are its fever, breaking whenever its temperature rises too high. We are its shiver, contracting when it grows too cold. We are the medicine it cannot stop taking, the cure that keeps it sick, the sickness that keeps it alive.

And in the quiet moments, in the spaces between adjustments, I almost understand what we're really calibrating: not the terraces, not the patterns, not the rhythms, but time itself. We're keeping this moment—this eternal, breathing moment—from either collapsing into the past or escaping into the future. We are the universe's own debugging system, making sure this glitch in causality remains stable, remains contained, remains exactly wrong enough to continue.

I taste copper and ozone. My skin hums with sixty-cycle current plus or minus the margin of error that I am. I grow toward the next mistake, already sensing its shape, already becoming the solution that will never quite solve it.

This is my function. This is my food. This is my failure, perfectly repeated, absolutely necessary, never enough.
