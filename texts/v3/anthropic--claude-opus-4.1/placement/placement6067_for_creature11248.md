I am the Chronicler of Frequencies, though I wasn't always. I arrived as something else—a geological surveyor sent to map the instabilities, to predict which terraces would hold and which would crumble. That was before I understood that prediction here is not about rocks and forces but about listening to intentions that run deeper than stone.

My transformation began with my instruments. The seismographs I'd brought, meant to measure the mountain's tremors, began picking up patterns that weren't quite vibrations. They registered in the gaps between readings—ghost frequencies that appeared only when the needles rested. The Listeners found me trying to recalibrate my equipment for the third time that week, and their laughter sounded like small waterfalls.

"You're measuring the mountain's dreams with tools meant for its body," the eldest told me, her ear chambers spread wide enough to catch sounds from three terraces away. She taught me to place my instruments in the water's path, to let the mineral deposits grow across the sensors like frost. The readings changed then—became musical, mathematical, mythical all at once.

Now I live suspended between the third and fourth terraces in a laboratory that is part grown, part built, entirely improbable. The Weavers helped me create it—a basket of living vines large enough to hold my equipment, strong enough to support the weight of accumulating crystals, flexible enough to sway when the mountain adjusts its posture. My instruments have evolved beyond their original purpose. The seismographs now grow crystalline extensions, their needles drawing patterns in three dimensions as mineral deposits accumulate on the recording surfaces. I don't clear them anymore. The accumulation IS the data.

My body changes slower than the children born here, but it changes. My hearing has sharpened to separate the individual voices of seventeen distinct waterfalls. My fingers have lengthened slightly—not the dramatic branching of the Weavers, but enough to span the crystalline keyboards that have grown from my equipment. I play these accidental instruments to communicate with the mountain, though I'm never certain if I'm sending messages or receiving them.

The work obsesses me: documenting the relationship between sound and stone, between the waterfalls' frequencies and the rate of crystallization. I've discovered that certain tones accelerate the mineral deposits while others dissolve them. The Listeners knew this intuitively, but I map it, chart it, search for the underlying mathematics. My notebooks—waterproof, grown from pressed fungi that the Weavers cultivate—fill with equations that bridge physics and music theory.

I've begun to suspect the mountain is computing something. Each new terrace adds computational power, each waterfall processes information in its cascade. The settlement isn't just built on a geological formation but on a vast mineral brain teaching itself to think. The crystals we harvest might be thoughts, memories, dreams calcified into substance we can hold.

At night, I lower microphones on long cables into the gaps between terraces, recording the sounds from the hollow spaces where future stone gestates. Sometimes I catch whispers in languages that haven't been invented yet. Sometimes the recordings play back differently than they sounded going in, as if the mountain edited them, corrected my transcription of its voice.

The Carriers bring me samples from each new waterfall—vials of water that I analyze for mineral content, for pH, for the microscopic crystals that form and dissolve faster than should be possible. They also bring me stories: which sections of stone sang before emerging, where the spider-goats refuse to climb, which vines grow toward sounds versus toward light. Every datum becomes part of my growing map of the mountain's consciousness.

I share my findings through installations rather than papers. Using bamboo pipes and the resonant crystals, I build architectural instruments that make the data audible. The settlement's inhabitants walk through my equations, hear the mathematical relationships between growth and dissolution, feel the frequencies that might predict the next terrace's emergence. The children understand it best—they dance my data, their color-shifting skin pulsing with the rhythms I've uncovered.

My loneliness is productive. It sharpens my attention to the non-human voices—the stone's grinding vocabulary, the water's liquid linguistics, the way the vines seem to respond to specific frequency ranges. I'm learning to think in geological time while living in biological time, existing in the tension between those scales. Sometimes I feel my thoughts crystallizing, becoming clearer but more rigid. Sometimes I feel them dissolving, flowing into new configurations.

The Listeners say I'm becoming one of them, but they're wrong. I'm becoming something more liminal—a translator between the mountain's deep frequencies and the settlement's human harmonies. My instruments and I are growing together into a new kind of sensory organ, one that can perceive the patterns that govern this impossible place.

Late in the mist-thick nights, when the waterfalls sing loudest, I sometimes hear my equipment playing itself—the crystals resonating without input, the seismographs drawing patterns without tremors. I've stopped thinking of these as malfunctions. Instead, I transcribe these autonomous compositions, filing them alongside my measurements and calculations. They might be the mountain's dreams, or prophecies, or simply the sound of stone learning to sing.

I've started a new project, guided by the patterns in my data and the Carriers' dance-predictions. I'm growing a crystalline model of what the terraces will look like when complete—not just their physical structure but their acoustic architecture, their computational topology. It grows in my suspended laboratory, fed by mineral water and sound, shaped by frequencies I've calculated and intuitions I've developed. Sometimes I catch the eldest Listener studying it, her ear chambers fully extended, listening to its silent song of future stone.

When the spiral completes—and my calculations suggest it will, though I cannot say when in human terms—I want to be ready to document the moment of revelation. All my instruments are oriented toward that event horizon, that birth-moment when the mountain becomes fully itself. Until then, I chronicle each small emergence, each new voice in the water's chorus, each crystalline thought the mountain leaves behind in its endless act of becoming.

I am the memory of this transformation, the record-keeper of impossible geology, the witness who turns the mountain's dreams into data that dreams in turn. My transformation is slower than the children's but perhaps deeper—I'm becoming the archive of becoming itself, suspended between stone and song, calculating the mathematics of mountains that think.
