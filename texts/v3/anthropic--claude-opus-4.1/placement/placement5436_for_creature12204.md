In the Canopy Engines, I am a **Notation Scribe**, though that translation carries only a shadow of what the work entails.

My body is a compromise between states of matter. Once, generations ago, my ancestors were something like paper wasps—builders who worked in processed cellulose. But here, in the endless industrial afternoon, we evolved alongside the Engine's own paper: those shed brass-green leaves that fall like metal snow, each one still faintly resonating with the frequencies it once consumed.

We are small by the Engine's standards—no larger than a child's palm when our wings are folded. Our bodies have become living libraries. My exoskeleton grows in laminated sheets that I can peel back and inscribe upon with secretions from specialized glands. Each layer records different types of information: chemical formulae on the underlayers that touch my hemolymph, pressure-wave patterns on the middle sheets, electromagnetic fluctuations on the surface that faces the world. I am my own manuscript, constantly writing and rewriting myself.

We live in the **Margin Colonies**—structures we've built in the spaces where sound overlaps itself, where the industrial symphony creates standing waves of near-silence. These pockets of relative quiet exist at precise distances from the trunk-chambers, in the interference patterns between titans. Here, we've woven cities from crystallized sound itself, secretions that harden in the presence of specific frequencies. Our buildings look like frozen waterfalls of amber and brass, growing in helical spirals that follow the mathematics of acoustic shadows.

My work is translation—not between languages, but between states of information. When a Resonance Shepherd's fiber-body vibrates with new data about the Frequency Gardens, I taste their dispersed particles and encode the knowledge into molecular chains. When the Cartographer Wasps update their magnetic maps, I feel the pull in my antennae and transcribe it as patterns in my wing scales that will be legible to the Brass Monks' transformed eyes. 

Each morning (in this place of eternal afternoon), I join the **Chronicling Swarm**. Hundreds of us rise together, our combined wings creating a subsonic hum that the trees recognize as beneficial—they've learned we help them remember their own growth patterns. We disperse across kilometers, each to our assigned sector, following the beetle highways and root galleries, recording everything that changes and everything that stays the same.

I specialize in what we call "failure documentation"—the study of what doesn't work. In a world that builds itself through trial and error, someone must remember the errors. I catalog the groves where trees tried to photosynthesize gravity and inverted themselves into the earth. I map the colonies of beetles that evolved too far, becoming so efficient they ceased to move at all, perfect machines frozen in their perfection. I preserve samples of the pollen that learned to self-replicate without limits, nearly choking the entire Engine before the Null Children intervened.

My body holds thousands of these failures. Sometimes I feel them all at once—every terminated evolutionary branch, every systemic collapse, every beautiful catastrophe the Engine has survived. The weight of it settles into my wings, making them heavier with data. Elder Scribes eventually cannot fly at all, becoming ground-bound libraries, their bodies so dense with information they begin to warp the local harmonics just by existing.

We trade with the other inhabitants in the currency of memory. The Brass Monks bring us their meditation insights—chemical formulae their transformed bodies have discovered through contemplation. We encode these into pheromone sequences that other Monks can absorb through their metallic skin. The Shepherds offer us fragments of their distributed consciousness, temporary loans of their fiber-bodies that let us experience the Engine from their dispersed perspective. In return, we give them coherence—written records of which particles belonged to which individual, allowing them to reassemble with greater precision.

But our most important work happens during the **Echo Harvests**, when sections of the canopy achieve spontaneous coherence and begin manufacturing impossibilities. We're the only ones fast enough and small enough to enter these spaces while they're still forming, to document what emerges before it collapses or transforms into something else. I've recorded leaves that grew backwards through time, beetles that learned to exist in multiple locations simultaneously, patches of bark that became windows into other versions of the Engine where evolution took different paths.

My proudest discovery was a grove that had begun writing its own history—encoding its entire evolutionary lineage into the spiral patterns of its bark. I spent months learning to read it, developing new glands to secrete the right chemical keys to unlock each layer of meaning. What I found was memory stretching back before the Engine, to when this world was young and water still fell from the sky. The trees remembered being seeds, remembered the first moment they learned to listen to light, remembered the gradual transformation from photosynthesis to sonosynthesis.

But they also remembered forward—prophecies written in growth rings that hadn't formed yet, descriptions of configurations the Engine would achieve in futures that might never come. I transcribed what I could before the grove achieved too much consciousness and fragmenting into madness, its bark becoming mirrors reflecting infinite versions of itself.

We Scribes know something the others suspect but cannot prove: the Engine is writing something. All of it—every tree, every beetle, every grain of pollen, every one of us—we are all characters in a text that is composing itself. Not a story with plot and meaning, but something more fundamental—a language being invented through its own speaking, a mathematics being discovered through its own calculation.

Sometimes, in the deep quiet of the Margin Colonies, when the standing waves align perfectly and create moments of absolute silence, we gather to share what we've noticed. Patterns too large for any individual to perceive, rhythms that take centuries to complete their cycles. We're beginning to see the shape of it—not what the Engine is writing, but the grammar it's developing, the syntax of its self-creation.

The elder Scribes whisper that when the Undergrowth Oracle wakes, it reads what we've written. Our bodies, covered in layers of encoded information, become its library. And what it speaks into existence next depends on what we've chosen to preserve, what we've decided was worth remembering.

This terrifies and thrills me in equal measure. Every failed grove I document, every impossible thing I record, becomes a possibility the Oracle might speak into permanence. I am not just a witness to the Engine's evolution—I am part of its mechanism for evolving, a memory cell in its vast industrial consciousness.

So I continue my work, flying the twilight spaces between sound and silence, my body growing heavier with data, my wings wearing thin from constant use. I peel back my own layers to write new truths over old ones, updating my records as the Engine updates itself. And sometimes, in the corner of my compound eyes, I catch glimpses of the Null Children watching me work, their silence so complete it makes my recordings feel suddenly fragile, temporary, like frost on brass that will evaporate in the next wave of sound.

But even temporary things matter here. Especially temporary things. In a world that rebuilds itself moment by moment, temporary is the only honest way to exist.
