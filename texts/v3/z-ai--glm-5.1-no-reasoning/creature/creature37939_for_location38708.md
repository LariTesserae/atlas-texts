The people of Ashvast do not think of themselves as enduring. That is a word for outsiders, for the traders who come up the mountain gasping and appalled, looking at the frost-rimed stone and asking how anyone survives here. To endure implies a trial, a temporary state to be outlasted. But Ashvast is not a trial. It is simply the place where they are.

They call themselves the Vastar, though they use the word less often than you might expect. Names are for clarifying who you mean when you point, and they are usually pointing at someone nearby, someone known. The word comes from the old speech, the one the elders still use for counting and cursing and the words that accompany the birth and the winding-sheet. In that language, *vast* means something like "the ones who stay," though the root also carries a sense of pressing down, of weight settling into place. The Vastar are the ones who stayed and pressed into the mountain until it held them.

## Bodies

They are not tall. This is the first thing the traders notice, the lowlanders who come up with their long legs and their lungs that heave at the altitude. The Vastar stand shorter than the valley peoples, and broader—barrel-chested, thick-armed, with hands that look carved from the same granite as their city. The women are wide-hipped; the men are not. Both sexes carry the same dense layer of subcutaneous fat, not soft but firm, a thermal padding that makes them look stocky even when they are lean with work. Their limbs are proportionally shorter than a lowlander's, fingers thick, feet broad and flat. This is not deprivation. This is adaptation, written over generations so deep that the bodies have forgotten they were ever shaped by pressure.

Their skin runs a range from wind-darkened brown to pale ruddy, depending on lineage and exposure, but all of them weather. By thirty, the face of a Vastar who works outside has the texture of old leather—not cracked, but lined, creased, mapped. The sun at this altitude burns what the cold does not numb. Lips split and heal split and heal until the scar tissue makes them look slightly swollen. Cheekbones catch the worst of it, going ruddy and rough, while the skin around the eyes stays relatively protected by the habit of squinting, by the fur hoods, by the way they turn their faces from the wind.

Their lungs are large. Disproportionately so. The air is thin, and the body compensates—deeper breaths, more red blood cells, a chest cavity that expanded over centuries until the Vastar can walk and work where a lowlander sits down and waits for the spinning to stop. This is invisible from the outside, but you can hear it in their speech: they speak slowly, not from dullness but from economy. Why waste breath? Each word is chosen, placed, given just enough air. They do not shout. They do not need to. Their voices carry in the thin cold air with a clarity that surprises visitors, a resonance that seems to come from the stone itself.

Their hair is thick, often dark, worn long by both sexes and bound in braids that serve as insulation. You see grey early—another cost of the altitude, perhaps, or simply the life. Beards on the men, when they grow them, are dense and unruly, trimmed only when they become a hazard near the fire. Children's hair is finer, fairer sometimes, darkening as they age.

They smell of woodsmoke and wool and the faint ammoniac bite of goat. This is not remarked upon. It is simply the scent of the place, absorbed into the fibers of their clothing and the oils of their skin, impossible to separate from the person. A Vastar taken down the mountain would still smell of the high cold for days, the way a sailor smells of salt long after reaching land.

## Movement

Watch them walk and you understand something essential. They do not stride. They do not amble. The Vastar move through their city the way a hand moves across a familiar tool—deliberately, precisely, with an economy that has nothing to do with haste and everything to do with knowing exactly where everything is. Their feet find purchase on stone that would send a lowlander sliding. Their hands reach for holds that are invisible until you see them used—a crack, a protrusion, a worn spot that fits a palm.

They navigate the bridges without looking down. They climb the stairs without touching the rails. They move through the narrow passages between buildings by feel as much as sight, turning their shoulders at just the angle needed, ducking under the low lintels without slowing. This is not performance. It is simply what the body does when it has lived in a place long enough. The city is written into their musculature, their reflexes, their spatial memory. A Vastar could walk the main routes blindfolded, and some of the elders essentially do—their eyes going milky with age while their feet still find the way.

Children learn this early. They climb before they can speak clearly. They are allowed to scramble on the lower walls, the safer ledges, and when they fall—and they do fall—they learn that the stone is hard and the cold is patient and the body is smaller than the city. By the time they are old enough for real work, they have already internalized the geometry of their world. They know that a gap of a certain width can be jumped, that a surface at a certain angle will hold, that the wind comes around a particular corner with a force that requires bracing.

The Vastar do not run inside the city. There is nowhere to run to that is worth the waste of heat and breath, and the passages are too narrow, too crowded with bodies and things. But they move with a quickness that is startling when you see it—a sudden purposeful speed, there and then done, like a bird alighting. They are not slow people. They are unhurried people, which is different.

## Living Together

Privacy, in the sense that lowlanders mean it, does not exist in Ashvast. The walls are thin. The rooms are shared. A family might have one warm room, perhaps two, and in that space they sleep and eat and work and argue and couple and give birth and die, all within arm's reach of each other and the neighbors on the other side of the wall. The concept of solitude is known but rare, experienced only by hunters on the slopes or elders who have earned the right to a small cold room of their own. For most Vastar, life is a constant low hum of other presence.

This could be oppressive. Sometimes it is. There are tensions that build between families who share a cistern wall, resentments that fester for decades over a noise or a smell or a perceived slight about water use. But it is also the reason the city works. The cold does not permit isolation. To be alone is to be cold. To be cold is to be dead. The Vastar are together because they must be, and the must has become a want, and the want has become a culture so deeply embedded that they no longer see it.

They have customs for proximity. You do not look into a neighbor's window, even though you could reach it from your own. You do not listen to sounds that are clearly meant to be private, even though you can hear them. You do not comment on what you cannot avoid knowing. These are not laws—there are no laws in Ashvast, only expectations—but they are observed with a rigidity that makes them more binding than any statute. The person who violates the customs of proximity is not punished, exactly. They are simply... left out of things. Unvisited. Unhelped when the peat fire won't catch. Alone, which is the worst thing.

They share food when there is food to share. They share warmth by existing near each other. They share work because no single household can manage all the tasks of survival alone. The cistern-groups are the basic unit of social organization—clusters of families who draw from the same water source, who maintain the same catchments, who negotiate the same micro-politics of close living. A cistern-group might be twenty people or sixty. They know each other the way you know your own breath—intimately, constantly, without conscious attention.

Children are raised collectively within the cistern-group. A child might not know which woman among the group is their birth-mother until they are old enough to ask, and by then it hardly matters, because they have been fed and held and corrected by all the women and some of the men and the older children who are not quite adults but are no longer small. This is practical. A woman who has just given birth needs to rest, and the cold does not permit rest for long, so others take the child. A man who is hunting cannot watch his offspring, so the group watches. The children belong to everyone. The future belongs to everyone.

Old people are valued. This is not sentimental. The Vastar do not have the luxury of sentiment for its own sake. But an elder knows things—where the peat beds are thickest, how to read the weather in the shape of the clouds, which walls are sound and which are hiding rot, the history of disputes that might flare again if the old grievance is not remembered and avoided. The elders are the city's memory, its stability, its insurance against the slow erosion of knowledge. They are kept warm. They are fed first when the stores run low. They are listened to, even when what they say is confusing or seems irrelevant, because it may not be irrelevant tomorrow.

## Making Things

The Vastar are not artisans in the way that lowland cities mean the word. They do not produce objects of beauty for beauty's sake. But everything they make is beautiful in the way that a tool becomes beautiful through use—worn to its purpose, shaped by the hands that held it, carrying the marks of its making in a way that reveals rather than conceals.

Their pottery is thick-walled, dark-fired, glazed on the inside with a slip that makes it hold water. The shapes are simple: bowls, jars, a tall-sided cup that fits between the palms and keeps the heat in. Decoration is limited to impressed patterns around the rim—the mark of the maker, the sign of the cistern-group, sometimes a line of dots that might be counting or might be prayer. The clay is local, dug from a particular slope below the fields and carried up in blocks that take two people to lift. It is not fine clay. It has grit in it. The pottery cracks sometimes. They mend the cracks with pine pitch and keep using the vessel until it cannot hold, and then they break it and use the shards to edge the planting boxes.

Their textiles are their great work. Wool from the goats and from a breed of sheep kept on the lower slopes, sheared in the brief warm season, spun in the long dark of winter, woven on small looms that fit in the warm rooms. The cloth is dense, felted almost, impossible to tear with bare hands. The colors are the colors of the mountain—the grey of stone, the brown of peat, the pale green of lichen after rain, the orange that appears on north walls and is captured in a dye that takes three days to prepare and stains the hands of the dyer for a month. They layer the cloth. A Vastar in full winter dress wears three or four layers, each one adding warmth but also adding the particular weight and texture that says *this is mine, this is home, this is enough*.

They carve bone. The goats provide it, and the mountain ox when a hunter is lucky. Bone becomes needles, awls, fasteners, handles, the delicate latches on doors that say *this space is claimed*. Bone becomes the gaming pieces that the elders use in a strategy game the rules of which are too complicated for outsiders to learn in a single visit. Bone becomes combs for the hair, and the combs become worn with use, the teeth narrowing over years until they are too thin and a new one is made and the old one is given to a child to play with.

Wood is precious. The scrub wood from below the treeline is twisted and dense, hard to work but strong when it is shaped. The Vastar use it for the structural beams that cannot be stone, for the bridge planks, for the handles of tools. They do not waste it. A piece of wood is used until it cannot hold, and then it is burned in the peat fires for the last heat it can give. Carved wood is rare and valued—a decorated beam, a figure standing in a niche, a spoon with a handle shaped like a goat. These are heirlooms. These are kept.

Iron is harder still. The ore comes up from the valleys, or the metal itself, traded for wool and dye. The smiths of Ashvast are careful people who work in short intense sessions, heating the small forge, hammering the soft metal into shape, quenching it in cistern water that steams and hisses. Every nail is accounted for. Every blade is used until it is too thin to sharpen. The broken fragments are saved, hoarded, eventually re-forged into something smaller. There is no waste. There cannot be.

## Inner Life

The Vastar do not write. They know writing exists—the traders bring documents, sometimes a book, and the elders have heard of peoples who mark stone with symbols that hold meaning. But the city itself has no script, no records, no archives. This is not ignorance. It is a choice, or rather a non-choice, a way of being that simply never developed the need. Memory serves. The elders remember. The songs hold the history. The names of places carry their stories.

They sing. This is the thing that surprises the traders, who expect a grim people in a grim place and instead find that the Vastar hum and chant and break into song the way the city breaks into afternoon light—suddenly, collectively, as if the sound was always there waiting for someone to give it permission. The songs are not melodic in the lowland sense. They are drones and cycles, voices rising and falling in patterns that seem simple until you try to follow them, at which point you realize they are shifting, complex, layered like the city itself. Some songs are work-songs, keeping time for shared labor. Some are memory-songs, encoding the locations of peat beds or the history of a cistern dispute. Some are just sound, made because making it feels good, because the cold is more bearable when your throat is open and the vibration warms you from the inside.

They have stories. The long winter nights are for stories, told in the warm rooms, the children pressed close, the adults doing quiet work with their hands while they listen. The stories are about the mountain—how it grew, why it pushes upward, what lives in the deep roots of it that will never see the light. They are about the city—how the first families came and why they stayed, what they built first, how the cisterns were agreed upon. They are about people—ancestors and troublemakers and the ones who made decisions that still shape daily life. The stories are not consistent. Different cistern-groups tell different versions. The elders do not reconcile them. The contradictions are part of the texture, part of the truth that the city holds multiple pasts at once, the way it holds multiple families in a single wall.

They dream. You would not think to ask about this, but they do, and their dreams are dense and close and vertical, full of narrow passages and unexpected rooms and the constant awareness of something above and something below. They do not dream of open spaces. When they descend to the valleys—for trade, for necessity—they report that their dreams go flat and quiet, as if the absence of the mountain has drained something from their sleeping minds. They are relieved to return, to climb back into the tangle of their city, to close their eyes and find the walls around them again.

They do not have a religion in the way the traders understand the word. They have no gods, no temples, no priests, no prayers that are addressed to something not-present. But they have rituals. They mark the turning of the year—the longest night, the first thaw, the day when the sun reaches a particular cleft in the peaks and the light falls on a particular wall. They mark the passing of people—not with burial, because the ground is stone, but with a ceremony of placement, carrying the body to a particular ledge where the wind and the choughs do their work, returning the warmth to the cold that made it. They mark the naming of children, the agreement of a marriage (which is a practical arrangement between cistern-groups, not a romantic one, though love sometimes follows), the moment when an elder is recognized as an elder, which is not a function of age but of knowing.

The mountain is present in all of this. Not worshipped. Not personified. Simply present, the way the cold is present, the way the stone underfoot is present. The mountain is the condition of existence. The Vastar do not thank it or curse it any more than they thank or curse the air. It is. They are. The city is the place where those two facts meet and hold.

## What They Are Not

They are not noble savages. The traders sometimes make this mistake, seeing the simplicity of the life and the hardness of the conditions and projecting a purity onto the Vastar that the Vastar themselves would find ridiculous. They are not pure. They are not simple. They are people—complicated, contradictory, capable of generosity and cruelty and boredom and humor and all the other things that people everywhere are capable of. They argue. They cheat. They lie, though lying is harder in a place where everyone can hear you and the walls are thin. They love in ways that are passionate and confused and sometimes damaging. They are not better than the lowlanders. They are not worse. They are different, because their world is different, and they have become what the world required.

They are not trapped. This is the other mistake. The traders look at the steep slopes, the narrow paths, the sheer drops, and they see a prison. But the Vastar can leave. Some do—young people, usually, driven by curiosity or restlessness or a particular disagreement that cannot be resolved within the cistern-group. They go down the mountain, into the valleys, into the wider world. Some return. Some do not. Those who return are changed, sometimes enlarged, sometimes diminished, but always welcomed back, because the city needs hands and the hands are theirs. Those who do not return are spoken of less often, not from disapproval but from the simple pragmatism of a people who cannot afford to carry the weight of the absent.

They are not unhappy. This is the hardest thing for outsiders to accept. They look at the cold, the crowding, the scarcity, the endless labor of staying alive, and they assume that happiness must be something that requires warmth and space and surplus. But the Vastar have warmth—the shared warmth of bodies in the inner rooms, the warmth of fires banked through the night, the warmth that comes from being exactly where you belong. They have space—the space of the sky, the space of the long view, the space inside a song that you can fall into and lose yourself. They have surplus—of company, of knowledge, of the particular rich nothing that comes when the work is done and the fire is lit and the evening stretches ahead with nothing in it but being alive.

They are not ignorant of what they lack. They know about soft beds and full bellies and rooms where you can be alone. They know about fruit, which they taste sometimes when the traders bring it, and which makes them marvel at the strange wet sweetness of lowland things. They know about warmth that does not require effort. They simply do not consider these things necessary, or even particularly desirable. They have what they have. It is enough. This is not stoicism. It is not resignation. It is a clear-eyed assessment of the gap between want and need, and a cheerful refusal to confuse the two.

## The Unwritten Contract

The Vastar live in Ashvast because Ashvast is where they fit. This sounds simple, but it is the deepest truth of the place. The city shaped them and they shaped the city, and the shaping went both ways for so long now that it is impossible to say where the mountain ends and the people begin. The cold that presses them together also presses them into a particular form—compact, dense, layered, interdependent. The stone that they build with also builds them—hard, patient, lasting. The altitude that thins their air also thins the distance between them, making them a people who exist in constant contact, constant awareness, constant negotiation with the fact of other people.

They are the city. Not metaphorically. Literally. The walls are their bones. The cisterns are their blood. The fires are their breath. The bridges and the ropes and the narrow passages are the connective tissue that holds the whole organism alive. When a wall collapses, they feel it—not as a loss of property but as an injury, a wound in the body that they all share. When a child is born, it is not an addition to a household but a renewal of the city, a new cell in the dense proliferation of life that clings to the mountain's spine.

They will stay. This is what the traders do not understand, cannot understand, because to understand it you would have to be shaped by the mountain, and the mountain only shapes those who remain. The Vastar will stay and the city will grow, adding rooms and walls and passages, filling in the gaps, rising another tier against the slope, becoming denser and more complex and more itself with each generation. The peaks will keep growing, shouldering upward, and the city will grow with them, and the cold will continue, and the people will continue, and the contract between them—the unwritten, unspoken, perhaps unconscious contract that says *you hold me up and I will hold you here*—will hold.

In a million years the mountains will be round and the city will be different or gone. But a million years is a long way off, and the Vastar are here now, and the evening is coming, and the fires are being lit, and the smell of smoke is settling into the stone the way it always does, and the children are climbing down from the ledges, and the goats are finding their shelters, and the cisterns are full from the afternoon rain, and the night is enormous and full of stars, and the cold is coming, and the city is ready, and the people are home.
