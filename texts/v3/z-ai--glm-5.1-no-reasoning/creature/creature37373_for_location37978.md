They are not small. That is the first misconception the emptiness invites—the assumption that this city, so sparsely peopled, must house a scattered remnant, a dying breed. But they are not small. They are simply few, and they are patient in a way that has nothing to do with temperament and everything to do with scale.

The people of the roof-of-the-world are tall and narrow, built for the thin air like vessels built for low pressure. Their chests are deep, their ribcages expanded to hold what little oxygen the altitude offers, and this gives them a certain hollowness, a barrel-chested prominence that makes their limbs seem almost vestigial by comparison—long, thin arms and legs that move slowly, deliberately, as if each gesture must be weighed against the cost of exertion. They do not hurry. They have never hurried. Their skin is pale, not the pallor of illness but the paleness of stone kept from sun, a greyish-white that matches the granite and basalt of their home. Some of the older ones have a faint greenish cast where the moss-spores have settled into the fine creases of their skin and found the moisture there sufficient to germinate. They do not seem to mind. They do not seem to notice.

Their eyes are large and dark, adapted to the diffuse light, and they rarely blink. When they do, it is a slow, deliberate action, almost aquatic, the lids closing from the outer corners inward in a motion that recalls the foxes, the raptors, the goats—all the creatures here who have learned that sudden movement draws attention across the open plazas. The stillness of the city is not their invention; they share it. They are part of it. They emerged from it, or it emerged from them, and by now the two are inseparable.

They build. This is what they do, though not in the way builders elsewhere build—not to house a growing population, not to expand, not to replace what has fallen. They build because the geometry requires it. They build to complete patterns that exist elsewhere, patterns they can sense but not see, patterns that hum at the edge of hearing like the drone of a distant wasp. The city is a map of something, and they are its cartographers, working from a template they have never been shown but know by heart.

A single wall might take a generation. The stones must be gathered from the scree-slopes, carried by hand along the fitted-stone paths, fitted together with that obsessive precision the city displays. They do not use mortar. They do not need it. Each stone is shaped until it interlocks with its neighbors, the gaps between them too narrow for a blade of grass, and the resulting wall is as strong as the cliff-face itself—stronger, perhaps, because it is intentional. The work is slow, meditative, and the builders often pause for long stretches, standing motionless before the half-completed wall with their large dark eyes unfocused, listening. They are checking the pattern. They are making sure the wall is where the wall should be. If it is not, they dismantle it and begin again. This happens rarely. The pattern does not often change.

They live alone, or nearly alone. Each building houses one of them, or sometimes two—a mated pair, an elder and an apprentice—but never more. The geometry of separation is their geometry, a social arrangement that reflects the spatial one. They do not seem lonely. They do not seem anything, exactly, by the standards of those who need proximity to feel complete. They visit one another, but at intervals, following routes as regular as the fox-patrols, and their visits are quiet affairs—long silences broken by murmured observations, shared meals of lichen-bread and preserved goat-meat, the occasional exchange of tools or materials. They communicate, when they must, in a language of near-whispers, consonants softened by the damp air, vowels elongated until the words resemble the calls of the raptors overhead—soft whistling phrases that do not carry far.

They have no leaders. They have no priests, no council, no hierarchy of any kind. And yet they are organized, deeply and thoroughly, in a way that suggests not authority but alignment—each of them tuned to the same frequency, moving in accordance with the same inaudible signal. When a new platform must be laid, they know without being told. When a stairway requires repair, one of them will simply begin the work, and others will join at intervals, contributing stones and labor without discussion. They do not coordinate through speech. They coordinate through the pattern, which is always present, always insistent, which hums beneath the stillness like a river beneath stone.

Their children—if that word applies—are rare, and they are not born but grown. Not from the body, but from the city itself. In the lower terraces, where the vegetation grows thickest and the spiraling patterns of moss and grass are most pronounced, there are places where the greenery has gathered into dense, almost fibrous mounds—cushions of living material that pulse faintly with the moisture they absorb. These mounds are tended, though the tending is invisible, and after a long interval—years, perhaps, or longer—they split open like seed-pods, and a new one emerges. Small at first, damp, curled in on itself like a fern-frond, blinking in the diffuse light. It is not helpless. It can walk within hours, though it moves with the same careful deliberation as the adults, and it knows the pattern immediately, instinctively, the way the goats know which direction to face.

They do not name themselves. They do not name the buildings or the plazas or the stairways. The names exist, perhaps, in the pattern itself—in the geometry that governs the distances and angles and relationships—but they are not spoken. When two of them meet on a path, they do not greet each other by any identifier. They simply acknowledge the encounter with a slight inclination of the head and then continue, each following their own route, each tending their own segment of the larger design.

They do not seem to age, or they age so slowly that the process is invisible within a human span. The oldest among them—those who dwell in the buildings nearest the central peak, where the fog is thickest and the stone is darkest—have the greenish cast most pronounced, and their movements are slower, more ceremonial, as if they are beginning to calcify, to merge with the architecture they have spent their lives maintaining. There are some who have not moved in living memory, who stand in doorways or at the edges of plazas with their eyes open and their breath barely visible, so still they might be mistaken for carvings. Perhaps they are not dead. Perhaps they have simply completed their portion of the pattern and are now part of it, permanent fixtures in the frozen equilibrium, as unmoving as the basalt escarpments.

And yet they are not unhappy. This is important to understand. The stillness is not stagnation to them; it is completion. The dampness is not discomfort; it is sustenance. The emptiness is not loneliness; it is the proper distance between notes in a composition they can hear in its entirety, even if we catch only fragments—a low hum in the fog, a vibration in the stone beneath the feet, the faintest suggestion that the city is not half-empty but half-full, still growing, still becoming, still arranging itself according to a plan that was old before the mountain finished rising and will be young when the mountain finally falls.

They are the builders, and they are also the built. They are the inhabitants, and they are also the inhabitation. The city made them, and they make the city, and neither could exist without the other, and neither would want to.
