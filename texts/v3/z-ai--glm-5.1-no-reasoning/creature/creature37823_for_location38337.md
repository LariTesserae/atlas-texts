# What Lives in the Booming Forest

---

## The Crawlers

Let us begin with what you can see, because they are the ones who do not hide.

They have been here longest — or rather, they have been here *as themselves* longest, unchanged in body plan since before the canopy closed, since before the trunks reached their current circumferences, since before the mineral floor was packed to its glassy hardness. They remember the open sky. Not in any cognitive sense. In their bodies. In the way their dorsal plates still carry a faint photoreactive pigment that serves no purpose in the shaded interior, a vestigial alertness to light that now triggers only when a bright patch of canopy-transmitted sun crosses their path, causing them to pause, lift the anterior third of their body, and orient toward the warmth before resuming their work. A ritual inherited from an ancestor that lived on the open mineral plain, before the trees grew tall, before the sound became a wall. They are the only creatures here who carry that memory.

Six legs, yes, but the legs are not uniform. The forelimbs are shorter, thicker, and end in a serrated edge — not a claw, exactly, but a chitinous scraper that has evolved to fit the precise contours of the trees' venting valves. Each crawler species — and there are at least eleven, differing in size, in dorsal pigmentation, in the number of antenna segments, in the chemical signature of their defensive secretions — carries a scraper geometry unique to the valve architecture of the tree species it tends. The fit is exact. The scraper enters the valve's external groove, follows its spiral path, and dislodges the mineral particulate that accumulates there — a crust of alkaline dust and compressed gas-residue that, left unchecked, would seal the valve within a decade and silence that frequency of the tree's output.

They work in shifts. Not organized shifts — nothing so deliberate — but in a rhythm that emerges from their own life cycles and the chemical signals they leave along the trunk's surface as they move. A crawler deposits a trace pheromone as it scrapes, a temporary marker that decays over approximately six hours. Other crawlers encountering the trail will follow it, assess the valve's condition, and either continue tending or move on. The result is a coverage pattern that distributes effort across the trunk's surface without any central coordination. The valves that need attention get attention. The valves that don't, don't. The system is simple, local, and robust. It has survived because it cannot fail in any way that matters. If one crawler species is lost, another expands its range. If a trunk falls, the crawlers that tended it migrate to the next nearest tree of compatible valve architecture, or they die, and their chemical markers fade, and the gap in coverage is filled by migrants from adjacent trunks within a season. The system heals the way water fills a depression. Without effort. Without intent.

They do not perceive the forest as a whole. They perceive their trunk. Their trunk is the world — a vertical surface, warm with vibration, textured with scaled integument and the raised lips of valve openings, carrying in its grooves the chemical history of every crawler that has crossed it in the last day. They move in a landscape of scent and texture and vibration, and the landscape is bounded by the trunk's circumference, and beyond that circumference is a gap — the cleared ground between trees — that they cross only rarely, only under duress, only when the trunk they occupy has fallen or has shifted its chemical output in a way that makes it inhospitable. These crossings are the crawlers' only experience of the forest's scale. They are migrations of desperation. A crawler crossing the floor between trunks moves fast, low, legs tucked, dorsal plates sealed flat, and if it survives the crossing — if the ground-dwellers do not find it — it arrives at the new trunk exhausted, dehydrated, its scraper edges worn to uselessness by the abrasive mineral floor. It begins tending anyway. It does not know how to do anything else.

They have no eyes. The anterior carries a cluster of chemoreceptive bristles and a single vibration-sensitive plate that detects the trunk's pulse and orients the crawler to its position on the vertical surface — up, down, the gradient of the tree's internal pressure, which tells the crawler which valves are active and which are dormant. This is all the information they need. This is all the information their world contains. They are creatures of a single surface, a single organism, a single task, and they perform that task with a competence that has been refined over a timescale that makes the trees themselves seem youthful. The trees may be millennia old. The crawlers' lineage is older. They were here first. They were tending something before the trees were trees — perhaps a smaller plant, a precursor, a valve-less ancestor that nonetheless produced some structural feature that required scraping. The scraper preceded the valve. The need preceded the structure. They have been maintaining something for so long that the thing they maintain has grown to fill the shape of their attention.

There is a melancholy to them, if you are inclined to see it. Not in any emotional sense — they do not have emotions, they have stimulus-response loops and chemical gradients and a life cycle that runs from larval emergence to reproductive deposit to death in a span that the forest measures in valve cycles, which is to say in the thousands. They are melancholy the way a ruined arch is melancholy: they carry a form that was built for a world that no longer exists. The photoreactive pigment. The dorsal plates. The desperate gap-crossings. They are a letter from an earlier age, still legible, still performing its function, addressed to a recipient who died long ago and opened by a stranger who does not understand the language but recognizes the care with which it was written.

## The Fliers

The fliers are younger. Everything above the crawlers is younger. The canopy is a newer ecology than the trunk surface, and its inhabitants carry the hallmarks of species that evolved *into* a niche rather than *from* one — they are specialized, optimized, tightly fitted to their environment in a way that suggests recent adaptation. Give them another million years and they may be as vestigial and ritual-bound as the crawlers. For now, they are still becoming.

They are not birds. They are not bats. They are not insects. They are something that fills the ecological role of a small canopy-dwelling flier in a world without water, and they have arrived at a body plan that is functional rather than elegant — a flat, kited body, roughly the size of a human hand, with a single membrane stretched between anterior and posterior structural spines. The membrane is not skin. It is a translucent, slightly iridescent tissue that serves as both flight surface and solar collector. In the bright patches of canopy light, a roosting flier orientates its body to maximize exposure, and the membrane darkens from translucent to a deep amber as it absorbs radiation. The absorbed energy drives — something. The internal chemistry is not fully understood. What is observable is that the membrane, when darkened, produces a fine mist of volatile compounds on its underside, and these compounds accumulate on the flier's feeding apparatus and are then deposited on the canopy wood as the flier moves.

They are feeding the trees. Or rather, they are feeding *something* in the canopy — a growth, a film, a layer of organic material on the surfaces of the light-transmitting branches that appears to be neither plant nor animal but a third thing, a symbiont or a parasite or a product of the tree's own chemistry activated by light. The fliers harvest this growth with a brush-like mouthpart, process it internally, and redeposit the volatile compounds as they move. The cycle is rapid. A flier will harvest from a light patch, fly to a roost, process, and return to the same patch within minutes. The growth replenishes as long as the light falls. When a light patch shifts — as the canopy moves, as the branches adjust — the growth dies back and the flier relocates.

They are territorial, and their territory is *light*. Not space. Light. A flier does not defend a volume of canopy; it defends a pattern of illumination, a shifting territory that moves as the canopy moves. This creates a constantly renegotiated landscape of ownership — a flier's territory may overlap with another's spatially but not temporally, because the light that defines it falls at different times in different places. The result is an aerial politics of light that is invisible from below, detectable only in the calls: three sharp notes for *this is mine, now*, answered from across the canopy by a counter-claim, and then a rapid negotiation — a burst of clipped, percussive exchanges that resolves in seconds, one flier yielding, the other holding, the light patch changing hands without violence. They have never been observed fighting. Their disputes are acoustic. Their territories are luminous. Their wars are songs.

They do not land on the ground. The ground is death to them — not because of the ground-dwellers, though the ground-dwellers would take them, but because the membrane, designed for canopy light and filtered radiation, is destroyed by direct exposure to the unfiltered sky. A flier that falls to the mineral floor in the open, beyond the canopy's edge, will be blinded and chemically disrupted within minutes. Its membrane will lose iridescence, go opaque, crack, and the flier will die in a state that observers have described as looking like a piece of burnt paper. Inside the canopy, where the light is filtered and broken, the same flier is a creature of precision and competence. The canopy is not just their habitat. It is their *protection*. The thing that makes their world livable is the thing they feed.

They roost in clusters during the dark cycle — for there is a dark cycle, a period when the sky's output diminishes and the canopy's light patches contract to their smallest size. The fliers gather on specific branches, always the same branches, selected for structural qualities that are not immediately apparent — perhaps thermal, perhaps acoustic, perhaps related to the branch's resonance frequency during the trees' nighttime venting patterns. They roost in contact, membrane folded, bodies pressed flat against the wood, and they are silent. The forest's sound continues, but the fliers' layer goes quiet. They wait. In the morning, the first light patch opens, and a single call — three notes — breaks the silence, and the canopy comes alive again.

## The Walkers

You asked what lives here. The crawlers will show themselves. The fliers will announce themselves. The walkers will not do either of these things. They are known by their effects.

A section of mineral floor, swept and packed to its usual glassy finish, will overnight become scored with shallow parallel grooves — four sets, spaced at intervals suggesting a wide stance and a slow, deliberate gait. The grooves are not deep. They are pressed into the packed surface by weight, not cut by claws. Whatever made them is heavy. Whatever made them walks on four limbs, or at least places four limbs on the ground in sequence.

The grooves appear in the morning. By midday they have been erased by the floor's constant vibration — the mineral fines settle and re-pack under the trees' rhythmic pulse, filling the impressions like water filling a footprint in sand. By evening the floor is smooth again. By the next morning, new grooves appear, in a different location, oriented in a different direction. The walker — or walkers — have moved through in the dark cycle, between the fliers' last call and their first. They are nocturnal by the forest's standards, though "nocturnal" is the wrong word for a place where the dark cycle is defined not by the sun's absence but by the sky's diminished output, a dimming rather than a darkness, a gray lowering of the light rather than its extinction.

They leave other signs. Deposits on the floor — mineral-rich extrusions, compacted and dry, containing fragments of something organic that is not wood and not crawler and not flier. They are eating something. The deposits suggest a diet that includes mineral substrate — they are consuming the ground itself, or rather specific strata within it, selected for content that their digestion can extract. The mineral floor is not uniform. Beneath the packed surface, the substrate layers vary in composition — some strata richer in certain compounds, others carrying trace elements that are scarce on the surface. The walkers dig. Their grooves are often associated with shallow excavations — divots in the floor, a few centimeters deep, where the surface has been broken and the underlying material has been scooped and ingested.

They are miners. They are eating the floor.

And the floor, in turn, is what the trees root in. The walkers are consuming the trees' substrate. Not the roots — they do not appear to damage the root systems, which extend deep and wide through the mineral substrate — but the material *around* the roots, the matrix that the roots penetrate and draw from. They are eating the trees' growing medium. And yet the trees do not seem to suffer. The excavations heal — the mineral substrate, under the trees' pressure-regulated chemistry, reconstitutes itself, compacting and recrystallizing, filling the divots within days. The walkers take, the trees replace. A steady-state extraction. A balance that has been running long enough that the floor's composition is uniform to a depth of several meters — the walkers have been mining and the trees have been replacing for so long that the substrate has become a homogenized product of both processes. It is not natural ground. It is *manufactured* ground, the output of a collaboration between the trees' root chemistry and the walkers' digestion, each shaping the material to the other's requirements.

This is the relationship you cannot see from below. The crawlers tend the trees' exterior. The fliers tend the canopy's surface ecology. The walkers tend the trees' foundation. Three maintenance systems, three layers, three sets of creatures whose lives are organized around the continuation of the organism that houses them all. And the organism — the continental-scale plant, the pneumatic factory, the thing that makes the sound — does not direct any of this. It does not need to. The system has organized itself around the organism's outputs and inputs, and the organization is stable, and the stability is old, and the oldness is the only quality that pervades every level of the forest's life. Everything here is old. Everything here has been doing what it does for a very long time.

## The Walkers, Up Close

You will not see one. Not on your first visit. Not on your second. But if you stay long enough — if you learn to read the floor, to predict where the grooves will appear, to position yourself in the dark cycle at the intersection of two freshly scored trails — you may, eventually, see a shape move between the trunks.

It is larger than you expected. The grooves suggested something the size of a large animal, something heavy and four-legged. The reality is bigger. The walker stands three meters at the shoulder, its body a low, broad mass carried on four thick limbs that end not in feet but in flattened, pad-like structures that distribute its weight across the mineral floor without breaking the surface — usually. When it digs, it uses a different part of the body: a protruding structure on the ventral surface, a hardened plate that it presses into the ground and works in a circular motion, breaking the substrate and scooping the underlying material into a mouth located just above the plate. The digging is noisy — a grinding, crunching sound that is, remarkably, audible even against the forest's ambient output. It is one of the few sounds in the forest that is not produced by the trees. The walkers are the only creatures here whose activities generate noise loud enough to register independently.

Its body is covered in a material that matches the mineral floor — pale, fine-grained, slightly reflective. It is not camouflage in the active sense. It is *convergence*. The walker's exterior is composed of the same mineral compounds as the floor, incorporated into its integument through a bioaccumulation process that deposits ingested minerals into the outer body layer. The walker is made of what it eats. It eats the floor. It is the floor, walking. Its coloration is the floor's coloration. Its texture is the floor's texture. When it stands motionless between trunks — which it can do for hours, processing a meal, or simply resting in the cool air — it is nearly invisible. A shadow on a shadow. A thickening of the gloom that resolves, on long observation, into a shape.

It has eyes. Two, set wide on a head that is broader than it is long, giving it a panoramic field of view that takes in the full spacing between trunks. The eyes are simple — not compound, not camera-type, but a intermediate form, a cup-shaped receptor with a single lens that produces a low-resolution image sufficient for detecting movement and the silhouettes of trees. The walker navigates by the trees' positions. It knows the forest as a grid of vertical references, a map of trunk-locations that it carries internally and updates as it moves. It does not perceive the canopy. It does not perceive the floor's fine detail. It perceives trunks — their locations, their sizes, their spacing — and it moves between them with the confidence of a creature that has never encountered an obstacle it did not already know about.

The eyes are sensitive to the trees' venting. The walker can see — or rather, can detect — the pressure waves that radiate from a venting valve, a thermal shimmer in the cool air that is invisible to human eyes but that the walker's mineral-laced visual system registers as a bright point. To the walker, the forest is a field of lights — each trunk a column of bright points, each bright point a valve in operation, the whole forest a glittering array of active sources overlaid on the grid of trunk-silhouettes. The walker sees the forest's *activity*, not its structure. It sees what the forest is *doing*, not what the forest *is*.

This is how it finds its food. The substrate richest in the compounds it needs is the substrate closest to the roots, and the roots are closest to the trunks, and the trunks are most active at the base, where the gas compression chambers operate. The walker follows the bright points to the ground, digs where the points are brightest, and eats the material that the trees' root chemistry has most recently processed. It is eating the freshest product. It is eating the trees' waste. The mineral compounds it extracts are the byproducts of the trees' pneumatic cycle — the residue left after the roots have extracted what they need and released what they don't. The walker recycles this residue. Its digestion breaks down the compounds into forms the roots can re-absorb. Its deposits — the extrusions it leaves on the floor — are not waste but *fertilizer*, pre-processed mineral substrate that the roots take up and reincorporate.

The walker is a stomach that the forest has grown legs for. A mobile digestive organ. A piece of the system that has been externalized, given autonomy, and set loose to roam the factory floor, processing material and redepositing it in a pattern that distributes nutrients across the root network. The walker does not know this. The walker is not aware of the forest as a system. The walker is hungry, and the ground is food, and the food is near the bright points, and the bright points are everywhere, and the walker moves between them and eats and deposits and moves on, and it has been doing this for longer than the canopy has been closed, and it will continue doing this for as long as the floor produces material and the trees produce bright points and the walker's legs carry it between them.

## What Else

There are other things.

The forest is a continent, and a continent holds more than three species. There are organisms here that you will not see because they are small — the size of a crawler's antenna-segment, or smaller — and they live in the grooves of the trees' scaled integument, in the microscopic spaces between the layers of woody tissue, in the mineral substrate's pore structure, in the chemical films that coat the valve interiors. There is an entire ecology at a scale that human eyes cannot access, and it is no doubt as complex and as balanced and as old as the ecology of the visible creatures. The forest is not three species. The forest is three *visible* species and an unknown number of invisible ones, and the invisible ones are probably doing most of the work.

There are also organisms you will not see because they are rare. The forest is large, and large systems produce niches that are narrow but real — a species that lives only on the upper surfaces of the canopy's light-transmitting branches, feeding on the photosynthate that the fliers harvest from below. A species that lives only in the deepest excavations, in the freshly exposed substrate that the walkers have mined, occupying the brief window before the material reconstitutes. A species that lives only on fallen trunks, in the slow decomposition of the wood as it is absorbed back into the floor — a specialist in death, in the forest's rare moments of structural failure, taking apart what the rest of the system spends its life maintaining.

And there are things you will not see because they are the forest.

The trees are not trees. The description said this, and it is true, but it is more true than the description could afford to acknowledge. The trees are *organs*. The root connections — the description's "almost certainly connected" — are not connections between individuals. There are no individuals. There is one organism, and the trunks are its columns, and the canopy is its ceiling, and the valves are its pores, and the sound is its breathing, and the weather is its climate regulation, and the crawlers are its skin maintenance, and the fliers are its canopy symbiosis, and the walkers are its digestion, and the forest is not a forest. The forest is *a body*. A single body the size of a continent, lying on the mineral plain with its roots in the earth and its canopy in the sky and its skin in the air and its stomachs walking its floor, and it has been alive for a time that is difficult to express in human units because human units are calibrated to human lifespans, and this organism does not experience time at that resolution. It experiences time in venting cycles and canopy shifts and mineral substrate renewals. It experiences time the way a mountain experiences erosion. Slowly. In bulk. By the layer.

## What It Is Like to Live Here

You asked what lives here, and the answer is: one thing lives here. One thing, expressed as a forest, expressed as a factory, expressed as a sound, expressed as a climate, expressed as a floor and a canopy and a column of trunks and a population of creatures that are its skin and its stomach and its canopy-tenders. One thing, wearing the shape of a landscape. One thing, so large that it has become a place, so old that it has become a climate, so loud that it has become a medium, so complete that it has become a world.

And within it — on it, in it, around it, through it — the small lives. The crawlers on their trunks. The fliers in their light. The walkers on their floor. The invisible things in their invisible niches. Each one a part of the body, and each one a life of its own, and each one unaware of the other, and each one sustaining the other, and each one sustained.

This is what lives in the Booming Forest. Not a collection of creatures. Not an ecosystem. A body. A single, continental, ancient, deafening body, and the things that live on its skin and in its gut and along its arteries, doing what they do, being what they are, tending what they tend, in the sound and the light and the cool dry air, for as long as the body breathes, which is to say for as long as the sound continues, which is to say for as long as anyone can remember, which is to say: for longer than the question.
