# What Lives Here

The first thing to understand is that nothing here thinks of itself as living *in* the city. The city is the medium. The city is the weather, the terrain, the seasonal clock. To the things that remain, the buildings are geology—no more inhabited than a mountain is inhabited by the lichen on its face. They are surface. They are substrate.

---

**The Fungal Mats**

Begin with what isn't fauna at all but which everything else is built upon. The coatings—that's what they should be called, the coatings—are not single organisms. They are consortiums. Layer upon layer of compatible biochemistries, each metabolizing the waste of the last, drawing energy from gradients that should not exist: the sulfur dioxide seeping from ancient flues, the ferrous ions bleeding from corroded rebar, the slow oxidative chemistry of iron pyrite deposits exposed to perpetual moisture. What looks like a smear of black-green on a dripping wall is actually a stratified community—dozens of distinct growth forms, each a millimeter thick, each consuming the layer beneath it as it dies and compresses into a dark, dense paste. The paste hardens eventually. It becomes a deposit. The deposits build up over decades and fill in cracks, seal joints, and create new surfaces for the next generation to colonize. In this way, the city is slowly being swallowed by its own immune response, sealed and remodeled by something that has no intention and no mind.

Some sections of the coatings glow. Faintly. A dull phosphorescence, deep blue-green, visible only in the deeper tunnels where the external light fades entirely. These patches pulse on cycles that correspond to the rise and fall of pressure in the steam mains beneath them. Whether the fungi are responding to vibration, temperature, or chemical changes in their substrate is unclear. What is clear is that the deeper one descends into the city's layers, the more prevalent the glow becomes, and the more it seems to synchronize across entire corridors, breathing in time with the great machines.

---

**The Isopod-Forms**

The pale, thumb-sized isopods are the closest thing this world has to a keystone species. They are everywhere. Their formal classification, if such a thing still mattered, would place them far from any terrestrial isopod—they are not crustaceans in the strict sense—but the convergence of form is extraordinary. They are dorsoventrally flattened, oval in cross-section, their backs covered in overlapping plates of a translucent, mineral-hardened chitin that ranges from milky white in the youngest to a deep amber in the oldest and most mineral-saturated. They possess seven pairs of legs, each tipped with a fine, double-hooked claw adapted for gripping wet, vertical surfaces. Their antennae are unusually long—twice the length of their bodies—and they move constantly, sweeping the air in front of them, tasting the moisture film for chemical gradients.

They feed by scraping. Their mouthparts are a complex arrangement of rasping mandibles and brush-like maxillae that can shear through fungal coatings down to bare substrate in minutes when they feed in numbers. A wall thick with growth in the morning can be stripped clean by evening if a migration passes through it. They do not feed individually—they swarm a surface, strip it, and move on, leaving behind a faintly scored, raw-looking patch that will be recolonized within days.

Their reproduction is tied to the cycles of the deep engines. When the pressure in the steam mains builds to its peak—on a cycle that takes approximately eleven days to complete—the isopods mass in the warmer, drier crevices near the exhaust vents and molt simultaneously. The sight of it, in the aggregate, is a thing of terrible, quiet intensity: millions of creatures, pressed flank to flank, splitting their old skins and emerging pale and soft, the cast-off shells accumulating in drifts in the corners of chambers. The newly molted are nearly translucent, their internal organs visible through their skins, and they remain still for several hours as their new exoskeletons harden and mineralize. During this period, they are entirely vulnerable, and the centipede-forms know it.

The molting drifts do not go to waste. The cast-off chitin is rich in accumulated minerals, and the isopods that have already hardened will return to feed on the shells of their kin, completing a mineral cycle that is, in its way, perfect. Nothing leaves the system. Everything is re-consumed.

There is a variation—a larger form, two or three times the standard size, darker in coloration, with a more elongated body and proportionally shorter legs. These are not a different species. They are simply older. Their growth is indeterminate, continuing slowly across a lifespan that may exceed decades. The very largest, perhaps the length of a hand, are solitary, territorial, and aggressive, defending prime feeding territories near the warmest exhaust vents with sudden, lunging charges that send smaller individuals scattering in all directions. Their shells are thick and dark, nearly opaque, and they move with a deliberate, weighty patience that distinguishes them from the skittering urgency of the young.

**The Centipede-Forms**

If the isopods are the city's herbivores, the centipede-forms are its wolves.

They are long—the largest specimens reach well over a meter—and they are built for the vertical world they inhabit. Their bodies are ribbon-flat, allowing them to slide through gaps between overlapping facades only centimeters wide. Each segment bears a pair of legs, and the legs are not uniform: the anterior pairs are shorter and thicker, adapted for gripping, while the posterior pairs are longer and more slender, providing propulsion. The result is a gait that is not the smooth, ripulating flow of a true centipede but something more mechanical, more deliberate—the creature grips with its forward legs, pulls, grips again, and its rear legs trail behind, making micro-adjustments to maintain contact with the surface. It is a climbing machine.

Their coloration is striking. The dorsal surface is a deep, lacquered maroon, almost black in low light, but the ventral surface—the belly, the legs—is a pale, waxy yellow. When they move across a ceiling, this pale underside is what one sees, a ribbon of yellow flowing across the damp stone, and the effect is deeply, viscerally unsettling. Their heads are dominated by a pair of forcipules—modified front legs that have evolved into piercing, injecting pincers—and these are proportionally enormous, nearly a quarter of the body length, curving inward like the mandibles of a stag beetle. They hunt by ambush. They find a position above a well-trafficked route—a crack where isopods funnel through in single file, a ledge above a feeding aggregation—and they wait. They can wait for days, their metabolism slowing to a crawl, their bodies flush against the stone, nearly invisible against the dark patina. When prey passes within range, the strike is instantaneous. The forcipules drive downward, piercing the isopod's shell at the joint between plates, and the injection begins. The venom is not neurotoxic. It is proteolytic—a digestive enzyme, essentially acid, that begins dissolving the prey's internal tissues before it is fully dead. The isopod's legs continue to move for some minutes after the strike, a reflexive scrabbling that gradually diminishes as the body empties.

The centipede-forms do not congregate, except during their own reproductive phase, which is mercifully rare and which involves a process so alien and so unsettling that it suffices to say only this: it involves the deep substrate, it takes weeks, and those who have witnessed the aftermath describe the scene as resembling a wound in the city's flesh, a place where the boundary between organic and mineral has broken down entirely.

**The Fliers**

The air is not empty.

The fliers are the hardest to characterize because they are the hardest to observe. They are small—the largest no bigger than a thumbnail—and they exist in such dense aggregations that individual forms are nearly impossible to distinguish. What one sees, looking up through the mist, is a shimmer—a shifting, particulate haze, like smoke that moves with purpose. They drift in columns, in eddies, in slow-rotating spirals that correspond to the convection currents rising from the warm lower layers. They are riding the city's breath.

Their wings are not wings in the conventional sense. They are membranes of a thin, mineralized tissue—almost crystalline, almost glass—supported by a framework of tubular veins that carry not hemolymph but a pressurized fluid. The wings flex and oscillate at frequencies too high to track visually, and the mechanism is not muscular in the traditional sense. There are tiny structures at the wing base—chambers, valves—that appear to be driven by changes in the ambient pressure of the steam that permeates the air. When the air is warm and the pressure is up, they fly. When the air cools, they settle, clinging to walls in dense, shingled masses, their wings folding flat against their bodies, their metabolic rate dropping to near-zero. They become, in effect, part of the wall.

What they feed on is unclear. They do not appear to feed on the fungal mats. They do not appear to prey on the isopods. They may be filter-feeders, straining the mineral-rich moisture from the air itself, extracting trace compounds from the perpetual mist. Their bodies, when examined—which is to say, when one settles on an exposed hand and is caught—seem to contain little but a diffuse network of fine tubes and a small, dense concentration of tissue near the anterior end that might be a nervous system, or might be something else entirely. They register against the skin not as impact but as sensation—a faint static crackle, a prickling, as though the air itself were becoming slightly more alive where they touch it.

Their populations fluctuate wildly. On some days, the air is so thick with them that visibility drops to a few meters, and the sound of their collective wing-movement is a high, thin whine that blends with the hiss of venting steam. On other days, they are nearly absent, and the air feels strangely, emptily clear. The pattern, if there is one, seems connected to the deep pressure cycles—the same eleven-day rhythm that drives the isopod molting—but offset, arriving on a delay, as though the fliers are responding to changes that have not yet propagated through the system.

**The Deep Things**

Below the first hundred meters of construction, the character of the Crawl changes. The isopods become larger, paler, and slower. The centipede-forms become longer, thinner, and lose their pigmentation entirely, their translucent skins revealing the dark tracery of their internal plumbing. The fliers do not descend this far. Instead, the air in the deep layers is occupied by something else—something that does not fly but floats.

They are called, for lack of a better term, the Drifters. They are gelatinous. They are roughly spherical, ranging from the size of a marble to the size of a head, and they hang in the saturated air of the deep chambers by means of gas-filled sacs within their bodies. They are almost entirely transparent, and they move with excruciating slowness, nudged through the corridors by the faintest air currents. Their surfaces are sticky—not adhesive, but tacky, like wet gelatin—and they collect particulate matter from the air: dust, mineral grains, fragments of shed chitin, spores. This debris coats their surfaces and is slowly, over the course of days or weeks, drawn inward, engulfed, and digested by enzymes within the body's matrix. They are, in essence, mobile filters, living traps for the city's airborne detritus.

They reproduce by budding. A Drifter that has fed sufficiently will develop a small protrusion on its surface—a bulge that grows over days into a smaller copy of the parent, connected by a thin stalk that eventually severs. The new Drifter drifts away. The parent continues. There is no death in the conventional sense among the Drifters; they simply accumulate material until they become too dense to stay aloft, at which point they sink, slowly, to the floor of whatever chamber they occupy, and settle into a permanent, dormant state, their outer surfaces hardening into a mineral shell that, over decades, becomes indistinguishable from the deposits of the Weep. In this way, the deep chambers are slowly filling—not with water, but with the fossilized remains of the Drifters, stacked layer upon layer, a geological stratum of living origin.

---

**The Apex**

There is one more form of life in the High City that must be acknowledged, though it is seen so rarely that its existence is, for many inhabitants of the Crawl, merely an environmental fact—a pressure, a presence, felt but not understood.

It occupies the deep factory levels. It does not move, or if it does, it moves so slowly that no observer has ever witnessed the motion. It is not a creature in the sense of something with a body, a head, limbs, a distinct front and back. It is a network. A web of dark, fibrous tissue—something between mycelium and nervous system—that extends through the mineral deposits of the factory floors, threading between the ancient machinery, infiltrating the ductwork and the steam pipes. It is warm to the touch. It pulses, faintly, with a rhythm that matches the stroke of the great pistons. It has, over centuries, grown into the machines themselves, its tendrils wrapping around drive shafts, its fibers lining the interiors of boilers, its mass filling the spaces between bricks where mortar has dissolved.

Whether it is feeding on the machines or driving them is a question that assumes a distinction the organism itself may not recognize. The machines still turn. The steam still rises. The pressure still builds and falls on its eleven-day cycle. And the network grows, incrementally, filling the city from below like a root system filling soil, its tendrils reaching upward through cracks and conduits, always upward, always toward the moisture, toward the warmth, toward the surface where the isopods swarm and the centipede-forms hunt and the fliers shimmer in the gray light.

The Crawl do not avoid it. They do not approach it. They exist around it, within it, indifferent to it, as fish are indifferent to the water. It is the medium in which they live. It is, perhaps, what they have always lived in. The city above, the machines, the Weep, the Crawl—all of it may be, in the end, merely the surface expression of something vast and patient and incomprehensibly old, growing upward through the accumulated strata of a world that forgot its makers, filling the empty spaces with itself, one slow pulse at a time, on a cycle that takes eleven days, that has always taken eleven days, that will always take eleven days, the heartbeat of a city that does not know it is alive.
