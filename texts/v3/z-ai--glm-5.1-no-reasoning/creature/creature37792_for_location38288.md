The first thing to understand about them is that they do not see. They have not needed to see for perhaps ten thousand years. In a place where light is a rumor and distance is measured in the decay of sound, eyes are an insult, a vestigial lie. They were born into the black, and the black bred the sight out of them the way deep caves breed the pigment from fish. Their skulls slope back, smooth and unblemished by brow or socket, and the faces that anchor them are broad, flat, and dominated by the nose: a structure of such intricate, scrolled cartilage that it resembles the furling of a fern frond. They smell the forest the way you might read a book—absorbing narratives of predator proximity, the moisture index of the moss, the chemical distress of a Gargantua root two kilometers distant. Their olfactory language is so dense that a single exhalation of the cold, peat-heavy air tells them everything: who passed this way, how long ago, what they ate, what they feared, and whether the condensation is trending toward freeze or thaw.

And their ears. Their ears are the instruments of their geography. They are large, asymmetric, and articulated—cupped forward to triangulate the arrhythmic drip of condensation off a leaf forty meters away, or swiveled back to catch the subsonic groan of trunk-flex. The shell of each ear is shaped like the specialized echo-chamber of a bat, but scaled to the register of the Gargantua. They hear the geometry of the dark. When one of them cups a hand behind their ear and turns their head, they are not looking; they are mapping the void, building a topology of space from the rebound of a single, wet drop striking a hollow fern node. The forest is a continuous, ringing carillon to them, and they are its listeners.

They are not tall. Tallness is a liability in a place where the vertical axis belongs to the trees. They are low, dense, and center-heavy. A full-grown adult stands perhaps a meter and a half, but their shoulders are absurdly wide, their torsos thick with the coiled musculature necessary to navigate the moss-sea. They move through the vegetative substrate the way a swimmer moves through water—lowering themselves, pushing with broad, splayed feet, pulling with arms that are disproportionately long and end in hands of startling dexterity. Their fingers are thick, the pads broad and calloused, but the tips possess a sensitivity that borders on the magnetic. They read the bark. They read the moss. They read the stone beneath, sensing the tremor of root-movement and the percussion of distant collapse through their palms and the soles of their feet.

Their skin is the color of the substrate—a mottled, variable darkness that shifts from the deep green-brown of sphagnous mat to the mineral grey of fresh basalt. It is not pigment alone. From childhood, they anoint themselves with the forest. They rub the gelid lichen into their pores, press the dark, mushroomy paste of half-rotted moss into the creases of their joints, allow the condensation to bead and merge with the oils of their skin until they are, by any functional measure, an extension of the surface they crawl across. They do not wash. To wash would be to announce yourself, to become a naked, screaming presence in a world of subtle chemical signatures. They carry the rank, cold, chlorophyll-and-decomposition smell of the place as their primary identity.

They live in the inter-trunk voids, the vast, cathedral-like spaces where the Gargantua roots do not touch. They do not build. The rough, basalt circles near the cliff-edge are theirs, yes—but they are the habitations of their youth, their history, their edge-dwellers. The ones who still look outward. The ones who remember that there is an elsewhere. The ones who still harvest the swarm-crickets and strip the useful fibers from the lower fronds and return to the rim to watch the darkness below. But deeper in, where the Gargantua are the only architecture and the moss is meters deep and the sound never stops, they do not build. They inhabit. They find the root-caverns, the hollows where two trunks have grown together and left a cleft warm with metabolizing wood, the bowls in the substrate where the sphagnum has compressed into something like firm, damp felt. They curl together there—families, kin-groups, what you might call tribes if the word did not imply too much structure—and they sleep to the sound of the dripping, and they wake when the sound changes.

They are few. The vastness swallows them the way it swallows everything. A population that might number in the low hundreds is stretched across hundreds of thousands of cubic kilometers of moss and black and tree-groan. They find each other by the same principle that governs all exchange here: the acoustic and chemical signatures of the forest itself. A group in distress alters the local soundscape—striking the trunk of a Gargantua with a stone in a rhythm that does not belong, releasing the sharp, acrid sap of a bruised frond to carry on the cold thermal wind. The nearest group hears, smells, and turns their path. But "nearest" is a word with little meaning here. The response may come in hours, or days. The forest is patient. They are patient. They have no concept of urgency that maps onto your understanding of the word. When the nearest group is a hundred kilometers of crawling through the moss-sea away, you learn to wait, and you learn to trust that the sound will carry, and that the ones who hear will come.

They do not speak. Not in the way you understand. Their communication is the communication of the substrate. They drum. They strike the surfaces of the Gargantua roots with stone and bone and the hard, chitinous shells of the prey they harvest, producing deep, resonant impacts that travel for kilometers through the wood fiber. The trunks are their telegraph lines, and the Gargantua are, unknowing, the medium of their language. A complex rhythm tapped into the root-system of a tree may arrive at the ear of a distant group distorted, delayed, attenuated—but it arrives. They have no words for things that cannot be expressed in rhythm: the approach of a predator, the ripeness of a fungus-field, the death of a member, the need to move. They speak in time and impact, and they listen with their whole bodies.

They do not make tools. Not in the sense of shaped, persistent objects. They use what the forest provides—they pry up the sharp, fresh shards of basalt that the young stone exhales when it fractures, and they carry them until they dull, and then they drop them back into the moss. They harvest the dense, fibrous cord of the epiphytic tangles to bind and pull. They use the hollow nodes of the giant ferns as vessels, as drums, as the resonant chambers of their acoustic lives. But they do not keep. They do not hoard. A tool that leaves the hand returns to the substrate; a vessel that empties is set down; a stone that dulls is given back. They leave nothing in the moss that the moss cannot digest. Perhaps this is why the cliff-edge structures feel so forlorn—because they are the exceptions, the stubborn persistence of form in a place that devours form. The ones who built them were trying to make a mark. The rest have long since stopped trying.

They eat. They eat the things that the forest offers in the brief, explosive moments of life and death that punctuate the grinding cycle. They eat the swarm-crickets when the chirring clouds boil up from the moss-pits—they wade into the dense, whining mass and scoop them by the handful, crushing the chitin between their broad molars and swallowing the hot, fatty bodies whole. They eat the gelatinous fungi that fruit from the Gargantua roots, the pale, phosphorescent nodules that push up through the sphagnum in the warmest hollows and give off the faintest, greenish luminescence—a light so weak it is felt more than seen, but to their adapted eyes, it may be the only light they have ever known. They eat the eggs of the canopy-predators when those eggs fall, and they eat the predators themselves when the forest—through disease, through age, through the violence of a localized storm—brings them down.

They do not hunt. They harvest. The distinction is critical. They are not the apex of this ecosystem. The apex is the forest itself—the Gargantua, the moss, the never-ending process of growth and decomposition that is the true consumer here. They are scavengers, marginalia, an afterthought of the biomass. They survive because the forest produces more than it can contain, and the overflow—the dead insect, the fallen fruit, the cracked egg—is sufficient for a population so small. They do not compete. They do not war. They have no territory to defend, because territory implies boundaries, and in the moss-sea, the only boundary is the limit of the sound.

They are old. Or rather, their way of being is old. The cliff-edge structures date back centuries, perhaps millennia—but the people who made them were not different from the people who now crawl in the deep. They were the ones who stood at the edge of the dark and tried to build a wall against it. Their descendants went further. Their descendants stopped building. Their descendants learned that the dark is not an enemy but a medium, and that the only way to survive in the lung of something that respires over geological time is to respire with it.

They do not think of themselves as separate. This is the hardest thing to understand. They do not have a word for "human" or "person" or "self" that excludes the moss, the tree, the drip. When they drum the root, they are not sending a message; they are participating in a conversation the forest is already having. When they anoint themselves with lichen, they are not disguising themselves; they are completing themselves. When they curl in the root-caverns and sleep to the sound of the dripping, they are not hiding; they are resting in the palm of the place that made them.

They do not die in the way you understand. They do not bury their dead, because burial requires a distinction between the body and the ground, and they do not make that distinction. When one of them ceases to breathe—when the cold or the predator or the simple exhaustion of the long crawl through the moss takes them—they are left where they fall. The substrate receives them. The decomposition begins within hours. The sphagnum compresses around them, the fungi colonize them, the roots draw them down. By the time a month has passed, there is nothing left but a warmth in the moss and a faint, sweet chemical signature that lingers in the noses of their kin for a season.

They do not grieve. Or rather, they grieve the way the forest grieves—by absorbing, by continuing, by converting the loss into substrate. When a Gargantua falls—a cataclysm that reshapes the local soundscape and sends a shockwave through the root-network that they can feel in their bones—they attend. They crawl for days to reach the site of the fall. They climb the warm, metabolizing trunk and sit in the clefts of the bark. They listen to the new sounds the collapse has made—the open space, the changed acoustics, the altered drip-patterns. They do not mourn the tree. They witness its transformation. They are the congregation at the funeral of a god, and the funeral lasts for decades, and the god becomes the ground, and the ground becomes them.

They do not wonder what lies beyond the plateau. The ones who did—the ones who looked out from the cliff-edge into the darkness below and felt the pull of the lower altitudes—those ones left. Perhaps they are the ancestors of the peoples of the lowlands, the warm places, the places where light is real and the sky is more than a rumor. Perhaps they are lost. Perhaps they found something better. But the ones who stayed, the ones who went deeper, the ones who let the forest close around them like water over a stone—they are the ones who chose the dark, and the dark chose them back.

They are not unhappy. They are not anything you would recognize. They are a frequency in the hum, a pattern in the drip, a warmth in the moss. They are the breath in the exhalation. They are the listeners at the edge of the green throat, and their song is the sound of the forest listening to itself, and their silence is the silence of a place where nothing needs to be said because everything is already known, felt, heard, smelled, tasted, drummed into the roots of the titans and carried on the cold, wet air to the farthest edge of the young, dark, endless world.
