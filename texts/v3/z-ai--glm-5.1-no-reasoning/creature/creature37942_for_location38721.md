No one *lives* in the Weeping Ward in the way people live above it—those who cross the retaining walls and enter the dry, vertical city where water is a utility, not a condition. Up there, life is a series of choices. Down here, life is a series of accommodations. 

The residents of the ward are defined by the pressure of the water table, by the humidity that makes a membrane of the air. They are not a community in any civic sense; they are a sediment, the human residue left behind in a place that resists human occupation. They are the ones who sank when everything else was raised.

They are called, by those above, the Sumped.

---

You do not see them at first because they have adapted to the ward's palette. Their clothing—layers of salvaged cotton, wool, and synthetic mesh—absorbs the ambient wetness until it hangs from their frames like the fronds of the ferns. They wear the dark greens and rust-browns of the ward, stained by the iron-heavy water that weeps from the pipes and the moss that grows on the shoulders and hems of garments left too long in the damp. When they are still—watching from the gaps between the pumping stations, standing in the recessed doorways of the half-sunk buildings—they dissolve into the background, becoming just another vertical element in the ward's humid machine.

Their skin tells the story of the place. It is pallid, bloodless, pulled taut over the knuckles and cheekbones, pruned at the fingertips and the pads of the feet in a permanent state of maceration. They do not sweat; there is no point, when the air is already a liquid. Instead, their skin has thickened into a kind of organic seal, a barrier against the fungal spores that drift through the haze. Yet even this adaptation is imperfect. You see it in the dark patches on their necks and the creases of their elbows—the green-gray bloom of ward-rot, a slow, superficial colonization that they treat with scrapings of lye soap and the astringent sap of the tougher ferns. They do not cure it; they negotiate with it. The rot is just another tenant.

They move with a specific economy. The Sumped do not hurry; to hurry is to slip on the red granite, to tear a muscle, to displace the heavy, wet air in a way that exhausts the lungs. They walk with a low center of gravity, a slow, deliberate pacing that matches the four-or-five-times-a-minute exhalation of the piston pumps. They navigate by the feel of the cobblestones through the thin soles of their boots, knowing which pools are shallow impressions and which are collapsed drains that would swallow a leg. They do this in the near-dark, navigating by the smeared amber reflections on the wet stone, their eyes dilated wide to catch the faintest light. Their eyes are the most startling feature: pale, often rheumy, but sensitive to a spectrum of gloom that a drylander would perceive as absolute blackness.

---

The Sumped do not build; they occupy. They live in the interstitial spaces of the ward's machine. Their homes are the boiler rooms of the pumping stations, the upper floors of the sagging brick tenements that the plants haven't fully claimed, the crawlspaces beneath the walkways where the steam pipes run. They do not fight the water; they elevate themselves above it by inches. A bed is a wooden pallet suspended by rusted wire from a ceiling beam, a cocoon of synthetic blankets that are never truly dry, only damp and damp and damper. A hearth is a salvaged electric coil glowing a dull red, warming a dented kettle, boiling the ward-water to kill the iron-taste and the microbes.

When the water reclaims a space, the Sumped simply move to the next driest elevation. They are nomads in a space of two hundred meters, shifting in a slow vertical migration, following the line of least resistance.

They are caretakers, though they would not use the word. They are the ones who re-wrap the tarred felt on the sweating pipes, who clear the drainage channels of fern-root and moss before the water can back up and overwhelm a junction. It is a symbiosis born of necessity. If the pumps fail, the ward fills, and they drown. They know the sound of the pistons better than they know their own heartbeats; a change in the rhythm, a missed beat in the mechanized exhalation, will bring them out of the gloom in silence, converging on the station with wrenches and wire, working in the suffocating heat until the breath is steady again. They are the immune system of the Weeping Ward, fighting off the infections of stagnation and collapse.

---

There is no animal life in the ward, and so the Sumped have become strangely vegetal themselves. They do not speak much. The humidity is hostile to the vocal cords, and the noise of the pumps drowns out casual conversation. When they do speak, it is in a low, guttural register, a sub-vocalization that travels through the vibration of the wet brick more than through the air. They communicate primarily through touch—a hand on the shoulder to indicate presence, a tap on a pipe to signal a shift in the water pressure, a shared grip to haul a heavy pump-component across a flooded floor. 

They have no songs, but they hum. A continuous, tuneless drone that harmonizes with the thrum of the machinery and the white noise of the water. It is a sound of endurance, a way of marking time in a place where the days are measured only by degrees of warmth. 

They do not look at the sky. There is no sky in the ward, only the yellow-gray haze and the undersides of the higher city's infrastructure. They look down, at the water, at the stone, at the slow encroachment of the green. They know the names they have given to the ferns—not scientific names, but names of the dead, of those who have sunk into the ward and stayed. When a building finally succumbs to the roots and the rot, and the vines pull the bricks down into the wet earth, the Sumped say the ward has *remembered* it.

---

To stand in the Weeping Ward is to feel the system working around you. The heat on your skin. The wet in your lungs. The sound in your bones. But you are a visitor, and the system will eventually reject you, push you back up the verdigris stairs to the dry air above. 

The Sumped are not visitors. They are the ones who were not rejected. They are the human element that the machine has absorbed—the gaskets and filters made of flesh and bone, holding on in the persistent wet, breathing in time with the pumps, growing still, sinking slowly, becoming part of the green and the damp and the breath.
