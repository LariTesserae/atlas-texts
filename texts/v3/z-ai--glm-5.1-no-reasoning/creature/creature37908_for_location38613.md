To live on the Plateau of the Great Engine is to never know the sun. It is to be born into a world of gradients, where shadow is not an absence of light but a medium you swim through, thick and warm. The beings here do not look up; the churning ash-wool of the stratosphere offers nothing to them. They look down. They read the ground the way a sailor reads the sea, feeling the endless, bone-deep shudder of the gears beneath the crust. To them, the Great Engine is not a machine; it is the earth, the tide, the weather, and the god of the damp dark.

And the place is crowded. It teems. Life here is not sparse; it is simply hidden, wearing the texture of the world.

**The Oretreaders**

You would not see the herds until they move. They are the Oretreaders—the great, six-legged ungulates whose bodies are a mirror of the plateau. Their hides are not fur or skin, but a thick, overlapping leather, the exact hue of the wet, ferric slate. When they rest, they are indistinguishable from the massive, whale-backed swells of rock. They are the color of rust and rain.

They are born blind, their eye sockets sealed over with calloused skin, but they are far from senseless. Their wide, splayed hooves are their primary organs of perception. Through the pads of their feet, they feel the world: they can detect the minute seismic shifts of a predator's weight three miles off, and they can feel the specific, harmonic frequency of a distant vent-fissure exhaling. They follow the warmth the way a compass follows north.

When an Oretreader drinks, it does not lower its head to a pool—there are no pools. Instead, it drags its broad, flat tongue across the shallow slicks of condensation, lapping up the tepid, mineral-rich film that beads on the dark stone. They congregate in the bruised-orange halos of the vents, grazing on the pale, chimney-like fungi. They are ruminants of the deep. When they chew the ash-white stalks, the sound is a low, wet crunch, immediately swallowed by the hiss of the wind. Their young—smaller, with disproportionately long legs that splay out like spiders—are born running, their hooves slipping on the damp stone, already attuned to the heartbeat of the Engine.

**The Hollow-Wings**

Above the herds, in the perpetual twilight, the sky is not empty. It is a thoroughfare for the Hollow-Wings. They are not birds; they are closer to monstrous, gliding manta rays of the dark, their wingspans stretching wide to ride the hot updrafts that vent from the world’s lungs. They are leather and sinew, silent, lacking any vocalization.

To navigate the pitch, the Hollow-Wings have evolved echolocation sacs—bioluminescent organs that pulse along their underbellies and throats. In the smothering dark, these sacs bloom with sudden, ghostly flashes of deep-sea green and twilight blue. It is a silent language, a radar painted in cold light. A flash of blue might signal an updraft; a rapid pulse of green, a diving strike.

When they hunt, the light goes out. They fold their wings and drop from the overcast like stones, opening their huge, hinged jaws at the last second. They do not bite; they engulf, swallowing smaller grazers whole. The only warning an Oretreader receives is a sudden shift in the barometric pressure, a subtle wind-shadow passing overhead, and a faint, bioluminescent afterimage seared into the darkness.

**The Ash-Grazers and the Drip-Worms**

The smaller life of the plateau lives and dies in the micro-climates of the fungal groves. The Ash-Grazers are multi-segmented, armadillo-like creatures that scuttle on dozens of bristly legs. They are detritivores, feeding on the dead, maroon-colored thermophilic stalks that wither at the edges of the vents. Their shells are encrusted with a symbiotic slate-moss, making them look like nothing more than animated piles of damp gravel.

Then there are the Drip-Worms, pale, gelatinous things that live in the shallow depressions where condensation gathers. They are barely sentient, simply living filters. As the mist falls, they absorb it, their translucent bodies swelling with tepid water until they look like blobs of smeared glass. When the thermals shift and the heat rises, they excrete the moisture, leaving the faint, mineral trails that the Oretreaders follow from vent to vent. They are the rivers of this place, living, blob-like streams that crawl an inch a day.

**The Keepers in the Deep**

But who maintains the Great Engine? The plateau is entirely untouched by intentional design—no roads, no outposts, no buildings. Yet, the machinery below is planetary in scale. It roars, it spins, it breathes. The vent-fissures glow, the exhaust booms, the heat rises. It is a dynamic, ongoing process.

There are rumors among the herd-beasts, a genetic memory that makes even the great Oretreaders shy away from certain deep cracks in the slate. It is a pheromone of ancient warning, a smell of burnt ozone and old blood.

Down in the sickly, orange-lit fissures, where the sulfurous air is thick enough to chew, the mechanical bleeds into the biological. The Keepers are not builders; they are an extension of the Engine itself. They are tall, thin, grey-skinned humanoids, hairless and eyeless like the rest of the fauna, but they walk on two legs. They do not speak. Their hands are not hands; they are fused, chitinous tools—wrenches, picks, and pincers that click and scrape against the stone. They do not think, not in any way we would understand. They are biological subroutines, spawned from some ancient, forgotten hatchery deep in the planet's crust.

When a vent fissure clogs with mineral buildup, the Keepers crawl up from the dark. They work in absolute silence, chipping away at the crystallized sulfur and iron with their fused hands. They do not notice the Oretreaders; they do not notice the Hollow-Wings. They only notice the pressure readings in the pipes, the vibrations of the gears. When the vent is clear, they file back down into the orange-lit depths, disappearing into the mechanical bowels of the world, their chitinous tools clicking a slow, rhythmic fade into the hum of the Engine. They are not the masters of this place; they are its white blood cells, its immune response, tending to a lung that has been breathing for eons and will breathe for eons more.

The Plateau does not care. It simply exhales, and the mist rolls on.
