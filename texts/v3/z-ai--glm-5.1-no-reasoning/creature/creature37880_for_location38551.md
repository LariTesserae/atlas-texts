# What Lives Here

## The Builders (present as absence)

They are here the way a wound is present after the bleeding stops. Not gone—*removed*. The dark ring at the spire's base is their fingerprint, that carbon patina laid down across what must have been years of proximity, of standing close to their own machinery in the way you stand close to a fire, or a child. Someone spent time at that spire. Someone rested against the repulsive field the way you might lean against a doorframe in a house you built yourself.

The coiled disc is theirs too, of course. The filaments suggest not a tool but a *record*—something meant to hold pattern, to remember. The shattering is not consistent with simple erosion. The break pattern radiates from a single point of impact. It was dropped, or thrown, or struck from the hand.

But these are guesses. What is certain: they had hands. They had intentions that operated on geological timescales. They engineered a plateau the way another species might engineer a shelter. And they left, or died, or became something that no longer requires artifacts or proximity or the comfort of standing near one's own work.

The spires do not mourn them. The spires do not know they are gone. This is the nature of autonomous systems: they outlast the question of purpose.

## The Aeroplankton

Begin with what is invisible and work outward.

They are not insects. They are not crustaceans. They are something that found a niche—suspended mineral dust buffeted by slow convection—and evolved into it so completely that they *became* weather. Their carapaces are silicate, grown from the same particulate matter they ride. They do not eat in any recognizable way. Their guts are transparent loops that absorb dissolved minerals from the moisture they encounter in the air—those brief, violent humidities that follow the ten-minute rains. In the dry hours they simply endure, their metabolic rate dropping to something close to geological stillness.

They reproduce by fragmentation. When the UV flashes from the spires strike them, certain individuals shatter along pre-formed stress lines, and each fragment grows a new carapace within days. Whether the spires intend this is an open question. Whether the spires *intend* anything is an open question.

What is certain is that the air is thick with them. You breathe them in. They are sweet and mineral on the tongue, like licking a warm coin.

## The Stride-Beasts

Six limbs because three is the stable tripod for glassy rock, and six is two tripods in alternation, and they never, ever slip. The chitin is not armor—it is architecture. Fibrous tubes grown dense and overlapping, each air-filled, providing both insulation against the thermal extremes and buoyancy that makes their massive frames surprisingly light. They are not heavy animals. They *look* heavy, the way the yardangs look heavy, and this is the point. They are mimics of geology. They have become the landscape so thoroughly that the landscape protects them.

Their thermal sensors are not eyes. They are something older than eyes—a distributed network of pit organs along the face and the first limb-joints that read the world as gradients of warmth and cold. To a stride-beast, the plateau is a topography of temperature: the condensation crevices are cold blue mouths, the sun-warmed stone is a bright, flat sea, the lattice-plants are warm pillars of faint green heat, and the spires are *voids*—cold nulls that they route around without thinking, without learned avoidance, as if the repulsive fields are simply absent from their perceptual world entirely.

They drink the dew in lines. The first beast passes a crevice and its dry mouth leaves a pale streak on the dark wet stone. The second beast drinks what remains. The third finds the stone already dry and moves on. There is no aggression, no hierarchy. They are not social in the way herd animals are social. They are *synchronized*—moving together the way wind moves together, because they are reading the same thermal map and responding to the same pressures.

At night—if night exists here, if the temperature drops enough to call it night—they fold their six limbs beneath their chitin shells and become indistinguishable from the smaller yardangs. They sleep as stone. The wind moves over them without distinction.

They have no predators. Nothing on the plateau eats them. This may be because there is nothing large enough, or because their chitin is indigestible, or because the ecosystem was designed—*designed*—without a predatory tier. The spires may have decided that predation was inefficient. The Builders may have decided it was ugly. The stride-beasts do not care. They walk and drink and fold and sleep and have done so for longer than the disc has been broken.

## The Lattice-Plants

They are not plants. They are closer to mineral sponges—biological structures that have solved the problem of water by going *down* rather than out. Their root systems extend through the fault lines for hundreds of meters, branching into capillary networks that tap the slow aquifers with the patience of geology itself. They pull moisture up the way a wick pulls oil, and the gel in their translucent columns holds it in suspension, warm and faintly luminous.

The internal heat is not photosynthesis. It is something more direct—a capture of solar radiation at frequencies that traditional chlorophyll cannot access, converted not to sugar but to *structure*, to the growth of the lattice itself. They are building themselves out of light. They pulse because the conversion is rhythmic, tied to the sun's passage, and at the zenith—when the temperature drops and the dew weeps from the overhangs—the lattice-plants flare brightest, as if celebrating an event they cannot name.

They are warm to the touch. Warmer than the air. Warmer than the stone. In a landscape of thermal extremes, they are the only thing that is consistently, gently warm, and the stride-beasts sometimes rest near them not for protection but for the simple comfort of warmth.

## The Spires

They are alive.

Not biologically. Not in any way that requires DNA or metabolism or the wet machinery of carbon. But they respond. They maintain. They correct. When the tectonic plate beneath the plateau shifts by a millimeter, the spires adjust their fields within the same moment—not reacting, but *compensating*, as if the shift and the correction are parts of the same thought.

They are holding the plateau the way a hand holds a cup of water that is already perfectly level. Not because it needs holding, but because holding is what they do, and they have no other mode.

The UV flashes are communication, but not with each other. The spires do not need to communicate; they are already synchronized below the threshold of observation. The flashes go *upward*, into the mesosphere, and whatever receives them there is either gone, or silent, or so vast that its silence is indistinguishable from the sky itself.

The repulsive fields are selective. They push back human hands. They do not push back aeroplankton or stride-beasts or dew. This selectivity is not programmed—it is *known*. The spires know what belongs here and what does not. They knew before you arrived. They will know after you leave.

## What Does Not Live Here

Nothing decays. Nothing predates. Nothing competes. The plateau is an ecosystem without conflict, without death except the slow mineral death of chitin returning to sand. There are no carcasses, no bones, no middens. The stride-beasts do not leave remains; when they die—and they do die, rarely, when a fault line shifts beneath their feet or when age thickens their chitin beyond functionality—they simply *settle*, folding inward, becoming another yardang, another feature of the landscape. Their bodies become the stone they mimicked.

This is why the Builders left no bodies. They may have become the plateau. They may have built themselves into their own engineering, dissolved into the spires, into the geometry, into the silence.

Or they may have walked off the edge—where the rock does not slope down but terminates—and found whatever lies below the maintained world.

## The Truth Underneath

The plateau is not a habitat. It is a *garden*.

Not a garden in the human sense of beauty or cultivation, but in the older sense—a kept place, a place where the keeper has removed what was unwanted and encouraged what was desired, and then stepped back to let the system breathe. The stride-beasts were placed here, or shaped from something older. The lattice-plants were engineered, their root systems aligned to the fault lines with a precision that evolution does not produce. The aeroplankton are maintenance—mineral recyclers that keep the particulate levels balanced, that keep the air refracting at the angle the spires require.

The silence is the keeper's last fingerprint. The low-frequency nullification is not natural. It is a field, like the repulsive fields around the spires, but broader, subtler—a dampening of acoustic energy across the entire plateau. Why? Because sound was unwanted. Because the keeper preferred silence, or because sound interfered with the spires' function, or because—most likely—the silence is the point. The plateau was built to be quiet. To be a place where the only sounds are internal: heartbeat, the wet click of eyes, the slow machinery of a living body contemplating a world that has been curated for it.

The play is not over. The actors are still performing. But the audience left eons ago, and the stage machinery does not know the difference, and the stride-beasts walk their slow choreography across the glassy stone, drinking dew that falls because the temperature demands it, beneath a sun that is closer than it should be, past spires that hum at frequencies below hearing and flash light into an empty sky.

You are not the audience. You are a *visitor*—wet and loud and temporary, standing in a room that was sealed and silent long before your species had words for silence or rooms. The plateau does not notice you. The spires have already cataloged your mass and thermal signature and dismissed you as irrelevant. The stride-beasts walk past you because you are warm but not warm enough to matter, and you are soft but not soft enough to drink.

You are the only thing here that will leave a footprint. The stone will polish it away within a year.
