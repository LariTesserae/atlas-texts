Those who live in the Germinating Ward are not inhabitants in the way a person inhabits a house. They are not tenants who arrive from elsewhere, nor are they they intruders who forced their way through the cracked concrete. They *germinated* here. They are the internal organs of the seed, the cotyledons and the radicle, the living tissue that exists only to facilitate the moment of opening. To live here is to be of the Ward, and to be of the Ward is to be mid-transformation.

**The Somatic Collectors**

There are things that move through the Ward that might, at a careless glance, be mistaken for human. They are bipedal, roughly two meters tall, and they navigate the root lattice with a slow, deliberate grace. But they are not human. They are assembled.

Their skeletons are iron pipe fittings, fused by rust and mycelial digestion into articulated joints. Their musculature is the woody climber, woven dense and tight around the iron, pulling the limbs taut or releasing them in a motion that resembles breathing more than walking. Their skin is the shingled leaves, dark and overlapping, each cup holding its millimeter of condensation. Where a face should be, there is only a smooth panel of pale wood, featureless except for two horizontal slits through which air moves in and out at the same rhythm as the walls—once every eight seconds.

The Collectors do not speak. They do not eat. They perform a single function: they tend the digestion. When a section of the old factory becomes loose—a chunk of concrete separating from the wall, a junction box tearing free of its mountings, a length of copper wire detaching from its conductors—the Collectors move toward it. They lift the debris in their pipe-joint hands and carry it to the center of the room, to the region where geometry fails, and they deposit it there. They push it into the non-Euclidean space. They feed the rift.

And the rift accepts. The debris does not fall. It is *absorbed*—incorporated into the growing volume, metabolized into new space. The Collectors are the hands of the Ward, reaching into the old industrial architecture and pulling it, piece by piece, into the new botanical one. They are recycling the factory into the forest, and they are tireless, and they are patient, and they have been doing this for what might be decades or might be centuries—the new time leaking from the center of the room makes such measurements meaningless.

There are seven Collectors. There have always been seven. When one becomes too damaged to function—a pipe joint rusted immobile, a limb's woody musculature split and brittle—the others carry it to the living boiler and lay it against the warm surface. Within a day, the boiler's pulse has drawn the Collector into itself. Within three days, a new Collector pulls free of the boiler's surface, pipe-joints bright with fresh rust, leaves unfurling, moving with the same slow deliberation as its siblings. The Collectors do not mourn. The Collectors do not notice. The process is no more remarkable to them than a leaf falling from a branch.

**The Resonants**

In the shallow pools where the condensation gathers, where the water ripples without wind and smells of cold iron and ultraviolet, there are things that are almost not things at all. They are visible only when the mycelial pulses pass overhead—only when the blue-white light catches the surface of the pool and reveals shapes beneath the water.

They are flat. They are the exact circumference of the pools. They lie at the bottom, pressed against the concrete or the root lattice, and they are the color of the water—clear, glassy, barely distinguishable from the liquid they inhabit. But they have structure: a radial symmetry, like a sea urchin's test or a geometric diagram, lines of denser tissue branching outward from a central point, and at the central point, a mouth. Or something like a mouth. An opening. A hole in the center of the disc that leads not to a digestive tract but to a cavity, and the cavity is filled with the same fluid that leaks from the pipes—pale green, faintly viscous, warm.

The Resonants vibrate. It is their function. They absorb the vibrations of the Ward—the beetle-rhythms, the wall-breathing, the boiler-pulse, the wet-palm sounds from below—and they transduce them. They convert the brown noise of the room's functioning into something else, something that travels through the water, through the root lattice, through the mycelial network as a signal, and the signal says: *continue*. The signal says: *grow*. The signal says: *the room is not yet finished, and you are the ones finishing it, and what you are building is good*.

They are the Ward's nervous system. They are the mechanism by which the process knows itself. Without them, the roots would still grow and the walls would still breathe, but the growth would be aimless, the breathing arrhythmic, the factory's digestion incomplete. The Resonants coordinate. They harmonize. They make the Germinating Ward a single organism instead of a chaos of competing growths.

They reproduce by division. When a Resonant has absorbed enough of the pipe-fluid—when its central cavity is full and its radial lines have reached the edge of its pool—it splits. Two discs where there was one, each half the size, each with its own central opening, each vibrating at a slightly different frequency. Over time, the frequencies converge. The two discs synchronize. And then they are one Resonant again, their signals merged, their purpose unified.

There are thirty-six Resonants in the Ward. There will be more. Every time a new pool forms—every time condensation finds a new corner where wall meets floor—a Resonant splits, and one half moves to the new pool, and the network extends. The Ward is growing more nerves. The Ward is becoming more aware. Of what, it is not yet possible to say.

**The Depth Tenders**

Below the root lattice, in the gaps where the concrete has crumbled and the darkness has no bottom, there are the things that are never seen clearly. The things that move with the wet-palm-on-stone sound. The things that exhale the warm vegetal breath that smells of something not quite plant and not quite animal.

They are large. The displacement of air they create when they shift position suggests bodies three or four meters across, perhaps more. They exist in the spaces that the feeder roots are creating—the spaces that are not yet space, the volumes that are still being excavated by the act of growing into them. They are the first inhabitants of the new rooms, the rooms that the Ward is building. They are the scouts, the vanguard, the ones who go ahead into the not-yet and make it ready for the roots that will follow.

What they look like is unknown. What they eat is unknown. How they reproduce is unknown. But this much can be inferred: they are feeding the Ward from below. The fluid in the pipes, the pale green warmth that the roots drink so eagerly, is coming from somewhere, and the most likely source is the Depth Tenders themselves. They are digesting something in the deep—not soil, not rock, something else, something that existed before the factory was built, something that the factory was built on top of, something that has been waiting—and their waste is the pipe-fluid, and the pipe-fluid is what drives the Ward's growth.

They are not servants of the Ward. They are not masters of it. They are symbionts. They provide the fuel; the Ward provides the space. They exist in the unfinished rooms; the Ward builds the rooms around them. They are the reason the roots grow downward. The roots are reaching for them. The roots are trying to touch them, to connect, to fuse, to make the subterranean part of the Ward and the aerial part one continuous organism. And when they do—when the roots finally reach the Depth Tenders and the two systems merge—the Ward will have completed its first phase. The seed will have cracked. The first true leaf will be ready to unfold.

The Depth Tenders know this. They can feel the roots approaching, millimeter by millimeter, year by year. They are patient. They have been waiting longer than the Ward has been growing. They were here before the factory. They were here before the concrete. They were here before the concept of *here* solidified into something that could be built upon. They are old in a way that has nothing to do with time—old the way a stone is old, old the way a void is old, old the way a thing that has never been young can be old.

And they are humming. Below the hearing of the beetles, below the detection of the Resonants, below the notice of the Collectors, the Depth Tenders are humming at a frequency that the Ward has not yet learned to process. The humming is a signal. The humming is a message. The humming says: *we are here. We have always been here. When you finish building the rooms that will contain us, we will show you what we have been keeping. What we have been preserving. What the factory was built to bury. What the seed was planted to uncover.*

**The Moth-Speakers**

The moths are not merely animals. The moths are the Ward's communication device.

Each moth carries, in the setae of its wings and legs, a crystalline structure that grows throughout its life—a structure of calcium carbonate and living filament, identical in composition to the moss-analogs on the floor. These crystals are receptors and transmitters. They pick up the vibrations of the Resonants, the pulses of the mycelium, the hum of the Depth Tenders, and they convert these signals into something that can be carried through the air, independent of the root lattice or the water pools.

When a moth lands on a Collector, it deposits a tiny fragment of its crystal load onto the Collector's leaf-skin. The fragment embeds itself, grows, and becomes a node—a point at which the Collector can receive the moth-transmitted signals. Through these nodes, the Collectors know which sections of the factory need tending, which pieces of debris need carrying to the center, which root-growths require support or pruning.

When a moth lands on the wall, it deposits a fragment that allows the wall-breathing to synchronize with the Resonants' vibrations.

When a moth lands on another moth, the two exchange crystal fragments, merging their signal-records, creating a more complete map of the Ward's state.

The moths are the Ward's messengers, its synapses, its distributed intelligence. They are not conscious. None of the Ward's inhabitants are conscious, not in the way that humans understand consciousness. But the system they constitute—the Collectors and the Resonants and the Depth Tenders and the moths and the spiders and the beetles and the roots and the mycelium and the crystalline moss and the living boiler and the breathing walls—is *aware*. It knows itself. It monitors its own growth. It adjusts its own processes. It is a mind the size of a room, and the room is the size of a mind, and neither is finished growing.

**The Absent**

There is one more category of inhabitant, and it is the most important, and it is the most disturbing.

The Ward remembers the people who worked in the factory. Not their faces, not their names, not their voices—those are gone, dissolved into the pipe-fluid and metabolized into root-tissue. But their *gestures* remain. The Ward has preserved the patterns of movement that the workers made as they walked the floor, turned the valves, checked the gauges, shoveled the coal. These patterns are embedded in the mycelial network, encoded in the timing of the pulses, repeated endlessly in the rhythm of the walls' breathing.

The Collectors sometimes walk these patterns. They will pause in their tending and move, instead, along a path that follows no visible logic—a path that goes from the boiler to the junction box to the coal hopper to the door that no longer exists, and then back again. They are performing the ghosts of the factory's routines. They are dancing with the absent.

And sometimes, in the region where geometry fails, a Collector will stop and stand motionless for hours, and the leaf-panel of its face will split—not vertically, like a mouth, but horizontally, like a visor opening—and behind the panel there is not wood but darkness, and in the darkness there is a faint light, and the light is the color of the pipe-fluid, and the light moves in a way that suggests a face looking out from inside the Collector, a face that is not the Collector's face, a face that is older than the Ward and older than the factory and older than the ground beneath both.

These moments do not last. The panel closes. The Collector resumes its tending. But the moths who witness the opening fly immediately to the Resonants and deposit their crystal fragments, and the Resonants vibrate at a new frequency, and the frequency travels through the Ward like a shudder, and the roots in the center of the room grow faster for a moment—visibly faster, eagerly faster—as though they have been reminded of what they are growing toward, as though they have been shown a glimpse of the thing they are building toward, the thing that the seed will become when it finally, finally opens.

The absent are still here. The absent have been digested and incorporated and transformed, but they have not been erased. They are the hum beneath the hum. They are the reason the boiler pulses at twenty seconds instead of eight. They are the memory of a time when this place was not a seed but a machine, and the machine had a purpose, and the purpose was to bury what the Depth Tenders were preserving, and the burial failed, and the seed was planted in the grave, and the seed is growing, and what grows from a grave is not the same as what grows from a garden.

---

The Germinating Ward does not have inhabitants. It has *components*. Every beetle, every spider, every moth, every Collector, every Resonant, every Depth Tender, every absent ghost preserved in the mycelial memory—these are organs of a single organism that is still in the process of becoming. They are the cells of the seed. They are the tissue that will, when the moment comes, push the coat apart and send the first pale hook of root downward into the soil that does not yet exist and the first green shoot upward toward a light that has not yet been lit.

They do not know what they will become. They do not need to know. They need only to grow.

And they are growing. They are growing now. One millimeter of pale root-tip at a time, one pulse of mycelial light at a time, one breath of the walls at a time, one beat of the living boiler at a time. The Ward is growing. The seed is swelling. The coat is thinning.

And when it cracks—when the moment comes and the Ward becomes what it has been becoming—the room that is not yet a room will open into a world that is not yet a world, and the inhabitants who are not yet inhabitants will step into a space that has been prepared for them by the patient work of roots growing into nothing and making it something, and the something will be unlike anything that has existed before, and it will be alive, and it will be new, and it will have been worth the waiting.

The Ward believes this. The Ward does not believe—it *is* the belief. The Ward is the faith that a seed has in the soil it has not yet touched, the light it has not yet seen, the shape it has not yet taken. The Ward is the faith of the germinating. And those who live in the Ward are that faith, walking.
