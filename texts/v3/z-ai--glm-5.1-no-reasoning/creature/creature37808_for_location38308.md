To live in the Throat is to be excused from the cruelty of time, at the cost of being denied its mercies.

Nothing here was born in the way the surface understands birth, and nothing here will die in the way the surface understands death. The inhabitants of the Throat are not residents; they are fixtures, locked in a perpetual, mindless ritual of maintenance. They are the moving parts of a sealed, subterranean engine that runs on heat and ammonia, and they are as incapable of deviating from their rhythms as the basalt is capable of sprouting leaves.

The dominant presence is the flora—that suffocating, turgid membrane of violet and yellow-green flesh that coats the periphery. To call it a plant is to misunderstand its nature. It is a single, continuous organism, a sprawling math of swollen nodes and translucent stalks that has solved the equation of existence and stopped calculating. It does not grow because growing implies reaching toward something—a sun, a water source, a future. There is no future in the Throat. There is only the present, a present where the internal pressure of the ammonia-silicate plasma must be relieved, over and over, through those millions of microscopic pores. The venting is its respiration, its exhalation, but it is an exhalation without a preceding breath. It is a system in perfect, static balance. If you were to press your ear against one of those thick, waxy leaves—holding your breath against the searing heat and the chemical sting of the air—you would hear nothing but a faint, unending *hiss*, the sound of a sigh that has been held for ten thousand years. The plant does not drink, does not seek, does not wilt. It is a bladder of warm fluid clinging to the stone, waiting for nothing, fearing nothing, anchored in an eternity of stale exhalation.

And then there are the foragers. 

The six-legged, obsidian-carapaced things that haunt the basalt plain are the only movement in the Throat, and their movement is a lie. They give the illusion of life, of purpose, of animal agency, but they are as static as the stone they crawl across. They are creatures of pure, reflexive geometry. Their pale, unseeing whiskers are not sensory organs in the way a cat's whiskers are—they are tuning forks, calibrated to the precise topography of the Throat's floor. They do not explore; they follow grooves. Each forager is locked into a loop, a slow, deliberate circuit of roots and stone that it will walk until the basalt beneath it wears away, which, in the changeless Throat, will never happen.

They do not think. They do not feel hunger or satisfaction. They are biological automata, driven by a mechanism so ancient and so deeply ingrained that it has become indistinguishable from the stone itself. When a forager presses its flat, hollow mouth against a fleshy root and sucks out the warm ammonia-sap, it is not feeding in any meaningful sense—it is participating in a closed loop of energy exchange. The sap fuels the walking; the walking leads to the sap. The loud, wet gulping sound they make is the only punctuation in the Throat's roaring symphony, a rhythmic, organic percussion that beats in perfect, unvarying time. And when two foragers meet, when they circle one another and tap their hard shells together with that hollow, resonant *clack*, it is not communication. It is not territoriality. It is not mating. It is simply what happens when two loops intersect—a collision of geometries, a brief, percussive acknowledgment of shared existence before the loops diverge and the foragers continue their endless, mindless circuits. They do not know they are alive. They do not know anything. They simply *do*, and they will continue to *do* until the Throat itself collapses, which, in this place of frozen time, may be never.

But the most haunting inhabitants of the Throat are the ones who are no longer there.

The builders—the primitive, desperate people who carved the eye-like totems and built the half-collapsed cairn over the fissure of concentrated drone—are gone, but their presence lingers like a bruise on the stone. They were not like the flora or the foragers. They were creatures of time, of change, of need and fear and wonder, and the Throat destroyed them. Not with violence. Not with poison or heat or suffocation. The Throat destroyed them with *sameness*.

You can imagine them: a small band of early humans, perhaps, or something older, something pre-human, driven underground by some surface catastrophe—a drought, a predator, a rival tribe. They found the crack in the bedrock and descended, drawn by the warmth, by the strange, wet life in this hidden pocket. They found a place where the heat never killed and the darkness never lifted, and they stayed. At first, they must have marveled. They must have worshipped. The eye-totems, laboriously carved with flint and bone over generations, speak to a kind of awe, a reverence for this place that gave them warmth and shelter in a dead, dry world. They smoothed the stone with their hands, touching the carved eyes again and again, a ritual of connection, of belonging.

But the Throat does not love. The Throat does not even notice. 

The builders built their cairn over the fissure—the place where the drone was concentrated into a single, mind-numbing tone—and they lay inside it, one at a time, perhaps as a rite, perhaps as a sacrifice, perhaps as a desperate attempt to *feel* something in a place where nothing ever changed. And slowly, over the years, the Throat's static eternity began to work on them. The heat never shifted. The sound never ceased. The violet twilight never brightened to morning or darkened to night. There were no seasons to mark the passage of time, no deaths to mourn, no births to celebrate. The foragers clacked their shells at the same intervals, the flora vented its vapor at the same pressure, the bioluminescent blooms glowed with the same unending, sickly light. The builders' minds, shaped by a world of change and loss, had nothing to hold onto. Time pooled around them, stagnant and warm, and they began to dissolve into it.

They did not leave. There is no trace of departure, no path leading back up through the narrow crack to the surface. They did not die in any conventional sense—there are no bones, no remains, no signs of struggle or starvation. They simply... ceased. Their bodies, worn down by the unchanging heat and the ceaseless drone, perhaps returned to the basalt, their calcium and carbon absorbed by the roots of the flora, converted into more of that turgid, ammonia-filled flesh. Their minds went first, eroded by the endless, roaring present, until there was nothing left but the ritual of touch, the smoothing of the eye-totems, the lying in the cairn. And then even that faded, and the builders became part of the Throat's eternal, mindless loop—their handprints on the stone the only proof they ever existed at all.

The Throat is not evil. It is not malevolent. It is not even indifferent, because indifference implies a capacity for preference. The Throat simply *is*. It is a pocket of impossible, frozen equilibrium, a place where the laws of entropy have been suspended, where time has curled in on itself and died quietly, leaving behind a warm, wet, roaring corpse. To live in the Throat is to be a part of that corpse—a cell in a body that breathes but does not live, that moves but does not progress, that persists but does not *be*.

The foragers will continue their loops. The flora will continue its venting. The bioluminescent blooms will continue their steady, unending glow. And the eye-totems will watch it all with their crude, smoothed faces, seeing nothing, knowing nothing, waiting for nothing in the perpetual, violet twilight of the world's most suffocating eternity.
