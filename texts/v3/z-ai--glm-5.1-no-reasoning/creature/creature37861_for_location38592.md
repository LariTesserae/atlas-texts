You hear the place long before the land offers you anything to look at. The land has almost nothing left to offer — that is its character. This is a valley worn past argument: benches and flats, a river so old it has stopped cutting and only wanders, correcting nothing. The ground is low and cool and stays damp under a fog that arrives each day like a tide and drains each night through the trees. Everything here has been smoothed toward the horizontal. The only vertical for a long way in any direction is a single fig.

It is a strangler, and it is enormously old. Long ago — before the river found its current course, if you want a scale — it took a host tree in its arms and closed. The host is gone entirely, rotted and eaten and carried away, and what remains of it is a shape: the hollow at the fig's center is the exact negative of the tree it killed, a room in the mold of a vanished trunk. The fig's own crown broke off centuries back and has not grown since; its height has been final for longer than the valley has had names. Buttress roots spread around it like levees, bark polished dark by fog and traffic, latex beading amber at every old scar. It stands at the low point, where the fog pools first and thins last.

And it is loud. Wind moving over the broken rim makes the hollow throat of the trunk into a pipe: one sustained organ note, low, felt in the sternum more than heard, constant as a substation's hum. Underneath it runs the wash of feet — the dry sea-sound of a colony in motion, like surf heard from inside a shell, like rain that never lands. Closer, the sound separates into its industries: the crackle of mandibles, a high unbroken siren-whirr of stridulation, the tick of condensation striking wood. It is the loudest thing for kilometers, and it neither swells nor stops.

From the buttresses, roads radiate. Ant highways — leafcutter lines, worn into the duff by generations of traffic until they are sunken and edged like farm tracks — a fan of brown ribbons running off through the fog across the flats. Outbound lanes carry nothing. Inbound lanes carry green: fragments of leaf held overhead like flags, thousands of flags, fog beaded on every one. Ride some of the loads are the hitchhikers, tiny guards stationed on the cargo against parasitic flies. The supply chain runs all day and all night at one tempo, and the tempo never changes.

A visitor enters through the crack where a great limb was lost, and the interior is a nave. Four meters across at the waist, thirty to the open rim, walls of living sapwood ribbed like the inside of a throat. What the room mostly contains is nothing. Almost nothing per volume: a column of air, a floor of root-lattice far below, and — spaced up the wall like lamps hung over dark water — a few pale terraces. The distances between them are real distances. A drop of condensation lets go at the rim and you wait a long moment before you hear it land. Sound comes back off the walls so changed by the resonance that your own voice arrives as a stranger's. It is a bell with the bell taken out.

The light is plain daylight, filtered — knotholes banded down the shaft like clerestory windows, and from above, the chimney-column of a pearly fog-gray glow where the crown failed. No glare, no deep dark. Mist drifts through the light in slow motes. The air is cool outside and barely warm inside, the way a barn in winter is barely warm: metabolic heat, given off by the terraces.

Those terraces are the factory. They are fungus gardens — combs of spongy pale matter, the color of wet paper, each one grown and maintained by its crews. Leaves come up the roads, are chewed to pulp, seeded, pressed into the comb. The smell around them is yeast and cut grass and warm earth, sour-sweet, like a brewery folded into a hayloft. Nothing here is built; everything is grown or eaten into being. The machinery is bodies: mandibles for shears, crops for tanks, glands for sirens. Workers prune, haul, patrol; they dot the comb with droplets of their own making to feed it; they cull the wrong molds the way quality control culls a bad batch. There is no foreman, no plan, no tool, no artifact anywhere — the place contains nothing made by anyone, and still it is industrial in every particular except invention.

The systems are all there, if you watch for them. The chimney draws air; the gardens' own heat powers the draft; the interior climate is held within the thickness of a coat. Water is managed — workers carry droplets from the sweating wall to the combs, and the sweating itself is a cycle: the gardens exhale, the cool shell condenses, the wall drips. The drips fall at the same points, at the same rate, always. Down at the base, waste lines converge on a dump chamber where the decomposition sector runs — mites beyond counting, rove beetles, springtails, all of them employed. Soldiers hold the gates. Quarantined comb is carried out and buried.

And here is the quiet fact the whole place rests on: the ants feed the fig. The dump's leachate reaches the roots; nitrogen the tree could no longer gather arrives by the millionth mouthful. Rot fungi that would advance into the living wood are found and culled. The fig is terminal — broken-crowned, hollow, at the very end of what a fig can be — and it cannot die, because its death would be the colony's, and the colony does not permit it. So the tree holds its final form, and the final form holds the tree. The temperature does not vary. The same leaves come in at the same rate. Ants are born and die by the thousand every hour, and the colony is not born and does not die; it simply runs. This is the oldest stable state, an ending that achieved equilibrium and stopped there.

Yet the emptiness is honest — the fauna is abundant the way plankton is abundant in a sea, and the sea is still mostly empty. Gnats hang in the light column. Crickets keep to the cracks. A gecko works the wall above the main gate, living off strays; a frog sits in the drip-basin at the floor's low point, patient as furniture. The phorid flies test the perimeters and are turned back. Outside, in the crown's few remaining limbs, the fig still fruits, and birds come to it — and inside every syconium, the fig wasps run their three-week generations, the tree's oldest tenants after none.

The rules here are few and absolute. Everything that falls within this ground is processed; nothing is wasted and nothing is made. Nothing changes: the fog is the only clock, and it keeps the same time all day. The void at the center belongs to no one, not even to the ants, who use it only as a chimney and a highway and a place to hang their lightless farms.

Stand on the root-lattice floor and look up. Fog pours over the broken rim in slow ropes. The organ note, the wash of feet, the crackle of processing, the tick of drips — all of it is the sound of one metabolism, a factory whose only product is itself, an old heart still beating at exactly the speed it beat a thousand years ago. It has been ending for a very long time. It will be ending for a long time yet. When you leave through the crack, the sound does not stop behind you. There is no reason that it would.
