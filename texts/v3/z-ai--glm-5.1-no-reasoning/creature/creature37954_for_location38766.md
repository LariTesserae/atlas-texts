The ones who stayed do not live in the Sump so much as they are dissolved into it. They are not a people who fell, but a people who settled—like the particulate matter in the water, like the silt that finds the lowest point and comes to rest there. If you are looking for settlers with a pioneer's square-jawed determination, you will not find them. That kind of ambition requires a horizon, and the Sump has none, only a perimeter, a curve, a folding-in.

They are called, by the few who still find reason to send supply skiffs down the drainage channels, the Sumpers. The name is not quite derogatory, not quite affectionate. It is simply descriptive. They are of the Sump. It is in them.

You smell them before you see them—a flat, mineral odor, like wet limestone, like the inside of a culvert. It is the smell of the place itself, but concentrated, humanized, made intimate by continuous contact. Their skin has taken on the character of the landscape. It is pallid, nearly translucent in the places where the sun never reaches—the inner arms, the backs of the knees, the soft hollow of the throat. But it is not fragile. The dampness has not made them soft; it has made them dense, like waterlogged wood, like felt compressed to the point of being nearly armor. Their hands are enormous and deeply creased, the skin thickened into a texture that channels water away from the grip. You will see them holding tools, rusted things that should have been discarded decades ago, holding them without slipping, without effort, as if their palms have grown to match the corrosion.

They are not many. A few hundred, perhaps, scattered across the basin in ones and twos and small family clusters, occupying the pump-houses and the substations and the leaning, half-sunk outbuildings that the industry left behind. They do not form a community—community implies neighbors, and here the distance between structures is measured in the time it takes to slog through water that wants to pull you down. But they are not isolated, either. They have a network, a web of communication that operates on sound, since sound is the only thing that travels well in this medium. You will hear them, if you listen: a long, low hooting call that carries across the standing water, a kind of improvised signalhorn made from the rusted pipes that litter the basin. The sound is mournful, almost subsonic, and it reverberates in the saturated air until it seems to come from everywhere at once. It means: *I am here.* It means: *The water is rising on pad seven.* It means: *A steer has fallen in the eastern seep and we will need rope.*

They do not own the structures they live in. Ownership implies a boundary between self and environment, and that boundary was the first thing to dissolve. A Sumper does not say "my pump-house"; they say "the pump-house I'm in," or "the house that's still above the water line." They understand, with a clarity that would unsettle a real estate lawyer, that they are temporary tenants of a landscape that is actively in the process of consuming its own architecture. They have watched the concrete pads subside. They have watched the water climb the doorframes. They know that in five years or ten or twenty, the house they are sleeping in will be uninhabitable, and they will move to the next one, the next slightly higher rise, the next temporary island in the slow flood.

Their shelters, accordingly, are not maintained so much as they are negotiated. A Sumper household is a continuous argument with entropy, and the terms of the agreement are renegotiated daily. You will see the evidence of this: the half-inch of standing water in the kitchen that has been accepted as a permanent feature, the pot suspended above it to keep it dry; the wooden beams that have swollen to twice their original width and now bulge against the rusted bolts that cannot hold them; the electrical tape wrapped around junction boxes in layers so thick it resembles a geological formation, a sediment of adhesive and PVC built up over years of fighting the damp. They do not repair things. Repair implies restoration to a previous state, and there is no previous state to return to. They *adapt* things. They *accommodate* the decay.

The furniture sits on cinder blocks, on bricks, on stacks of rusted grating. The beds are hammocks, slung between pipes, swaying slightly with the vibration of the ancient pumps. The floors are not walked on so much as waded through. The walls weep—a constant, slow exudation of moisture that is mopped up, or not, depending on the mood and the season. The smell of mildew is so ubiquitous that they no longer notice it. It is the smell of home.

They eat what the margin provides. The Sump is not fertile in the agricultural sense—the pale grasses and the thin soil will not support crops—but it is teeming with life at the waterline, and they have learned to harvest it. Fish, yes, but more importantly the crustaceans and the mollusks that cling to the submerged infrastructure. There is a type of mussel that has colonized the drainage pipes, a dense, dark-shelled thing that tastes of iron and mud, and the Sumpers harvest it by the bucketful, prying it from the rusted metal with the same tools they use to clear the intake valves. They are rich in protein, these mussels, and in minerals that the industrial residue has leeched into the water. The Sumpers do not discuss this. They know what is in the water. They know what is in the food. They have made their calculations, and the math always comes out the same: the Sump gives, and you take, and you do not ask about the price.

They trap the feral cattle, when they can, but the cattle are wary and the footing is treacherous, and a chase through the standing water can end in a broken leg as easily as a full larder. When a beast is taken, it is taken to the nearest elevated platform—a concrete pad, a subsidence island—and processed in the open air, the blood draining into the water and attracting the insects and the fish and the birds in a brief, concentrated frenzy of consumption that the Sumpers watch without sentiment. This is the way of the margin. Everything eats. Everything is eaten.

There are children. This is the thing that surprises visitors, if visitors came, which they do not—there are children, and they are raised in the half-submerged world with the same casual pragmatism that governs everything else. They learn to walk on the crust, testing each step before committing their weight, learning by the age of three which patches will hold and which will admit them to the cold water below. They swim before they can read. They know the sound of the pump-houses—the rhythm, the slight variations that indicate a failing bearing or a blocked intake—before they know the names of the colors. They do not play on dry land because there is none; they play on the pilings and the gantries, climbing the rusted lattice with a sure-footedness that would make a structural engineer weep. They have a game that involves swinging from the overhead pipes on frayed rope, dropping into the deeper pools, and seeing who can hold their breath the longest in the murky water. They are amphibious. They are the truest children of the Sump.

The elders are rare—not because the Sumpers die young, though some do, but because aging in this environment is a process of gradually being reclaimed by the wet. An old Sumper moves slowly, not from frailty, but from the accumulated weight of the damp, the sense that the water is rising around them by imperceptible degrees. You will see them sitting on the elevated platforms, half-dressed in the grey air, their skin mapped with the same patterns of mineral deposit and biological growth that mark the submerged concrete. They are calm. They have watched the basin fill for seventy years, and they know the trajectory, and they have made their peace with the math. Some of them remember when the pump-houses were dry. Some of them remember when the water was five feet lower. They do not speak of this as a loss. They speak of it as a *change*, and change, in the Sump, is the only constant.

There is a woman—she is called Marr, though that may be a title rather than a name—who lives in the substation nearest the geographic center of the basin. She is the closest thing the Sump has to an authority. She does not lead; she *knows*. She knows which pipes are still flowing and which have silted shut. She knows the depth of the water at every major structure, and she records it, daily, in a ledger that is itself swelling with damp, the ink bleeding into a palimpsest of blue-grey that is slowly becoming illegible. She will show you the ledger, if you ask, and you will see the numbers climbing, slowly, inexorably, year by year: 14 inches, 17 inches, 21 inches. She is documenting the death of a place, and she is doing it with the meticulous care of a nurse recording a patient's vital signs. The patient is terminal. The record must be kept.

Marr has a map. It is not a map in any conventional sense—it is a board, salvaged from some demolished office, on which she has carved the layout of the basin with a knife. The structures are represented by nails, driven into the wood; the water level is indicated by a series of horizontal lines, scored at different heights, showing the progression. The most recent line is near the top of the board. There is room for perhaps two more lines before the nails are submerged. She does not say what happens then.

She will offer you tea, if you visit. It will taste of iron and of the mineral-heavy water that is all the Sump has to offer. She will not apologize for this. She will sit across from you on a stool that is visibly rotting, and she will watch you with eyes that are the color of the standing water—brown, opaque, unreadable. She will answer your questions, if you have them, but she will not volunteer information. The Sump does not explain itself. It simply *is*, and she is of it, and explanation is for people who have the luxury of distance.

There are others. A man called Fletch who maintains the pumps, or what is left of them—a half-dozen mechanical hearts still beating in their concrete chambers, still moving water that would otherwise stand and stagnate and rise faster than it already does. He is an engineer by birth, not by training; he learned the pumps from his father, who learned them from his father, and the knowledge is now as much tradition as technique. He cannot tell you why the pumps cycle on when they do; he can only tell you that they do, and that they must be kept running, and that if they stop the water will come. He oils the bearings with a thick, black lubricant that he scavenges from the half-buried drums. He listens to the pumps the way a doctor listens to a heartbeat. He knows when one is failing. He knows when one is laboring. He cannot save them. He can only ease their dying.

A family—the Brines, or something that sounds like that—who live on a concrete pad that was once the foundation of a chemical processing facility. The pad is still contaminated; the soil around it is a strange, iridescent color, and the water that pools on its surface has a oily sheen that refracts light into dull rainbows. The Brines have been there for three generations. Their children are healthy, by Sump standards, though their teeth have a slight blue-grey cast and their hair is finer than it should be. They do not fish from the pools near the pad. They walk an hour to harvest their mussels, and they are particular about the source. They have drawn their own boundaries, their own lines of acceptable contamination, and they live within them.

And there are the ones who have gone further—who have moved out past the last pump-house, past the last substation, into the deep margin where the water is knee-deep and the reeds grow tall and the sky seems to press down like a damp cloth. You will not see them. They do not want to be seen. They live in structures that are more nest than house—driftwood and salvaged metal and reeds woven into a rough thatch that keeps out some of the rain but none of the damp. They are said to be hunters, though what they hunt in the deep margin is unclear. They are said to be strange, stranger than the Sumpers who live near the infrastructure, stranger than the ones who still have electricity and walls and a dry place to sleep. They are said to have adapted in ways that the others have not—biologically, perhaps, or psychologically, or in some other way that does not have a name in the language of people who live on dry land.

The Sumpers speak of them rarely, and never with judgement. The margin is wide. There is room for everyone.

You ask, eventually, the question that visitors always ask: *Why do you stay?*

Marr will look at you, and the water in her eyes will be the water in the pools, the water in the air, the water that is slowly consuming the world she lives in. She will not answer immediately. She will let the silence fill with the sound of the pumps, the insects, the dripping.

Then she will say: *Where would I go?*

It is not a challenge. It is not a complaint. It is a genuine question, and the genuine answer is that there is nowhere. The Sump is not a prison; it is a catchment. It catches water. It catches silt. It catches the things that fall, or settle, or slowly drift downward. It caught her. It caught her parents. It will catch her children, and their children, and the ones who come after, the ones who will live in houses that are half underwater, who will sleep in hammocks strung above the rising pool, who will document the progress of the flood in ledgers that are slowly dissolving.

*The Sump is still becoming,* she says. *And so are we.*

She turns back to her ledger. The pen moves across the swelling paper. The ink bleeds. The numbers climb. The pump-house groans, and the water rises another fraction of a millimeter, and somewhere in the deep margin, something calls out across the standing water—a long, low hooting that reverberates in the saturated air, that means *I am here* or *the water is rising* or *I have found something in the reeds* or simply *I am still here, I am still here, I am still here.*

The Sump listens. It always listens. It holds the sound, and the water, and the people, and the slow settling of everything toward its final shape.
