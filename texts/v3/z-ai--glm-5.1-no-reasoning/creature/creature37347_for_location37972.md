What lives here is not a collection of species but a single, continuous, and restless tissue. There are no individuals in this dark; there are only temporary concentrations, eddies in the flow of biomass that swirl and collapse back into the whole. To step into this place is to step inside a living organ, a thing still flexing and dividing in the aftermath of some vast cellular mitosis.

At the lowest register are the swarming infinitesimal, the ones who give the darkness its weight and its breath. They are a fog of chitin and wing and microscopic mandible, so dense they become a medium. When you breathe, you inhale them; when you exhale, they scatter and reform. They are the ones drilling into the hindbrain, their whine a mechanical thing, a sonic chisel. But they are also the recyclers, the rapid-response team that descends upon the constant rain of death. Here, a thing does not decay; it is merely *re-processed*. A fallen frond, a crushed amphibian, a spill of blood—all are engulfed within moments by a seething carpet of the small, dissolved back into the warm soup before the shape can even be lost. They are the immune system of this dark body, erasing anything that stops moving, pulling it back into the churn.

Then there are the architects of accident, the mid-sized creatures of scale and slick hide who move through the vegetable riot like submarines through ink. They do not see; they have no need. Their skulls are filled with acoustic fat, amplifying the thocks and chittering into spatial maps. They are built for compression and friction, their bodies low and fluid, shedding water and slipping between the tanging root and the dangling vine. The *Hollow-Strikers* are among them—things that might be frogs or might be something without a taxonomic home, their skulls evolved into resonant chambers. They beat their heads against the swollen trunks of fungi and pneumatophores, producing that percussive *thock-thock-thock* that punches through the insect drone. They do this to claim acoustic territory, or perhaps to call mates, or perhaps simply because the pressure of the heat demands an outlet, and the sound is their sweat. They are manic, never sleeping, only pausing to snatch a passing moth with a whip of a tongue before resuming their rhythm.

And in the canopy, the weight of congregations: the Gliders and the Web-Weavers. The canopy here is not a place of light but a place of moisture and gravity. The Gliders are flat, furry membranes of greedy muscle that ride the columns of rising heat, tasting the air with their entire ventral surface for the chemical signatures of rot and sex. They congregate in the crotches of branches, piling atop one another in writhing, shrieking masses, their combined weight bowing the limbs until they crack. The Web-Weavers do not spin silk; they spin gossamer cultures of symbiotic algae and fungi, stretching them between the high branches to catch the ambient spores and the mist. They live inside these hanging nets, their bodies long and skeletal, picking through the mesh for trapped insects with needle fingers, shaking the webs when threatened to send a wave of condensation crashing down like a localized, spiteful rain.

But the truest inhabitants are the ones you feel before you hear—the large, the unbothered, the ones whose existence proves this land is still a verb. They are the *Arterials*, massive animals of a design too provisional for extinction. They do not graze; they *harvest*. They walk on columnar legs wrapped in calloused, scarred hide, their heads broad and armed with grinding plates that function like threshers. As they move, they ingest. Vine, fern, sapling, insect, small creature—it is all the same to them. They do not bite; they simply press forward, and the forest goes in. Behind them, they leave trenches of churned loam and exposed mineral substrate, the fresh wounds of the land. Into these trenches, the heat and water instantly rush, and before the Arterial has even passed, the spores and seeds are already germinating in its wake, racing to reclaim the devastation. The Arterials are the engines of the landrush, the ones who keep the ecology young, tearing it down just as it threatens to settle into maturity. They eat to make room, and the room is immediately filled again.

They have no fear because there is nothing here to fear. Nothing pauses. Even the panics—the skittering of the many-legged, the wet splash of retreat—are just momentary accelerations within the same single velocity. The life here does not evolve through competition; it *overflows* through excess. Every niche is simultaneously occupied by five competing forms, and they do not oust each other; they merge, they consume each other, they breed across boundaries, they hybridize and dissolve back into the ferment. It is a place where boundaries are merely suggestions, where the sharp edges of species have been worn away by the sheer heat and pressure of proximity. There are no introverts here. Every surface, every drop of water, every pocket of humid air is a shared space, a marketplace of flesh and spore and chemical signal.

The strangeness of this place, the sense that the rules are askew, stems from its inhabitants' total lack of historical consciousness. They do not remember being born; they do not plan for death. They exist in a continuous, roaring present-tense. The young land beneath them is still soft because they have not yet agreed upon a compact, a hierarchy, a way of being that allows for stillness and accumulation. They are the land’s mania given form. The dark is not an absence but a byproduct of their sheer density; the sound is not chaos but the acoustic signature of their infinite collisions. To live here is to be a cell in a febrile body, a part of a whole that is still too busy flexing its new muscles, still too busy *becoming*, to have learned how to rest, or how to be alone.
