What lives here is not a collection of organisms, but a single, continuous, unbroken recurrence. This is not a place of individual lives that begin and end; it is a place of a single, immense *verb*—a relentless, throatless roaring of becoming. To ask what lives on this plateau is to ask what lives in a fever: a process, a state of excitation, a metabolic emergency that has learned to hold itself perpetually at the edge of its own dissolution.

They have no names, because they have no boundaries. The mind instinctively wants to categorize them—the walls as one species, the catch-basins as another, the spanning walkways as a third—but this is a false taxonomy imposed on a body that refuses to be dissected. The architecture is not a community of plants; it is the *tissue* of the plant, the outward expression of a singular, subterranean sprawl. What lives here is a rhizomatic god, a connected mass of root and urge that wears its organs on the outside. The chamber you stand in is a lung, briefly inflated. The walkway spanning the ravine is a tendon, briefly taut. The dripping membrane is skin, briefly taut with water. And tomorrow, the lung will collapse, the tendon will snap, the skin will slough away, and the body will have already grown new ones elsewhere, forgetting the old as quickly as a wave forgets its foam.

What lives here is an *appetite* made geometric. It is hungry for the space that the young, uplifted rock has offered, and it fills that space the way a gas fills a container—not with contemplation, but with the blind, thermodynamic imperative to occupy every available void. Its appetite is for tension and release. It builds the walkway not to be crossed, but because the span exists, and the span demands the fiber. It grows the chamber not for shelter, but because the space between the columns aches to be enclosed. The architecture is the shape of its craving, made visible in hexagonal stems and load-bearing buttresses.

It is, in its own empty way, a *thinker*—though it thinks in geometry and water, not in symbols. When a chamber collapses, the shock of the falling water, the sudden shift in weight, the rupture of the membrane, these are its *stimuli*. The new growth that curves toward the gap, the channel that redirects the deluge—this is its *reflex*. It does not consider the gap and plan the bridge; the gap *pulls* the bridge out of the living fiber, the way a vacuum pulls air. It is a nervous system without a brain, a network of feedback loops so dense and so rapid that it mimics intent. If you stand still long enough, you can feel the plateau *deciding*—not with consciousness, but with the slow, creaking, groaning weight of a system adapting to its own continuous ruin.

What lives here is a *climate* that has learned to build its own shelter. The heat, the pressure, the water—these are its raw materials. The architecture does not resist the climate; it is the climate's *exhalation*. The weeping surfaces, the suffocating humidity, the chambers that fill with condensation—these are the plateau sweating, breathing, crying. The constant cycle of growth and rot is a homeostatic engine, a massive, biological heat-sink that transforms the violent thermal inversion into the slow-motion kinetics of cellulose and water. The place is not simply *in* the heat; the place *is* the heat, made thick and fibrous and temporarily upright.

And yet, for all its terrifying, cyclopean scale, what lives here is profoundly, heartbreakingly *lonely*. It has no one to shelter. It has evolved the perfect corridor, the luminous courtyard, the spiraling ramp—spaces that ache with the resonance of footsteps that will never fall. The geometry of its arches implies a gaze that will never look, the cup of its catch-basins a hand that will never be held. It builds and builds, an endless, frantic generation of rooms and halls and spanning bridges, as if the sheer volume of architecture might eventually conjure an occupant. But there is only the sound of water, the groan of fiber, the thud of collapse. It is a cathedral that builds its own liturgy and its own congregation of falling drops, then collapses the altar before the mass can be said.

There are no eyes here to see the amber light, and so the light becomes a part of what lives here—the way it catches the droplets, the way it bends through the taut membranes, the way it illuminates the dust of cellulose floating in the thick air. The light is unremarkable, but the *catching* of it is extraordinary. What lives here is a *surface*, an endless, intricate, self-generating surface that exists solely to interact with the light and the water. It is a texture, a topography of pure, wet expanse, turning and returning the world's basic forces—heat, light, gravity—into the slow, groaning music of the plateau.

So what lives in this place? The *becoming* lives here. The *turbulence*. The *process*. An organism of staggering scale and no self, a city of pure potential and no purpose, an architecture of breath and water and geometric hunger. It is alive in the way a storm is alive—in the way a fire is alive—feeding on the raw basalt and the rising heat, building itself from its own ashes, howling its drip-drip-drip into the humid emptiness.
