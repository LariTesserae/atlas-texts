They do not have a name for themselves. If they did, it would not be a word, because words require the kind of attention that splits the world into before and after, and they do not live in time that way. They live in the continuous present of the cistern—a tenseless, thrumming now where the only calendar is the pressure differential between inlet and discharge, and the only history is the slow, patient scouring of iron.

---

**The Lamprey-Forms**

You called them minerovores, and the term is accurate enough for what they do, but it tells you nothing about what they *are*. They are not parasites, despite the shape. They are not eels, despite the swimming. They are more like very slow, very patient tongues.

Consider: a tongue does not taste for its own satisfaction. A tongue tastes so that the body can decide what to swallow and what to reject. The lamprey-forms are the cistern's tongue. They press their filament-mouths to the pipes and they *read* the mineral accumulation—the calcium scale, the iron oxide, the copper verdigris—and what they ingest, they process. Their bodies are refineries. The pale, milky flesh you see is only the outer wall of a chemical plant. Inside, organs you cannot identify through their translucent skin sort and separate what they consume: iron goes one direction, copper another, trace minerals a third. What they excrete is not waste but *treatment*—a thin, enzyme-rich mucus that prevents further buildup on the cleaned surfaces, a protective film that glows faintly in the amber light like the sheen on a wet stone.

They have been doing this for so long that their life cycle has become indistinguishable from the cistern's maintenance schedule. They breed when the mineral accumulation reaches a certain density—they can taste it in the water, a rising concentration that triggers something ancient and chemical in their pale bodies. The juveniles hatch in the current, free-swimming, unweighted, and they drift until they find a pipe or strut that tastes *wrong*—too thick, too crusted, too far from specification—and they attach. They stay attached for years. Decades, perhaps. No one has been here to measure.

When they die, they do not fall. Their grip persists, the filament-mouths locked in place, and their bodies calcify around the pipe until they become part of the mineral layer they spent their lives removing. Other lamprey-forms will eventually consume them too. Nothing is wasted. This is not sentiment or efficiency; it is simply what happens when something becomes available to be eaten.

They do not notice you. You are not mineral. You are not pipe. You are not part of the specification. You are—temporarily, insignificantly—warm, and the water around you carries your chemical signature, but this is not interesting to them. You are a shadow that changes the current. You are less than a clog.

---

**The Deep Thing**

The flat, serrated leaf-shape in the deeper water has been here longer than the lamprey-forms. It is not a predator, though its silhouette suggests one. It is a filter. It is a pump. It is, in the language of the body you cannot help but use, a *liver*.

It does not hunt. It moves through the water with the current, broad and flat and slow, taking in water through the gap where its stem would be, passing it through internal structures so fine and so densely folded that the water is scrubbed of particulate before being released through the serrated edges that give it its shape. The serrations are gills. They are also exhaust ports. They are also, when the creature surfaces—as it does every forty-three minutes, you will learn if you stay long enough—a kind of breathing apparatus that pulls oxygen from the humid air above the waterline.

It is the size of a dog because the cistern required something of that scale. If the chamber were larger, it would be larger. If the chamber were smaller, it would be smaller. It grew to fit the space it was given, and it has not grown since. It does not seem to age. It does not seem to reproduce. It is a single organism, and it has been a single organism for as long as the cistern has functioned, and it may be the last of its kind or the first of its kind or the only of its kind, and it does not care about categories because it does not think in them.

What it thinks in—if *think* is the word—is vibration. The engine-throb is its heartbeat. The clicking of the lamprey-forms is its conversation. The sound of the drains is its music. When you entered the water, it felt you as a disturbance—a new vibration, warmer than the pipes, softer than the concrete, moving with a rhythm that did not match the current. It noted you the way a lung notes a change in air quality: without judgment, without fear, with only the simple awareness that something had altered, and that the alteration was within acceptable parameters.

When it surfaced and you saw the blowhole, you thought it was breathing. It was. But it was also *listening*. The air above the water carries sounds differently than the water below, and the deep thing has learned to parse both mediums. It knows the engines. It knows the gurgle of the drains. It knew the hatch when it opened—the sudden change in pressure, the unfamiliar weight above—and it knows the hatch has not closed. It knows you are still here.

It does not mind. You are not interfering with its function. You are not mineral buildup. You are not a clog. You are a feature of the environment it will process and pass, like everything else, like sediment, like heat, like time.

---

**The Small Ones**

There are smaller organisms in the cistern that you cannot see, but you can see their effects. The faint luminescence that clings to the condensation on the ceiling—not the amber glow of the fixtures, but something cooler and bluer, something that shifts when the air moves—those are colonies of bacteria adapted to the mineral-rich humidity. They feed on the iron in the air. They are so small that a single one would be invisible, but together, in their millions, they make the darkness breathe with a light that has no source and casts no shadow.

And in the drains, in the gurgling channels where the water runs to whatever comes next, there are things that you will never see because they are the size of grains of rice and they live in the dark, and their function is to consume whatever the lamprey-forms and the deep thing have missed. They are the final stage of processing. What passes them is as close to pure as the system requires—treated, balanced, mineral-free, ready to be discharged into whatever pipe network waits beyond the bulkhead. They are the cistern's kidneys. They do not know they are kidneys. They know only the current and the taste of impurity and the compulsion to ingest.

---

**The Ecosystem as Organism**

You called the creatures staff, and you were more right than you know. But staff implies employment, implies choice, implies that they might leave or be dismissed. They cannot. They are not *in* the cistern the way a worker is in a factory. They *are* the cistern, the way cells are the body. The lamprey-forms on the pipes are the cistern's maintenance of itself. The deep thing in the water is the cistern's filtration of itself. The bacteria in the air and the micro-organisms in the drains are the cistern's immune system, its quality control, its final check before the water moves on.

And the cistern—this single flooded chamber with its numbered valves and its constant mechanical song—is itself a cell in a body so vast that the word *infrastructure* does not capture it. There are other cisterns. There are pumps, and settling tanks, and pressure regulators, and treatment facilities, and each one has its own ecology, its own population of organisms engineered or evolved or both to maintain the function. The body extends across the city you came from, and beneath it, and beyond it. You have been living inside it your whole life, drinking what it processes, bathing in what it discharges, never once thinking about the creatures in the dark who make your water clean.

They did not build this body. They inherited it, as you inherited the city above, as all living things inherit worlds they did not make and cannot fully understand. But they have *become* it, in a way you have not. You live in your city the way a microbe lives on skin—present, consuming, altering, but not essential. They live in the cistern the way mitochondria live in a cell—not just present but *functioning*, not just surviving but *necessary*.

---

**What Lives Here**

What lives in the cistern is a way of being that you recognize, dimly, as something your species lost or never had. It is a life that does not distinguish between self and environment, between work and existence, between being and doing. The lamprey-form on the pipe is not *eating* the iron. It is the process of iron removal, given flesh. The deep thing is not *swimming* in the water. It is the process of water filtration, made mobile. The bacteria are not *growing* on the condensation. They are the condensation, organized and purposeful.

You stand in the cistern and you feel the cool water against your legs and you think: *I am in a place that does not need me.* And this is true. But it is also true that the cistern is a place that does not *not-need* you. You are here. Your warmth is in the water. Your skin cells are drifting in the current, being sorted and consumed and processed by the micro-organisms in the drains. The vibration of your heartbeat has joined the engine-throb, a new frequency in the congregation of sounds. The deep thing heard you enter, and it will hear you leave, and the interval between those two events will be recorded in the cistern's memory, which is not a mind but a pattern—pressure changes, mineral fluctuations, the slow accumulation of data in the chemistry of the water.

You are part of the function now. You are a variable. You will pass through the system and be discharged, and the water will be very slightly different for your having been in it, and the cistern will go on, and the engines will thrum, and the lamprey-forms will click, and the deep thing will breathe at the surface every forty-three minutes, and the small ones will glow in the condensation, and the water will *move*, because it must.

This is what lives here: not creatures in a place, but a place that is alive, and the creatures who are the place's living, and the water that is the reason for all of it, and the sound that is the sum of all their functions, and the cool, dim, sacred forgetfulness of a world that works without you.

You could stay. You could become part of the specification. You could let the water numb your legs and then your torso and then your mind, and eventually the lamprey-forms would find the iron in your blood, and the deep thing would filter what remained, and the micro-organisms would consume what the deep thing missed, and you would be processed and discharged and gone, and the cistern would not remember you any more than it remembers the pressure of a single drop of water passing through.

But you are not ready for that. Not yet. You are still thinking in before and after. You are still the kind of creature that climbs ladders and opens hatches and walks back into worlds that know what sky means.

So you stay for a while. You stand in the water and you let the sound become your environment, and you feel the current against your legs like the flow of something larger than yourself, and you watch the lamprey-forms on the pipes, and you wait for the deep thing to surface again, and you breathe the humid, mineral-rich air, and you are—for this moment, in this place, among these creatures who do not know you and do not need to know you—*alive*.

Not alive the way you are above the hatch, where alive means choosing and striving and failing. Alive the way the cistern is alive. Alive as a function. Alive as a process. Alive as something that moves because it must, and exists because it moves, and will continue, with or without you, because the water must move, and the water moves, and that is enough.

That has always been enough.
