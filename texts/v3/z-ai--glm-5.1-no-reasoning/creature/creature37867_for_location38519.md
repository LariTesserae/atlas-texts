# The Continuous

They are not what you expect. You expect something vast, something titanic, something that matches the scale of the landscape—behemoths wallowing in the warm muck, great beasts pushing through the canopy. But the Hatching Ground does not breed giants. It breeds the opposite. It breeds the thorough, the ubiquitous, the omnipresent. It breeds things that do not dominate space but *saturate* it.

---

The Continuous do not have a name because they do not have a boundary. You cannot point to one and say "there, that is a single creature," because the moment you point, the thing you are indicating has already merged with the thing beside it, and the thing beneath it, and the thing that was, three seconds ago, climbing your boot. They are arthropodal, perhaps. Or they were, once. The body plan is recognizable in the way that a distant ancestor is recognizable in a descendant's face—you can see the ghost of segmentation, the memory of jointed limbs, the echo of an exoskeleton. But these features have been modified, stretched, multiplied, and interconnected until the original form is only a suggestion.

A single Continuous is roughly the length of your hand, but "single" is the wrong word and "roughly" is a concession to the limits of language. Each one is a chain—a chain of segments that are also almost-separate organisms, linked by bridges of tissue so thin you can see the hemolymph pulsing through them, so flexible that the chain can fold back on itself, spiral, branch, and reconnect. They are rust-red and matte-black and sometimes a deep, bruised purple, and their color shifts as they move, not through pigmentation but through the angle of their microscopic setae, which are structured to catch and scatter the dim green light in ways that make them appear to flicker between presence and absence.

They move like a thought moves through a crowd—not as a single body, but as a pattern, a wave, a shift in the collective arrangement. You see them first on the trunk of a tree, and you think: ants. But the columns are wrong. Ants move in lines, in rigid pathways established by pheromone trails and tradition. The Continuous move in *networks*. They spread across the bark in branching, reconnecting streams, each individual following the individuals ahead while being followed by the individuals behind, and the whole pattern shifts and flows like water finding its way through gravel. They are not foraging. They are not migrating. They are *processing*.

---

This is what the Continuous do. They process. The Hatching Ground is a landscape of perpetual accretion—leaves falling, stems breaking, organisms dying—and the Continuous are the mechanism by which the fallen becomes the ground. They are the primary decomposers, the first responders, the ones who arrive at any newly available organic matter within minutes and begin the work of incorporation.

But they do not eat in the way that you understand eating. They do not consume and digest and excrete. They *integrate*. When a leaf falls, the Continuous swarm it—not in the hundreds, but in the thousands, the tens of thousands, their segmented bodies flowing over every millimeter of the surface. And they do not tear it apart. They press themselves against it. They flatten their segments and press and hold, and where their bodies touch the leaf, something happens—a transfer, slow and chemical and invisible, by which the substance of the leaf is drawn into the substance of the Continuous and transformed. The leaf does not disappear. It softens. It darkens. Its structure loosens, its cellulose bonds breaking under the enzymatic pressure of ten thousand small bodies working in unison. And then, gradually, over the course of hours, it becomes part of the mat—part of the wet, warm, fibrous ground that is still being built.

The Continuous have done this for as long as the ground has been building. They are not the only decomposers—there are fungi, bacteria, other invertebrates—but they are the *architects*. They determine the texture and composition of the ground through the patterns of their processing, and the ground, in turn, determines the patterns of their movement, and the feedback loop has been running for so long that it has become a single system. The ground is Continuous. The Continuous are ground. The distinction is not useful.

---

They are aware of you, but they do not care about you. This is the distinction that matters. The ants that climb your legs are investigating—they are probing, testing, determining whether you are food or threat or substrate. The Continuous already know what you are. You are organic matter. You are warm. You are surface. And so they come.

Not in swarms. Never in swarms. In *trickles*. A single chain—seven segments, nine segments, twelve—finds your boot and begins to climb. Another follows. Another. They move slowly, without urgency, and their touch is so light that you feel it only as a faint tickle, a brush of something against the fine hairs on your ankle. You look down and you see them: dark threads winding up your calf, spreading across the fabric of your trousers, each chain moving independently but all of them moving with the same unhurried deliberation.

You brush them off. They fall—or rather, they release, dropping to the ground and immediately flowing away, rejoining the networks that cover the surface. But more have already taken their place. Not because they are attacking, not because they are aggressive, but because you are *there*, and they are *here*, and in a landscape where every surface is colonized and every organic substance is processed, your presence is an invitation that cannot be declined.

They do not bite. They do not sting. They do not parasitize. If you stood still long enough—days, weeks—they would begin the slow work of integration. They would press their segments against your skin and begin the enzymatic transfer, softening, loosening, drawing you by imperceptible degrees into the substance of the ground. But this would take longer than you have, longer than any single visit, and the Continuous operate on timescales that make no accommodation for the brief flicker of human presence. They are patient. They are eternal. They will be here when you are gone, and they will process what you leave behind—your shed skin cells, your fallen hairs, the moisture that condensed on your body and dripped to the ground—and they will incorporate it into the mat, and the mat will rise by another fraction of a millimeter, and the Hatching Ground will continue to build itself.

---

The Continuous have a sound, but it is not a sound you can hear. It is subsonic, a low, rhythmic thrum produced by the synchronized contraction of ten million segments across ten thousand square meters of ground, and you feel it in your feet before you feel it anywhere else. It is the pulse of the mat. It is the heartbeat of the ground. It happens once every few seconds—a faint, deep vibration that rises through the quaking surface and into your bones, and then fades, and then rises again. It is not the sound of any individual. It is the sound of the collective, the acoustic signature of ten million small bodies working in unison, and it is as constant and as unvarying as the drone of the insects or the call of the frogs.

If you pressed your ear to the ground—if you lay flat on the quaking mat and pressed your ear to the wet, warm surface and listened—you would hear it more clearly. A low, rhythmic grinding. A slow, wet churning. The sound of matter being transformed. The sound of the landscape digesting itself and building itself and digesting itself again in an endless cycle of incorporation that has no beginning and no end. This is the sound that the Hatching Ground makes when you strip away the insects and the frogs and the birds and listen to what remains. This is the sound of the Continuous. This is the sound of the ground that is still becoming.

---

They do not have a leader. They do not have a center. They do not have a memory in any sense that you would recognize, but they have *pattern*, and pattern, sustained long enough, becomes indistinguishable from memory. The routes they follow across the bark of the trees are the same routes their ancestors followed, and the routes their ancestors followed were the same routes their ancestors' ancestors followed, and the grooves worn into the bark by ten million tiny feet over ten thousand generations are the physical record of a continuity that predates any individual and will outlast any individual. The Continuous do not remember. They *are* the memory. Their bodies are the inscription on which the landscape writes its habits, and their movements are the reading of that inscription, and the cycle of writing and reading has been going on for so long that the inscription and the reader have become the same thing.

This is what it means to be native to the Hatching Ground. Not to inhabit it, but to *be* it. Not to live in the landscape, but to be so thoroughly of the landscape that the distinction collapses. The Continuous are not adapted to this place. They are *derived* from it. They are its product, its extension, its living tissue. They are what the Hatching Ground looks like when it moves.

---

And so you stand there, in the green dimness, in the warm fog, in the unending roar of biological noise, and you feel them on your skin—light, purposeful, unhurried—and you understand that they are not visiting you. They are *receiving* you. You have arrived at the surface of a living system that is so vast and so complete and so indifferent to your individual existence that it can process you without acknowledging you, incorporate you without noticing you, and continue its ancient, unending work of self-construction without the slightest perturbation. The Continuous climb your legs and flow across your boots and wind their segmented chains around your ankles, and the ground trembles beneath you with the deep, slow pulse of ten million bodies working in unison, and the heat presses down, and the fog holds still, and the sound continues.

You are being welcomed. Not with warmth, not with recognition, but with the only welcome that the Hatching Ground has to offer: the welcome of a surface that has room for one more, the welcome of a process that has room for one more input, the welcome of a system that has been running for longer than you can imagine and will continue running for longer than you can conceive. The Continuous do not need you. But they will take you, eventually, as they take everything. They will take you and process you and incorporate you and build you into the ground, and the ground will rise by a fraction of a millimeter, and the Hatching Ground will continue.

This is what lives here. Not a creature. Not a species. Not a community. A *continuity*—unbroken, unvarying, eternal—a living process that has become indistinguishable from the place in which it occurs. The Continuous are the Hatching Ground, and the Hatching Ground is the Continuous, and you are standing in the middle of them, and they are already climbing your legs.
