# The Inhabitants

What lives in the Combers has no name in any language that was spoken before the factories came. The workers who tend the processing houses have words for them—*drain-runners, gutter-gloss, the moving dark*—but these are descriptions, not names. The creatures require no names among themselves. They communicate through the chemical traces they leave on brick and stone, a writing that is also a path, also a memory, also a call to gather or disperse. They have been writing the same messages for so long that the brick itself has learned them.

Consider a single one. A medium-sized specimen, perhaps the length of your hand from wrist to fingertip. Its body is low and segmented, built for the thin liquid films that coat every surface here—not for swimming, not for wading, but for that intermediate state the Combers specialize in, where water and stone have become a single medium. Fourteen legs, perhaps sixteen—the number varies, and not in any way that suggests injury or irregularity. They simply grow as needed in the early molts, then stop. Each leg ends in a foot that is more a brush than a claw, a spread of fine bristles that spreads the surface tension of the water-film without breaking it. They move across damp brick the way a water-skimmer moves across a pond, except their pond is vertical, inverted, curved through pipe-interiors, stretched across ceilings.

The carapace is the first thing anyone notices—that oil-slick iridescence, the rainbow-on-petroleum shimmer that makes them seem manufactured, product of the same processes that built the processing houses. But this is a misunderstanding. The iridescence is not metallic. It is *moistural*. It comes from the same phenomenon that claims the brickwork, that film of chemical reaction that spreads where water lingers. The creatures cultivate it. Their shells are not hard in the way a beetle's is hard—not a rigid armor but a semi-permeable membrane, constantly damp, constantly reacting with the air around them. What you see when you see that shimmer is not a surface but a process, an ongoing conversation between the creature's chemistry and the district's.

They eat the film. This is the simplest way to say it. They move across the damp brick and the wet cobble and the rust-flaked iron, and they consume the chemical residue that forms there—the interaction-product of water and stone and a century of factory exhalation. Their mouths are beneath them, slit-like, pressed always against the surface they traverse. They graze. The way sheep graze a hillside, except the hillside is vertical and the grass is a compound that has no name because no one has ever had reason to isolate it. They have grazed the Combers so thoroughly that no surface accumulates more than a molecular layer before it is consumed. The district stays clean in its way—not the cleanliness of absence but the cleanliness of appetite.

And they are, in their grazing, the district's digestion. What they take in, they process. What they excrete, they excrete in the gutters, in the pooled water, in the tight spaces between cobbles—a substance that feeds the film, that catalyzes the reaction between damp and stone and chemical air. They close the circuit. They are the reason the equilibrium holds. Without them, the residue would build, would crust, would eventually choke the brickwork and corrode the iron and gum the ancient mechanisms of the processing houses. With them, the Combers sustain themselves in perpetual, depthless present.

There are millions. The word feels approximate—how would anyone count?—but it is the only word that captures what you see when you look into a gutter at midday and find the water not water at all but a flowing mass of them, moving together, iridescent in the diffused light, pooling and dividing and re-pooling around the drainage grates the way water itself would pool and divide if you gave it legs and a slow, mechanical purpose. They do not crowd each other. There is no competition in the gutters, no jostling, no aggression. The film they feed on is everywhere, renewed constantly by the damp and the air and their own byproduct. There is enough. There has always been enough. The concept of scarcity has never had cause to evolve here.

They molt. This is the only time they become still, and the only time they become visible as individuals. When the carapace loosens and splits, they press themselves into the gaps between cobbles, into the mortar joints, into any crevice that can hold them, and they wait while their new surface grows beneath the old. The molted shells remain where they are abandoned—translucent, faintly shimmering, so thin they dissolve in the next rain into the same chemical compound they once protected against. In dry spells, the shells accumulate in the crevices like shed dreams, like the district remembering itself in fragments. Then the damp returns and they are gone.

They do not sleep. They do not need to. The engines never rest, and neither does the film, and neither does the damp that produces it. There is no cycle of activity and repose, no diurnal rhythm, no season. The processing houses run and the air breathes out its chemicals and the film forms and they graze and they excrete and the film forms again. At dusk the light changes and the iridescence sharpens, but the creatures do not change with it. They are the same at midnight as at noon. They have never known another way to be.

Do they think? The question is a trap, shaped by minds that think in the way minds think. They *respond*. When the factory rhythm shifts—when a venting pipe opens early or an engine cycles to a different pace—they respond, flowing away from the vibration or toward the warmth, adjusting their grazing routes to follow the new pattern of chemical deposit. They learn in the way water learns, which is to say they find the path of least resistance and follow it until it changes, then find the new one. They remember in the way stone remembers, which is to say the paths they have followed longest are etched into the district as faint discolorations, and new generations follow them without knowing why, because the route is there and it works.

They do not notice you. This is perhaps the most disorienting thing about standing in the Combers, in the center of the slot-streets, with the sound pounding through your chest and the damp settling on your skin. They flow around your boots and they do not notice. They climb the wall beside your hand and they do not notice. They pour into the gutter at your feet in their thousands and they do not notice, because you are not part of the circuit, not part of the equilibrium, not a source of film or warmth or chemical trace. You are an obstruction, and they flow around you the way water flows around a stone—not with recognition, not with avoidance, just with the simple physics of a body in a path.

You could stand there forever. You could become a fixture, another element in the equilibrium, and they would incorporate you the way they've incorporated the gutters and the cobblestones and the rusted boilerplate walls. Your skin would grow damp and the film would form on you and they would graze it away and you would feel their tiny mouths on your surface, their brush-feet moving across you, and you would become a surface in the Combers, a place where things happen, where the chemical conversation continues.

But you don't. You leave. You always leave, eventually, because there is nothing here that needs you, nothing that wants you, nothing that even acknowledges you as separate from the brick and the iron and the standing water. And this, perhaps, is the true equilibrium of the Combers—not the stasis of the district itself, which is merely mechanical, merely the persistence of a system that has found its groove and cannot imagine leaving it. The true equilibrium is the way the district declines to notice anything that isn't already part of it. The way it simply continues, with or without you, in the same direction it has always continued, which is no direction at all.

The creatures go on grazing. The engines go on turning. The damp goes on forming and the film goes on spreading and the iridescent multitudes go on consuming it, and the district breathes its slow chemical breath, and the sound leans against your sternum even after you've left, even after you've climbed out through the slot-streets to where the grid resumes and the city remembers it has a shape.

You will taste it for hours. The mineral damp. The faint chemical fume. The way the Combers have of getting inside you—not the creatures themselves, never the creatures, but the principle of them, the endless mechanical grazing, the closed circuit, the equilibrium that does not know you and will not know you and will be here, still here, long after the grid has crumbled and the city's throat has closed and the processing houses have finally, finally gone silent.
