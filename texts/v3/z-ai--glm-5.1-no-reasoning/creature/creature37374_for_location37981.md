What lives here is not a creature. It is a *condition*.

It is the grief of the room itself.

When the water forced entry—when the sea found the cracks and the weight became too much for the hand-hewn beams—something was born in the moment between dry and drowned. Not a spirit in the way the surface-dwellers might understand, with intentions and a face, but an emergent presence, a low and constant hum of *refusal* that lives in the swelling wood fibers and the screaming iron.

It is young. It has only existed since the flood. And it is dying even as you float here, because what it is will not last. Within a season, the wood will be soft enough to push your thumb through. Within two, the iron will be orange mush. The lime will leach, the joints will slacken, and whatever this place is—this stubborn, screaming thing that does not want to be an aquarium—will have quieted into the patient indifference of the sea floor.

But right now, in this bright and deafening moment, it is alive.

You can feel it in the way the room *responds*. When a current drums the outer wall, the structure does not simply transmit the sound; it answers. The groan of timber under pressure is not passive physics. It is the voice of something that remembers being rigid, that remembers holding fast, and that is discovering—second by second, tide by tide—what it means to give way. The boom of a shifting stone is a syllable in a language of collapse.

The sea grass is not apart from this. The sea grass is the first visitor, the first witness, and—unknowingly—the first mourner. Its roots are too fine and too new to hold anything together. They enter the mortar seeking purchase, and in seeking it, they pry. They are small green fingers pulling at the scab. The room does not hate them for this. The room does not have the capacity for hate. But the room contains them, as it contains you, and its warmth presses against their delicate strands as it presses against your skin: without preference, without welcome, with the blank generosity of a thing that has no choice anymore about what passes through it.

The light is what makes the grief visible. In cold water, in deep water, a drowned thing can hide. It can go dark and quiet and become a secret. But here the sun will not allow it. Every flake of rust is illuminated. Every gap where the mortar has already begun to crumble is exposed. There is no dignity of shadow, no softening. The room is being watched by the sky as it dies, and it cannot close its eyes.

If you were to press your ear to the wall—which you can do, because the ceiling is close enough to brush with your knuckles—you would hear something beneath the drumming and the groaning. A frequency so low it is almost a feeling rather than a sound. A shudder that passes through the stone and into your jawbone and down into your chest.

That is the heartbeat. Not a pulse of blood, but a pulse of *stress*. The entire structure, under the uniform pressure of the warm sea, is vibrating at the frequency of its own failure. It is the sound of a thing holding its shape against the force that is reshaping it. It will not last. The frequency is dropping. The periods between the shudders are lengthening. The room is exhaling, one long and terrible breath that began when the water came in and will end when the last fitted stone tumbles from its bed and the space becomes not a room but a cavity, not a made thing but a found one, not a drowning but a drowning-completed.

And yet.

And yet the iron brackets still grip. The wood, though swelling, still bears the ceiling. The mortar, though weakening, still holds the stones in their courses. Someone drove those brackets with a hammer. Someone planed that wood. Someone mixed that mortar and laid those stones and stood in this space when it was still a space for standing, and the work of their hands has not yet been undone. The room is still, in this moment, a room. The sea has claimed it but has not yet digested it. There is a gap—you can feel it, in the warmth and the noise and the unbearable brightness—between *flooded* and *gone*.

What lives in this pocket is that gap.

It is the interim. The meanwhile. The still. The *not yet*.

It will not be here when you return. If you return in a year, or five, or ten, the processes will have finished. The room will be a ruin, and ruins are quiet. Ruins have accepted what they are. But this place has not accepted. This place is still arguing with the water, still shouting in the language of creaking wood and shifting stone, still holding the shape its makers gave it even as the warmth and the salt and the patience of the sea take it apart.

What lives here is a room fighting to remain a room.

It will lose.

But not today. Not while you float inside it, and the light pours down, and the rust flakes drift like brown snow, and the green threads root in the mortar, and the sound—deafening, constant, alive—fills your chest and will not let you forget that something, in this warm and bright and drowning chapel, is still refusing to be quiet.
