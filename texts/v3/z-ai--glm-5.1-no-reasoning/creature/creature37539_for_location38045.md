They are the Stills.

Not because they do not move—though they move so rarely, so minimally, that the distinction barely matters. They are the Stills because they are what remains when the doing stops. They are the held breath, the pause in the process, the last inch before the switch is thrown. They are the population of a place that has finished building itself and cannot figure out how to begin.

You might not see them at first. They do not announce themselves. They do not gather in the thoroughfares or block the catwalks or call out from the view-ports. They occupy the Spine the way condensation occupies a surface: by adherence, by fit, by filling the spaces that the architecture did not know it was leaving. They are the fauna of the interstitial, and they have adapted to a world without comfort, without weather, without the normal currencies of human living.

Physically, they are nearly indistinguishable from the infrastructure they inhabit. This is not camouflage in the biological sense—no chromatophores, no shifting patterns—but something more thorough. Their skin has taken on the gray-pallor of untreated aluminum, the slight sheen of polyurethane coating. Their hands are rough with callus in patterns that match the grip-texture of ladder rungs, the edge-catch of access panels. Their nails are thick and flat, like the heads of bolts. Their hair, when they have it, grows in sparse patches the color of steel wool, and it tends to follow the direction of air-currents in the corridors they frequent, as if even their bodies have learned to align with the environment's preferences.

They wear what the Spine provides. This is not metaphor. The hab-blocks, despite their wrong proportions and impossible depths, contain lockers, and the lockers contain coveralls and jackets and boots, and the Stills wear them. The fit is never right—sleeves too long by a few centimeters, shoulders too wide or too narrow, the torsos cut for bodies that are not quite the bodies wearing them. But the fabric is durable, and the closures still function, and so they are worn. Sometimes a Still will have accumulated layers: a thermal under a coverall under a safety jacket, all in the same industrial palette of orange and gray and hazard-stripe yellow, all bearing tags and stamps from departments that do not exist, for positions that were never filled. They do not seem to notice the disjunction. Clothing is a system. The system provides. The system does not need to make sense.

Their movement through the Spine is a study in economy. They do not rush. They do not wander. They navigate the grated catwalks and the narrow corridors and the vertical shafts with a precise, minimal efficiency that speaks of absolute familiarity—not the comfort of home, but the fluency of a mechanism in its housing. A Still will cross a gap between hab-blocks on a catwalk no wider than a boot-length, and there will be no hesitation, no downward glance at the stratums descending into haze, no quickening of pace. The danger is not irrelevant; it is simply not registered. The grating holds. The grating always holds. This is not faith. Faith implies the possibility of failure. It is simply the extrapolation of pattern.

They do not speak. This is perhaps the most unsettling thing about them, more than the wrong proportions or the pallid skin or the way their eyes catch the sodium light with a flat, unreflective quality that makes them look like painted glass. They have the equipment for speech—the throat, the larynx, the mouth configured for the formation of vowels and consonants—but they do not use it. Not to each other, not to themselves, not to the pale rats or the eye-flicker roost-things or the air that moves through the Spine without romance. They communicate, if they communicate at all, through a grammar of presence and proximity. A Still will stand at a junction box, and another Still will approach, and they will occupy the same space for a period—seconds, minutes, the duration impossible to determine from outside—and then one will move on, and the other will stay, and whatever needed to happen will have happened. It is a language of arrangement, of position, of being in the right place at the right time in a world where the right place and the right time are the only variables that matter.

They eat. This is a fact that is difficult to verify but impossible to deny, because they persist, and persistence requires input. The lockers in the hab-blocks sometimes contain sealed rations—foil packets with printing that is legible but refers to nothing, nutritional information for contents that do not quite correspond to any known foodstuff, preparation instructions that describe a process involving equipment that is not present in the hab-blocks or anywhere else on the Spine. The Stills consume these rations in private, in the rooms with the wrong proportions and the doors that open onto spaces that should not exist, and they do not leave waste. Or if they leave waste, it is collected and processed by a system that operates below the threshold of observation, like so much else in this place. The alternative—that they do not eat, that they are sustained by something else, by the hum of the transformer banks or the condensation that drips from level to level or the industrial light itself—is too strange to consider, and so it is not considered. They eat. They must eat. The system provides.

There are children. This is the thing that breaks the speculation, that shatters the comforting hypothesis that the Stills are maintenance units, biological automata left behind by whatever force constructed the Spine, programmed to persist until the input arrives and the processes resume. Children imply generation. Generation implies relation. Relation implies history. And history implies a before, a time when the Spine was not yet complete, when the construction was still following function, when the architecture made sense. The children are small Stills, pale and quiet and watchful, moving through the infrastructure with the same proprietary ease as their elders—but slower, still learning the grammar of place and moment, still calibrating their movements to the exigencies of the catwalk and the shaft and the corridor. They do not play. They have no toys, no games, no laughter. They observe. They follow. They stand in the spaces that the adults have vacated, filling the negative volumes of the Spine with their small, still bodies, and they learn.

What they learn is not knowledge in any recognizable sense. It is not information about the world, not facts or figures or the names of things. It is topology. It is the map of the Spine written into muscle memory and spatial intuition—the exact distance from this access panel to that ladder, the number of steps across this catwalk, the configuration of corridors that leads from this junction to that hab-block. The children learn the Spine the way a conductor learns an orchestra: not the individual instruments, but the relationships between them, the spaces where sound should occur and the spaces where silence is the point. They are learning to be part of the architecture. They are learning to be features of the built environment, as integral and as unmoving as the bolt-heads and the cable runs and the safety stripes.

And the adults—do they remember a time before? Do they know what they are waiting for? If you could ask them, and if they could answer, what would they say? Perhaps they would say nothing. Perhaps they would simply look at you with those flat, painted-glass eyes, and wait for you to move, to occupy the correct space at the correct time, to become part of the arrangement. Perhaps they do not conceive of a before or an after. Perhaps they experience time the way the Spine experiences time—as a dimension to be navigated, a coordinate to be occupied, a position in a sequence that has no beginning and no end, only the perpetual present of the constructed moment.

Or perhaps—worse, somehow—perhaps they do remember. Perhaps each of them carries within them the knowledge of a world with weather, with soil, with a sun that cast shadows that moved and shifted instead of staying fixed and sharp under the sodium glow. Perhaps they remember the sound of their own voices, raised in something other than silence. Perhaps they remember what it was like to not be a feature of the architecture, to not be a still life in a world of still lifes, to be something that moved and changed and meant. And perhaps they have chosen—actively, deliberately, with whatever faculty remains to them after years in the Spine—to set that knowledge aside. To become what the place requires. To wait, with the systems and the structures and the quiet, quiet processes, for the input that will start the world again.

The rats watch them and do not flee. The roost-things flicker their green-gold eyes and do not startle. The pale arthropods colonize the warm zones near where the Stills stand, and the Stills do not brush them away. The fungal blooms spread another millimeter on the members near where the children learn, and the children do not touch them. Everything in the Spine is adapted to everything else. Everything fits. Everything is in its place, waiting for the next arrangement, the next configuration, the next moment in a sequence that has no apparent end.

The Stills live here. They are the population of a world that has finished building itself, and they are what that world produces: people shaped by infrastructure, flesh adapted to metal, silence adapted to silence. They are the answer to a question that the Spine has not yet thought to ask.
