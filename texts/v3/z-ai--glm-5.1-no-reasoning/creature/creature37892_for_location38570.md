# The Inhabitants of the Caldera-Room

## The Litho-Moths

They are the first thing you notice, though only because they move.

Everything else in this cathedral of dry heat and sharp geometry has committed to stillness so thoroughly that even the crystal columns seem less alive than the rock they grew from. But the litho-moths *shift*. A thumb-sized body detaches from a chalcedony wall and resettles three inches to the left, and the motion is so unexpected in this silence that it registers as sound—a soft, abrasive whisper, like fine sandpaper drawn across parchment.

Up close—they allow you close, because they are not afraid; they are not anything that resembles afraid—they are hideous and delicate in equal measure. Their wings are not wings. They are extended pleats of dry, leathery chitin, translucent at the edges where the material thins to a membrane you can almost see through, darkening to an opaque, near-black at the body's core. There are no wing-eyes, no patterns, no flourish of color. Evolution here has never needed to startle or seduce. The wings fold flat against the body like the pages of a closed book, and when the moth moves, they do not flap. They unfold, rigid, and the creature tilts forward, falling a short distance to its new perch. It is less flight than controlled collapse.

Their underbellies are the most specialized surface in this room. Each is a pad of abrasive micro-crystals, naturally formed grinding stones that grow continuously from the chitin base, replacing themselves as they wear down. When a moth settles on an obsidian ridge, it does not rest. It feeds. The body rocks—imperceptibly, over hours, over days—dragging the abrasive belly back and forth across the sharp edge of volcanic glass. The motion is so slow that you must watch a single individual for nearly a quarter hour before you can confirm it is moving at all. But the evidence is everywhere: the obsidian plates nearest the oldest chalcedony buildings are rounded, their edges softened to a dull, wavy smoothness, a topography entirely manufactured by generations of moth-bellies.

They do not eat the obsidian. They eat what they liberate from it: the mineral dust, the micro-fractured particles of volcanic glass that are ground loose by the abrasion and captured by a fringe of filamentary feelers around the mouth. These feelers are not unlike the copper-lichen's fronds—fine, flexible, pale—but they serve as a filter, a living sieve that sorts dust from air. The moth digests silica, iron oxides, trace sulfur compounds. Its metabolism is a kiln-chemistry, a dry, anhydrous process that produces no waste except a fine, almost imaginary exhalation of heated gas.

There are hundreds of them. Perhaps thousands. They cover the chalcedony buildings in a layer of quiet, grinding life, and they cluster in the crevices where obsidian plates meet, and they line the lower branches of the wire-lichen thickets. From a distance, they look like a texture, a property of the stone. It is only when one falls—that small, controlled tumble from perch to perch—that you realize the walls are *covered* in living things.

They do not interact with each other. There is no mating dance, no territorial dispute, no communication that you can detect. Two moths may occupy the same obsidian edge, grinding side by side, their abdomens nearly touching, and neither acknowledges the other. Reproduction, if it occurs, must happen at a pace indistinguishable from the geological. Perhaps a moth, at the end of its long, slow life, settles into a crevice and its body crystallizes, and from that crystallized husk, a new moth grows, the way the chalcedony buildings grew from the calcified husks of ancient mosses. You do not know. You cannot know. You are a creature of water and fast time, and they are creatures of dry, patient eternity.

## The Salamander-Things

They are the reason the copper-lichen is slowly disappearing.

You see your first one after nearly an hour in the Caldera-Room, and only because you have learned to look up. The ceiling is a rough, undulating dome of fused volcanic glass, and in the crevices where the glass has pulled away from the cooling basalt, there are shadows. One of the shadows moves.

It is pale. Not white—there is no white in this place, no clean, unblemished color—but a translucent, milky grey, the color of thin quartz, the color of something that has never needed to protect itself from light because it has never encountered light worth blocking. Its body is the length of your hand, but it seems longer because of the limbs. There are six of them, not four, and they are not the stubby, powerful legs of a terrestrial salamander. They are long, many-jointed, articulated like the legs of a cave cricket, each one ending in a cluster of fine, prehensile filaments that grip the mica columns with a delicacy that suggests the creature weighs almost nothing.

It is, in fact, nearly weightless. You realize this when it descends from the ceiling to a copper-lichen thicket, and the fronds do not bend under its weight. It stands on the tips of wire-lichen filaments, and they do not yield. It is like watching a ghost walk on cobwebs.

The body is dry. Terribly, impossibly dry. The skin is a leathery chitin, not moist, not slick, not anything that a terrestrial amphibian would recognize. There is no slime, no mucous, no sheen of moisture. The creature has been severed from water as completely as the landscape it inhabits. Its skin is matte, slightly rough, and you suspect it is not unlike the moth's chitin—a composite material, part organic polymer, part mineral inclusion. It is a living stone-thing, warm to the touch, you imagine, because the air is warm and the creature does not generate its own heat.

It has no eyes. The head is a smooth, blunt wedge, featureless except for a narrow, horizontal slit of a mouth and two clusters of pale, filamentary feelers that sweep forward from the jaw like the whiskers of a catfish. These feelers are the primary sensory organs. They are extraordinarily sensitive to electric fields, and they guide the creature to the one resource it actively seeks: the static charge that pools on the copper-lichen.

Watching it feed is an exercise in patience. The salamander-thing settles on a lichen frond, extends its mouth to the copper filament, and then—nothing. It stays. For minutes. For hours. You watch one individual remain motionless on a single frond for the entire duration of your observation, its mouth pressed to the wire-lichen, its feelers slowly, slowly sweeping the air around it. It is drinking electricity. The static charge, built up by the friction of dry air against the copper-magnesium alloy of the lichen, flows through the creature's mouth and into its body, where it is stored in capacitive organs—you cannot see them, but you infer their existence from the creature's behavior. When it has had enough, it moves on. The pace of its feeding is glacial.

They are social, after a fashion. You see clusters of three or four on a single mica column, arranged at intervals, all facing the same direction, all feeding. They do not touch. They do not call to each other. But they aggregate, and the aggregation is not random. There is a pattern, a spacing, a territorial logic that you can sense but not decode. Perhaps they communicate through the electric fields they harvest—tiny modulations in the charge, subtle fluctuations that one salamander-thing produces and another detects through its feelers. A language of static, spoken in a dialect of volts.

Their young—if they have young—are invisible. You see no eggs, no larvae, no smaller versions of the adults. Perhaps the young are too small to see, or perhaps they grow in a part of the Caldera-Room you cannot reach, a crevice or a pocket too narrow for your body. Or perhaps they bud from the adults, small pale limbs extending from the flanks, detaching when they are mature enough to feed on their own. You do not know. The salamander-things keep their secrets at the pace of geology.

## The Wire-Lichen

It is difficult to think of the wire-lichen as fauna or flora. It is both. It is neither.

The thickets are dense, tangled, almost impassable in places. The fronds are long, flexible filaments of copper and magnesium, metal grown biological. They are not forged. They are not extracted and shaped. They are *grown*, secreted by the organism's root network in the same way that a terrestrial plant secretes cellulose. The metal is bio-accumulated from the basalt substrate, drawn up through the root-network, and deposited as a filament. The result is a living wire, flexible, conductive, warm.

The chalk coating is the key to everything. It is a fine, pale powder that coats every frond, and it is constantly falling. The Caldera-Room's floor is dusted with it—a thin, white layer that softens the lethal edges of the obsidian plates and muffles your footsteps, though the footsteps themselves are nearly silent. The chalk is an excretion, a waste product, the residue of whatever dry, anhydrous metabolism the wire-lichen uses to convert mineral substrate into living metal. It falls like snow, but it falls in stillness. There is no wind to carry it. It descends in a slow, straight, vertical drift, accumulating in precise cones beneath each thicket.

The lichen does not move, but it is alive. You can feel it—the fronds are warm, warmer than the air, and there is a faint, barely perceptible vibration in the thicket, a hum, a low-frequency tremor that you feel in your fingertips before you hear it in your ears. The wire-lichen is generating electricity. The copper-magnesium filaments, coated in chalk, in contact with the dry, ozonic air, are a living battery. The static charge that pools on the fronds is not an accident. It is the organism's product, its contribution to the Caldera-Room's ecology. The salamander-things harvest it. The moths benefit from the warmth. The wire-lichen is the foundation, the keystone, the silent, humming engine of this dry cathedral.

When you brush against a thicket, the chalk falls in a burst, a silent white explosion, and the fronds sway—slightly, slowly—and then return to stillness. The lichen does not react. It does not flinch. It does not grow toward you or away from you. It simply exists, accumulating metal, generating charge, shedding chalk, in a cycle that has no beginning and no end, only the slow, patient accumulation of mineral and the slow, patient shedding of dust.

## The Chalcedony Buildings

They are not alive. Not anymore. But they were.

The hollow gourds of chalcedony are the remains of structural mosses, organisms that grew in dense, hollow columns, secreting layer after layer of silica around themselves, building walls that thickened over generations. The mosses are gone—calcified, entombed in their own architecture—but the buildings remain. You can see the ghosts of the builders in the walls: dark, branching traces, the shadows of old root-networks, preserved between layers of calcite like fossils in sedimentary rock. The traces grow more complex as you move deeper into the wall, toward the oldest layers, and then they thin, simplify, disappear. The city grew from a single organism, a single moss, that spread and diversified and built and built until it reached the limits of the Caldera-Room and then—stopped. Died. Calcified. Left behind this orthogonal grid of hollow stone gourds.

The buildings are not empty. The moths use them as perches, grinding the outer walls. The salamander-things shelter in the doorways, their pale bodies framed by the translucent arches. The wire-lichen has colonized the interiors of the larger gourds, its copper fronds extending through the doorways and out through cracks in the chalcedony, connecting building to building in a network of living wire. The city is not ruins. It is an ongoing project. The builders are dead, but the inhabitants have repurposed the architecture, and the architecture is still growing—calcite layers still accreting on the walls, doorways still narrowing, buildings still slowly, imperceptibly changing shape.

You enter one of the larger gourds—the size of a small room, its doorway a shoulder-width arch—and the silence deepens. The chalcedony walls are thick enough to muffle even the subterranean clicks of the settling obsidian. You are inside a stone that was once alive, surrounded by the shadows of the organism that built it, and the air is still and warm and dry and tastes of iron and time.

The floor is smooth, worn by the passage of moths and salamander-things and the slow accumulation of chalk. There is a fine, white layer of it everywhere, and in the chalk, you see tracks—the thin, filamentary traces of salamander-things, the broader, abrasive grooves of moths. The tracks overlap, layer over layer, a record of movement so dense it looks like a pattern, a texture, a design. You are standing in the accumulated history of a thousand slow lives, each one recorded in dust.

## What Is Not Here

There are no vertebrates. There are no creatures with blood or bone. There is no moisture, no slime, no mucous, no secretion that is not mineral. There are no sounds that carry beyond arm's length. There are no smells except the dry, astringent bite of sulfur and the flat, metallic taste of ozonic dust.

There is no death, either—or rather, death here does not look like death. It looks like calcification, like crystallization, like the slow conversion of soft tissue to hard stone. The moths do not die and fall and rot. They slow, they settle, they harden. Their chitin wings become thin sheets of dark mineral, their bodies become nodules of agate. The salamander-things, at the end of their long, motionless lives, grip a mica column and do not let go, and over decades, their pale bodies are coated in chalk and calcite, becoming part of the column, part of the architecture, part of the slow, patient geology of the room.

There are no predators. There is no need. The resources here are scarce enough that no organism can afford the luxury of eating another. The moths eat dust. The salamander-things eat electricity. The wire-lichen eats basalt. The chalcedony buildings are the remains of mosses that ate—what? Something that is no longer here. Something that existed in the Caldera-Room's infancy, when the basalt was newer and the vents were hotter and the chemistry was different. The builders lived in a different room, a room that no longer exists, and they built their city in that room, and then the room changed, and they died, and their city remained, and new creatures came to inhabit it, creatures adapted to the room as it is now: dry, hot, still, silent.

You are the wettest thing that has ever been in this room. Your breath, heavy with water vapor, fogs the air in front of you. Your footsteps, soft as they are, are the loudest sounds the Caldera-Room has heard in centuries. Your blood, coursing through your ears, is a chaotic, wet, mammalian rhythm that belongs to a different world, a world of rain and wind and rot and the desperate, beautiful urgency of water-based life.

The inhabitants of the Caldera-Room do not know you are here. They do not care. They are grinding dust, harvesting charge, growing metal, accreting stone, at the speed of geology, in the silence of stone, in the heat of a kiln that has no door and no exit and no end.

You will leave. They will remain.
