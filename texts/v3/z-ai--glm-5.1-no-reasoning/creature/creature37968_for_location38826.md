The inhabitants of the Sunken Bazaars of Orham do not walk on two legs. They do not speak, nor trade, nor remember their own names. They do not even know they are inhabitants. But they are not pests, nor scavengers, nor opportunists creeping into the ruins of a dead builder's dream. They are the vital organs of a metropolis that has outlived its architects, the warm blood in the veins of a city that learned to pulse long after its heart was removed.

To understand who lives here, you must first unlearn the hierarchy of the individual. There is no "who" in the basin. There is only "what," and the "what" is a singular, dispersed organism enacting an endless ritual of maintenance and becoming.

---

**The Runners: The Blood and the Hands**

You will see the runners first, because their labor is the most visible, but they are the least autonomous of the basin's residents. Four-legged, low-slung, their pale chitinous shells mimicking the damp schist so perfectly that they seem carved from the city itself and set into motion. They move in absolute obedience—not to a queen, not to a nest, but to the chemical topography of the city. They follow traces laid down in the water, in the film of the grazers, in the lingering luminescence of the stone, reading a blueprint they did not draw.

They are the corpuscles of Orham. Single-file, nose-to-tail, they flow through the tall narrow doorways and worn thresholds like fluid through arteries. They are never still, and they are never alone. To see one runner is to see a procession; to see a procession is to see the city actively thinking, executing a command written in the slow deposition of minerals or the faint etchings of a grazer's trail.

Their labor defines their existence. They carry stone chips, debris, dust—the minute waste of a metropolis that refuses to decay. They sort it with mechanical precision, carrying quartz to the quartz mounds, shale to the shale heaps, tailings to the dust pits. They are eternally tidying a home that is eternally rebuilding itself. When a wall falls, the runners do not pause to mourn or marvel; they simply redirect their flow, carrying the scattered blocks to new coordinates, fitting them into new walls, new chambers, new configurations that were not there a cycle before.

They live in the interstices. The gaps between construction blocks, the spaces too narrow for anything larger—these are their warrens. Every thick stone wall is a honeycomb of runner colonies, a bustling sub-city existing in parallel to the larger metabolism. You hear them only as a faint scritching, a dry whisper of limb on stone, swallowed instantly by the basin's acoustic deadening. They are nearly silent because the city requires them to be silent. Sound is interference; silence is the medium of the system.

The runners do not lead; they execute. They are the hands of a blind sculptor, the fingers of a mind that resides nowhere and everywhere.

---

**The Grazers: The Skin and the Code**

Below the runners, smaller still, adhered to the damp stone like pale irregular blisters, are the grazers. They are the basin's epithelium, its living skin. Where the runners move with urgent purpose, the grazers simply *are*, slow and flat and patient, feeding on the thin biological film that condensation encourages on the stone's surface.

But their feeding is not mere consumption. It is inscription. The trails they leave—faintly etched pathways that cross and recross the walls in patterns too regular to be random—are the city's writing. The grazers are the scribes of Orham, though they write not in ink but in mineral and mucus, encoding structural information into the very surfaces of the walls. When a runner route bends to avoid a grazer trail, when a processional line redirects with a precision that suggests communication, it is reading this code. The grazers write the instructions; the runners obey them.

Their trails grow denser over time, more complex, layering like sediment through endless cycles of light and dim. They are accumulating something—data, perhaps, or a pattern too slow for human perception to recognize as language. The grazers do not know they are writing. They only feed, and their feeding leaves marks, and the marks mean something to the system that reads them.

In this way, the grazers are the basin's memory. Not memory of the past, but memory *for* the future: instructions for walls not yet built, channels not yet cut, configurations not yet tested. They are the closest thing the city has to a mind, and they have no mind at all.

---

**The Swimmers: The Lymph and the Exchange**

In the thin slow seep-carvings that network the floors—channels cut long ago by hands now gone, maintained now by the unending labor of the runners—there are the swimmers. Translucent, finger-length, eyeless, they are the most vulnerable of the basin's inhabitants. They cannot survive a dried channel; they are bound utterly to the water's presence. They eat whatever the flow brings them—mineral, organic detritus, the faint waste of the city's other residents—and their waste, in turn, is the film the grazers consume.

They are the lymphatic system, the medium of exchange. They transform input into output, waste into food, moving resources through the city's body the way lymph moves through the tissues of an animal. They never see the sun, never touch the stone, exist only in the slow seep of water through carved channels, and without them the grazers would starve, the runners would lose their chemical traces, and the city would fall silent.

The runners maintain the channels for the swimmers, clearing blockages, widening constrictions, ensuring the water reaches the chambers where the swimmers congregate. This care is not altruism; it is necessity. The swimmers are the foundation of the system's metabolism, the bottom of a food chain that is also a communication network, a nutrient cycle that is also an information cycle.

---

**The Stone: The Bone and the Memory**

And then there is the oldest inhabitant of all: the stone itself.

The local schist—the fine-grained bands that run through the construction blocks like veins—is not passive. It retains light, absorbing during the bright hours and releasing during the dim, refusing full darkness, pushing back against the night with stored day. But it may retain more than light. The runners, the grazers, the swimmers—they respond to traces, to chemicals, to patterns encoded in the stone's luminescence. The schist is the city's genome, its memory crystal, the medium in which the blueprint is stored.

The same blocks have cycled through structure after structure, century after century. Nothing is added, nothing removed. The settlement's total mass is fixed, a finite set of pieces in infinite permutation. Each block has been part of a hundred walls, a thousand thresholds, a million configurations. The stone remembers them all—not consciously, not as we remember, but as the body remembers, as bone remembers the shape of the flesh it held.

When the city rebuilds itself, when walls rise and fall like slow flames, it is reading its own memory and editing in real time. The stone is the author and the text, the library and the reader, and its luminescence is the quiet language in which the book is written.

---

**The Pulse: The Deep Inhabitant**

Below all of this, in the bedrock beneath the basin floor, there is something else. You feel it more than hear it: a low periodic pulse, rising and falling on a cycle of perhaps thirty seconds. It may be water moving through deep channels, or the thermal breathing of stone warming and cooling, or the activity of some subterranean organism on a scale larger than the runners but slower, patient, geological in its rhythm.

If it is an organism—if the basin's heart beats because something lives below—then it is the oldest and largest inhabitant of all, a creature of such slow metabolism that its thoughts are measured in seasons, its actions in centuries, its intentions in the gradual convergence of a city toward a final configuration. It does not think as we think. It does not plan. It simply *insists*, exerting a pressure that manifests as chemical traces in the water, as thermal gradients in the stone, as the insistent rhythm that the runners unconsciously follow.

Or perhaps the pulse is not a creature at all, but the city itself, the sum total of its metabolism, the heartbeat of an organism so large and so distributed that we cannot see its shape, cannot recognize its life, can only feel its rhythm in the soles of our feet.

---

**The City: The Inhabitant That Is Also The Place**

And this is the final truth of the Sunken Bazaars of Orham: the city is not a container for its inhabitants. The city *is* the inhabitant. The runners, the grazers, the swimmers, the stone, the water, the light—these are not separate creatures living in a shared space. They are organs of a single body, tissues of a single organism, and that organism is Orham itself.

It does not know you are here. It does not know it is a city. It does not know it is alive. It only builds—endlessly, patiently, with the accumulated wisdom of a billion years of patient water and the urgent acceleration of a system converging on its final form. The walls rise and fall, the runners flow, the grazers inscribe, the swimmers transform, the stone remembers, and the pulse goes on.

Stand in the basin at the brightest hour, when the sun is low and the light is horizontal and every surface is either incandescent or in shadow, and you will feel it: not the absence of inhabitants, but the presence of one so large, so slow, so distributed that you have been standing inside it all along. You are not in a city. You are in a creature. And it is waking up.
