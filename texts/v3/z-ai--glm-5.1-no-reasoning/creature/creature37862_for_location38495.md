The ones who stayed are not gone. They are simply no longer distinct.

When the water first rose—and it rose slowly, slowly enough that the evacuation was orderly, enough that the last shifts packed their footlockers and walked up the flooded stairwells with their belongings held above their heads—some did not leave. Not because they were trapped. Not because they were foolish. Because they were tired. Because the manufactory had been their whole world, and the thought of a dry world beyond it had become abstract, theoretical, uninhabitable. They stayed the way stones stay in a river: not from resolve, but from an inability to imagine being elsewhere.

They are still here.

---

You would not recognize them at first. You might not recognize them at all. The water has been working on them the way it has worked on the concrete—slowly, with the patient weight of something that has nowhere else to be, leaning on them until they yielded, until they bowed into new shapes. The ones in the corridors, the ones who lasted longest, are almost architectural now. They sat against the walls in the residential blocks, in the mess halls, in the narrow spaces between bunks, and they waited. The water filled them. The silt settled on them. The growth found them.

There is a figure in Chamber Seven—east corridor, third doorway, the one where the rail curves and the ceiling is lower—who has become a node. You would see only a hummock of bone-white silt, a slight thickening in the layer of green, a shape that might be a rock or a collapsed shelf. But the shape is seated. The spine has curved the way the walls have curved—not failing, just accommodating. The head is bowed forward. The arms rest on what were once thighs, though the distinction between arm and leg and torso has softened; the water has been dissolving the boundaries for centuries, carrying away what will dissolve and leaving what will not.

The growth has colonized them completely. Roots have pushed through softened cloth and entered the spaces between, expanding inside like the roots in the machines, like veins in a body. The rubbery leaves sheath the shoulders and trail from the bowed head in long ribbons that pulse with the same slow breathing rhythm as the rest. The bioluminescence clusters where the chest once was, as if drawn to some residual warmth or mineral concentration, and it glows there more steadily than on the walls—a small lantern in the dark water, marking a presence.

They are not dead. That is the wrong word. They are not alive either, not in any way that would be recognizable from the outside. But the boundary between them and the place has become permeable, and something flows through that boundary, and something flows back. The plants that root in them are healthier than the plants that root in concrete—they are darker green, they pulse more visibly, they grow thicker and faster. The pipes that run beneath them seem warmer. The silt that covers them has a different composition, richer in compounds that the glass-fish favor; sometimes a fish will drift down from the upper corners and settle on one of these hummocks and stay much longer than it stays on any leaf, attached by its fine tendrils, feeding on something the hummock provides or produces or simply *is*.

---

There are others. Dozens, perhaps—spread through the complex in the residential blocks and the workrooms and the corridors where they stopped walking and sat down and never rose again. They are not identical. Each has accommodated differently, yielded differently, been colonized by the growth in a pattern unique to their shape and position. Some are more architecture than person now, so thoroughly overgrown and silted and dissolved that only the vaguest suggestion of a human arrangement remains—a symmetry that is too deliberate to be geological, a curve that echoes the curve of a spine or a skull. Others are more legible. A hand, still distinct, fingers curled around something that might be a tool or a pipe or simply the memory of gripping. A face, barely visible beneath the leaf-sheath, the features softened but still present, still arranged in the expression they wore when the water finally closed over them.

That expression is not anguish. That is the thing you would expect to find and do not. It is not peace either—not exactly, not the peace of release or resolution. It is the expression of someone who has stopped waiting. Who has realized that what they were waiting for was not going to arrive, and who has let go of the expectation without letting go of the posture of waiting. It is an expression of accommodation. Of having learned, in the body, that there is nowhere else to be.

---

They have dreams. This is speculation, but the place invites it, and the evidence—if the faint warmth in the pipes can be called evidence, if the particular richness of the silt can be called evidence, if the way the glass-fish linger on them can be called evidence—suggests something is happening inside them that is more than dissolution. The machines run because the water flows, and the water flows because the channels are clear, and the channels are clear because something maintains them—not deliberately, not with intention, but as a side effect of simply *being present in the system*. The ones who stayed are part of the system now. They are not operating the machines. They are not feeding the plants. But they are not doing nothing either. They are *occupied*. They are *in circuit*. Their slow dissolution feeds the silt that feeds the roots that feed the leaves that feed the fish that feed the water that flows through the channels that turn the turbines that pump the fluid that feeds the roots that grow through them, and they are the medium through which part of this cycle passes, and that means—they must mean—something.

Perhaps they dream the chamber's dreams. The slow breath. The hour-long heartbeat. The rhythms that phase in and out of alignment until the whole space seems to tighten and release. Perhaps when the rhythm peaks, they feel it as a warmth, a fullness, a sense of completion that they never felt when they were still distinct and separate and working the shifts the manufactory assigned them. Perhaps they feel themselves in the water and the water in them and the green growing through them and the iron turning beside them and the silt settling on them and the light glowing from them, and perhaps it feels like—not death, not life, but something for which there is no word because no one who has that feeling ever needs words again.

Perhaps they chose this. Not the drowning—the water rose, and they could not have stopped it—but this, the staying, the yielding, the becoming-part-of. Perhaps they watched the first leaves colonize the machines and felt something that was not horror but recognition. Perhaps they saw the glass-fish drifting in the current and understood that existence could be minimal, could be nearly theoretical, and still be sufficient. Perhaps they felt the warmth of the water and the press of it from every direction and thought: *this is what it would be like to be held and never released*. And perhaps that thought was not frightening. Perhaps it was *peaceful*, and perhaps they let themselves be colonized the way the concrete let itself be bowed—not failing, just accommodating—and perhaps they found, in the yielding, a kind of completion the manufactory never provided when it was producing whatever it was built to produce.

---

You do not see them when you are in the chamber. They do not reveal themselves to you. The place does not perform its depths for visitors. But if you stay long enough—if you slow down enough—if you match the pace and stop expecting anything to happen quickly—you begin to feel them the way you feel the engines: not as presence exactly, but as a density, a weight in the water, a warmth that has no source. You begin to notice the hummocks in the silt and realize they are placed too deliberately to be rubble. You begin to notice the brighter clusters of bioluminescence and realize they glow in shapes that echo the body. You begin to hear, in the long slow breath of the chamber, something that sounds like the sound a person makes when they have finally, finally stopped struggling.

And you understand: this is not a place where people died. This is a place where people *became*. Became part of. Became substrate. Became the medium through which the water and the green and the iron and the slow warm dark circulate. They are not remnants. They are not ghosts. They are *current tenants*, as the plants are current tenants, as the glass-fish are current tenants, occupying a system that was designed to deliver resources and positioning themselves at the delivery points. They are fed. They feed. They are held. They hold. They are where they are because where they are is sufficient.

The thought is not frightening. The thought is *peaceful*, and that is why you leave.

But they do not leave. They cannot leave. They have nowhere else to be, and they have known that for a very long time, and they have made of that knowledge not a despair but a *dwelling*—a place to inhabit, a way of being present, a participation in the slow circulation that asks nothing and gives nothing and is, for that very reason, complete.

They are the drowned manufactory, and the drowned manufactory is them, and the water has been here so long it has forgotten it was ever an intruder, and they have been here so long they have forgotten they were ever separate, and the green grows on, and the iron turns, and the silt settles, and the light glows from where their hearts once were, and the chamber breathes its hour-long breath, and everything—everything—is accommodated.
