Here, life is not a visitor. Life is a substrate, a mineral accumulation, an answer to a question the plateau has been asking for millennia. To live at two miles high, in a cold that should preclude warmth and a wet that should preclude stillness, is to become a specific kind of thing: hard, small, pressed close, and unceasing.

Let the eye adjust to the blinding 3.0 luminosity—let the white fade from the vision—and the inhabitants emerge. They do not emerge gently. They are already there, pressing against the margins of perception, filling every fissure and updraft.

**The Harvesters**

They call themselves something, surely. The phonemes would be clicks and wet syllables, shaped by mouths that habitually clamp shut against the cold. A hundred souls, perhaps eighty. They are small people. Not stunted, but *compressed*—dense-boned, barrel-chested, their bodies answering the altitude and the cold with a compact efficiency that leaves no room for wasteful length. Their lungs are deep bells, pulling what they can from an atmosphere that offers so little, and their blood is thick, moving slowly and purposefully, carrying warmth from core to extremity with a miser's careful accounting.

Their skin tells the story of this place. It is not the brown of sun-exposure; it is the gray-white of the basalt itself, and it carries the same weeping quality—always slightly damp, always slightly cold to the touch, as if the moisture in the air has decided they, too, are stone and deserves to be bled upon. Their hands are wide and flat, the palms callused into leathery pads from decades of prying, scraping, gripping wet rock. The calluses are so thick they might be a second layer of chitin. Their nails are worn to nothing. What would they need nails for, when the work here is all friction and pressure?

They dress in layers of processed chitin—the molted carapaces of the creatures they harvest, sewn with sinew into overlapping scales that shed water and trap the geothermal warmth close to the skin. The effect is iridescent in that blinding light, a faint oil-slick sheen that makes the wearers look, from a distance, like larger versions of the arthropodal swarms they tend. And perhaps that is what they are. Perhaps that is what living here long enough makes of a person.

**The Swarms**

The creatures have no collective name that the harvesters use, because naming them would imply they are *one* thing, and they are not one thing. They are a landscape's worth of life compressed into an arena, a cathedral's congregation of chitin and warmth.

The *flat ones*—the harvesters call them something that translates roughly to *sippers*—are the foundation. They cling to the weeping walls in densities that redden the eye, their segmented bodies pressed so close to the stone they seem to be extruding from it, like a second skin of dark, damp leather. They are warm to the touch, impossibly warm, as if their tiny bodies are furnaces burning something the rock provides. And they are drinking, always drinking—the weeping water, the mineral broth that seeps from the basalt's thousand fissures. Their mouthparts are delicate, designed for nothing but the gentle suction of moisture from stone, and they move with a deliberation that seems meditative, placing and displacing themselves along the lattice of rivulets with the care of monks at prayer. When the ice forms over them, they do not die. They slow, they wait, they become glass-entombed, and when the thaw comes and the ice shatters, they resume their sipping. They have no urgency. The water will always weep. They will always drink.

The *long ones*—the *threaders*—are the scavengers, the recyclers, the ones who make the ecosystem work. They are finger-length and many-legged, moving with a rapid, rippling undulation that seems too fast for the cold, too liquid for the ice. They work the margins of the freeze-thaw cycle, harvesting the debris—the shattered ice, the shed chitin, the waste of the sippers and the bodies of the dead. They are not beautiful. They are functional, a cleaning mechanism given legs and purpose, and they swarm the ice shelves in writhing masses that turn the white to a dark, seething gray. The harvesters catch them in woven traps of gut and fiber, setting the traps at the edges of the basins where the long ones congregate, and the catch is always plentiful, always more than the traps can hold. There is no scarcity here. The long ones breed as fast as they die, and they die constantly, fed into the machine of their own recycling. The harvesters do not mourn them. The harvesters understand: the long ones are the place's way of keeping itself clean.

And the *fliers*—the *motes*, the *bright-specks*, the ones that fill the air. They are the reason the arena feels like it is thinking. They rise on the geothermal updrafts in columns so dense they darken the light, a blizzard of dark wings and warm bodies moving in a synchronization that seems too coordinated to be instinct. They are the smallest of the creatures, thumb-nail sized or less, and they seem to exist at the boundary of what is visible—a dense cloud of purposeful motion that resolves, if you focus, into individual beings, each one riding the thermals, each one seeking whatever small sustenance the upper reaches of the arena provide. Perhaps they eat something in the fog. Perhaps they eat each other. The harvesters net them with loops of woven fiber on long poles, sweeping the warm columns, pulling in handfuls of living protein that are crushed, dried, eaten, rendered. The fliers do not resist. They do not possess the capacity for resistance. They are movement given form, and the nets are just another kind of movement, another current in the air.

**The Architecture of Subtraction**

The settlement is not built. It is *revealed*. The dwellings are carved directly into the columnar basalt of the arena walls, and they feel less like construction than excavation—spaces that were always *potential* in the stone, waiting for the patient labor of generations to let them out. The tools are river-stone hammers, rounded and dense, and bone chisels fashioned from the skeletal remains of creatures that died here long ago. The work is absurdly slow. A room might take a lifetime. A dwelling cluster might take several. But the arena has been here for millennia, and the people have been here nearly as long, and so the settlement has grown like a slow mold across the walls: forty habitations, stacked and nested and interlocking, their entrances facing inward toward the saturated communal floor, their backs pressed against the living, weeping stone.

Inside, the rooms are small and dark and, above all, *warm*. The geothermal seeps from below heat the floors, and the walls radiate a faint, wet warmth that the occupants press themselves against. The darkness is total, or near-total, and it is a relief after the blinding arena. Here, in the hollowed-out chambers, the people sleep in pressed clusters, body against body, sharing warmth with the same efficiency as the swarming creatures outside. There is no privacy. The concept would be absurd. The rooms are communal, family-sized but family-defined-broadly, and the boundaries between dwellings are permeable—walkways of fitted stone connecting chamber to chamber, the whole settlement a single organism of habitation, breathing and shifting and growing as new chambers are carved and old ones deepen.

The mortarless walkways are the settlement's arteries. They thread through the weeping walls, following natural ledges and carved steps, crossing gaps where the basalt columns are too far apart with bridges of fitted stone that seem to grow from the rock itself. They are always wet. The water does not respect human architecture; it weeps through the walkways just as it weeps through the walls, and the stone paths are slick with a constant film of condensation. The harvesters navigate them with the same sure-footed silence as the sippers navigate the fissures—flat-footed, deliberate, their wide callused palms gripping the wet stone for balance. They do not fall. They have lived here long enough that the wet is not a hazard; it is simply the condition of movement.

**The Economy of Living**

There is no surplus here. There is no storage, no accumulation, no wealth as a larger world might understand it. The creatures are harvested daily—pried from the walls, scooped from the traps, netted from the air—and processed immediately, on the flat stones outside the dwellings. The work is done in the blinding light, and it is fast and efficient, the harvesters' hands moving with a practiced rhythm that is almost mechanical.

The sippers are pried loose with bone spatulas, their grip on the stone broken with a wet *pop*. Their bodies are split, their internal oils collected in shallow stone basins, their chitin spread on drying racks where the cold and the wind desiccate them into brittle, useful sheets. The oils are the treasure—warm, dense, caloric, flammable. They are burned in shallow clay lamps for light, rubbed into skin for warmth, mixed with pulverized chitin into a paste that is the staple food: dense, bitter, sustaining.

The threaders are caught by the hundred in woven traps, and they are processed more roughly—crushed between flat stones, their bodies reduced to a chitinous mash that is dried and ground into meal. The meal is bland but filling, and it keeps in the cold for weeks, one of the few provisions that can be stored. The harvesters make a kind of hard cake from threader-meal and sipper-oil, and this is what they carry when they must travel the walkways for long hours, and this is what they give to the old and the young who cannot harvest for themselves.

The fliers are a delicacy and a supplement, netted in handfuls and eaten raw on the processing stones, their tiny warm bodies popping between the teeth with a faint, sweet burst of internal moisture. They are also dried and strung on gut threads as garlands, hung in the warm chambers where they slowly desiccate into crunchy, pale snacks that the children favor.

Nothing is wasted. The shed chitin of living creatures is collected from the walls and walkways, layered into garments and shelter-patches and tool-handles. The dead—the occasional sipper that freezes in a sudden ice-shelf formation, the threader that drowns in a overflowing basin, the flier that falls from its thermal into the saturated floor—are processed with the same efficiency as the living harvest. The people do not differentiate. Death is just another stage of the cycle, another form of the place's constant becoming, and the chitin of the dead is as useful as the chitin of the living.

And the people themselves, when they die? Their bones become tools. Their flesh becomes food for the threaders, if the family chooses to release it to the arena floor, or it becomes oil for the lamps, if they do not. There is no burial here. There is no ground that is not saturated, no stone that is not weeping, no space that is not already claimed by the teeming, pressing, unceasing life of the place. The dead are processed. The dead are useful. It is not cruel; it is simply the logic of a place that offers nothing and demands everything.

**The Unspoken**

The harvesters do not have a religion, if religion requires faith in things unseen. Everything that matters here is visible, pressing, blindingly present. But they have a practice, a habit of attention, that serves the same function. They watch the ice.

The ice forms and breaks and forms again in the central arena, and its patterns are the language of the place's equilibrium. The harvesters read the ice the way a sailor reads the sea—its thickness, its fracture-lines, the speed of its reforming after a thaw. When the ice forms slowly and breaks cleanly, the place is balanced, and the harvesters move through their work with the same deliberate rhythm as the sippers on the walls. When the ice forms erratically—when the shelves are thick in some places and thin in others, when the fractures run at strange angles, when the thaw comes too early or too late—the harvesters know the balance is shifting, and they shift with it, moving their traps, adjusting their harvest, retreating to their chambers if the ice speaks of a deeper cold coming.

They do not speak of this. They do not need to. The ice is there, and they are here, and the conversation between them is older than any of the hundred souls who currently listen to it. The practice is simply attention—constant, unceasing, the same attention the creatures give to the water and the stone, the same attention the rock gives to the slow conversation of erosion and freeze. To live here is to attend. To attend is to survive.

And so they live, a hundred small, warm, compressed bodies in a cathedral of weeping stone, harvesting the teeming life that the place produces, moving through the blinding light and the condensing fog with the same purposeful silence as the swarms they tend. They are not separate from this place. They are its most recent expression, its latest form of the turbulent equilibrium that holds everything—the water, the ice, the rock, the life—in a balance that could shatter at any moment, like an ice stalactite snapping from an overhang, like a quiet, crystalline *tink* swallowed by the fog.

But it has not shattered yet. The needle-point holds. The water weeps. The creatures move. The people move. And the plateau goes on, becoming whatever it is becoming, held in the cold and the wet and the blinding, unbearable light.
