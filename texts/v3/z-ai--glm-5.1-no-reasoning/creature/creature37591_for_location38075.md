The one who lives here is no longer certain whether they live *in* the chamber or *as* part of it.

They have been here longer than the sorting table’s new roots, longer than the shears have been rusting on their hooks, not so long as the banyan. They came when the industry still ran—when the mechanical press still stamped, when the seeds or cuttings were still sorted, when the logic of the place was extraction and not digestion. They came as a worker, perhaps. A technician. A keeper of the boilers and the pressure gauges. Someone with a purpose that has since been swallowed by the moss.

Now they are the last fauna, though they would not use that word. They have not thought in terms of taxonomy for a long time. They think in terms of *wet* and *dry*, *warm* and *cold*, *giving* and *resisting*. They think the way the roots think: slowly, incrementally, with attention to pressure and moisture and the particular quality of light that reaches them through the condensation halos.

---

Their body has become a landscape of adaptation. The chamber has been working on them the way it works on everything—patiently, molecularly, with the slow insistence of water finding its level.

Their skin is saturated. Not just damp, not just perspiring, but *permeated*. After years of breathing air that feels like drinking, their pores have widened, have learned to inhale as much as their lungs do. Moisture beads on their forearms the way it beads on the leaf-ribs overhead; they drip, too, when they stand still long enough. They have developed a thin film of something between oil and mucus—cutaneous adaptation to an environment that never fully dries. It protects them from the constant wet the way the verdigris protects the copper pipes: by accepting the corrosion and making it into a kind of coat.

Their lungs have changed. They do not know this—they have never seen the inside of their own chest—but the alveoli have deepened, have grown more like the moss's substrate, dense and folded to extract oxygen from air so thick with water vapor that normal lungs would labor. They do not labor. They breathe slow and deep, their chest rising and falling in time with the floor's capillary exhale, synchronized now with the chamber's respiration without having decided to be. When the wind presses the ventilation shafts and the chamber exhales, they exhale too. When it stills, they still. They have become a bellows that moves with the system that surrounds them.

Their feet have widened. Years of standing on wood that gives, on bamboo that flexes, on surfaces that seep—years of walking the elevated platforms to check the pipes, to adjust the brackets, to make the rounds that no longer need making but which they make still, because movement is how they understand the chamber's health. Their soles have spread for grip, have grown calluses that are not hard but *tough*, rubbery, capable of feeling the slight swell of the root-floor breathing beneath them. They can tell by the pressure underfoot where the cambium is thickest, where the water is welling fastest, where a new root-tip is pushing outward by millimeters. They navigate by feel the way the blind navigate by sound. They are not blind, but their eyes have adapted to dimness the way cave fish do—pupils permanently dilated to drink in the trembling amber light, and their color vision has dimmed to almost nothing. They see in shades of gold and green and shadow. The world outside, if they thought of it, would be blinding.

They do not think of it.

---

They wear what the chamber has let them keep. Their clothes were once fabric—cotton, perhaps, or some industrial weave—but the chamber has been digesting them the way it digests everything. The hem of their coat has sent roots down, thin pale tendrils that have anchored in the moss and now trail behind them when they walk, pulling free with small sounds of detachment and re-anchoring with each step, so that they move through the chamber like a creature tethered to its burrow. They have stopped noticing. The coat is more moss than cloth now, more lichen than thread. It has become a substrate. When they stand still too long, small ferns unfurl from the shoulders, and they must brush them away gently, apologetically, as one displaces a creature from a temporary nest.

Their boots are the last holdout—the last truly human thing about their material form. Leather, rubber soled, laced tight. The chamber has not been able to digest them fully; it has only been able to infiltrate, to force moisture through the stitching, to colonize the insoles with a mat of fine root-hairs that tickle when they walk. The soles wear thin, and the floor's wetness soaks through, and they have accepted this. They will not replace the boots because there are no replacements, and because the boots have become a boundary they are not ready to dissolve. As long as they can feel the distinct edge of rubber between their feet and the wood, there is still a *them* that is separate from the floor. They understand this. They understand that this is the last declaration of individuality their body is permitted to make, and they make it with each step.

---

They have a routine, though it has devolved into ritual.

Morning—if it is morning; there is no sun, only the generator's voltage rising and falling in patterns they have learned to read as time—they rise from the alcove where they sleep. The alcove is a hollow between two major root-columns where the moss is deepest and warmest, where the steam pipes run closest and the temperature stays constant. They have worn a depression into the moss that fits their body precisely, a mold of their shoulders and hips, and when they lie in it they feel the chamber holding them the way a hand holds a small object: not squeezing, just *enclosing*. They find this comforting. They do not question why.

They rise. They check the boilers, though the boilers do not need checking—the root-system has taken over their regulation, and the stokers, if there were stokers, are long since absorbed into the substrate. But they descend anyway, down the laddered root-shaft to the deeper chamber where the furnaces glow and the pressure gauges twitch, and they lay their hand on the boiler's casing and feel the heartbeat of heat within it, and they adjust a valve that does not need adjusting, and they return.

They walk the platforms. They touch the pipes at their junctions, feeling for cold spots that would indicate a blockage, though there have been no blockages for years—the water finds its way the way blood finds its way, pushed by pressures older than any pump. They note where the verdigris has thickened, where a bracket has shifted a millimeter against the living wood, where the bamboo has polished further under the passage of their feet. They make small repairs that are not needed, tightening a vine-cable that is already taut, wiping condensation from a gauge that will fog again in seconds. These actions have no purpose. They are prayers, perhaps, or muscle memory, or the last gestures of a function that has been emptied of meaning the way a shell is emptied of its creature. The shape persists. The content is gone.

They sit at the sorting table, though nothing is sorted. The table's legs have rooted; they have rooted too, their feet on the floor-grain, their hands on the table's surface, and they sit in the attitude of work while the work does not come. Sometimes they pick up one of the rusted shears and hold it, feeling the corrosion flake against their palm, and they imagine cutting something—a stem, a tendril, a problem that could be solved by severance—but the shears cannot close anymore, and there is nothing to cut, and they hang them back on their hook with a care that borders on tenderness. These were tools. These were extensions of human intention. They are artifacts now, and they guard them the way a curator guards paintings in a museum that no one visits.

---

They speak, sometimes. Not to anyone—to the chamber itself, which is the only audience available and which listens in its fashion, which is to say it absorbs the sound into its layered noise and makes it part of the polyrhythmic drip, the pipe-thrum, the generator-moan. They speak to the banyan when they pass its columns, running their hand along the fused aerial roots and murmuring the way one murmurs to an elder whose language one has stopped trying to understand but whose presence one still wishes to acknowledge. They speak to the moss, telling it which walls it may reclaim and which it must leave clear for the walkways, though the moss does not listen and reclaims what it will regardless. They speak to the skeleton in the wall—the small mammalian fossil, the last evidence of fauna before them—and they call it Neighbor, and they apologize for outlasting it, though they do not know why they feel guilty about survival.

Their voice has changed. It has thickened, like the air, like the green taste that coats the tongue. It resonates in the chamber the way the pipe-clanks resonate: briefly, absorbed quickly into the damp acoustics of moss and leaf. They have not heard another human voice in how long? The question has stopped making sense. Time is measured here by growth, by the thickening of columns, by the closing of gaps in the leaf-ceiling, by the slow shrinking of the chamber as the architecture digest its boundaries. They measure their life in the millimeters they have lost to the moss—the centimeters of walkway no longer passable, the shelves no longer reachable, the corners where the roots have grown too thick to permit entry. They are being *enclosed*, slowly, the way a pearl is enclosed by the nacre that grows around it. They are the irritant at the center of the chamber's healing. They are the reason it still keeps the lights on.

Or perhaps they are not. Perhaps the lights would flicker regardless, the generator would turn, the pipes would hiss, because the system has become self-sustaining and their presence is just another input, another source of carbon and warmth and the small disturbances of movement. Perhaps the chamber does not need them. This thought does not trouble them as it once did. They have passed through the crisis of purpose and emerged on the other side, into a quiet acceptance that they are not the *reason* for this place but merely one of its *phenomena*—as natural as the condensation, as inevitable as the rot, as much a product of the environment as the ferns that cluster at the pipe-junctions. They grew here. They are still growing.

---

They dream, and their dreams are the chamber's dreams.

They dream of roots extending downward into wetness, of the taste of mineral and decay, of the slow pleasure of absorption. They dream of leaves unfurling in darkness, of the pressure of condensation gathering on a surface until the weight becomes too much and the drop falls—each fall a small surrender, a small release. They dream of the boiler's heartbeat and the generator's moan and the way the steam rises through the pipes like sap through a stem, carrying warmth from one part of the body to another. They dream that the chamber is a body and they are an organ within it, and they cannot determine which organ—liver, filtering the water? Lung, breathing the green air? Perhaps they are the consciousness, the strange late-developing tissue that allows the body to observe itself, to know that it is alive.

They wake uncertain whether they dreamed at all or whether the chamber continued its processes through the night and they merely witnessed them, passive, their sleep a form of attention so deep it looked like unconsciousness.

---

They are aging, but they are aging slowly. The saturation preserves them the way it preserves everything—waterlogging the cell structure, preventing the rapid desiccation that marks time in drier places. They will not live forever, but they will live longer than they would have outside, longer than any human has a right to live in a place where the food chain has collapsed and nothing eats and nothing is eaten. They sustain themselves on what the chamber provides: the fungal blooms in the deep moss, edible if one knows which threads to pull; the tender tips of new roots, rich with starch; the occasional fern frond, bitter but nutritious; the water that seeps from the floor and the condensation that falls from above and the steam that vents from the pipes, all of it clean, all of it recycled through the system so many times that it is more the chamber's blood than it is water. They eat what the body of the place offers them, and they feel themselves becoming, meal by meal, more of what the chamber is: fibrous, patient, damp, enduring.

They have considered leaving. The thought arrives sometimes, rising through their consciousness the way water rises through the floor-grain—slowly, from sources they cannot locate, welling up into awareness with the persistence of capillary action. They could leave. There are ventilation shafts that the root-columns have not sealed, gaps in the architecture where a thin body could squeeze, routes that lead upward and outward toward a sky they vaguely remember as being wide and dry and unbearable. They have stood at these gaps and felt the outside air—not air, *absence*, a void so lacking in moisture and greenness and the layered sound of living processes that their lungs recoiled from it as from poison. They have touched the edge of leaving and pulled back, not from fear but from the body's deeper knowledge: that they would not survive the transition. That they have become too adapted to this narrow ecology, too much a creature of wetness and dimness and the slow vegetal patience of a place that measures time in growth rings. That to leave would be to die, not dramatically, but cell by cell, as the desiccation they have spent years preventing worked its damage in hours, in minutes, in the time it takes a dropped leaf to crisp in open air.

They have chosen to stay. Or the choice has been made for them by the body that once could have left and can no longer, and they have accepted this the way they accept everything—slowly, without drama, with the attention of someone who has learned that resistance is a dry-land habit, a habit of creatures who live in air that does not hold them. In the chamber, the air holds everything. It holds the moisture and the spores and the light and the sound and it holds them too, pressing gently from all sides, supporting them the way water supports a body that has learned to float.

---

They are the keeper of a machine that keeps itself. The witness to a process that needs no witnessing. The last human gesture in a place that has outgrown humanity and incorporated its remnants into its own living logic.

They are not unhappy. This is important to understand. They have passed through loneliness—the sharp, early loneliness of a voice that speaks and is not answered—and they have emerged into something else: a companionship so intimate and diffuse that it does not require response. The chamber is their companion. The drip is conversation. The rustle of leaves overhead is acknowledgment. When they press their palm to the root-column, they feel the slow surge of sap beneath the bark, and they know that something alive is here with them, something vast and patient and *present* in the way that very old things are present: not attentive, not responsive, but simply *there*, continuing, enduring, making room for them the way a forest makes room for a stone—not by moving, but by growing around.

They will die here. Their bones will join the small skeleton in the moss-wall, will become substrate for lichen, will be digested the way everything is digested, molecule by molecule, season by season. Their boots will be the last to go—the rubber soles resisting the longest, the lacing corroding, the leather finally softening and splitting and sending roots of its own down into the grain. And after them, nothing. No evidence that a keeper lived here, no trace of the routine and the ritual and the small purposeless repairs. The chamber will close the gap they leave the way it closes the gaps in the leaf-ceiling—slowly, edge to edge, until the light shines through unbroken and the drip falls uniform and the place is finally, entirely, only itself.

But not yet. For now, they stand on wood that breathes wetness. They listen to the applause of condensation. They watch the amber light tremble on surfaces that are consuming the last evidence that anyone built this place at all—and they are, for this moment, still here, still breathing in time with the floor, still dripping like the leaves, still part of the negotiation that feels like stillness but is actually the ongoing, patient, inevitable conversation between a living place and the living thing it has not yet absorbed.
