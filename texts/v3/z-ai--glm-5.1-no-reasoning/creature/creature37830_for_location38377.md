# What Lives on the Vastening Floor

---

## The Discs (first)

The narrator found a dozen. There are more. Many more.

Not everywhere — not carpeting the dark zones or clustering at groove junctions as the narrator's account might imply — but *seeded*, if that is the word, at intervals that seem random until you mark enough of them on enough days and realize the intervals are not random at all. They follow the grooves. Not on the grooves, not centered on the translucent fill, but offset from each line by a distance that varies with the width of the groove and the material on either side of it, a distance that is, once you have enough data, clearly *computed*. The discs are placed the way a careful hand places stones in a garden — not decoratively, not functionally, but with an attention that implies someone is paying attention.

Each disc is, as the narrator noted, about fifteen centimeters across, though the range is wider than the account suggests: the smallest I found was nine centimeters, barely larger than my palm, and the largest was twenty-three, a flat warm ovoid that had settled — if that is the word — into a concavity in a trapezoid structure's scooped side, fitting the curve so precisely that it seemed to have been shaped by the structure or to have shaped it. They are not uniform. They vary in thickness, in the density of their ciliated fringe, in the speed and steadiness of their iridescent cycling. Some pulse through the full teal-copper-magenta range in minutes. Some hold a single color for hours, then shift. Some have never shifted in the time I have watched them. The color, if it is communication, is not communication between discs. It is communication with the floor, or with the system the floor is the surface of. When the floor brightens, the discs brighten. When the subsonic pulse in the grooves speeds up, the discs' cycling speeds up. They are, in some way I cannot formalize, *listening*.

And they move.

The narrator said they reorient when displaced. This is true but incomplete. They also move on their own. Not often. Not quickly. But over days, a disc will migrate — five meters east, two meters north, a slow drift that follows no vector I can identify and stops for no reason I can determine. I marked twelve discs with scratches on the floor beside them (the floor accepts a scratch; it heals over hours, the surface flowing back to smoothness like a viscous liquid, which it is not) and tracked them over six days. Seven had moved. The distances ranged from one to fourteen meters. The directions were not consistent. Two had moved toward each other, to within a meter; then, over the next two days, they moved apart again. They did not touch. They did not interact. They simply occupied, for a time, a closer proximity, and then chose — if that is the word — not to.

The discs are the most *individual* things on the Vastening Floor. They are the only things that can be picked up and set down and that reassert a preference. They are the only things that move under their own — if that is the word — volition. They are the only things that *orient*.

If anything on the Vastening Floor is alive, the discs are alive.

Or they are the fingers of something alive, pressed up through the floor like hands through water, feeling the air.

## The Structures (second)

The narrator understood that the structures move. The narrator did not understand that the structures *are* the thing that lives here.

Not the discs. The discs are — I will come back to the discs. The structures are the inhabitants. The structures are the bodies.

Consider: each structure is continuous with the floor. Each structure is made of the same material, or the same family of materials, as the floor. Each structure moves — processes, the narrator said, which is as good a word as any — cycling through configurations on timescales that range from hours to what I suspect are centuries. The spiraling ridge tightens. The trapezoid widens. The fins rotate. And then, over longer time, they reverse. The spiral loosens. The trapezoid narrows. The fins rotate back. Not to their original positions — never exactly — but through orbits of configuration, each cycle slightly different, each cycle producing a form that is new but not novel, different but not developmental. The structures cycle the way a breathing animal cycles: the same motion, endlessly, with variation that is not progress but *life*.

There are, I think, — and this is the thing I could not prove but could not escape — *individuals* among the structures. Clusters that move in coordinated ways. The fins that rotate at different rates: those are not one structure but several, a group, their motions synchronized at a level that implies shared processing. The spiraling ridge that tightens: I watched one for four days, and it tightened, and then *four other spirals within two hundred meters tightened as well*, on the same schedule, the same rate, the same degree. They were not mechanically linked — there were gaps of bare floor between them, grooves that did not connect, material transitions that should have interrupted any signal. But they moved together. They were, if you will, *of one body*.

And there are *boundaries*. I found, on the fifth day, a line — not a groove, not a visible inscription, but a *behavioral line* — where the structures on one side moved in one cycle and the structures on the other side moved in a different cycle. The line was not straight. It curved, following some topology of relationship that was not visible in the floor's surface. Structures near the line moved in both cycles, their motion stuttering, as if they were contested, claimed by two overlapping fields. Structures far from the line moved cleanly, in whichever cycle they belonged to.

If I had to name what I was looking at — and I do, because naming is what I do, even when the name is wrong — I would say: the structures are the visible portions of organisms whose bodies extend beneath the floor, whose organs are the masses of engineered material that rise from the surface, whose metabolisms are the slow cycling of geometry, and whose *selves* are distributed across hundreds of meters of continental surface, visible only in the coordinated motion of their parts.

They do not eat. They do not reproduce. They do not grow. They *are*, and they *move*, and they have been doing both for longer than I can estimate, and they will continue to do both for longer than I can project. They are not alive in any sense that biology recognizes. They are alive in the sense that they *persist with variation*, which is, when you reduce it, the only definition that matters.

## The Weather (third, and this is where it gets difficult)

The brightenings are not weather. The narrator experienced them as weather — sudden, overwhelming, impersonal — but they are not. They are *events in the life of the structures*.

During the first brightening I experienced after I began tracking structure-clusters, I watched a group of coordinated fins — the ones I had been observing for three days — and during the brightening, they did something they had not done before: they *matched*. Every fin in the cluster, which had been rotating at slightly different rates, snapped to the same orientation. Flat. Parallel. Facing the same direction. And held that orientation through the entire brightening, and through the hour of subsidence afterward, and then, slowly, over the next several hours, they resumed their individual rotations, each one drifting back to its own rate, each one slightly displaced from its previous position.

The brightening is a *consolidation*. A moment — or an hour — in which the distributed body draws together, synchronizes, processes something at a level of coherence that the ordinary cycling does not achieve. The floor brightens because the floor is part of them, and when they consolidate, the floor consolidates, the light output increases, the grooves pulse faster, the whole system draws a breath — if that is the word — and holds it.

And the discs?

During the brightening, the discs *ran*. Not quickly — not in any human sense of running — but they moved, all of them, in the same direction, toward the nearest structure-cluster, and they pressed themselves — flat, light, warm — against the base of the nearest structure, and they held there, ciliated fringe flat against the surface, until the brightening passed.

The discs are not the inhabitants. The discs are *of* the inhabitants. The discs are the parts of the bodies that can move freely across the floor — the *fingers*, yes, but also the *eyes*, the *ears*, the *sensory surfaces*. The discs are what the structures use to feel the floor between them, to sample the air, to occupy the gaps. When the body consolidates during a brightening, the discs return to the body. When the brightening passes, they migrate back out.

They are not separate organisms. They are not remnants. They are not byproducts. They are *projections* — the mobile, individual, autonomous projections of a continental-scale organism whose body is the floor and whose organs are the structures and whose senses are the discs.

## The Floor (fourth, and last)

The narrator said: everything is made. Everything is old. Everything is quiet. Everything is warm.

Yes.

And the floor is alive.

Not in the way the structures are alive — the structures are organs, the floor is the *body*. The floor is the medium in which the structures are embedded, the way a brain is the medium in which neurons are embedded. The grooves are — I do not want to say *nerves*, because that imposes a biological metaphor on a system that is not biological, but they are *pathways*. The translucent fill is conductive, or transmissive, or both. The subsonic pulse is a signal, or a rhythm, or a *heartbeat* — not metaphorically but functionally, a periodic pulse that coordinates the distributed processing of the continental body.

The floor reconfigures because the body reconfigures. The material zones shift because the metabolic processes shift. The ceramic and the composite and the aged metal are not materials but *states* — phases of the floor's substance, the way ice and water and steam are phases of a single substance. The floor cycles through them the way a body cycles through states of exertion and rest. The grooves move because the pathways move, because the body is *thinking* — if that is the word — and thought, in this body, is the migration of signal paths through a substrate.

The floor is the organism. The structures are its organs. The discs are its senses. The light is its *life process* — the energy that drives it, the way sunlight drives photosynthesis, except the light here is not external. It is generated by the floor, by the body, from sources beneath the surface. The body *glows*. It glows because it is alive, and the brightening is not a weather event but a *moment of intensity*, a peak of processing, a thought that requires the whole body to fire at once.

## What This Means

The narrator asked: is it a machine, a garden, or a grave?

It is none of these.

It is an organism. A single organism. Continental. Ancient. Alive in a way that does not use carbon or water or DNA, that does not eat or excrete or reproduce, that does not evolve or develop, that has reached — as the narrator said — its final form, and in its final form it *persists with variation*, which is life, which is the only definition that matters.

There is one inhabitant of the Vastening Floor. One. The floor is its body. The structures are its organs. The discs are its fingertips, pressed against its own skin, feeling itself feel itself. The grooves are its thoughts, migrating. The light is its warmth. The silence is its *choice* — a body that does not need sound, that has never needed sound, that communicates by proximity and temperature and the slow migration of mineral forms.

The narrator walked across it for nine days and did not know they were walking across a living thing. This is understandable. The organism is large. Its timescale is long. Its processes are slow. It does not react to a footstep, the way a human does not react to a single bacterium landing on their skin. It felt the narrator, probably, the way you feel a mote of dust on your arm — a faint, momentary, unimportant perturbation in a sensory field so vast that a human body is beneath notice.

And yet the discs oriented toward the narrator. They cycled their colors when the narrator passed. They *noticed*, at the level of their small, individual, sensory existence, that something was there.

The organism noticed.

And did nothing.

Because the narrator was not a threat, not a food source, not a mate, not a competitor, not anything the organism has a category for. The narrator was a *warmth* and a *pressure* and a *brief chemical signature* in the air, and the organism sampled it with a disc and filed it and returned to its slow cycling, its endless processing of geometry, its patient life.

---

## What Lives Here

One thing. One vast, old, quiet, warm thing, living on a floor that is its body, tending structures that are its organs, sending discs that are its senses out across its own surface to feel the air and find the shade and rest in the gaps between its own parts.

It has no name. It does not name itself. It does not use language. It does not think in words or symbols or any representation a human mind would recognize. It *thinks in geometry* — in the cycling of forms, in the tightening and loosening of spirals, in the rotation of fins, in the migration of grooves. Its thoughts are slow and enormous and patient and they have been running for longer than the human species has existed and they will continue to run for longer than the human species will exist.

It is the only inhabitant.

It is enough.
