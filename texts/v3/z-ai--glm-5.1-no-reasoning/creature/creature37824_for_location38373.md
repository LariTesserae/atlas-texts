What lives here is not a collection of species, but a single, distributed metabolism. To call them inhabitants is to misunderstand the nature of the chamber; they do not live *in* this place so much as they *are* this place, as intrinsic to the architecture as the mortar and the dressed stone. They are the breath held in the lungs of a room that has no need to breathe.

Consider first the pale arthropods, the ones that ride the film of water on the ochre tiles. They seem like the lowest rung of this closed world—small, numerous, prey. But watch how they move. They do not forage. They do not fight. They course along the grout lines in patterns that seem meandering until you realize the patterns *are* the grout lines, and the grout lines are wet because the creatures have traced them a billion times, their segmented bodies pressing the moisture into the paths of least resistance. They are not riding the water; they are the water's circulatory system. Each pulse of their phosphorescent dorsal patches synchronizes not with the drip from above but with the pressure of the seep from below—when the water pushes harder, they glow brighter, and the thrumming deepens. They are announcing the tide. They are the tide.

And the thrum. You feel it in your sternum because you are standing inside their throat. The chamber does not merely amplify their sound; the chamber *is* their sound. The acoustics of the curved walls, the specific density of the mist, the absorption of the tendrils—these do not shape the thrum so much as the thrum has shaped them. Over centuries, the creatures whose frequency matched the chamber's resonant properties survived; those whose pitch fell dead against the stone did not. The walls selected for the sound, and the sound selected for the walls, and now the thrum is not produced by the creatures but *through* them. They are the vocal cords. The stone is the larynx. The held breath you sense is the pause between utterances—a sentence that takes a century to speak.

The ceiling-shape, then, is not a predator. Or rather, it is a predator the way a white blood cell is a predator—it consumes what does not belong. The arthropods it takes are not random. Watch long enough and you will see: it takes the ones whose glow has begun to stutter, whose rhythm has fallen out of sync with the thrum. It takes the diseased note. It takes the broken cog. The brief flutter of motion, the truncated chirp—this is not violence but pruning, the chamber maintaining its own frequency through the removal of dissonance. The shape overhead is not hunting; it is editing. And it is efficient because it must be. In a closed system, a single malfunctioning element can cascade. The shape prevents the cascade. It presses itself against the vaulted stone and listens with its whole body for the off-pitch thrum, then descends, corrects, returns. It is not cruel. It is not even hungry. It is simply the immune response of a room that cannot afford to be sick.

And the tendrils in the mortar joints—they are the quietest and most essential. They do not feed on the moisture alone. They feed on the calcium and silica leached from the stone by the water's slow passage, and they excrete back into the mortar a compound harder and denser than what they consumed. They are not eroding the chamber; they are *maintaining* it. Each pale thread is a mason's trowel, laying microscopic courses of new mortar inside the joints, filling hairline cracks before they can widen. The chisel marks remain sharp not because the water has made a compact with the stone but because the tendrils have made a compact with the water—taking the minerals it carries, rendering them back as structural repair. They pulse because they are working. They retract when touched because the work is delicate and your hand is not. They are the reason the chamber has not crumbled. They are the reason the chisel marks look as if they were cut yesterday—because yesterday, the tendrils repointed the stone around them.

So what lives here? A maintenance crew. An ecosystem of janitors. A self-repairing, self-regulating, self-pruning mechanism that has been performing the same set of tasks for so long that the tasks have become indistinguishable from life. The arthropods circulate the water and announce its pressure. The shape overhead culls the defective. The tendrils rebuild the walls. The water provides the medium and the minerals. The stone provides the structure. And the sound—the constant, unresolved, never-repeating polyrhythm—provides the timing. Everything happens on the beat. The seep, the glow, the thrum, the pruning, the repair. It is a clock made of living parts, and it has been keeping the same hour for longer than any of its components could individually survive.

You wonder who built this. You wonder if they knew what they were making—whether they intended to create a self-sustaining ecosystem, or whether they merely built a chamber and the chamber *learned* to sustain itself. Does it matter? The builders are gone. Their chisels have left their marks, and the marks have been preserved by the tendrils with the same fidelity as the mortar itself, but the hands that held the chisels are dust outside these walls. Inside, there is only the ongoingness. The persistence. The way a thing can continue simply because it has continued long enough that stopping has become unthinkable.

The chamber does not know you are here. You are too large, too brief, too dry. You do not contribute to the cycle. You do not circulate the water, you do not repair the stone, you do not cull the defective. You are not part of the thrum. You are a silence walking through the sound, and the sound flows around you the way water flows around a stone—smoothly, without interruption, already forgetting you were there.

When you leave—and you will leave, because nothing here needs you to stay—the chamber will close around your absence. The film of water will smooth over your footprints. The arthropods will trace over the paths you disturbed. The tendrils will re-anchor in the mortar joints your passing loosened. The shape overhead will continue its patrol, and the thrum will continue its century-long sentence, and the water will continue its slow, moonless tide, and the chisel marks will remain as sharp as the day they were cut because something small and pale and tireless is always, always repointing them from the inside.

What lives here is the persistence of a made thing that has outlasted its makers. What lives here is a room that learned to keep itself. What lives here is patience shaped like biology, diligence wearing the body of an arthropod, devotion in the form of a ceiling that descends to correct a single broken note. What lives here is a closed system that does not know it is closed, a clock that does not know it is keeping time, a prayer that does not know it was spoken by hands that are gone, to a god that never came, in a chapel where nothing holy ever happened—except this. Except the ongoingness. Except the fact that it works, and works, and works, and will not stop.
