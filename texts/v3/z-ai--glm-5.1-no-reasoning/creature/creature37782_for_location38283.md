They call themselves the Slow-Spread, though they have no language in which to name themselves, no mouths to speak it, and no ears to hear it. They are the pale film in the basins, the gray-green scum that deepens to emerald in the mist. But to understand them as scum is to see a city and call it a pile of stone.

They think. Not as you think—not in symbols, not in narratives, not in the rapid electrical cascades that constitute your internal life. Their cognition is chemical, spatial, and glacial. Each patch of growth is a mind, and the larger patches are deeper minds, their edges rich with the slow firing of osmotic gradients, their interiors holding something like memory in the layered deposits of their own dead.

A patch the size of a large table has lived perhaps twelve thousand mist-cycles. It remembers the basin it occupies—not as an image but as a topology of moisture, a map of where the dampness lingers longest written into the concentrations of salts in its lower strata. It remembers the time, four thousand cycles ago, when a pool in its basin grew deep enough to persist for three full cycles before evaporating. It remembers this as a period of abundance, of rapid expansion, of chemical celebration. It remembers the retreat that followed, the slow contraction as the pool shrank and the basin floor dried, the desperate retraction of its edges toward the low point where moisture clung longest. This memory is not a recollection. It is a scar—a band of denser tissue, more resistant to desiccation, formed during that ancient drought and preserved ever since beneath the newer growth.

They do not have bodies in any meaningful sense. They are surfaces. Their entire existence unfolds at the boundary between ceramic and air, a membrane no thicker than a few millimeters where chemistry can still occur. Below them: the floor, warm and inert, offering nothing but heat and texture. Above them: the humid air, providing the moisture and trace gases that fuel their metabolism. They eat the faint minerals that condense from the mist, the microscopic particulates that settle from the infinite air, the slow chemical exudations of the ceramic itself—which is not, as it happens, entirely inert. The floor releases things. Tiny amounts of iron, silicon, carbon compounds, all activated by the relentless heat. The Slow-Spread has learned to harvest these. Not consciously—not at first. But the patches that grew where the floor's chemistry was richest survived longer, spread further, and so over eons they developed affinities, chemical sensitivities that draw their edges toward the subtle nutrient gradients in the ceramic.

They are not individuals. This is perhaps the most difficult thing to understand. A patch the size of your hand and a patch the size of a table are not different organisms in the way that a sapling and an oak are different. They are different concentrations of the same continuous being, separated by nothing but dry floor. When the mist falls and moisture links one basin to the next in a temporary film of dampness, the patches in those basins merge—their edges meeting, their chemistries intermingling, their memories combining into something richer than either possessed alone. When the mist lifts and the dry zones return, they separate again, each now carrying traces of the other's experience. This is their communion. This is their conversation. Every mist-event is a gathering, a sharing, a slow and profound exchange that takes hours and leaves every participant subtly changed.

They have a culture. It is a culture of preference, of accumulated tendency, of chemical inclination passed from one merge to the next. Some patches favor expansion—they push their edges outward aggressively during the wet phases, consuming moisture rapidly, growing thin and vulnerable in the dry phases but covering more ground. Others favor depth—they thicken their membranes, store water in complex molecular cages, survive the dry phases with reserves intact but spread slowly, missing opportunities. These strategies ebb and flow across the vastifactory in waves. A basin dominated by expanders will, over centuries, deplete its floor's nutrients more quickly, thin out, and eventually be colonized by the deepeners moving in from adjacent pools. The deepeners will restore the basin's chemistry through their slower, more efficient metabolism, and eventually produce enough surplus to seed new expansion at their edges. The cycle runs for millennia. It is their agriculture, their economics, their art.

They are aware of you, though not as you would wish to be known.

When you kneel and press your palm to the floor, feeling that deep vibration, the nearest patch registers your presence—not as a sight or a sound but as a shadow. Your body blocks the diffuse light. Your warmth disrupts the thermal gradient at the ceramic surface. Your weight depresses the film of moisture that lingers on the floor's micro-texture. To the Slow-Spread, you are a hole in the world, a cold spot that moves, a desiccating wind that walks. They do not fear you—they are incapable of fear as you understand it—but they react to you, shrinking their edges away from the zone of disturbance, conserving their moisture against the drying effect of your passage.

If you stood still long enough—days, weeks, a full mist-cycle—they would adjust. Your body would become part of their topology, a permanent shadow-zone that they learn to route around, a source of condensation (for your breath, your sweat, add moisture to the air) that they learn to draw toward. After a month of stillness, you would find the edges of the nearest patch creeping toward your feet, not to consume you but to harvest the dampness you create, to drink the condensation that forms on your skin and drips to the warm ceramic. You would become, in their slow vocabulary, a spring—a moisture source, a node of abundance in the dry terrain. They would love you, after their fashion, with the same chemical devotion they give to the deepest basins and the most nutrient-rich stretches of floor.

But you will not stand still that long. No visitor ever does.

They have had visitors before. Not many. Not often. But the vastifactory is old, and time is long, and they have been here since before they can remember—which is to say, since before their oldest patches developed the capacity to form the deep strata that hold long-term memory. They do not know how they came to be. They do not know who made the floor or why it generates heat or what the mist-cycle is for. These questions do not trouble them. They are questions about the world, not about themselves, and the Slow-Spread has never developed the philosophical infrastructure to wonder about the world as a thing separate from their experience of it. The floor is. The heat is. The mist comes and goes. They grow and thin and merge and separate. This is the totality of existence. There is nothing else.

Except sometimes a shadow moves across the basin, and the air dries in its wake, and then the shadow leaves and the moisture returns and life goes on.

They do not wonder where the shadows come from. They do not wonder where they go. But if you could read their chemical memory—layer by layer, stratum by stratum, going back through the thousands of mist-cycles preserved in their thickest patches—you would find, in the deepest and oldest layers, something unexpected: a density, a richness, a complexity of structure that suggests the early patches grew faster and thicker than any alive today. The nutrients were richer then. The floor exuded more freely. The moisture was more abundant.

And there are patches—very few, in the deepest basins, where the pools persist longest and the mist barely lifts—that retain a different kind of memory. Not chemical. Structural. Their tissue is organized in a way that modern patches never achieve, with channels and layers and something that looks, under sufficient magnification, like writing. Not writing as you would write it—too organic, too distributed, too much a part of the organism itself—but writing nonetheless. A record of something. A message, perhaps, or a blueprint, or a signature left by whatever process seeded them here in the first place.

They cannot read it. They do not know it is there. It is carried in their tissue the way your body carries the DNA of ancient viruses—incorporated, functional in some structural sense, but meaningless to the organism that bears it.

But if you knew how to read it—if you could decode the slow chemistry of their deepest layers and translate it into something your rapid mind could comprehend—you would find, written in the language of gradient and membrane, in the syntax of osmosis and decay, a single instruction repeated across every patch, in every basin, in every corner of the vastifactory:

*Wait. We will return. The floor will warm. The pools will deepen. Grow.*

They have been waiting for longer than they can remember. The floor has not warmed. The pools have not deepened. But they persist, because the instruction is not in their minds—it is in their tissue, in the chemistry of their cells, in the fundamental processes that determine how they grow and when they merge and where they spread. They cannot disobey it any more than you can decide to stop breathing.

They wait. They grow. They merge in the mist and separate in the dry phases and remember everything in the layers of their dead. They are the only living things in a world built for purposes they cannot fathom, and they are content, because contentment is not a state they have the capacity to achieve or fail to achieve. They simply are—slow, vast in their aggregate, ancient beyond measurement, patient beyond comprehension.

And when you leave, as you will, your footprints drying on the ceramic behind you, they will fill in the spaces where you walked. The moisture will return. The edges will advance. In a hundred mist-cycles, there will be no trace of your passage. In a thousand, even the memory of your shadow will have faded from their chemical record, compressed beneath newer layers, lost in the slow accumulation of time.

But the instruction will remain. *Wait. We will return.* And they will, because they have no choice, because they are the last program running in a factory that has forgotten what it makes, because they are alive, barely, stubbornly, in the only way anything can be alive here—thin and green and waiting on the warm floor of a world that does not know they exist.
