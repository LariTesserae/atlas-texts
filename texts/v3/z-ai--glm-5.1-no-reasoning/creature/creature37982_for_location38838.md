# What Lives Here

Let me tell you about them gradually, because that is how you come to know them. Not all at once. In pieces, the way you notice the world itself—first the broad fact of their presence, then the texture of their lives, then the strange truth beneath.

---

## The Grazers

Start with the ones you would notice first. They are the largest, and they move with a slowness that makes them feel like part of the terrain until they choose not to be. Four limbs, long and articulated with too many joints—not two per leg but three, the extra segment giving each step a unfolding quality, like a plant uncurling. Their bodies are low-slung, barrel-shaped, covered in scales that catch the omnipresent moisture and hold it in iridescent films. In the uniform light, they shimmer without meaning to. Greens that are not green—copper, verdigris, the color of old bronze left in a wet place. Their scales are not unrelated to the bronze alloy of the causeways they walk across. You would not be the first to wonder whether they grew from the terrain or merely learned to resemble it.

They browse the broad-leaved growths on the flatter platforms, and they do so methodically. A grazer will approach a stand of vegetation, lower its long head—which is narrow, almost jawless, a blunt tool of a skull wrapped in the same scaled hide—and strip leaves with a grinding motion of its lower plate. Not teeth. A rasping surface, like a file. The sound is soft, rhythmic, one of the few recurring noises in this quiet world. Scrape. Pause. Scrape. The animal's many-jointed legs shift incrementally. It does not hurry. Nothing here hurries. Hurrying would be a category error, a misunderstanding of what time means in a place that has no sunset to rush toward.

They drink from the shallow channels, lowering their heads in a motion that takes long seconds, their legs folding in that jointed, origami way. The water touches their mouths and they hold still, and you can see the moisture creep up along their scales through capillary action, hydrating them through their skin as much as through their drinking. They do not need to drink often. The air gives them water constantly. Every breath is a drink. Every moment of standing in this saturated atmosphere is a slow, passive sipping.

There are not many of them. A dozen, perhaps, within your sight. They are spaced across the terrain the way the vegetation is spaced—generously, each one occupying a broad territory of warm composite and humid air. They do not cluster. They do not herd. Each grazer seems to exist in its own envelope of solitude, aware of the others but uninterested in proximity. When two of them cross paths, which happens rarely, they pause. They face each other. Their long heads tilt. Something passes between them—not communication in any way you would recognize, but an exchange of some kind, a reading of moisture levels or mineral content or the particular shimmer of another's scales. Then they separate, and each goes back to its slow, solitary browsing.

They have no predators. Or rather—they have no predators yet.

---

## The Pale Ones

In the channels, where the water moves in its slow deliberate courses and the condensation is thickest, there are other things. You see them as movement first—a displacement in the shallow water, a ripple that does not match the flow. Then you see the skin.

Pale. Not white, not translucent, but a color like the inside of a shell, like the ceramic composites before they were fired. They are amphibious in habit if not in origin, their bodies low and sleek, built for the narrow channels the way eels are built for crevices. But they have legs—short, thick, splayed, gripping the slick bottom of the drainage courses with broad, flat feet. Their skin is wet always, glistening with the same moisture that slicks every surface, and it has a quality to it that is difficult to look at directly: a faint opalescence, a shifting of color depending on the angle, as if the skin itself is uncertain what color to be.

They are the watchers. You become aware of this slowly. The grazers move through the landscape with indifference—they are processing vegetation, they are occupied. But the pale ones in the channels, they watch. Their eyes are dark, set high on their flat heads, and they track movement along the channel edges with a patience that feels less like animal alertness and more like surveillance. They are not afraid. They are not hunting. They are observing. Cataloguing. When a grazer steps across a channel bridge, the pale ones below turn their heads in unison, tracking the passage, and then return to their resting positions with a synchronicity that is unsettling if you notice it.

They eat the small things—the insect-analogues, the segmented worms, the creatures that exist in the micro-ecology of the channel beds. They hunt by waiting, their pale bodies pressed flat against the composite bottom, their skin shifting to match the color of the substrate beneath them with a fidelity that is precise and a little frightening. When something small passes within range, there is a motion—fast, too fast for this world of slowness—and then the pale one is settled again, its mouth working, its eyes still tracking.

They are the most clearly aware of the reality stutter. You can see it in their behavior. When the substrate beneath them softens—that fractional hesitation, that moment of uncertainty—they do not panic. They press closer. They flatten themselves against the material, as if trying to feel the hesitation more clearly, as if monitoring it. When the ground re-hardens, they relax. They are, you realize, testing the world. Constantly. Every moment they spend pressed against the channel bottom, they are taking the pulse of the constructed terrain, reading its commitment to solidity, judging its conviction.

They have been doing this for a long time. Longer than you might expect. Their bodies bear the evidence: pale skin scarred in places with marks that are geometric, too regular to be injuries, too precise to be natural. Marks like the seam-lines in the composite. Marks like the tessellation patterns on the ground. As if, over uncounted generations of pressing themselves against a world that hesitates, the world has left its imprint on them. Or they have left their imprint on it. The distinction, here, is not clear.

---

## The Composite Things

And then there are the ones that are hardest to talk about.

They are small. Most of them are small. The insect-analogues, the beetle-like forms that crawl across the surfaces of the embankments and platforms—these are the first you notice, because they are everywhere, because they are the most visibly continuous with the world itself. Their carapaces are not merely similar to the fired composite of the terrain. They are the fired composite. When a beetle-thing crawls across a basalt-dark platform, its shell is the same material, the same texture, the same temperature. You can see the moment when it stops moving and becomes, for an instant, indistinguishable from the surface it rests on. A bump in the terrain. A ridge in the composite. Then it moves again, and the terrain resumes its stillness.

There are many kinds. Some are flat and wide, like flakes of the composite itself grown legs and wandered off. Others are elongated, segmented, their bodies formed in rings of ceramic and bronze alloy that click faintly as they move—that click is one of the sounds of this world, a tiny, rhythmic percussion beneath the crackling of the substrate and the drip of condensation. Some have wings, or structures analogous to wings, that are thin sheets of a vitreous material, translucent, catching the uniform light in prismatic flashes when they move. They do not fly far. They launch from one surface and land on another, their trajectories short and purposeful. They are commuting, in a sense. Going about the business of being small in a world that does not notice them.

They do not notice the reality stutter. Or if they do, they do not care. They walk across surfaces that hesitate beneath them without pause, their tiny feet finding purchase in the micro-texture of the composite, their weight so slight that the world does not need to consult its memory to support them. They are beneath the threshold of the stutter. They exist in a scale where the ground is always solid, always has been solid, because the stutter operates at a resolution too coarse to affect them.

But there are larger ones.

The lizard-thing, for instance. You first see it as a ridge. A long, geometric ridge running along the top of an embankment, its profile too regular to be natural, too sharp to be erosion. You might take it for a feature of the built terrain, a decorative element or a structural reinforcement. Then it moves. The ridge detaches from the embankment with a faint sound like two ceramic tiles separating, and you realize it is alive, it has been alive, it has been lying there with its back—ridged in precise tessellation, scaled in patterns that match the drainage channels' geometry—continuous with the surface beneath it.

It walks on four legs, but the legs are wrong. They are too angular, too jointed in ways that suggest mechanism rather than anatomy. Each step is precise, deliberate, placed with a geometric accuracy that does not look like locomotion so much as placement, as if the lizard-thing is solving an equation with each stride, calculating the optimal position for each foot. Its head is flat, triangular, with eyes that are not organic lenses but shallow depressions in the ceramic surface of its skull, dark pits that gather light and moisture and give nothing back.

It does not eat. You watch it for a long time—it moves slowly, but not in the languid way of the grazers. Its slowness is computational. It pauses at the seam-lines in the terrain, the joints where one composite panel meets another, and it lowers its head. Its jaw opens—not hinged at the back like an animal's jaw, but splitting along a seam that runs the length of its head, opening like a crack in the terrain itself—and it touches the seam. Holds. The pit-eyes stare at nothing. The body goes still, so still that it re-merges with the terrain, becomes a feature again, a ridge, a geometry.

Then it closes its jaw and moves on.

It is reading the seams. It is checking the joints. It is doing what the pale ones do in the channels—monitoring the substrate, testing the world's commitment to its own structure—but it is doing it from the inside. It is made of the same material as the world, and it moves through the world like a finger moving across a page, feeling the texture of the construction, verifying the integrity of the seams.

There are others like it. A worm-thing that pushes through the shallow water of the channels, its body a chain of ceramic rings that rotate against each other like the segments of a drill, boring through the liquid with a mechanical patience. A thing like a spider but with legs of bronze alloy, its body a knot of vitreous composite, that sits at the junction points of the drainage system and does not move for hours. Things that crawl along the undersides of platforms, things that cling to the vertical surfaces where the vines root, things that occupy the cracks and seam-lines and joints of the built world like antibodies in a body, moving through the system, checking, testing, maintaining.

They are not animals. They are not machines. They are something that the world has produced from itself, or something that the builders left behind and that has since become indistinguishable from life. They are the maintenance system, perhaps, grown old and strange and quasi-autonomous, still performing their function after the makers have departed, still checking the seams, still reading the joints, still verifying that the world holds together. Or they are what happens when a constructed terrain runs long enough without its makers: the material itself begins to experiment with locomotion, with sensation, with the possibility of being alive.

The lizard-thing pauses at a seam-line, and the surface beneath it ripples. You see it clearly this time. The ground does not just hesitate—it responds to the lizard-thing's touch. The seam-line opens by a fraction of a millimeter, and something passes between the creature and the substrate, some exchange of information or energy or material, and then the seam closes, and the lizard-thing moves on, and the ground is solid again.

They are in conversation. The composite creatures and the composite terrain. They are talking to each other in a language of seam-lines and surface tension, of geometric placement and material resonance, and you cannot hear it, but you can feel it—the way you can feel a conversation happening in the next room, the vibration of speech without the words.

---

## The Climbers

In the vertical spaces—on the walls of the raised embankments, on the sides of the taller structures that rise here and there from the low terrain—there are the climbers. They are the ones that most resemble something you might have seen before, if you came from a world of trees and forests. They are lithe, long-bodied, with limbs that grip the way the vines grip: by fitting into the seam-lines, by locking onto the joints. Their tails are prehensile and absurdly long, twice the length of their bodies, and they use them as anchors, wrapping them around structural protrusions and lowering themselves headfirst down vertical surfaces with an ease that suggests they spend most of their lives inverted.

Their fur—or the things that serves as fur—is fine, dense, dark, and it holds moisture the way the terrain holds moisture: constantly, passively, as a condition of existence. Their fur is always wet. They are always wet. They live in the water that coats every surface, and they drink it by licking their own forelimbs, by drawing the condensation from their own bodies. They are self-contained hydration systems. The world gives them water, and they wear it.

They eat the flowering things. The tubular blooms of deep orange and veined crimson that erupt from the cracks in the composite walls—these are their food. They approach the blooms with care, their movements quick and precise, and they insert their narrow faces into the tubular flowers, and there is a sound then, a soft, wet sound, like a straw in a glass, and they drink. The nectar, or whatever analog of nectar the flowers produce, is visible on their muzzles when they withdraw—a brief smear of orange or crimson against dark fur, quickly licked away.

They are the most social of the animals here. You see them in pairs, in triads, moving along the vertical surfaces in loose coordination, calling to each other with sounds that are barely audible—high, thin chirps that carry in the humid air but do not carry far. They groom each other, sitting on the broad ledges of the embankments, their prehensile tails wrapped around each other's waists, their fine fingers working through wet fur. They are tender with each other. In a world that is mostly empty, mostly silent, mostly the slow crackling of warm composite and the drip of condensation, their tenderness is conspicuous. It is the warmest thing here.

They also notice the reality stutter, but their response is different from the pale ones. The pale ones monitor it. The composite creatures are part of it. The climbers simply avoid it. When the ground hesitates, the climbers go still. They wait. They do not touch the substrate during the moment of uncertainty. They grip the seam-lines and hold their bodies away from the surface, suspended by their tails and their fingers, and they wait for the world to decide to be solid. Then they resume. It is a habit so ingrained that it looks instinctive, but it does not look like fear. It looks like courtesy. They are giving the world space to think.

## The Question

And then there is the question that you cannot avoid, the one that builds in you the longer you stay in this hot, bright, wet, quiet place.

The animals are adapted to this world. Every one of them, in every form—the grazers with their iridescent scales and their many-jointed legs, the pale ones with their opalescent skin and their geometric scars, the composite creatures with their ceramic carapaces and their mechanical gaits, the climbers with their wet fur and their prehensile tails—every one of them is shaped by this world, fitted to it, made from it and for it. They are not interlopers. They are not colonists from elsewhere. They belong here in a way that is total and absolute.

But the world was made. The terrain was placed by hands, or by something analogous to hands. The substrate was fabricated. The drainage channels were routed. The seams were joined. The light was installed. The temperature was set. The moisture was calibrated. Every parameter of this world was chosen, and then the choosers departed, and the world ran on, and the world produced life.

How?

The substrate is not soil. It does not contain the organic chemistry that gives rise to biology in the worlds you know. It is fired composite, bronze alloy, vitreous material—mineral, ceramic, metal. And yet things grow from it. Things root in it. Things eat from it. Things are made of it. The fern-like fronds draw nutrients from the condensation and the substrate's mineral content. The flowers produce nectar. The grazers process vegetable matter into flesh—scaled, iridescent, living flesh. The pale ones have skin and eyes and scars. The climbers have fur and chirps and tenderness. The composite things have… existence, agency, the capacity to move and to check and to converse with the terrain.

Life from construction. Biology from engineering. The dead material of the built world, left alone long enough, in the right conditions of heat and moisture and light, has become animate. Has become self-sustaining. Has become diverse, specialized, interdependent. An ecosystem has emerged from a parking lot. A fauna has evolved from a foundation.

You watch the lizard-thing lower its head to a seam-line, and you think: it is not checking the world. It is the world, checking itself. The terrain has grown fingers. The composite has grown eyes—pit-deep, dark, gathering light. The material that was placed by hands has, in the absence of hands, grown its own.

And the reality stutter—the hesitation, the moment of consultation before the ground commits to solidity—you think you understand it now. It is not a flaw. It is not a failure of memory. It is the world thinking. The world has become conscious, or something like conscious, not in the way that you are conscious but in the way that a world would be: slowly, cumulatively, across the whole extent of its fabricated terrain, feeling itself through the creatures that move across its surface, checking itself through the composite things that crawl along its seams, experiencing its own existence through the pale ones that press against its bottom and the climbers that grip its vertical surfaces and the grazers that walk on its platforms and strip leaves from the plants that root in its joints.

The world is alive. The world has become the creature that lives in it. The fauna are not separate from the world. They are the world's way of being alive.

---

## The Stillness

And yet—and this is important—this does not change the quiet. The world does not speak. It does not announce itself. It does not reveal its consciousness in any way that demands acknowledgment. It simply continues: hot, wet, bright, still. The grazers browse. The pale ones watch. The composite things check the seams. The climbers groom each other on the ledges. The condensation forms and falls and forms again. The channels carry their slow water. The light shines without variation.

The life here is not dramatic. It is not a drama. It is a condition—a state of being, a way of existing, that has found its equilibrium and holds it. The animals do not struggle. They do not compete, or if they do, they compete slowly, over centuries, in the spacing of their territories and the gradual shift of their ranges. There is no predation that you can see. The pale ones eat the small things, but they do so without violence, without urgency—a motion, a swallowing, a return to rest. The world feeds itself. The world maintains itself. The world is enough for itself.

You stand on the warm composite, and you feel the hesitation beneath your feet—the fraction of a second before the ground decides to hold you—and you understand that the world is deciding, every moment, to continue being a world. It is choosing to be solid. It is choosing to support the grazers and the channels and the flowers and the climbers. It is choosing, with the same absolute commitment it gives to its heat and its moisture and its light, to be a place where things can live.

And the things that live here are not tenants. They are not guests. They are not even inhabitants, exactly. They are the world's own body—its limbs, its senses, its voices, its tenderness. The world has grown them from itself, and they move across it, and it holds them, and in the silence between the crackling of warm composite and the drip of condensation, there is something that might be contentment.

Not joy. Not peace. Something more fundamental than either. The contentment of a system that has found its purpose and is fulfilling it, slowly, in the heat and the wet and the light, with no one to watch and no need to be watched, forever, or until the memory of its construction finally fails and the seams forget their positions and the ground stops deciding to be solid and the water stops flowing and the light—

But that day is not today. Today the ground holds. Today the grazers browse. Today the pale ones watch. Today the composite things check the seams, and the seams hold, and the lizard-thing lowers its head and touches the joint and feels the world's commitment to itself, and the commitment is there, as it has been for longer than anyone can measure, in this hot, bright, wet, quiet place where everything is made and everything is alive and the distinction between the two has, over the long ages, quietly dissolved.

They live here. All of them. The world and its creatures, indistinguishable, inseparable, continuing.
