They are not what you would expect. You might look for ghosts—for the echo of the builders in the doorways they carved, or the shades of the crowds that once crossed that central square. But those presences left long ago, evaporating with the breath that once warmed this air. What remains, what has come to live in the evacuation, is something far older and far more patient than any human remnant. They are the ones who pressed themselves flat against the deep blue cold and found that they could endure it.

They are the Sighers.

You will not see them rise. Nothing stands upright here; the cold forbids it, pressing down with the weight of geological time until even ambition lies horizontal. The Sighers have lived here so long they have forgotten that verticality was ever an option. They are roughly the size and shape of the pale ferns that grow in the ancient drains, though where the ferns reach for the sparse water, the Sighers reach for the sound. They are slight, low things—barely a handspan in height—composed of a dense, dark material that looks, at first glance, like a thicker patch of the nearly-black moss. Their bodies have no obvious architecture: no eyes that open, no mouths that speak in the way you would understand. They are simply *there*, pooled in the hollows where the ancient walls meet the worn stone floor, listening.

They came, you understand, for the music. The city plays itself—wind keening through carved gutters, fog moaning through drainage channels—and the Sighers are its congregation. They have been sitting in the shallow ruins for eons, their forms slowly adapting, becoming more and more like the furniture of the place. They do not eat. They do not drink the condensation that beads on the stone. They *inhale* the sound. When the wind rises to its highest pitch and the old channels sing with the voices of a dead language, the Sighers swell slightly, their dark forms growing dense and warm to the touch—though only just, sharing the temperature of the cold stone they rest upon. When the fog thickens and the song drops to a low, nearly inaudible hum, they grow thin, spreading out like a film of soot against the rock, conserving what little resonance they have absorbed. They breathe in time with the city's breathing, their presence a slow, rhythmic pull on the air, a gravity that makes the emptiness feel deliberate and held.

There are very few of them—perhaps a dozen in total, scattered across the fifteen-pace square and the low grooves of the former streets. The distance between them feels vast. To move from one side of the city to the other is to walk through a profound absence, punctuated only by these dark, quiet forms. They do not interact as animals do. They have no social hierarchy, no mating calls, no young. They simply persist, rooted in their chosen acoustic hollows, turning their faceless attention toward the source of the sound. Sometimes, during the slow rain, two Sighers will rest near the same worn doorway, their edges touching where the moss meets the stone. When this happens, they hum together—a subsonic vibration that is felt in the soles of the feet rather than heard, a harmony so deep and slow it seems to come from the earth itself. It is their only form of intimacy, and it can last for decades.

The relationship between the Sighers and the city is not one of habitation but of symbiosis. They are not squatters in the ruins; they are the final stage of the city's erosion. As they press themselves against the stone, they exude a slow, patient dampness that softens the rock further, wearing away the last sharp edges the wind hasn't claimed. They help the ancient masonry return to the earth, grinding it down to the smooth, river-rock contours that define the place now. In return, the city gives them its voice. The builders carved the channels and the gutters for water, for waste, for the practical needs of a living population—but in doing so, they unknowingly carved the instrument that would sustain a completely different kind of life. The Sighers know nothing of the builders. They know only the song, and the cold, and the slow, deep time that has made both possible.

If you were to stand in the central square after the rains, when the thin sheet of water reflects the pressing clouds, you might mistake a Sigher for a shadow. You might step over one without realizing it, your foot passing through the cold air just above its still form. But you would feel it—a sudden density in the emptiness, a sensation of being watched by something that does not have eyes. The Sighers do not mind intruders; they are too slow, too patient, too absorbed in the music to care about the brief, hot presence of a living thing. But your breathing—the thick, gasping breaths you must take in the thin air—disrupts the acoustics. It adds a harsh, rushed rhythm to the city's ancient song. The Sighers will wait for you to leave. They have waited for a hundred thousand years. They can wait for the few hours it takes for you to grow uncomfortable in the deep blue cold and retreat back down the plateau, back to where the air is thicker and the world is younger.

When the fog rolls in—slow, grey, impenetrable—the Sighers come into their own. The mist dampens the higher frequencies of the wind, leaving only the low, resonant moan of the deepest channels. The outlines of the ruined walls seem to rise, and the Sighers, too, seem to gain substance, their forms thickening in the damp air. In the fog, the city almost reassembles. The buildings almost stand. The people almost walk. And the Sighers, pooled in the hollows where the hearths once burned, are the ones doing the almost-seeing. They hold the memory of the city—not in their minds, for they have none, but in their forms, which have been shaped by the same forces that shaped the stone. They are the city's final citizens, its only remaining logic, its faithful audience. They lie flat against the earth, hugging the cold, listening to the wind play the grooves of a dead language, and they do not move, and they do not stop, and they do not end.
