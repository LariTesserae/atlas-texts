They are the Lade-born, and they move like water finding its level.

Not slowly, exactly — that implies a lethargy they do not possess. They move *deliberately*, with the economy of people who have learned that quickness draws the eye, and the eye of the Lade is something you do not want upon you. When they walk the catwalks between terraces, their weight settles into the iron grating with a practiced distribution, heel-toe, heel-toe, a gait that does not reverberate through the structure. Visitors stomp. The Lade-born glide. Visitors wonder how they can see where they're going in the dense undergrowth; the Lade-born wonder how visitors can fail to see the paths that are right there, obvious as breathing, written in the bent fronds and the worn edge of the railing and the particular way the moss grows thin where feet have passed for generations.

There are perhaps two hundred of them, though no one has counted, because counting would require a census and a census would require someone to care about the total, and no one does. They are spread across five of the middle terraces — the ninth through the thirteenth, which are the ones where the heat and the damp achieve the balance that suits human bodies best. Not the warmest. Not the coolest. The golden mean, the comfortable middle, which is where practical people always settle eventually.

They do not think of themselves as a community, which is one of the first things a visitor must understand. Community implies a cohesion, a shared purpose, a social contract. The Lade-born have none of these. They are people who live near each other, the way the birds and the goats and the cats live near each other — in proximity, in tolerance, in occasional competition, without any illusion that they are the same thing. They have no leaders. They have no council. They have no laws except the rules of the Lade itself, which they follow not out of reverence but out of the simple pragmatism of people who have watched what happens to those who do not.

What happens to those who do not is this: they go below the seventh terrace, or they move too fast, or they fail to notice the way the condensation pattern has shifted, and then they are gone. Not dramatically. Not violently. Just — gone. The Lade absorbs them. A missed footing on a rust-eaten railing. A wrong turn in the ductwork. A sleep that deepens and does not end, the body settling into the warm damp moss as if it were a bed prepared for it, the roots already threading up through the clothing by the time anyone thinks to look. The Lade-born do not speak of these disappearances. They are not tragic. They are what happens. Water moves down. Heat moves up. People who do not attend to the gradients become part of them.

---

Their bodies tell the story of the place.

They are small, most of them. Not stunted — not malnourished — but compact, efficient, the way island populations become compact over generations, resources limited and bodies adapting to fit. The women average perhaps a hundred and fifty centimeters; the men a few centimeters more. Their limbs are proportioned for climbing and crouching, for moving through spaces that were not designed for human occupation. The ducts between terraces, the maintenance corridors behind the thermal stacks, the gaps where the iron has warped and opened passages into the structure's interior — the Lade-born navigate these the way their ancestors must have navigated open ground, with an ease that is not skill but birthright, the body knowing before the mind does where to place a hand, where to angle a shoulder, where the footing is solid and where it is rust-rotted and treacherous.

Their skin is a particular thing. Not dark, not light — something in between, a warm olive that has nothing to do with ethnicity and everything to do with the light. The Lade is bright, relentlessly bright, but the light is diffuse, filtered through canopy and condensation and the pale reflection off iron, and the Lade-born have adapted to this specific illumination. Their skin is thick, not delicate, resistant to the constant damp, resistant to the fungal spores that drift through the air, resistant to the insect bites that would plague a newcomer. They do not sunburn. They do not chap. Their skin has the texture of old leather — not cracked, not worn, but *finished*, as if they have been cured in the same warm humidity that cures everything else on the Lade.

Their eyes are the feature visitors notice first. Large, and set slightly wider apart than is typical, and pale — not the pale of illness but the pale of adaptation, of generations living in a brightness that is not natural light, not direct light, but a ubiquitous washed-out luminosity that eliminates shadow. The Lade-born do not squint. They do not need to. Their irises have adjusted, the pigments reduced to the minimum, leaving them with eyes the color of the standing pools: amber, grey-green, a watery brown that shifts with the light. In the dim interiors of their dwellings, their pupils open wide as a cat's, and they can see clearly where a visitor would be blind. In the full brightness of the upper terraces, their pupils contract to pinpoints, and still they do not squint.

They have an uncanny stillness. A visitor to the thirteenth terrace might look at what appears to be an empty clearing — moss, ferns, the drip of condensation from a railing-pipe — and then realize, with a start, that a woman has been standing three meters away the entire time, watching. Not hiding. Simply *there*, her body held in a motionlessness that the birds and insects have accepted as part of the landscape, her pale eyes tracking the visitor with the patient attention of something that has never learned to hurry. The Lade-born do this without thinking. They hold still the way the Lade holds still — not inert, not passive, but *present*, attentive, the quiet of a machine idling rather than a machine off.

---

They do not name themselves as a people. They have personal names, of course — identifiers that distinguish one from another, though the system is idiosyncratic and follows no convention a linguist could map. Some have names that sound like words from old languages, fragments of whatever tongue the original tenders spoke, worn smooth by generations of mouths. Others have names that seem to be descriptions: a woman called Warm-Pipe, because she was born near a thermal stack that ran hot; a man called Moss-Blood, because his mother hemorrhaged into the moss during delivery and the color never left his mind, or so he says, and his name is a joke, or a memory, or both.

They do not have family names. They have families — or rather, they have kin, people they are related to by blood or by proximity or by the simple accumulation of shared time. The Lade-born do not formalize these connections. A child knows who their mother is, usually, because the mother has raised them, or because someone has told them, or because they have figured it out from the way the woman watches them across a clearing. Fathers are less certain, and less relevant. The children belong to the terrace where they were born, and they are raised by whoever is present and willing, which is usually everyone, because children are useful and because the Lade-born have no concept of child as burden. A child can fit into spaces an adult cannot. A child can hear sounds an adult has lost. A child is the future of the terrace, and the terrace knows it, and the child is fed and sheltered and taught without anyone deciding that this is what should happen. It simply does.

The children grow up wild in the way that the goats are wild — not feral, not abandoned, but *free*, ranging across the terraces in packs that are part social group and part scouting party, learning the Lade's geography the way other children learn the streets of their villages. They know every railing that will bear weight and every one that has rusted through. They know which thermal stacks are hot enough to dry wet clothing and which are cool enough to touch. They know the insects — which ones bite, which ones sting, which ones can be eaten raw and which need to be roasted over the small charcoal fires the Lade-born sometimes build in the iron gutters, the smoke rising up and mixing with the Lade's own plumes until it is indistinguishable.

The children are the ones who go highest. Not to the crown — no one goes to the crown, not anymore, not since the heat up there increased and the vegetation changed and the hawks grew aggressive in a way that suggests they are protecting something. But the upper terraces, the nineteenth and twentieth, where the air is thinner and the light is blinding and the flowers grow huge and strange: the children range there, small and quick and unnoticed by the Lade, or noticed and forgiven, because the Lade seems to have a regard for small things, for the seedlings and the insects and the children who move through its structure like blood through veins.

---

They do not believe in the Lade. This is important to state plainly, because visitors often mistake their relationship for worship. The Lade-born do not pray to the mountain. They do not make offerings. They do not speak of it with reverence or awe. They speak of it the way you might speak of a house you have lived in for a very long time: with familiarity, with a certain resigned affection, with the knowledge that the house has its quirks and its dangers and its moods, and that you accommodate these things because the alternative is leaving, and leaving is unthinkable.

They *know* the Lade. Not in the sense of understanding its mechanics — no one understands its mechanics, not anymore, and the Lade-born have no interest in recovering that knowledge. They know it the way you know a person's breathing: the rhythm of it, the variations, the way it changes when the weather shifts or the season turns. They know that the eastern thermal stacks on the eleventh terrace run cooler in the morning and warmer in the evening, for reasons no one has explained. They know that the condensation on the ninth terrace tastes of iron more strongly after the engines reach their afternoon peak. They know that the moss on the north side of the thirteenth terrace grows thicker and greener than the moss on the south side, and that the pools there are warmer, and that the insects that breed in those pools are larger and more docile, and that the cats avoid that area for reasons they have never shared.

This knowledge is not written down. It is not systematized. It is not taught in a formal sense. A child learns it by being present, by watching, by listening, by making mistakes and surviving them. The Lade-born do not have schools. They do not have elders who dispense wisdom. They have people who have been alive longer, and who have noticed more, and who will share what they have noticed if asked, and who will sometimes share it without being asked, and who will sometimes choose not to share it, because the learning is in the noticing, and to give someone the answer is to rob them of the observation.

They have stories, but they do not tell them often, and they do not tell them to visitors. The stories are not myths. They are records — of the year the seventh terrace flooded when the condensation channels clogged and the water rose ankle-deep across the whole circumference, and the moss grew six centimeters in a week, and the insects bred so thick the air was a drone. Of the man who went below the seventh terrace's foundations and came back three days later with his hair turned white and his eyes unable to focus and his skin cool to the touch, cool in a way that was wrong, that was not the coolness of death but the coolness of something else, something that had touched him and let him go and left its mark. He lived for years after, that man, but he never spoke again, and he would not go near the edges of the terraces, and he slept in the warmest spots he could find, pressed against the thermal stacks as if he could not get enough heat, as if something had taken his warmth and not given it back.

They have stories of the builders, too, though these are vague and contradictory and no two tellings agree. The builders were tall, or they were short. They came from below, or they came from above. They built the Lade as a temple, or a fortress, or a machine for a purpose no one remembers, or a machine for no purpose at all, because they were a people who built things and this was the thing they built. The Lade-born do not care about the builders. They are irrelevant. Whatever they intended, the Lade has become something else, and the people who live in it are the people who live in it, and the past is the past, and the only thing that matters is the next cycle of the engines, the next shift of the water, the next morning when the light comes bright and warm and the condensation gleams on every surface and the birds begin their calling and the Lade breathes its slow breath and another day begins in the high gardens where the iron meets the root and the root meets the green and the green reaches up toward the sky that is too blue, too clear, too close, and the people who live there wake and stretch and step out into the brightness and begin again.

---

They do not fear death. This is not courage. It is not philosophy. It is simply the absence of the expectation that death is something that happens later, elsewhere, in a context removed from the living of life. On the Lade, death is present the way the damp is present — always there, in the periphery, in the mold on a fallen leaf and the rust on a railing and the coolness of the shadows where the thermal stacks do not reach. People die. They fall. They sicken. They go below the seventh terrace. They grow old and lie down in the moss and do not get up, and by the time they are found the roots have begun their work, and this is not disturbing, because it is what the Lade does, and what the Lade does is what is.

The Lade-born do not bury their dead. They do not have the earth for it — the soil on the terraces is shallow, and beneath it is iron, and beneath the iron is the structure, and the structure has no room for graves. They used to, perhaps. The oldest residents remember stories of a time when the dead were wrapped in canvas and placed in the hollow spaces behind the thermal stacks, where the warmth would speed their decomposition and the condensation would wash the bones clean. But that was a long time ago, and the practice has fallen away, and now the dead are simply left where they are found, or moved to a spot where they will not be disturbed, and the Lade takes them. The roots come up. The moss grows over. The insects do their work. In a season, sometimes less, there is nothing left but a slight rise in the vegetation, a particular lushness that marks where a body has returned to the cycle.

The Lade-born do not visit these spots. They do not memorialize. They do not mourn, at least not in any way a visitor would recognize. When someone dies, there is a quietness — an absence, a space where a person used to be — and then the quietness fills, because the Lade does not tolerate emptiness, and the space is taken by moss or vine or another person who needed a place to sleep, and life continues.

A visitor might find this cold. The Lade-born would not understand why. They love each other, in their way — with a practicality that does not admit sentiment, with a closeness that comes from sharing a niche, with a fierce protectiveness that emerges only when the Lade itself is threatened, which is never, because what could threaten the Lade? They love the way the roots love the iron: by gripping it, by growing into it, by becoming inseparable from it, by drawing sustenance from something that was not designed to sustain them and that sustains them nonetheless.

---

They are not curious about the outside world. This is perhaps the hardest thing for a visitor to accept. The Lade-born know that there is an outside — they can see it, from the upper terraces, the valleys below stretching away into a distance that is blue and indistinct and irrelevant. They know that visitors come, occasionally, from that outside, climbing the paved riverbeds and the retaining walls and the lower terraces, arriving winded and wide-eyed and disturbingly loud. They know that these visitors come from places where the mountains are still mountains and the ground is still ground and the water falls from the sky without passing through a lattice first.

They do not want to go there.

Why would they? The Lade is warm. The Lade is wet. The Lade is bright and green and full of life and the engines hum in the deep places and the water moves down and the heat moves up and the food grows in reach and the shelter is everywhere and the only price is attention, constant attention, the price of living inside a machine that does not care whether you live or die but that will support you as long as you follow its rules.

The Lade-born follow its rules. They were born following its rules. They will die following its rules. And the Lade will continue, warm and green and iron and damp and bright, and the roots will grow deeper into the structure, and the engines will shift their rhythm, and the condensation will find new paths, and the people will adapt, because that is what they do, because that is what the Lade has taught them, because that is what the Lade teaches everything that lives within it:

Adapt. Or be absorbed. There is no third option, and the Lade does not judge which you choose, and the Lade does not notice, and the Lade does not care, and the Lade will go on regardless, the way it has gone on for centuries, the way it will go on for centuries more, a mountain that is not a mountain, a machine that has forgotten its purpose, a garden that no one planted, a home for everything that can survive its warmth and its brightness and its slow, patient, inexorable attention.
