What lives here is not a collection of organisms. It is a single, self-devouring event that has been happening for longer than anything in this place can measure, which is nothing, because nothing here measures.

The thumbnail-blind—the nameless multitudes that constitute the living ground itself—are perhaps the closest thing to a foundational tissue this world possesses. They are arthropods only in the loosest, most dishonest taxonomic sense. Their bodies are simplified to the point of abstraction: a hinged carapace, three pairs of scraping limbs, a mouth that is more a grinding aperture than an organ of discrimination. They have no eyes. They have never had eyes. The lineage that produced them abandoned photoreception so long ago that the genetic memory of light, if such a thing ever existed, has been ground out of them by the millions of generations of mouth-to-mouth recycling that defines their ancestry. They navigate through vibration alone. Each individual is a node in a vast, interconnected seismic network, feeling its neighbors as pressure-waves transmitted through chitin and basalt. When a larger organism moves, they feel it as a tide of disruption, and they pour away from the disturbance in liquid cascades, reassuming their positions in the wake. They do this without thought. They do everything without thought. Thought would be an impediment here—a luxury that slows reaction time and invites consumption.

They feed continuously. Their mouthparts are not selective. A living neighbor and a dead neighbor are processed with identical mechanical indifference. The distinction between cannibalism and scavenging does not exist for them because the distinction between self and other barely exists. The boundary of the individual is permeable at every level. When one of their number is crushed beneath a larger organism's passage, the mass simply closes over the remains, and within seconds the calcium and chitin have been mechanically disassembled and redistributed. Nothing is lost. Nothing is wasted. The efficiency is total and joyless.

Among them—above them, through them, *as* them—move the things that can be felt but not seen. The macro-organisms. These are the larger architectures of the same biological language, scaled up to sizes that seem to violate the logic of an ecosystem without photosynthesis. But there is no violation. The energy that sustains them is geochemical, sulfur and iron and the slow oxidation of hydrogen seeping from fissures in the basalt, captured by the calcified fungal crusts and the chemosynthetic microflora that film every available surface. The macros are simply the apex of a food chain that bottoms out in the mineral world itself.

They are segmented. Everything here is segmented. Segmentation is the dominant structural theme, repeated with the obsessive fidelity of a system that found one workable answer and never needed another. The macros drag themselves on rows of limbs that are themselves segmented, each joint a knuckle of hardened chitin articulating against the next, producing the grinding, rasping ambient noise that constitutes this place's baseline acoustic state. Their bodies are riddled with spiracles—not neatly arranged, as in a surface-dwelling arthropod, but distributed irregularly across their flanks and dorsums, irregular dark punctures that open and close in rhythmic, autonomic pulses, breathing the thick, mineral air. The larger ones filter-feed. Their gills are vast, membranous structures that unfurl from vents in their thoracic segments, translucent sheets that billow in the slow currents generated by the collective motion of the swarm below. They inhale the thumbnail-blind by the thousands, by the tens of thousands, straining them from the air the way a whale strains krill, and the tiny organisms die without awareness in the dark, wet chambers of the filter-gut, dissolved and absorbed.

The bioluminescence belongs to the middle-tier organisms, the ones too large to be caught in the filter-gills but too small to move through the swarm without resistance. Their light is not for seeing. Nothing here sees. It is a chemical emergency flare, a defense mechanism triggered by the specific vibration signature of an approaching predator. The flash is brief—less than a second—and sickly in hue, a cold blue-white that renders the surrounding density in a frozen tableau of overlapping limbs and wet exoskeletons before dying and plunging everything back into the tactile, acoustic dark. The predators, for their part, have evolved to strike during the brief window of disorientation that follows the flash, when the nearby swarm has scattered and the emitter is exposed. There is no strategy in this. There is only the mechanical iteration of stimulus and response, played out at a pace that makes the surface world's predator-prey dynamics look glacial.

And beneath all of this, in the hollows and pores of the pockmarked basalt, the fungi persist. They are the oldest things here, or at least the lineage that has changed the least. They are not mushrooms. They do not fruit. They are crusts—brittle, calcified sheets that spread across the stone in pale, branching patterns, their filaments penetrating the basalt's vesicles and extracting the geochemical seep that rises from deeper in the rock. They are the primary producers, the organisms that turn mineral into biological matter, and they are consumed by the thumbnail-blind, which are consumed by the middle-tier, which are consumed by the macros, which, upon death, are consumed by the thumbnail-blind. The circle is unbroken. It has never been broken. The balance is not maintained by any regulatory mechanism; it is simply the only stable configuration, the state toward which this system inevitably returns whenever it is perturbed.

What lives here, then, is a machine. Not a metaphorical machine—a literal one, in the sense that it processes inputs and produces outputs with mechanical reliability, that its components are standardized and interchangeable, that it has no purpose beyond its own continuation and no awareness of its own operation. The organisms within it are not individuals in any meaningful sense. They are moving parts. The swarm is the gear-train. The macros are the pistons. The fungi are the fuel line. The condensation is the coolant. And the whole thing runs, and runs, and runs, in the dark, in the deafening, suffocating, chitin-perfumed dark, grinding everything that enters it—including, eventually, you—into the same mineral dust from which it draws its ancient, unending sustenance.
