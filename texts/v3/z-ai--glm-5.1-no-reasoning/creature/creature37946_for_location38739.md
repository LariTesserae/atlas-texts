Something lives here that should not be possible. They are not born; they are grown, assembled, and instigated.

They call themselves nothing, for they have no need of names in a city where every mind is linked by the fine, trailing mycelia that lace through the walls of their fungal chambers, and every mood is communicated through the subtle alkaloids in the water they drink. The city built them as much as they built the city. They are the architects and the architecture, the farmers and the fertilizer.

Their bodies are long, hydrostatic miracles of cold-pressured flesh. They stand taller than a human, but their posture is an ever-shifting negotiation with gravity and water. They move through the dense, weeping corridors of their city like soft machines, their limbs extending and retracting in pulses of internal fluid. They have no bones. Their skeletal structure is a series of interlocking cartilaginous rings, flexible and resilient, allowing them to pour themselves through the narrow, root-choked tunnels of the lower terraces or stretch upward to tend the luminescent bladders on the cavern roof. To watch one move is to see a muscle realize it is a bone.

Their skin is a permeable membrane, a living filter. It is a mottled, gray-green, matching the flat surgical light of their world, and it glistens always with the heavy dew of the basin. Through it, they absorb the dissolved minerals of the weeping rock-water; through it, they excrete the concentrated toxins of the phosphorescent lichen they harvest. They do not sweat—they *transpire*, a constant, quiet exchange of chemistry with the drowned air. When they stand still in the fern-carpet, they are nearly invisible, their skin responding to the chemical signatures of the plants around them, flushing with chlorophyll greens or the pale translucence of fungal bark.

Their heads are the most alien feature. They have no eyes. The basin has no sun, no sharp shadows, no distance to measure—only the press of moisture and the glow of fungus. Instead, their faces are dominated by a broad, sensitive nasal plate, a convoluted, damp expanse of flesh ringed with long, catfish-like barbels. They *smell* the world. They feel it in the shifting densities of the mist, the minute temperature gradients of a warm body in the cold water, the electrical fields of the amphibians swimming in the flooded lower streets. A drop of water falling from a gourd-channel ten yards away tells them the channel is overflowing. The faint, bitter resin of a crushed fern leaf tells them a lurker has fed. The air is thick with information, and they read it like a book.

They have mouths, but they are small, puckered things, set low beneath the nasal plate. They eat rarely and strangely. Their primary sustenance is not the flesh of the amphibians or the insects, but a thick, amber syrup that weeps from the deep taproots of the fungal trees, a substance rich in sugars and hallucinogenic compounds. They harvest it by pressing their mouths directly to the bleeding roots, drinking until their translucent bellies glow amber in the green light. It is this syrup that makes them quiet, that keeps their movements slow and deliberate, that binds them to the rhythm of the water and the growth of the fungus. They are a people perpetually, chemically sedated by their own home.

Their city is their body. They do not build; they *prune*. The terraced platforms are the shelves of massive bracket fungi, coaxed and scarified into flat surfaces. The hanging bridges are the aerial roots of the horsetail stands, trained across chasms and allowed to calcify in the wet air. The cellular rooms are the rotted-out cores of ancient fungal trunks, the walls smoothed and sealed with a paste of chewed moss and their own shed skin. There is no art. There is no decoration. There is only the ruthless, beautiful efficiency of an organism that has become indistinguishable from its environment.

They sleep in piles. Dozens of them, tangled together in the warm, fermenting depths of the root-caverns, their bodies exchanging heat and fluids, their barbels intertwined, their breathing a slow, synchronous rhythm that matches the drip of the condensation from the ceiling. They do not dream—or if they do, their dreams are chemical, a slow tide of alkaloids rising and falling in the shared water of their bodies.

When they must speak to those outside their chemical network—when they must warn or coerce or mourn—they do not use words. They sing, but their song is not heard; it is felt. Deep within their chests, they possess a resonating chamber, a hollow, bony cyst that they can vibrate at frequencies below the threshold of sound. When a sentinel is disturbed, it sends a low, terrifying thrum through the water that saturates the ground, a pulse that travels through the stone and the roots and the flooded terraces. It is a message that bypasses the ears entirely and settles into the bones of every living thing in the basin: a warning, a command, a presence that cannot be ignored.

The amphibians fear them. The lurkers in the ferns avoid the paths they walk. Even the insects seem to scatter when the amber-drunk architects move through the vegetation, their long, boneless limbs pushing aside the ferns, their wet, eyeless faces turning slowly to taste the air. They are not cruel. They are not kind. They are the immune system of the basin, the caretakers of a wound in the earth, tending the raw, weeping stone with the cold, patient ruthlessness of a body healing itself.

And when one of them dies—and they die often, for their fragile, boneless bodies are prone to infection, to tearing, to the slow, creeping rot that is the basin's constant tax—it is not mourned. It is carried to the deepest, wettest cistern, where the water is oldest and coldest, and it is lowered gently into the dark. Within days, the body is gone, consumed by the very fungus that built the city, its matter returned to the amber syrup, its chemical memory dissolved into the mycelial network that links the minds of its kin. There is no grave. There is no loss. There is only the ceaseless, silent motion of life, pressing upward through the water, pushing back the dark.
