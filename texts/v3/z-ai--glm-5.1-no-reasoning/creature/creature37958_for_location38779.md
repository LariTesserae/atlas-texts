What lives in the Weeping Cirque lives according to the law of the negotiation.

It is not a singular creature. It is an accumulation of presences, a community of bodies that have so thoroughly accepted the damp, the grey, and the slow weeping of the stone that they have become expressions of the place itself. They do not dominate the cirque; they are its transient organs, fulfilling functions the rock and water cannot perform alone—the functions of seeing, of moving, of briefly holding the light before it escapes back through the circle of grey sky.

At the margin, where the dark mud meets the shallow water, lives the Margin-keeper.

It is the thing that made the three-toed tracks. It is small—not much larger than a human's cupped hands—and its life is a closed circuit between the water and the ferns. Its body is low and fluid, shaped like the spill of water itself, covered not in fur or scales but in a smooth, permeable skin that drinks the ambient wetness the way the sphagnum-like mats drink the mud. Its three toes are not for speed; they are for registering pressure, splayed wide to distribute its weight across the unstable mud, leaving those deep, deliberate press-marks. It has no eyes that function in the way we understand sight; the diffuse, directionless light of the cirque would render eyes useless things, prone to confusion. Instead, its head is dominated by a broad, flat sensory plate—a shape like the rachis of a fern—which reads the humidity gradients of the air. It breathes through its skin, like the larvae in the mud, and its blood is cold, matching the temperature of the weeping water. 

The Margin-keeper exists to patrol the boundary. It moves from the dark water to the ferns, ingesting the small writhing larvae and the mica-winged insects, and then it returns to the water to excrete and to breathe. It is a creature of transit. It never stops moving, because to stop is to risk drying out, to risk becoming a static mark on the stone rather than a process. If you sit long enough in the cirque, you will not see it, but you will feel it—a subtle displacement in the humidity, a slight shift in the rhythm of the dripping as something displaces a fern frond, drinks, and moves on. It is the watcher of the edge, ensuring the negotiation between soil and water remains unbroken.

Higher up, where the stone leans and the fissures weep, lives the Rock-drinker.

It is not a plant, though it might be mistaken for one of the twisted, modest trees if one were only glancing. The Rock-drinker is an animal that has abandoned the architecture of motion. It has wedged itself into the back wall of the cirque, nestled in a deep fissure where the water emerges most steadily, and it has stayed there so long that it has become part of the weeping. It is roughly cylindrical, the color of rust and pale bone, and its skin has toughened into a leathery, mineralized hide that fuses with the rock at its base—just as the tree roots do, bark becoming mineral becoming bark. 

The Rock-drinker does not hunt. It does not patrol. It does nothing but drink. Its mouth is a wide, cupping orifice pressed tight against the stone, creating a slight vacuum, drawing the weeping water into itself before it can run down the wall. Inside its long, slow body, it filters the water, extracting the faint traces of organic matter—the dead spores, the blowing dust, the microscopic decay carried by the damp wind. Its waste is a clean, mineral-heavy fluid that it exudes from its upper surface, contributing its own dendritic streaks to the stains on the rock. 

It is almost entirely still. It breathes once every few minutes, a slow expansion and contraction that is indistinguishable from the slow swelling of the wet stone. It is blind; it has no need for sight in its dark, wet crevice. It is deaf; the constant roar of the water is the only reality it has ever known, and it has become part of that roar. The Rock-drinker is the circulatory system of the cirque wall, a biological pump that takes what the rock gives and returns what the rock needs, binding the flesh of the place to its skeleton.

Above them both, in the grey air between the leaning crowns of the trees, lives the Wind-cipher.

It is the most elusive, the most abstract of the cirque's inhabitants. It is an airborne creature, dwelling entirely in the saturated air, never touching stone, soil, or water. It is small—no larger than a knuckle—and it is almost weightless. Its body is a thin, diaphanous membrane, like the dried wing of an insect or the husk of a seedpod, stretched taut over a structure of delicate, hollow struts. It has no discernible organs; it does not eat, it does not drink, it does not reproduce in any observable way. It simply exists to ride the slow, damp wind that pours over the rim of the cirque.

The Wind-cipher is a glider. It catches the thick air on its translucent membrane, tilting and banking in the wide gaps of sky between the trees. It is drawn to the changes in light. When the grey warms to amber, the Wind-ciphers ascend, riding the slight thermal lift created by the sun-warmed stone, spiraling upward in loose, lazy gyres. When the light cools to blue-grey, they descend, drifting back into the lower canopy, settling near the ferns. They seem to be the eyes of the place, though they possess no eyes—only a surface that reacts to the angle and intensity of the light, shifting its pigment from translucent to a faint, opaque white, as if absorbing the visual state of the cirque and storing it in their tissues.

They are silent. They do not flap; they have no muscles for it. When they die—perhaps from drying out, perhaps from simply reaching the end of their slow, floating arc—they do not fall. The damp air holds them, and they drift down like a leaf on still water, landing softly on the standing water or the wet mud, where the Margin-keeper or the larvae will eventually incorporate their mineralized membranes back into the cycle. 

Together, these three—the keeper, the drinker, the cipher—constitute the living breath of the Weeping Cirque. They do not interact. They do not need to. The Margin-keeper moves laterally, binding the horizontal layers of water, mud, and fern. The Rock-drinker moves vertically, binding the stone to the water. The Wind-cipher moves in the third dimension, binding the light and the air to the shape of the bowl. They are not a society; they are a grammar. They are the verbs that allow the cirque's nouns—the rock, the water, the ferns, the tree roots—to form sentences.

They do not know you are there. They do not care. If you sit long enough in the cirque, you will begin to feel yourself becoming part of their grammar. Your breath will sync with the slow exhalation of the wind. Your skin will drink the wetness, becoming as damp and saturated as the sphagnum. Your eyes will lose their focus, adapting to the diffuse, shadowless light. You will start to feel less like an observer and more like a feature—a temporary eddy in the flow of air, a momentary obstacle in the path of the water, a thing that is held by the bowl of stone until the bowl decides to let you go.

The Weeping Cirque does not have a spirit. It is not enchanted in any mythological sense. But it is inhabited, thoroughly and completely, by things that have so perfectly matched its rhythms that they have disappeared into its functions. To live in the cirque is to be an extension of the water, the stone, and the slow, unending weeping. To visit is to be reminded that life does not always roar, or hunt, or build; sometimes, it simply percolates, it simply drinks, it simply drifts in the damp, grey patience of a place that has found its state, and will not be moved.
