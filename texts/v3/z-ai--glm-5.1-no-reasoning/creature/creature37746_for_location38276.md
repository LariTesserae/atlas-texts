What lives in the Black Mesaural is not a collection of individual creatures so much as a single, continuous metabolism. Life here does not scatter from danger or hustle for survival; it *settles*, it *colonizes*, and it *digests*. In a world without light, where warmth and water are the only currencies, the boundaries between animal, plant, and machine are not just blurred—they are completely dissolved. What lives here is a thermodynamic imperative, a slow, relentless conversion of heat and rain into structure, into motion, into thought.

At the apex of this dark, wet world live the **Stewards**. 

You might mistake them for a grove of the near-trees at first, but they are the largest fauna the Mesaural possesses. They are massive, pillar-like organisms, towering meters above the vine-networks, their hides composed of the same dense, near-black, waxy tissue as the funnel-plants. They move so slowly that their motion is imperceptible to the human eye—a step might take a month to complete—but they are moving, migrating in a vast, centuries-long circuit across the plateau. 

The Stewards are tappers. Their immense, root-like feet seek out the geothermal vents and the deep cisterns of the vegetation. When a Steward settles, its toes uncurl and drive into the earth, plunging into the root-systems and nutrient-rich puddles, drinking deeply of the heated, chemically rich water. They are the gardeners of the Mesaural, though they do not intend to be. As they drain a localized water table, the surrounding vine-networks are forced to redirect their flow, shifting the hydraulic pressure of the landscape. The Stewards are also the only creatures that interact with the ancient factories as if they were geography. They lean against the corroded walls, using the structures as windbreaks against the slow, massive fronts of rain, their weight occasionally triggering a dormant mechanical valve, their body heat adding to the industrial warmth that drives the colonized turbines. They are blind, deaf in any acoustic sense, and possess no brain, yet they navigate the vast dark by reading the micro-currents of the steam and the rhythmic clanking of the machines, feeling the world as a map of vibrating heat.

Below the Stewards, in the tangled mid-layer of pulsing vines and waterfalls, live the **Wire-Weavers**. 

If the Stewards are the slow, dumb giants of the Mesaural, the Weavers are its intellect. They are the ambush predators of the vine-networks, but calling them predators implies a hunger they do not possess; they are more like immune cells. They are slender, multi-limbed creatures, their exoskeletons a latticework of chitin and living metal oxides leached from the corroded factories. They do not have eyes; instead, their entire dorsal surface is a tympanum, a drum-head stretched tight over a highly tuned neural cavity. They are physically plugged into the vine-networks, their hollow limbs piercing the warm, flowing cables to drink the circulating water and to listen. 

A Weaver feels the world as a web of vibrations. It feels the percussive rain on the canopy as a continuous hum; it feels the clank of a distant compressor as a rhythmic spike. When the fist-sized floaters descend to rest on the vines, their tiny weight shifts the tension of the cables by a fraction of a millimeter. To a Weaver, this is a scream. When it strikes, it is not a chaotic lunge but a precise, explosive hydraulic release. It unplugs its limbs from the vine, snapping its body like a coiled spring. The strike is a single, sharp *crack*—the sound of a branch snapping that echoes through the dark. By the time the ripples of the strike fade, the floater is already being dissolved in the Weaver's gut, and the creature has re-inserted itself into the network, disappearing entirely into the architecture of the warm, wet dark. 

On the ground, squelching through the spongy, iron-smelling mat of organic sludge, live the **Sump-Guzzlers**. 

They are the scavengers and the recyclers, resembling low, flat slabs of muscle and blubber, roughly the size of a man's torso, with no discernible front or back. They possess dozens of stubby, piston-like legs that move in chaotic, arrhythmic waves, making them look like crawling rugs. They are drawn to the factories. They congregate around the rusted seams and burst pipes of the ancient installations, their broad, lip-like mouths latched onto the exhaust vents of the colonized machines. The machines, driven by the vegetative networks, expel a slurry of processed heat, chemical runoff, and shredded organic matter—a waste product that the Guzzlers find intoxicating. They are the creature equivalent of the root-cisterns: bloated, warm to the touch, and constantly leaking a faint, metallic steam from their pores. They are entirely docile, possessing no defense mechanism save for their sheer density, and the salamander-things often use them as warm resting spots, draping their slick black bodies over the Guzzlers' backs to sleep.

And then there are the **Vessel-Mothers**.

The Vessel-Mothers are the reason the machines still turn, the reason the factories breathe. They are the missing link between the biology of the Mesaural and the engineering of its forgotten past. They look like bloated, eyeless salamanders the size of small whales, but their bellies are translucent, stretched taut over a pulsating, bioluminescent core—a core that is not organic. Millions of years ago, the ancestors of the Vessel-Mothers found the deepest, warmest parts of the ruined factories and sought refuge there. Over epochs, they ingested the decaying nuclear or geothermal cores, the power cells, the turbines. Instead of digesting them, their bodies encapsulated them. The metal and ceramic became the seed around which their biology grew, a symbiotic relationship so complete that the creature is now born with a mechanical heart. 

They live in the deepest basins of the factories, submerged in the warm, chemical-rich puddles that pool in the shattered concrete foundations. They do not move much; they do not need to. The mechanical cores within them generate immense heat, and their bodies act as organic radiators, pulsing that heat into the water, which in turn drives the vine-networks that power the rest of the machinery. The clanking and shuddering you hear in the walls of a factory is the mechanical heartbeat of the Vessel-Mother buried beneath it, an arrhythmic, ancient rhythm that keeps the entire ecosystem in motion. When a Vessel-Mother dies, the factory above her goes silent, the vines wither, and the rain reclaims the structure in a matter of decades.

What lives in the Black Mesaural is a choir of heat and pressure. They are creatures that have never seen the sun, that do not know what a shadow is. They are blind, but they are not lost. They hear the world through the vibrations of warm water in brass pipes; they feel the passage of time in the pressure of a rain front against their skin. They are the flesh of a world that has forgotten light, the living gears of a machine that runs on rain, thriving in the perfect, unending, absolute dark.
