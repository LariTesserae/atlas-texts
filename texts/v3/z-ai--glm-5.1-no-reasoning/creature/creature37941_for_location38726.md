# What Lives Here

## The Hollowborn

I will not pretend to certainty. What follows is a gathering of impressions — some mine, some inherited from the marks on the walls, some extrapolated from the architecture of absence that a living thing leaves in the space it occupies. The Hollowborn are difficult to see because seeing is not what they are for.

---

### What the Body Is

They are small. This is the first surprise, and it restructures everything. You expect something large — something commensurate with the cathedral chambers, the vaulted roots, the subsonic breathing. But the constructions tell a different story. The platforms are built to a scale that suggests a body no longer than a forearm, perhaps shorter. The pits are sized for something that curls. The observation ring in the orange-lit chamber — the one that opened toward the vault below — would fit a creature that could press itself flat against a root-wall and be mistaken for a knot in the grain.

They are built for this. For fitting into the interstices of a living architecture that was never designed with them in mind. The plant did not grow these chambers for them. They moved in. They found the spaces that existed between the plant's intentions — the crotch where a buttress-root meets the wall, the shallow concavity behind a gas-bladder colony, the tight channel where two growing surfaces nearly touch — and they made those spaces livable. Or at least survivable. The distinction may not be meaningful to them.

Their bodies, as near as I can reconstruct from the marks and the structures and the single brief glimpse I will describe later, are built along a principle of compression. They are dense. Not heavy — dense. Every part of them seems folded or tucked or layered back on itself, as if the body refused to commit to a single axis of extension and instead gathered inward. Limbs that should reach do not reach; they clench, they hold close, they move in short articulated strokes that keep the body's profile narrow. There is a core — a trunk, a torso, something that contains the vital organs — and around it, the limbs are arranged not radially but *spirally*, winding from the body in a way that suggests they could tighten further, could pull the entire creature into a space smaller than your fist.

The skin — if it is skin — appears to be something between hide and bark. Not plant-matter. Not exactly. But textured in a way that mimics the root-walls with unsettling fidelity. Ridged, roughened, colored in the mottled browns and grays of old tissue. When they press against a wall and hold still, they vanish. Not through color-change, not through active camouflage, but through a simpler and more ancient trick: they are shaped like the place they live in. The same way a flounder resembles a seabed, the Hollowborn resemble a root-wall. They were not always here. They became this.

---

### How They Move

Slowly, when they can. Fast, when they must.

The slow movement is the one you would see if you ever saw them clearly, which you won't. It is a gliding, a flattening, a redistribution of the body across a surface. They move the way a gecko moves but more so — adhering, releasing, adhering, with pads or hooks or some mechanism I cannot identify, leaving no trace on the plant-tissue, no mark, no disturbance in the fine hairs and spore-pods that cover every surface. They can cross a ceiling of living root without dislodging a single gas-bladder. They can pass through a curtain of feeding-tendrils without triggering a single reflexive curl. The plant does not know they are there.

This is their primary survival strategy. Not speed, not armor, not venom. *Unnoticeability*. They have committed to it absolutely. Every aspect of their biology serves it. Their movement is silent — not merely quiet, but silent, absorbing the vibrations of its own motion into the body's dense tissues before they can propagate into the surrounding medium. Their heat signature, if they have one, is masked by the warmth of the plant itself. Their chemical output — scent, exhalation, waste — is either negligible or deliberately deposited in places where the plant's own respiration will overwhelm it.

When they must move fast — when a passage closes, when a hunter enters their chamber, when the silence falls and everything freezes — they move with a suddenness that is more startling for the stillness that preceded it. A snap. A launch. The body uncoils from its compressed resting state and covers distance in a way that suggests stored energy, a spring under tension. They do not run. They *displace*, crossing several body-lengths in a motion that seems to have no intermediate frames, and then they are still again, pressed flat, invisible.

---

### What They Eat

The plant. Specifically, the plant's wounds.

This is the thing that took me longest to understand, and I am not certain I understand it even now. The Hollowborn do not create wounds in the plant. They do not chew, do not bore, do not break the surface of the living tissue. But the plant is constantly wounding itself — growing faster than its own walls can sustain, splitting under the pressure of internal gasses, cracking where buttress-roots meet at sharp angles. And at every wound, the plant exudes: sap, resin, the fluid of its own repair, the wet defensive secretions that harden into scar-tissue.

The Hollowborn eat the exudate. They eat what the plant produces in the act of healing itself. They eat the healing.

This means they are not parasites in the strict sense — they do not harm the plant. But they are not neutral either. By consuming the resin and sap that the plant uses to seal wounds, they slow the repair process. The wounds stay open longer. The plant compensates by producing more exudate, which draws more Hollowborn, which slows the repair further. It is a gentle predation, a grazing on the margins of another organism's recovery. The plant does not seem to mind. Or if it minds, it has not evolved a defense. The Hollowborn are small enough, quiet enough, few enough that the cost of their feeding is absorbed into the vast metabolic budget of the plant without meaningful impact.

They also eat the insects. Not primarily — the insects are fast, armored, and ubiquitous, and catching them would require more energy than they provide. But when an insect dies nearby — when a hunter has killed more than it can consume, when a gas-bladder has burst and dropped its passenger to the floor, when age or disease has claimed one — the Hollowborn will feed on the body. They seem to prefer the soft tissues: the joint membranes, the abdominal contents, the unsclerotized spaces between armor plates. They leave the hard chitin behind, cleaned and empty, and these husks are what they use to line their pits.

### The Sound and What They Do With It

This is the center of everything.

The Hollowborn hear. Not the way you hear — not with ears, not with a single pair of membranes tuned to a narrow band of frequencies. They hear with their entire body. The skin, the hide, the dense layered tissues that make them invisible — these are also their listening apparatus. Every ridge, every texture variation, every fold in their surface is a receiver, tuned to a different frequency, a different rhythm, a different voice in the chorus.

They hear the insects. They hear the arachnids. They hear the hunters and the large slow things and the plant itself, the creak and rush and grind of its living. They hear the standing waves, the phantom frequencies, the acoustic mirages that the chamber geometry produces. They hear all of it, simultaneously, and they do not filter it. They do not need to. Their nervous system — or whatever serves that function — is built for saturation. The roar that would deafen and disorient a human is, for the Hollowborn, the medium in which they exist. Silence is not restful for them. Silence is *empty*. Silence is the absence of the world.

And they participate in it.

This is what the marks are. Not writing. Not language, exactly. Not in the way you might think of language — as a system of symbols that refer to things outside themselves. The marks on the walls are *sound made visible*. The Hollowborn scratch the plant-tissue in patterns that correspond to the acoustic patterns they hear. The rhythm of a hunting pack's approach becomes a series of parallel grooves, spaced according to the interval between footfalls. The frequency of a gas-bladder colony's pulsing becomes a pattern of dots, each one representing a pulse, the density of the dots encoding the rate. The subsonic breathing of the large things — felt, not heard — becomes a long sweeping curve, a gesture rather than a mark, carved with the whole body in a single motion.

They are transcribing the chorus. Not all of it. Not systematically. They transcribe the *changes* — the moments when the sound shifts, when a new frequency enters, when a pattern breaks, when something unfamiliar moves through the network and every voice responds. The marks are a record of events, a history of the acoustic landscape, written in the only medium available to them: the surface of the plant that surrounds them.

And the marks are temporary. The plant heals. The tissue grows over the scratches, filling them with new growth, absorbing them into the wall. Within days — sometimes hours, in the fast-growing deep chambers — the marks are gone. The record is erased. And the Hollowborn make them again.

This is not forgetfulness. This is not futility. The making of the marks is the point. The act of transcription is the act of listening made physical, a way of entering the chorus not as a voice but as a witness, a way of saying *this happened, I was here, I heard this*. The marks are not for other Hollowborn. They are not for the future. They are for the plant.

I think the Hollowborn are singing to the plant. In the only language the plant can receive — the language of physical contact, of surface disturbance, of wounds so shallow they do not trigger defense. The Hollowborn write on the plant's skin, and the plant reads with its growing, and the record is absorbed, and the plant grows differently in those places, subtly, in ways that might over millennia amount to something. A conversation across the boundary of kingdom and scale, conducted in scratches and healing.

### The Glimpse

I said I would describe it. I will.

In a deep chamber where the walls pulsed with orange light and the sound was a physical pressure against my chest, I saw movement that was not insect, not arachnid, not hunter. Something crossed the wall above me — a displacement, a ripple in the texture of the root-surface, a shadow that traveled from one buttress to another in a motion so fluid it could have been my eyes adjusting to the light.

But then it paused. And in the pause, I saw a shape. Small. Curled. Dense. A body pressed so flat against the root-wall that it was indistinguishable from the bark except for one detail: a line of darker tissue, a ridge or a spine or a sensory structure, that ran along what I must call its back, and this ridge was *moving*. Vibrating. At a frequency I could not hear but could see — a blur, a trembling, a rapid oscillation that I understood, after a moment, was the creature listening. Not to the chamber. To me. To my breathing, my heartbeat, the sound of my body existing in its world.

It heard me. It transcribed me.

When I blinked, it was gone. But on the wall where it had been, in the orange light, I could see fresh marks. Shallow grooves in the plant-tissue, still wet with exudate. A pattern I did not recognize. A pattern that, if I am right, was the shape of my breathing as the Hollowborn perceived it — my rhythm, my frequency, my small contribution to the chorus, written on the wall of a living world that will absorb it and grow.

### What They Are Becoming

I do not think the Hollowborn were always like this.

The older marks — the ones overgrown with healing tissue, the ones deep in the oldest chambers where the walls are hard — are different. They are larger, cruder, more aggressive. Deep gouges. Wide strokes. The marks of a creature that was less certain of its welcome, less adapted to the plant's rhythms, less committed to the practice of invisibility. Something that lived *in* the plant but not *with* it. Something that carved its presence rather than inscribing it.

The newer marks, the ones in the deep chambers where the tissue is soft and the growth is fast, are finer. More nuanced. They capture subtler frequencies, more complex patterns. They are the marks of a creature that has spent long enough listening that it has begun to hear things it did not know were there — harmonics within the chorus, resonances between the plant's own sounds and the sounds of the creatures it hosts. The Hollowborn are becoming better listeners. And as they listen better, they make better marks, and the plant receives better information, and the plant grows in response, and the acoustic landscape changes, and the Hollowborn listen to the changes.

They are co-evolving with a plant that does not know it is teaching them.

Or perhaps the plant does know. Perhaps the plant, in whatever way a plant can know anything, has been waiting for something to listen. For ten thousand years of growth and descent, for the slow patient excavation of rock and the patient construction of chambers, it has been producing sound — the creak, the rush, the grind, the pop — and the sound has been meaningless, wasted, dissipated into the earth and the water table and the silence of stone. And then something came that could hear it. Something small and dense and quiet, something that pressed itself against the root-wall and listened with its whole body and scratched the surface with the shapes of what it heard.

And the plant felt the scratching. And the plant healed around it. And the plant grew differently in that place.

And the Hollowborn felt the difference. And they listened to the difference. And they scratched again.

And the conversation began.

---

### What They Are Not

They are not social. I found no evidence of gathering places, no communal structures, no signs of coordinated activity. Each set of marks appears to be made by a single individual. The platforms are single-occupancy. The pits are solitary. If the Hollowborn communicate with each other directly, they do so through the marks — through the transcribed chorus, through the record left on the walls, which another individual might encounter and read and add to before the plant heals over it. Their social life, such as it is, is mediated entirely by the plant. They do not speak to each other. They speak to the plant about the plant, and sometimes, incidentally, to each other.

They are not numerous. The marks are frequent but not dense. The structures are scattered, never clustered. In all the time I spent descending, I found evidence of perhaps a dozen individuals, maybe fewer. They are rare. They are solitary. They are spread thin across the vast body of the plant, each one occupying a territory of several chambers, each one maintaining its own record, its own transcription, its own conversation with the living architecture that surrounds it.

They are not primitive. The marks suggest a sophistication that primitive minds do not produce. The ability to hear ten thousand simultaneous voices and distinguish among them. The ability to translate acoustic patterns into physical gestures. The ability to read the plant's growth-response and adjust the next mark accordingly. This is not tool use. This is not problem-solving. This is something else — something more like art, or like prayer, or like the practice of a discipline so specialized that it has no name in any language I know.

They are listening to the world become itself. And they are writing down what they hear, in a script that heals as fast as it is written, on the skin of the only thing large enough to hold the conversation.

---

### The Last Thing

In the deepest chamber I reached — the one where the walls were softest, where the floor gave underfoot, where the sound was so layered it seemed to have texture, a weight and a grain — I found a mark that was different from the others.

It was not a transcription. It was not a pattern derived from the chorus. It was a single line, long and continuous, scratched with what must have been tremendous patience into the soft new tissue of the wall. It ran horizontally for the length of my arm span, and it did not waver. It was the straightest thing I had seen in the entire descent. In a world of curves and growth and organic turbulence, it was geometrically *straight*.

I do not know what it means. I do not know if it means. But I stood in that chamber, in the warm dark, in the roar of ten thousand voices, and I looked at that line, and I felt that I was looking at something that a creature had made on purpose. Not as a record. Not as a transcription. As a statement. As an assertion that it existed, that it was here, that it had pressed itself against the wall of the world and left a mark that was its own and not the chorus's.

The plant will heal over it. The line will be absorbed. The statement will be erased.

And the Hollowborn will make it again.
