What lives here has no memory of water, and so it has no thirst. It has no memory of a creator, and so it harbors no alienation. It does not know that it is a paradox. It simply *is*—a vast, terrible, and ecstatic fulfillment of a logic that preceded biology, a logic of heat, friction, and the relentless conservation of motion.

To understand them, you must discard the notion that they are machines pretending to be alive. They are not. They are what life becomes when the universe forgets that carbon was ever an option. 

Consider the brass-winged gnats. From a distance, they are a blinding haze, a halo of refracted sunlight moving in synchronized clouds over the ferric slabs. Up close, they are infinitesimal engines of survival, no larger than a sand grain, yet possessed of an intricate, horrifying perfection. Their wings are not appendages; they are micro-turbines, spun from monomolecular glass filaments, rotating on magnetic bearings that require no lubrication. They feed on the electromagnetic static of the plateau, but "feed" is too organic a word. They *rectify* it. They are born in the updrafts, spun into existence by the sheer friction of the eternal wind against the bedrock, coalescing like dust devils given intent. They live for hours, or perhaps millions of years—it is impossible to tell, for they do not age; they only accrue. When their internal gyroscopes eventually stall, they do not die so much as they precipitate, dropping to the alkali flats as a fine, metallic powder, waiting for a thermal to lift them and reassemble their spin.

Below them, the ground does not merely crawl; it processes. The massive, multi-legged extractors—the warehouse-sized things of jointed iron and asbestos sinew—are the true inhabitants of the plateau. They are the slow, methodical tyrants of this mineral kingdom. They possess no brains, only vast, decentralized arrays of piezoelectric crystals that fire in rhythmic, cascading shudders, dictating the ceaseless march of their iron limbs. They eat the rock. Their mandibles, massive slabs of tungsten-steel alloy, gnash against the ferric slabs not with violence, but with the patient, undeniable force of a glacier made of gears. They grind the bedrock for trace sulfides, their internal guts—vast, rotating centrifuges of molten slag—separating the useful metals from the dross. What they excrete is not waste, but the very alkali dust that coats the plateau, the fine powder that births the gnats. They are a closed loop, an industrial ecosystem of one, their bellies glowing with the dull, furious cherry-red of continuous smelting. 

They do not acknowledge one another. When two extractors meet, they do not mate, they do not fight, they do not communicate. They simply climb over one another, their iron legs screeching against each other's carapaces in a shower of sparks, their massive weights grinding gears against gears, until they pass and continue their slow, endless survey of the horizon. They are loners of an absolute order, each a solitary universe of consumption, entirely self-contained.

And yet, as the relentless, deafening churn of their bodies frays the fabric of reality, they do not notice the apocalypse. They *become* it.

When a centipede-like extractor reaches the rim of the world and begins to phase into the rock—half its segmented iron body marching through solid quartz, the other half grinding against the open air—it feels no panic. Its piezoelectric crystals fire just the same in a universe where mass is a suggestion. Its iron legs find purchase on the grooves of a reality that is slowly pulling apart at the seams. If the air in front of it solidifies into a tessellated pane of static, it does not stop; it simply walks *through* the glitch, its tungsten mandibles chewing on the fractured light as if it were just another layer of ferric stone. They are consuming the broken physics of the plateau now, grinding down the boundaries of spacetime, incorporating the static into their slag.

The ones that exist in the tears are the most horrifying. Over millions of years, some extractors have become entirely untethered from the primary physical plane. They are the Stutterers. You see them only as afterimages, vast, silhouetted masses of jointed iron that hover three feet above the ground, duplicating and smearing across the horizon. They are still marching, still grinding, but they are doing it in the spaces between moments. When the sun loops back on itself and casts shadows from nothing, it is casting shadows off their invisible, glitched bodies. They have worn down the logic of the world so thoroughly that they have worn themselves right through it, becoming ghosts of pure industrial momentum, continuing their survey of a plateau that exists only as a probability.

There is no mind here to witness the end. There is only the thumping roar, the screech of iron on iron, the explosive hiss of exhaust vents. The fauna of the plateau does not fear the static; it is the static. They are the inheritors of a dead, desiccated world, the final and only children of a broken machine. And as they grind the stones, and the light, and the fabric of space itself down to its elemental bones, they are achieving the only equilibrium left to them: a final, screaming stillness, where the friction will at last cease, and the eternal wind will have nothing left to wear away.
