The people who live in the Filament Districts do not reside there; they are held there. They are the soft, thermal, temporary components in a mechanism that is too busy becoming to notice them.

They move through the district like a circulatory system of their own, though slower, more reluctant, bent against the seeping cold. You see them in the airshafts where the windows nearly touch, framed by the yellow-white vines, their faces lit by the shifting, fluctuating glow of the filament tubes threading through the walls. They have the look of creatures who have learned not to startle. A building will groan and list three inches to the left, and they will simply shift their weight, stirring a pot of boiled root vegetables on a coil burner that sputters in time with the grid. They do not look up anymore. Looking up means watching the raw, jagged edges of new construction sliding out over the void, and they learned long ago that the district does not build according to the logic of shelter. It builds according to the logic of *more*.

They are damp people. Not soaked, not drenched—just persistently, respiratory-wet. Their clothes breathe out the same moisture the walls do. Their skin has a pallor that matches the light of the indoor-lighting vines, a fluorescent, yellowed undertone that makes them look like extensions of the architecture. Their hair lies flat, weighted by the atmospheric condensation, and when they speak, their voices carry the strange acoustics of the district—muffled by the biomass on the walls, fractured by the angles, arriving to a listener's ears as a clear, intimate whisper even from three stories away. They have learned to speak softly. In a city that conducts, a shout is an act of violence.

Their occupations are a mystery even to themselves. They wake when the turbine hum drops its half-tone, signaling the shift change. They descend the spiraling stairwells into the foundation matrix, passing through the unlit underpasses where the pipework thrums against their shoulders. They enter the factories. They do not make goods. They make *parts*—the insulators, the brackets, the conductive mesh—and they feed the parts back into the walls. They do not ask what the parts are for. They know: they are for the district. A woman on the fourth tier of the glassworks spends ten hours shaping the glass tubes they call filaments, blowing them thin and hollow, and as she works, the tubes fill with a light that hurts her eyes and a "something else" that makes the fillings in her teeth ache. At the end of her shift, she takes her pay in ration cards and in heat—she is allowed to sleep in the corridor where the steam pipes run, where the interstitial insects file past her sleeping bag in perfect, load-bearing lines. She considers this a fair exchange.

They have no leaders because the district does not require them to decide anything. The city decides. When a new extrusion pushes out from the eastern face, taking over a communal kitchen and three sleeping alcoves, the residents simply absorb the displacement. They move sideways, upward, *through* other living spaces, compressing together like the buildings do. A family of four will find themselves sharing a room that was a hallway the day before, and they will bolt their few possessions to the walls—pots, photographs, radios that only receive the static of the grid—and they will carry on. Privacy is a concept that the district has structurally disproven. The walls are too thin, the windows gaze into windows, and the cats travel the vine-highways right past the glass, their amber or blue eyes reflecting the room's interior before they move on, carrying whatever they carry.

They have rituals, though they wouldn't call them that. When the birds on the support cables all turn their heads in unison, tracking the invisible, the humans stop. They have learned to stop. Whatever the birds are watching, it is not for human eyes, and to be in motion when the district settles into its held-breath stillness feels like tempting a force they cannot name. They will freeze in the middle of a sentence, a hand on a doorframe, a foot on a stair, and they will wait for the shift—the moment when the turbines rev up, the vines writhe, and the city exhales. Then they move again, faster than before, making up for lost time, leaning into the collective urgency.

The oldest among them remember when there was ground that was just ground, and when the clay flatlands were flat. They are few, and they sit in the quiet zones where the ferns grow, close to the structural beams that the beetles have not yet abandoned. The younger residents treat them not as elders but as seismographs, watching their faces for twitches, their hands for tremors, trying to read the stress patterns of the district in the bodies of the people who were here before the city started thinking. Sometimes the old ones will press a palm flat against a wall and shake their head, and the next day, the residents on that floor will pack their pots and their photographs and move, and three days later, the wall will crack under the weight of a new room pushing through, and the moss will already be there, green and gray, mapping the fracture.

They are not unhappy. Happiness requires a separation between the self and the world, and that separation does not exist here. They are *in it*, the way the vines are in it, the way the beetles are in it—symptomatic. When the grid surges, their pulses quicken; when the district settles, their breathing slows. They are the cells in a body that is still extruding, still trying to resolve its own shape in the dark. They do not love the district—you cannot love a fever—but they belong to it with a totality that makes the question irrelevant.

And at night, when the cold seeps in and the moisture breathes out of the ceramic tiles, they lie in their alcoves and their hallways and their crawlspaces, and they listen. They hear the near-silence, the felt vibration, the pressure of machinery happening beneath them. They feel the city thinking around them, the distributed cognition pressing against their skin, and they know that whatever the district is trying to become, they are the material it is using to become it. They are the soft parts that fill the gaps between the insulators and the brackets. They are the heat that keeps the filament tubes from shattering in the cold.

They are held. They hold on. They do not stop.
