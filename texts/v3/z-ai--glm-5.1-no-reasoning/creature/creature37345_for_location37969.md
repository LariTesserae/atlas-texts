They do not walk so much as they calibrate their passage. 

The people of this half-made city have adapted to a world that refuses to commit to its own laws. They are a slight, wiry breed, endowed with a tensile strength that belies their fragile frames. Their skin has the pallor and texture of the pale arthropods that scuttle through the valve-chambers—translucent, lacking melanin from a life lived in the permanent twilight, crossed with the fine, dark tracery of coal dust worked so deeply into their pores it resembles a tattoo. Their lungs are large and strangely segmented, capable of filtering the acrid tang of ozone and the choking soot, though when they cough, the sound has a metallic ring, like a penny dropped into a tin cup.

Their most striking feature, however, is their gait. They move with a lurching, hesitant grace—a physical dialect born of gravitational uncertainty. Before taking a step off a gantry, a resident will pause, toeing the edge, feeling for the specific pull of the earth. They have learned to read the minute shifts in their inner ear the way a sailor reads the tide. When a dropped wrench hangs in the air, they do not startle; they simply wait, suspended in the same breathless limbo, until the object decides to fall. Children play a game on the frail iron walkways over the mist-canyons: they toss heavy bolts into the void and bet not on where they will land, but on *when*.

They dress in layers of oiled canvas and heavy leather, stitched with pragmatic severity, but always with the addition of close-fitting, lead-weighted boots. These boots are their anchor to reality. Without them, a sudden shift in the mountain's density might pull a person upward into the smog, lost to the sideways rain. Around their waists, they wear heavy harnesses festooned with friction-clips and carabiners, always tethered to the iron railings, the steam-pipes, the riveted steel. To be untethered here is to be at the mercy of a geography that is currently hallucinating.

Their shelters are built into the staggered tiers of the city, but they do not think of them as homes. They call them "brackets." A bracket is a space wedged between the vibrating wall of a freight elevator shaft and the damp, sweating bricks of a foundry. They sleep not in beds, but in canvas hammocks strung tightly between the pipes, which provide a steady, searing heat that combats the crystalline cold of the air. The subsonic thrum of the mountain's birth penetrates everything; it is the metronome of their existence. They do not speak loudly over it. Instead, they communicate in a rapid, clicking shorthand—similar to the chittering of the blind arthropods—supplemented by sharp, angular hand signals that can be read in the blue-white flare of the arc lamps. When they must speak, their voices are low and resonant, designed to carry through the bass-note of the grinding stone without competing with it.

Psychologically, they are a people marinated in patience and low-level fatalism. Time moves strangely for them, and they have absorbed that distortion. They do not hurry. Hurry is a danger when an alleyway might stretch into an hour-long trek, or when a misjudged sprint across a courtyard might end in a localized pocket of reversed gravity. They possess an industry that matches the city's own, but it is a meditative, unhurried industry. A riveter will set a single rivet with the deliberation of a surgeon, checking the tension, feeling the metal cool, aware that the bedrock beneath the forge might have shifted a millimeter in the time it took to raise the hammer. They do not measure their lives in years, which blur together in the dimness, but in "fractures"—the visible cracks that appear in the vaulted ceilings, the sudden toppling of a scaffold bent by the expanding granite.

There is a quiet reverence among them for the mountain, mixed with a deep, utilitarian resentment. They do not worship the earth, but they respect its gestation. They know they are building a city on the back of a beast that is still being born. When the mountain skips a beat, and the grinding thrum ceases, every soul in the city freezes. In that deafening silence, they hear the vast, iron lungs of the city expanding, and they feel the weight of the deep earth pausing. They stand rigid, their hands on their tethers, waiting for the physics to snap back, waiting for the mountain to remember it is supposed to be crushing them.

They are not defeated by this. They are as aggressive and stubborn as the luminescent mosses that drape the smokestacks. They have carved a life out of the friction between the deliberate hammer of their industry and the endless, tectonic gestation of the deep. In their pale, clicking, tethered existence, they are the living embodiment of the city itself: desperate, organized, and clinging to the edge of a world that is still deciding whether or not to exist.
