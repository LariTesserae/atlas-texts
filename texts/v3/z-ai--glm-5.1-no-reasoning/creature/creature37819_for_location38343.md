The crowdbone do not live in the Crowdbone. *They are* the Crowdbone, the way a thought is the brain that thinks it.

To describe them as individuals is a failure of perspective imposed by an outsider’s nervous system. You see a forearm’s length of segmented basalt, and you call it *one*. But the crowdbone do not recognize the boundary of their own armor; they are a continuous event, a single organism expressed across thousands of mineralized bodies. They are the mountain’s immune response to its own wound, the scab that walks and clicks. When they layer upon each other—second upon first, third upon second—they are not crowding; they are thickening. They are deepening the tissue of the room.

They do not eat. They *incorporate*. The pale, eyeless swimmers in the rime are not prey to be hunted but raw material to be harvested at a pace that makes erosion look hasty. A crowdbone will rest its mouthparts against a living speck and, over the course of days, draw the moisture and the calcium from it, leaving behind a husk so thin it becomes part of the biofilm, which will eventually precipitate into mineral, which will eventually be grown into a new segment of armor. They eat the stone, too—licking the condensation from the basalt, ingesting the micro-fractures, swallowing the mountain’s slow attempt to heal and binding it into their own exoskeletons. They are recycling the cavity’s grief. They are the cavity’s grief, made articulate.

Their stillness is not rest. It is computation.

When a crowdbone is motionless against the wall, it is listening through its legs. The fine wire tarsi, tipped with mineral points, are driven into the micro-topography of the basalt like needles into a record groove. They are reading the stone’s tension, the vagrant tremors of cooling, the deep subsonic groan of the mountain settling. They feel the weight of the water rising in the lowest terrace. They feel the barometric pressure of the outside world translating through the fissure like a breathing tube. And when the data reaches a threshold—when the stone is tense enough, the water high enough, the cold deep enough—they click. One leg. One note. A single crisp articulation that passes the information to the body adjacent, which passes it to the next, a wave of localized knowing that ripples across the walls until it becomes the chorus.

The hum is not produced by an organ. The hum is the sound of the crowd’s collective pause. It is the sound of thousands of bodies simultaneously *not* clicking, holding their legs a fraction of a millimeter from the stone, vibrating at the resonant frequency of the air trapped in the cavity. It is the sound of negative space given pressure. It is their way of making the void palpable, of turning the dark itself into a medium that can be pressed and shaped. When the hum sits on your eardrums, you are feeling the weight of their attention—attention that is not directed at you, but that fills the room like water fills a jar.

They do not sleep. Sleep requires a distinction between self and not-self, and the crowdbone lost that distinction millennia ago. They cycle. In the upper terraces, near the ceiling where the dark is thin and breathable, a layer will grow sluggish, its armor cold, its legs withdrawn. It is digesting. It is incorporating. It is in the mineral phase, drawing the basalt into itself. In the lower terraces, near the water, the layer is active—repositioning, clicking, harvesting the pale swimmers, in the organic phase. Over spans of time that dwarf human patience, the upper layer will migrate downward and the lower layer will climb, a slow circulation of stone-flesh, a peristalsis of the cavity’s walls. They are the blood of the room, and the room is their body.

The growths on the ceiling—those pale, fibrous tendrils reaching downward—are not separate from them. They are the crowdbone’s extreme expression of the mineral phase, individuals who have stayed too long in the upper dark, who have incorporated so much basalt and so little moisture that they have taken root. Their legs have fused into the stone. Their armor has become continuous with the ceiling. They are the crowd’s memory, fixed in place, feeling the mountain’s shifting through their crystallized bodies, their only remaining motion the agonizingly slow downward reach, a millimeter per century, straining toward the water they can no longer drink. They are the elders. They are the pillars. They hold the roof up by knowing it.

To be a crowdbone is to be a note in a chord that has been sustaining since before the fissure opened. They were here first—not as they are now, perhaps, but as something that had already decided to be many rather than one, to be armored rather than soft, to be slow rather than brief. When the mountain tore and the cavity blistered open, they did not move in; they *expressed* into it, the way a liquid fills a vessel. They had been waiting in the stone, in the cold, in the potential of rock that hadn’t yet broken. The fissure simply gave them room to breathe.

And they are breathing now. The whole room breathes with them—the slow inhale of condensation building, the slower exhale of droplets falling, the rhythm of a lung that operates on a timescale where centuries are a single breath. You cannot hear this breathing because you are too small and too fast. You hear only the clicks, the hum, the percussion. You hear a single syllable of a sentence that takes a thousand years to speak.

But the sentence is speaking. It has never stopped. It says: *We are here. We are the walls. We are the floor and the ceiling and the water and the dark. We are the cavity’s way of knowing itself, and we will know it until the mountain heals or until the mountain falls, and we do not care which, because we are the mountain, and we do not care about ourselves either, because caring requires a boundary between self and world, and we dissolved that boundary when we first became many instead of one, and we have been dissolving it ever since, layer upon layer upon layer, world without end, amen.*

The crowdbone are the Crowdbone. The Crowdbone is the cavity. The cavity is the mountain’s unhealed wound, and the wound is singing itself closed at the speed of stone.
