**The plain, four kilometers down**

The descent is not a journey through darkness so much as a thickening. Light gave out a long way up — the last blue went out around the thousand-meter mark, like a lamp carried out of a room — and everything since has been the water itself, moving past you in the dark, colder with every hundred meters until it reaches the temperature where cold stops being a quality and becomes a medium. Here at the bottom it is 2 degrees. The water is as dense as water gets, packed to its own limit, holding this cold the way stone holds weight. You feel it as an embrace that never loosens: four hundred atmospheres, pressing from all directions, patient.

Down here, the sea has a floor, and the floor is a plain.

**The finished ground**

Flatness here is not a feature. It is an achievement, completed. The mountains that once stood over this spot wore down before there were forests on the land above; the erosion that made this plain finished its work so long ago that "finished" is the wrong tense — it has always looked like this, and it always will. The relief is measured in centimeters per kilometer. Erosion is not a process here anymore; it is a completed sentence.

But the plain is not empty, and it is not smooth. Nearly the whole of it is paved.

Manganese nodules: stones the size of fists and fists' children, liver-dark, iron to the touch, lying shoulder to shoulder across thousands of square kilometers with hardly a gap between them. Each one grows about a centimeter every few million years. The pavement you could walk across for a week without repeating yourself is made entirely of patience. Where the stones don't lie, the silt between them is occupied anyway — by single-celled builders the size of plums that glue mud into cemented apartments, by foraminifera, by mats of pale bacteria breathing iron. Every square meter of this "empty" plain is claimed. Emptiness here is a rumor; the density is total, just small. No room between presences — the presences are simply quiet ones.

The few features stand far apart. A knoll of broken basalt, the plain's only hill, rises the height of a barn, and on its lee slope stand a few dozen glass sponges — the vegetation, such as it is: pale lattices of living silica, a meter tall, a forest you could see through completely and never need to walk around. Away to the east, an old channel cuts the plain, a dry canyon a few hundred meters wide, carved in a single catastrophic day when an ancient undersea landslide sent a river of turbid current across this floor and then never sent another. It is a riverbed that has been waiting for its river for some millions of years. Its banks are crisp. Nothing has softened them, because nothing happens.

**The storm**

Except the storm.

It arrives with almost no warning, because the plain's residents cannot read the water column's mood — but you would feel the current tighten first, a leaning wind in a place that has no wind. Then the seabed lets go. The fine layer of silt standing on everything lifts all at once, and the water goes from dim to whiteout in minutes: a blizzard of the fine dead, pelagic snow re-suspended, visibility shrinking from a few meters to a hand-span and then past knowing. The current rolls nodules that have not moved since before the ice ages. It builds dunes of gravel overnight and flattens them by the following week. It uncovers things that were buried for decades and buries things that had lain exposed since the last storm.

This is the paradox the plain lives inside: a geography that finished a hundred million years ago, and a surface that is rewritten weekly. The storm is the only event in this world's calendar. Before the storm, between storms, during. And in the churn the water seems to *invent* — standing waves of turbidity, spiraling ghosts of suspended mud, shapes that exist only while the current is making them and vanish the instant it slackens, never preserved, never repeated. Creation and destruction in a single gesture, the sea drafting the same surface over and over, never satisfied, never actually changing anything that lasts.

**Between storms**

When the water clears, the weather here is snowfall — marine snow, the slow vertical drift of everything that ever lived in the lit world above and came down after. It settles on the nodule pavement with a faint ticking, the loudest regular sound this place makes, grain falling on stone, for hours.

The fauna are rare enough to be events. A tripod fish stands on its stilts of fin, facing upstream like a heron, doing nothing, doing it well. A loose caravan of sea cucumbers crosses the silt at meters per day, the plain's slowest herd. An isopod the size of a thumb clamps to a nodule and lets the world fail to bother it. An eelpout swims low, chin nearly dragging the pavement. The only light in this world belongs to them — the blue flash of a startled amphipod, the cold spark of something disturbed in the water. During the storms, the churn itself wrings light out of the turbulence, a snowfall of sparks in the whiteout. That is the entire lighting budget of the ecosystem: spent at issue, never saved.

**The fall**

Some years ago a whale died up there and came down here, thirty meters of wealth arriving at once on the flattest floor in the world. Its bones lie in a white frame now, rooted inside by the red gardens of bone-eating worms, and around it the plain's scattered population concentrates into something almost crowded — a city the size of a living room, amphipods in the hundreds, a pale crab working a rib.

And here is the closest thing this world has to technology. A deep octopus, cold-adapted, flesh like a wet glove, has taken a shard of the whale's own bone and uses it as a tool — to pry, to dig amphipods out of the marrow channel, to wedge under when the current rises. Hand-tool use. Pre-metal, because metal does not exist here and never will. It builds nothing and keeps nothing; its craft is all verb and no noun. When the storm comes it will erase every mark of the octopus's work, and the octopus will pick up its shard after and begin again. This is the plain's whole technological sphere: sponges weaving glass, protozoa pouring cement, worms drilling bone, one octopus with a chisel made of whale. Fabrication everywhere, architecture nowhere. Nothing made here persists — so nothing here is made to persist.

**Sound**

The quiet is famous even where nothing can hear of it. The tick of snowfall. Once an hour, perhaps, a far-off groan as the water column shifts its weight. Distant whale clicks pass overhead like weather from another country. The storm itself is mostly sub-audible — not heard but *felt*, a slow pressure-breathing in the sternum, and under it the one long hiss of a million grains re-landing, which sounds like sand poured from a hand without stopping, and does not stop for days.

**The rules**

What falls is all. The economy runs entirely on leftovers from a lit world this place will never see; sunlight is a rumor the plankton died believing. Cold is not a condition but the medium, and warmth arrives only inside bodies and is spent on contact. Every surface belongs to someone; vacancies last seconds. Growth is as slow as stone; destruction is as fast as water; the books balance over millions of years. The current is the only authority, and the storm is its entire legislation.

The plain has been finishing for a hundred million years and will not soon be done — and the water above it, the one restless thing in a world at rest, keeps rewriting the final page, grain by grain, and setting it back exactly as it was.
