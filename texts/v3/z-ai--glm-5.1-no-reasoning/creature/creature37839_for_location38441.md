To live in the High Kelp is to be an ulcer in the gut of a god that refuses to acknowledge it is dying. 

They are not a people who evolved; they are a people who were *pruned*. They call themselves the Arborundum, though they have no vocal cords to speak the word aloud. They are the residue of the animal, the final stubborn refutation of a world that has systematically eradicated fauna. To survive, they have surrendered their animality almost entirely. They are flesh that has agreed to the terms of the vegetable.

Physically, they are distressingly narrow. An Arborund is roughly the width of a lamppost, seven feet tall, and clad in a flexible, chitinous bark that clicks softly when they walk. They have no heads, only a crown of tensile root-cables that flare from the collarbone, interfacing seamlessly with the city’s electrification grid. Their faces—such as they are—are smooth, pale wedges of cellulose-ossified bone, featuring a single horizontal slit that serves as both intake for the condensation-drip and an exhaust for the low, tuneful whistle of their breathing. They do not eat; they plug in. To watch an Arborund feed is to see a power line mate with a cistern-vine, their root-cables plunged deep into the bulging throat of the reservoir, their bodies shuddering as the cold, metallic water is pumped into their stomachs. 

Their labor is not an act of creation, but of digestion. The city has absorbed the functions of industry, and the Arborund have absorbed the functions of the factory floor. They are the scabs that form over the wounds of the city's endless self-consumption. When the hydraulic presses stamp out a celluloid panel, it is an Arborund who catches the searing hot edge with heat-blistered hands and feeds it into the vine-bridge lattice. When a sap-pipe bursts—a common occurrence given the devastating pressure of the thump-whirr engines—they do not fix it. They graft themselves onto the breach, their own bark swelling and merging with the ruptured wood, standing there for weeks, months, or years, slowly being absorbed into the city's infrastructure until there is nothing left of them but a slightly smoother knot on the pipe. 

They do not sleep. They rest in the interlocking grids of the lignin tenements, standing upright in vast, silent galleries, their root-cables plugged into the city's nervous system. In this state, they dream the city's dreams: the slow, agonizing pressure of the tectonic roots, the chemical euphoria of chlorophyll fermentation, the deep, paranoid hunger of a metabolism eating itself. 

And they are paranoid. It is the only emotion left to them. They live in absolute terror of the Rarities—the rare, blind creatures that occasionally writhe up from the deep pipes. When the pale millipede, the size of a train, erupts from the sap-lines, the Arborund do not flee. They swarm. They attack it with a fervor that has nothing to do with danger and everything to do with loathing. They hate the animal. They hate its wetness, its chaos, its independent motion. They descend upon it with sharpened forearm-spurs, hacking it to pieces, feeding the steaming, pale chunks into the nearest hydraulic press just to feel the satisfying squelch of its proteins being ground into pulp. It is a sacrament of denial. *We are not this*, the butchery says. *We are the machine.*

But as the sun dips and the bruised purple light bleeds across the plateau, the Arborund's greatest horror is not the animal, but the fraying. They are creatures of pure routine, of rhythmic, unending labor, because routine is the only anchor left in a world losing its ontology. 

When distance begins to stutter, they feel it in their root-cables. An Arborund walking the high boulevards might see the vine-bridge a mile away suddenly snap to the width of their hand, the perspective crashing into their single eye-slit with nauseating force. They will stumble, clicking their chitin in panic, reaching for the nearest trunk—a trunk that suddenly stretches into an infinite, hollow vanishing point. They fall to their segmented knees, pressing their faces against the wood, humming frantically to sync their internal rhythm with the thump-whirr of the sap-engines. 

The temporal delays break them. They are creatures who depend on the cause-and-effect of the press, the stamp, the flow. When they see the press fall, and hear the boom a second later, and feel the vibration five minutes in the past, their minds fracture. They stand frozen in the boulevards, caught in a temporal eddy, their arms raised to catch a panel that was stamped an hour ago, or will be stamped in a year. Some of them catch shadows that do not belong to them; they stare in mute, whistling terror at a shadow that raises an arm while they stand perfectly still, recognizing in the silhouette the ghost of the animal they once were.

At the sparse, open edges of the plateau, where the wind scours the clouds and the sky peels away, the Arborund do not look out. They turn their backs to the void. They know what lies beyond the bruised purple atmosphere. They have seen the knotted roots and the industrial piping that comprises the negative space of the heavens. They know that the lung they inhabit is inside something else, something vast and metallic and terminally ill.

And so they work. They work with a desperate, furious rhythm, stamping and pressing and grafting, plugging their root-cables into the bulging cisterns, drinking the rain that smells of wet ash and cold iron. They grind on, pumping sap into a sky that is slowly forgetting how to be solid, attempting to out-build the erosion of reality itself, terrified that if they ever stop moving, the god will finally exhale, and they will be nothing but the final, fading echo in the hollow of its chest.
