Something lives in the corbelled hut. Not merely the occasional hare seeking residual warmth, but the one who stacked the schist, who chipped the basalt, who left the axe beside the door. They have not departed. They are simply not here *now*—and in this place of frantic cycles, absence and presence hold the same weight.

Call them the settling. They came with the first rush of life, drawn perhaps by the same desperate hunger that pulls the moss into fissures and drives the flowers to seed before frost can steal their chance. They are not explorers, not pilgrims, not seeking summit or vista. They are *inhabitants* in the oldest sense—dwellers, nesters, creatures who look at raw stone and see the shape of a home.

Their body has learned this place in the way of all things here: quickly, completely, at a pace that defies expectation. Their skin is the pale granite of the escarpments, wind-burned to a darkness that shadows into schist where the sun does not reach—the inner arms, the ribs' cave, the soft hollow of throat. Their hands are something apart: massive-knuckled, broad-palmed, fingers thick as the roots of the alpine fescue that pry apart rock. These are the hands that stacked the hut, feeling each flat stone for balance, for the slight jut that locks it against its neighbor. The fingernails are worn to crescents, cracked, rimed with perpetual frost and the mineral grit that never fully washes away. When they press a palm to the morning stone, they feel the cold not as temperature but as *information*—the cold tells them where the moisture gathers, where the frost will heave, where the rock is still settling and might betray a shelter with a sudden crack.

They are not tall. The hut told you that before I did: chest-high, a trilithon arch that demands you fold yourself, bow, make yourself small to enter. They have folded so long that the posture has become architecture; their spine curves with a deliberate hunch, not deformity but *compression*, as if they have become denser to match the thinning air. Their lungs are vast relative to their frame—their chest broad, their breathing slow and deep like the mountain's own cold exhale. They can hold breath for minutes, lying motionless on a ledge while the snow leopard passes below, and the leopard will not see them because their stillness matches the stone and their coat—woven from the shed underwool of tahr, the downy feathers of snow finches—is the grey-white-moss of the terrain itself.

They are old. Or perhaps they are young and merely aged. In this place where flowers bloom and die in weeks, where water cycles through its phases in a single day, time presses with a weight that belies the thin atmosphere. A season here is an epoch. A year is a geology. They came with the first blush of moss, they built before the schist could learn to crumble, and every day since has been a fury of survival that etches itself into the body: the deep lines around the eyes from blinding light, the creases at the corners of the mouth from breathing cold so dry it steals moisture with every exhale. Their lips are often cracked, their eyes often narrowed to slits against the brilliance—but when they open those eyes fully, you see something startling: an iris the exact sapphire of the wildflowers, vivid and incongruous as the magenta blooms themselves. It is the only color on them that does not belong to rock or lichen or bone. It belongs to the *life* of this place—the urgent, inappropriate, hungry life that rushed into the void and refused to wait for time.

They have no name that matters, but if you need a word, call them *Fissure*—after the cracks they inhabit, the cracks they exploit, the way their own being has split and healed and split again in the cold. They do not speak. Sound dies here; they learned that early, perhaps in the first hour after they arrived and called out to the amphitheater and received nothing but the hollow swallow of the void. They have not spoken since, and their vocal cords, if they still exist, have frozen into thin, useless strings. They communicate with the mountain through the press of a palm, the shift of a foot, the slow blink of sapphire eyes against the surgical light. They listen with their whole body: the feel of frost forming on their shoulder tells them the afternoon will be clear; the ache in their left knee predicts the seep of moisture; the sudden silence of the finches warns of the leopard before it appears on the ridge.

They eat what the mountain offers, which is always barely enough. Tahr that have fallen—the young, the old, the ones who misjudged a geometric drop. They do not hunt; they scavenge, and the hand-axe is for breaking into the marrow bones that the snow leopard leaves behind. The marrow is golden, warm for a moment, and it tastes of the grasses the tahr grazed, of the damp that clings to the moss, of the stone itself. They also eat the wildflowers, pinching the vivid heads between cracked fingers, feeling the crunch of frost on the petals. They eat the tender centers of the fescue tufts. They lap at the temporary rivulets with a tongue that has grown rough as a cat's, pulling the cold water from the stone before the void can swallow it. They have learned to drink frost—pressing their mouth to the crystalline rinds, letting the warmth of their breath melt just enough to wet the throat. They are never sated. Hunger is the constant companion, the engine, the *hurry* that drives them as it drives every living thing in this cirque.

They are not alone, though they are solitary. The snow leopard is their neighbor, their rival, their silent partner in the ballet. They watch each other across the distances—the leopard on its ridge, Fissure on a ledge near the hut, both of them still, both of them patient, both of them waiting for the same tahr to stumble. Sometimes Fissure finds a kill before the leopard returns, and they work quickly with the basalt axe, prying loose the organs, the thick hide, the hindquarters that will dry in the blinding afternoon. They leave the rest—the bones, the head, the scraps of sinew—and the leopard returns to find its larder diminished and accepts this with the pragmatism of a creature that has never known abundance. They do not fight. There is no sound to a fight here, no echo to give it weight, and so the conflict exists only in timing, in who arrives first, in the subtle shift of territory as one cedes a ledge to the other. Sometimes, in the coldest hours before dawn, they share the same space—the leopard inside the hut, curled on the stone floor, and Fissure outside, pressed against the boulder that backs the shelter, separated by a wall of schist but sharing the residual warmth of two bodies that have learned to survive the same killing cold.

The finches know them. When Fissure emerges from the hut in the first light, a blizzard of grey and white erupts from the crevices nearby, and a few bold birds land on their shoulders, picking at the dried blood under their fingernails, the frost in their eyebrows, the seeds caught in the weave of their coat. This is not affection; it is the same hunger that drives everything here, the same desperate consumption of whatever protein or moisture can be found. But Fissure holds still for them, lets them peck and pluck, and there is something in this patience that looks—across the gulf of species, of silence, of cold—like gratitude.

The hares are more cautious. Fissure has trapped them before, setting snares of braided fescue in the paths between moss cushions, feeling for the runs where the pale bodies bound with their urgent, soundless twitches. But more often they simply watch the hares, studying the way their coats shift color, the way their bodies compress and extend, the way they die—suddenly, violently, in the jaws of the leopard or the talons of an eagle or simply from the cold that seeps into their bones on a night when the moisture freezes and does not thaw. Fissure has held a dying hare, felt the last heartbeat stutter against their palm, and then eaten it raw, still warm, and wept—not from grief, but from the sheer intensity of warmth entering a body that has forgotten what warmth feels like.

They do not think of the world beyond the cirque. There may be one; they may have come from it. But memory, like sound, dies here. The thin air cannot hold it. What remains is the hut, the axe, the routine: check the snares, break the frost, lap the rivulets, watch the leopard, press the palm to stone and *listen*. Sleep in the hut when the cold becomes too much, curled in a nest of dried fescue and shed tahr wool, breathing the smell of crushed ferns and ancient dust and the faint metallic tang of the schist. Wake when the light hits the east-facing door, a blade of brilliance that cuts across the floor and touches their closed eyelids like a warning, like a call to prayer.

And this is the thing: there *is* something like prayer in them. Not to a god—they have no gods, no stories, no language for the unseen—but to the mountain itself, the raw, young, violent thing that rose and made space for them. They pray by *paying attention*. They stand in the blinding light with their eyes narrowed to sapphire slits and they watch: the moss growing, the flowers blooming, the tahr leaping, the frost forming, the water weeping, the rock—so newly exposed, still learning to be ancient—shifting almost imperceptibly as it settles into its bones. They pay attention because inattention here is death, and they pay attention because the furious rush of existence in this newborn peak is the most beautiful thing they have ever witnessed, even if they have no word for beauty.

They know the place is wrong. Too young, too full, too fast. They feel it in the way the stone still moves, the way the moisture cannot decide its state, the way the leopard watches them with eyes that say: *this will not last*. The mountain will round. The cliffs will crumble. The amphitheater will fill with the debris of its own erosion, and the sharp drops will soften into slopes, and the snow line will rise, and the tahr will move higher or die, and the moss will give way to soil, and soil to meadow, and meadow to forest, and the cathedral will become a valley, and the silence will be broken by the sound of water *gathering*, becoming streams, becoming rivers, carrying the mountain away one grain at a time.

Fissure knows this the way they know the frost will come: not as thought, but as ache. The same ache in the knee that predicts the seep, the same crack in the fingernail that speaks of the dry cold, the same pressure in the chest that says *hurry*. They are racing the mountain's erosion. Every day, they build and rebuild the hut—the schist shifts, the capstone cracks, the arch sags, and they replace, restack, readjust with their massive-knuckled hands. Every day, they chip the axe a little sharper, using the granite itself as a whetstone. Every day, they eat and drink and breathe the cold, dry air and feel the world pouring through them like the temporary rivulets that drip into the void before they can gather.

They will not leave. The place has taken them as it has taken the moss, the flowers, the beasts. They are part of the juxtaposition now, the wrongness, the life that rushed into the void before the void was ready. When the mountain finally settles—when the rock rounds, the edges soften, the sound returns, the emptiness fills—they will be gone. Not dead, perhaps, but transformed, dispersed, their minerals returned to the schist, their moisture to the frost, their warmth to the cold exhale of the world.

But that is geology away. For now, they are here. They crouch in the hut at night, listening to the stone settle, and in the morning they emerge into the blinding light and watch the snow finches flow across the sky and the tahr pick across the scree and the leopard sit in its pool of white, and they press their palm to the frost and feel the mountain *thinking*, young and violent and full of the same desperate hunger that burns in their own sapphire eyes.

This is who lives here: not a person, not quite, but a *becoming*—a creature still settling into its bones alongside the newborn peak, as raw and urgent and temporary as the wildflowers, as the frost, as the water that cannot decide whether to freeze or weep. They are the inhabitant of the edge, the dweller in the fissure, the one who came when the mountain called and will leave when the mountain no longer needs them.

And the axe beside the door? They left it there deliberately. Not in abandonment, but in *faith*—the faith that they will return for it, that the mountain will still be here, that the furious, quiet, freezing rush of existence will continue for another day, another cycle, another breath of the cold, crystalline air.

They always return for the axe.
