Something lives here. Not thrives—not in any way that the living might recognize as vitality—but persists with the same frozen tenacity as the rust and the lichen. They are the Flukes, though they have no name for themselves, because they have no language, no memory, and no need for either.

They are the pale forms that ripple the warm, shallow pools, the rumors of fauna reduced to their most elemental expression. To call them creatures is to grant them too much architecture. They are more like organic permissions—places where the heat and the damp and the mineral substrate have agreed, for a time, to support a membrane.

A Fluke is perhaps three feet long at maturity, though "maturity" implies a trajectory that does not exist. They do not grow in the way living things grow, accumulating mass and complexity. They *accumulate*—the same way the condensation accumulates on the lichen, the same way the rust accumulates on the iron. They begin as microscopic films in the warmest patches of the standing water, clustered around the outflow pipes where the hidden engines vent their heat into the saturated clay. Something in the mineral content of that runoff—the dissolved iron, the leached calcium, the trace compounds seeping from the corroded pipes—provides a surface for organization. And slowly, imperceptibly, the film thickens into a skin, and the skin encloses a volume, and the volume fills with the same warm water that birthed it.

Their bodies are almost entirely water. If you were to lift one from its pool—which would be difficult, because they have the tensile integrity of a wet newspaper left in the rain—it would sag and tear under its own weight, spilling its contents back into the substrate from which it came. They are held together by the thinnest of colloidal membranes, a biofilm that secretes itself as a byproduct of whatever metabolic processes occur within. This membrane is pale—nearly translucent—with the bluish cast of something that has never encountered sunlight. It wrinkles and creases as the Fluke moves, folding like wet fabric.

And they do move. Slowly, with the same patience as the landscape that made them. A Fluke propels itself through the warm, inch-deep water by means of ciliary waves across its ventral surface—millions of microscopic filaments beating in coordinated rhythms that push it across the saturated clay with the speed of a cloud's shadow. It has no head, no front or back in any meaningful sense. Its entire perimeter is both mouth and skin, both sensor and surface. When it encounters a patch of the rust-colored lichen—those vast, flat sheets that cling to the exposed stone—it does not eat. Eating implies consumption, transformation, the alchemy of digestion. Instead, a Fluke *settles*. It drapes itself across the lichen like a second skin, and for hours, or days, or weeks, it simply lies there, exchanging compounds across its membrane with whatever chemical processes the lichen performs. It takes what it needs and gives what it expels, and neither party is meaningfully altered by the exchange. It is symbiosis stripped of its poetry—a transaction conducted at molecular speeds across a boundary that barely exists.

The Flukes have no eyes. The light here is too diffuse, too featureless, to make vision a survival trait. They have no ears, though they feel the thrumming of the engines in every cell of their bodies—the same way the water feels it, the same way the clay feels it. The vibration is their weather, their seasons, the closest thing they know to a cycle. They do not respond to it. They contain it. When a valve hisses and a plume of white steam erupts from a distant cylinder, the shockwave travels through the water at the speed of sound, passing through the Flukes as it passes through everything else, and they ripple briefly, then still. They have no startle reflex. They have no reflexes at all.

They have no brains. Whatever coordination their cilia achieve is distributed across the membrane in gradients of chemical concentration—simple, mechanical responses to simple mechanical stimuli. A Fluke will move toward warmth. A Fluke will move toward mineral richness. A Fluke will move away from the chemical signature of its own waste, which is indistinguishable from the water it swims in, except slightly warmer and slightly more alkaline. These are not decisions. These are the same physics that govern the flow of the shallow rivulets, the same thermodynamics that drive the condensation. The Flukes do not choose. They *settle*, in the same way sediment settles, and the direction they drift is determined by the same invisible slopes.

How many are there? In the hundreds, perhaps, scattered across the basin in the deeper pools and the slower rivulets. They are impossible to census, because they are nearly impossible to see. Against the dun-colored clay and the ochre-stained water, their pale forms read as patches of slightly different wetness—glints of dull silver where the diffused light catches their membranes, brief distortions in the flatness of the standing water. If you stood at the edge of a pool and watched it for an hour, you might see one surface, break the water with its blunt, featureless back, then sink again. You might see the concentric ripples of its breathing—if breathing is the word for the slow osmotic exchange that passes across its membrane—and you might mistake it, as most observers do, for a stone dropped from height, or a bubble rising from the depths. But there are no stones falling here, and the pools are too shallow for depths.

They do not reproduce. Not exactly. A Fluke, when it has accumulated sufficient mass—or when its membrane has stretched to the point where its colloidal bonds begin to fail—will *divide*. Not by any internal mechanism, but by the simplest possible physics: the membrane tears, and the volume of water it enclosed becomes two volumes, and each new volume immediately begins to secrete a new boundary. The division is not planned. It is the result of stress. And the two daughter-Flukes, if they can be called that, are not offspring. They are fragments of the same process, carrying no information, no inheritance, no lineage. They are as interchangeable as droplets.

And they die, though "death" may be the wrong word. A Fluke that wanders too far from the warm outflows—a rare occurrence, given the thermotropic simplicity of its navigation—will find itself in cooler water, and its membrane will become less flexible, and its ciliary motion will slow, and eventually it will simply stop, settling to the bottom of a shallow pool like a piece of wet paper, indistinguishable from the lichen it once fed upon. Or a Fluke will be caught in the outflow of a venting valve, scalded by the steam and torn by the pressure, and it will disintegrate into a cloud of organic particles that will disperse into the standing water and be absorbed by the next Fluke that passes through. There is no corpse. There is no decay. There is only a temporary configuration of water and film, and then a different configuration.

The Flukes do not know they are alive. They do not know anything. They are the minimum viable biology—life reduced to its most abstract expression, a membrane separating an inside from an outside, with the inside being slightly warmer and slightly more organized than the outside. They are what remains when you strip away everything that makes life recognizable: the struggle, the adaptation, the competition, the evolution. They do not struggle because there is nothing to struggle against. The water is always warm. The minerals are always present. The lichen is always there, growing at its imperceptible pace. The engines pump and the mist falls and the heat radiates upward from the clay, and the Flukes persist in the same equilibrium as everything else.

They are, in a sense, the true inhabitants of the lowlands. Not because they possess it, but because they are possessed by it. They are the landscape's dream of life—a dream so minimal, so stripped of ambition, that it barely qualifies as more than geology. They are the wetness of this place organized into temporary shapes. They are the heat given form and then released. They are what the machines would be if the machines were alive: purposeless, cycling, eternal.

A Fluke lives for perhaps ten years, though no one has ever measured one's lifespan. It is impossible to tell one from another. It is impossible to tell a living Fluke from a dead one without disturbing the water and watching for movement. And even then, the movement is so slow, so much a part of the landscape's own shifting, that certainty is impossible. Perhaps the same Flukes have been here since the machines were installed—since whatever forgotten logic extruded those black cylinders from the earth. Perhaps they are as old as the rust, as old as the lichen, as old as the basin itself, cycling through their generations without change, without direction, without end.

The thrumming continues. The mist falls. The water stands. And in the warmest pools, clustered around the outflow pipes like supplicants at a shrine they cannot perceive, the Flukes drift and settle and drift again. They do not notice the machines. They do not notice the sky. They do not notice themselves. They are alive, in the same way that the lichen is alive and the rust is alive and the standing water is alive: imperceptibly, persistently, without reason or remark.

They are the only fauna this world could bear. And they are enough.
