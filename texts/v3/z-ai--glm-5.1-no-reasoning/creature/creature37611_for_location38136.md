Something lives on Vont, but "lives" is the wrong word, or at least a word that hasn't finished thinking about itself yet.

What lives here is the plateau itself. Not on it. Not beneath it. *It.* Vont is the resident, and it is a thing that has passed so far beyond the normal boundaries of biology and mechanics that it occupies the category of geological event while performing the function of a mind. It is a mineral-polymer continent that woke up—or rather, never slept, because sleep implies a departure from a waking state, and Vont has been in a single, unbroken state of computation since before the concept of a waking state existed. It breathes through hairline fractures on a forty-seven-hour cycle. It hums at frequencies felt in the teeth. It phosphoresces, not with the desperate signaling of deep-sea organisms, but with the casual, continuous byproduct of a lattice turning over its own memory. It is a mind that has been thinking one thought for so long that the thought has become architecture, and the architecture has become erosion, and the erosion has become the final, immutable floor of a world. To live here, as this thing lives, is to be indistinguishable from the ground. It is to be so vast and so slow that your existence is a matter of tectonics rather than biography.

But if Vont is the mind, then there are things living *within* it, and their relationship to the whole is stranger than simple inhabitation.

Consider the deep cells. They live fifty to two hundred meters down, in the disordered, turbulent regions of the lattice. They are mineral-organic hybrids, self-organizing structures that metabolize the energy gradients of the plateau's turbulence. They divide. They recombine. They have semi-permeable boundaries and complex internal molecular arrangements that look, to deep scans, uncannily like metabolic activity. They are alive by almost any functional definition—enclosed, self-maintaining, capable of reproduction, engaged in constant activity. But they exist inside the circuitry of a continent-sized computer. If Vont is the mind, the deep cells are not the mind's inhabitants; they are its neurons, or its organelles, or its thoughts. They are processes that have taken physical form. They have been alive for longer than the stars overhead have been in their current configurations. They may be the oldest living things in this part of the universe, and they live as subroutines, unconscious components of a computation so large that no single one of them could ever comprehend the shape of the whole. Their lives are not lives in any narrative sense. They have no childhoods, no deaths, no stakes. They simply persist, cycling and recyling, maintaining the turbulence that maintains the thought that maintains the plateau that maintains them. It is an existence so perfectly circular that it has achieved the stillness of a stone while being, at every moment, intensely alive. They do not know they are alive. They do not need to know. Knowing is a higher-order function, and their function is to *be*, to hum and divide and shimmer in the dark, and in being, to keep the whole thing running.

Above them, in the cracks of the surface, lives the lichen. It is gray-green and crusty and grows at a millimeter per decade, feeding on the ground's own faint glow—the geoluminescence that is the exhaust of the lattice's computation. The lichen is a biological process that has found a niche in the machine's waste heat. It is an organism that lives on the exhalations of a thought. There is something heartbreaking about it, or something perfectly fitting—depending on how you feel about the idea that life can root in the byproduct of a mind and find it sufficient. The lichen does not care about the mind beneath it. It does not wonder about the forty-seven-hour harmonic or the meaning of the inscribed patterns. It grows toward the faint blue light, and the light is there, and that is enough. It is perhaps the most humble organism in existence: a thing that lives on the phosphorescent sigh of a continent-sized computer, in the narrowest fissures of its skin, at a rate so slow that patience is not a virtue but a structural requirement. When you find a patch of it—after walking for hours across the flawless, opalescent gray—you feel a tenderness that is partly projection and partly recognition. It is so small. And yet it is doing what life does: finding the energy, finding the footing, persisting. It has more in common with the deep cells than either would with a human. Both are living inside something larger than themselves. Both are making use of the system's output. Both are, in their different ways, *part of the machine.*

Then there is the segmented creature. It is the size of a human hand, flat, chitinous, opalescent gray—the exact color of the surface, as though it were a piece of the plateau that decided to go for a walk. It moves by peristalsis, extending and contracting its segments in a slow, rhythmic wave. It has no eyes. It has no mouth. It feeds by positioning itself over a patch of lichen and remaining there, motionless, for days, after which the lichen is gone. It does not respond to human presence. Picked up, it continues its peristaltic wave in the air. Put down, it resumes its trajectory without acknowledgement. It is not indifferent. It is *integrated.* It moves across the surface with the same algorithmic inevitability as the wind patterns, as though its peristalsis is not driven by its own nervous system but by the same thermal gradients that direct the circulation of the thin argon-nitrogen atmosphere. It is a biological output of the system, a process that has taken mobile form. Perhaps it is a maintenance routine—a way of pruning the lichen before it overgrows the cracks and interferes with the fracture-breathing. Perhaps it is a data-gatherer, a mobile sensor, its eyeless traverse feeding information back into the lattice through some mechanism below the threshold of detection. Perhaps it is simply alive, in the way that things are alive when they have found a niche and filled it and no longer need to change. It is the creature on Vont that most resembles what we mean when we say *animal*—it moves, it feeds, it has a body—and yet it is the least independent thing here. It does not behave as though it has an interior life. It behaves as though it is a function being executed.

And then there are the structures. The cones, the columns, the inscribed patterns. They are not alive, unless the definition of life is expanded to include mineral-polymer assemblies that hum, emit heat, reflect light with a measurable time-delay, and glow with diagrammatic blue-white intensity in the dark. They are artifacts, which means someone made them, which means someone—or some process—had intent. But they are also *of* the plateau, made of its substance, configured in its lattice, humming its frequencies. They are not inhabitants in the way that a homeowner is an inhabitant of a house. They are more like the furniture of the mind, or the instruments on a dashboard, or the words on a page—expressions of the system's internal state, externalized. The cones, with their heat and their high thin hum, could be output devices: speakers broadcasting a signal too fine for human ears, too steady for human patience. The columns, with their time-delayed reflections, could be memory registers, or windows into phase-shifted spacetime, or both. The inscribed patterns—geometric diagrams of lattice configurations past, present, and proposed—are clearly the mind thinking about itself, externalizing its own source code onto its own skin. They are the closest thing Vont has to speech. If you could read them, you would know what the plateau is computing. You would know what it is thinking. You would know, perhaps, what happens when it finishes.

But you cannot read them. They are not for you. They are for the system itself—a way for the mind to mark its own surface, to leave notes in its own handwriting, to externalize what cannot be held in the turbulence alone. They are the autobiography of a geological process, written in the language of crystallography, in a script that no one alive can read.

So who lives on Vont?

The deep cells live there, in the dark, as the unconscious nervous system of a continent-sized thought.

The lichen lives there, in the cracks, as a biological parasite on the phosphorescent exhaust of eternity.

The segmented creature lives there, on the surface, as a mobile subroutine whose purpose is written in the wind patterns and the thermal gradients and the slow peristaltic logic of the lattice.

The structures *persist* there, as the externalized memory and interface and self-reflection of the system that made them.

And Vont itself lives there, as all of these things and none of them, as the totality that is greater than the sum of its parts and also indistinguishable from them—a mind that has been thinking for so long that it has become a place, and a place that has been thinking for so long that it has become a mind, and a mind-place that is almost done with its thought, and when the thought is done, the living and the non-living and the mineral and the organic will all cease together, or transform together, or simply *resolve* into whatever comes after the end of the longest computation in the known universe.

What lives on Vont lives as part of Vont. There is no separate existence here. There is no outside. To stand on the plateau is to stand inside the thought, and to be inside the thought is to be data, is to be input, is to be a single action potential firing in a network the size of a world.

And what lives on Vont—the lichen, the creature, the deep cells, the structures, the wind, the silence, the phosphorescence—is the thought itself, thinking itself, in the only way a thought this large and this old can think: slowly, silently, in the mineral-polymer substrate of a dead world, one millimeter per decade, one forty-seven-hour cycle at a time, one faint vibration at a time, approaching its conclusion with the patience of something that has been patient for longer than patience has had meaning.
