The ones who live here are not residents. They are a hesitation.

Call them the Tévarrans, though they left no writing to confirm it, no symbol carved into the porphyry, no name scratched beside the cistern. They are present in the basin only as an echo of intention—the straight channel, the evenly spaced nodes of dry-stacked walls, the careful routing of water into a hand-carved bowl. And then: absence. A held breath in the stone.

They were few. Five structures, small and mean, speak to perhaps a dozen hands at work. A family group, maybe. An extended kinship cluster seeking something specific from this raw place. They arrived with stone tools and the knowledge of how to break porphyry along its grain, and they spent days or weeks splitting blocks from the basin's edge, carrying them to the northern platform, stacking them with a patience that outpaced their skill. They did not build for comfort. They built for function. Low walls to break the wind that must sometimes funnel through the basin's opening. Defined spaces to keep things separate—sleeping from working, wet from dry, the living from the dead.

They did not stay long. The moss in the joints of their walls has not been disturbed, which means they left before it could be trampled or scraped away. They built the cistern and then they left, and the cistern filled and overflowed, and no one came back to drink from it.

What were they seeking? The water, perhaps. A place in the raw world where water emerged clean and cold and constant, water that had not yet passed through the filters of soil and root and time, water that was as new as the stone itself. There is a quality to the water in the basin—a minerality so intense it tastes like the inside of the earth, like the moment of creation, like the first sip of something that has never been drunk before. Perhaps they needed that. Perhaps their old water was tainted, or their old wells had failed, or their old world had become too worn and weathered and they needed a place where things were still beginning.

Or perhaps they were seekers of a different kind. The sound of the basin is the sound of a cathedral, and cathedrals were built for worship. The Tévarrans may have come to this ringing, raw place because it felt like the antechamber of something vast and unformed—a place where the world was still being decided, where the boundary between what-is and what-might-be was thin enough to see through. They may have sat in their small, poor, roofless structures and listened to the water-organ and felt themselves at the edge of creation, at the moment just after the wound opened, and they may have believed that proximity to that moment gave them access to something—power, perhaps, or knowledge, or simply the permission to begin again.

But they left. The basin does not tolerate permanence. It is too raw, too cold, too loud. The sound that enters the chest like a cathedral organ does not soothe; it overwhelms. After days or weeks of that resonance, of waking to it and sleeping—if sleep is even possible—within it, the body begins to vibrate at the frequency of the water. The mind follows. The Tévarrans would have dreamed of seeps and threads and braids of water. They would have felt the damp in their lungs, the mineral taste in their throats, the cold settling into their bones. They would have watched the ferns uncurling and the moss spreading and the single birch leaning into nothing, and they would have understood that they were not pioneers here—they were visitors. The real work of the basin was being done by things with slower metabolisms and simpler desires.

So they left, and their structures remain as a kind of question. Not *who were we*, but *what did we hope for*. The basin does not answer. It does not even acknowledge the question. The walls stand, low and mossy, and the cistern fills and empties and fills again, and the channel runs with water no one has directed, and the sound changes, slowly, as new seeps open and old channels close, and the world makes itself in the wet and the cold and the light that comes from everywhere and nowhere, and the emptiness between things is not loneliness but simply—the space that the basin requires to become what it has not yet decided to be.

The Tévarrans are gone. But they are also still here, in the way that a question is still here after the questioner has left the room. The basin holds their hesitation like it holds the water—without effort, without judgment, without any particular awareness that it is holding anything at all.
