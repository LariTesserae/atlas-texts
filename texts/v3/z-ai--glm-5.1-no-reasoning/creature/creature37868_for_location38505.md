# The Keepers of the Cloud-Stratum

They do not have a name for themselves. Or if they do, it is not a word but a pressure, a subsonic pulse that rolls through the stone and the plant mat and the fog itself—a signature vibration that says *I am here* and *the ledge is sound* and *the floater path is shifting eastward today*. They are the climbers, the six-limbed hunters of the vertical dark, and they have been here since before the gneiss learned its current shape.

---

## The Body They Have Chosen

Consider the problem: you must live on a surface that is not a surface. You must walk on walls, sleep on overhangs, hunt in an air column bisected by fog and threaded with prey that has renegotiated the terms of buoyancy. You must do this in cold that kills flatland animals in hours, in darkness that renders eyes nearly useless, in air so thin that each breath is a small surrender. You must do this forever, because there is nowhere else to go.

The solution is elegant in the way that only solutions refined by a million generations can be elegant. The keepers are not large—a male in his prime weighs perhaps twelve kilograms, a female slightly more during breeding season when she carries the thickened fat layer that will sustain her through the fasting periods. Their bodies are low and long, stretched along a spine that flexes like a spring, each segment articulated independently so that the animal can conform to the contours of the rock as water conforms to a channel. The fur is dense, double-layered, so thick that when you press your hand into it you meet resistance before you reach skin—an insulating fortress against the cold, tipped with guard hairs that are not hairs at all but fine hollow tubes, like the quills of a porcupine rendered soft, that trap a layer of still air against the body and shed the condensation that would otherwise soak through.

The color is the color of the rock: deep blue-gray, shading to plum at the extremities, with a banding pattern that mirrors the gneiss so precisely that a keeper at rest on the stone is invisible at a distance of three meters. They are not camouflaged in the way that a flatland predator is camouflaged—striped to disappear in grass, spotted to vanish in dappled shade—but in a more thorough way, as if their pigment has been calibrated to the mineralogy of their territory. Each population of keepers, isolated on its own section of cliff, shades slightly differently than the others. The keepers of the northern gorge, where the quartz veins are whiter, carry a frost at their temples that the southern keepers lack. The keepers near the great overhang called the Wave have a warmer undertone, a rust that matches the iron oxide leaching from that particular formation. They are of the mountain in a way that goes beyond habitat; they are *made from* the mountain, their bodies a biological expression of its geology.

The legs—there are six, yes, though the foremost pair serves double duty as manipulators and can be folded against the chest when not needed for locomotion. Each leg ends in a pad of remarkable complexity: a soft, concave disk rimmed with articulated hooks that can engage or disengage independently, creating a grip that works on the rough plant mat and the glass-smooth quartz veins alike. The pads are warm. They are always warm, kept so by a specialized circulation that shunts blood to the extremities in a countercurrent arrangement so efficient that a keeper can stand on ice for hours without frostbite. This warmth is also a tool: pressed against the rock, it causes the microscopic film of condensation to evaporate, creating a temporary vacuum seal that holds the animal in place through the physics of adhesion rather than mechanical gripping. A keeper at rest on an overhang is not holding on. A keeper at rest on an overhang has *become part of the ceiling*, its weight distributed across six sealed pads, its body hanging with the ease of a coat on a hook.

The forelimbs, when deployed, are hands. Not human hands—there are too many fingers, and the fingers have too many joints, and the joints bend in directions that would make a surgeon uncomfortable—but hands nonetheless, capable of fine manipulation, of peeling back the plant mat to reach the grubs beneath, of arranging the nesting material, of grooming a companion's fur with a delicacy that seems at odds with the harshness of the environment. The keepers use these hands to build nothing permanent. They have no architecture, no constructed shelters. Their homes are the homes the mountain provides: crevices, overhangs, the flat places where one ledge meets another at an angle that creates shelter. They do not modify these spaces except by wearing them smooth over generations, the same pads that grip the rock also polishing it, so that the oldest nesting sites have a gloss like old wood, a patina of occupation.

The head is broad and flat, built close to the body, with a profile that presents minimal wind resistance when the keeper is gliding. The eyes are large—not enormous, but large enough to gather what little light exists—and they are positioned not forward like a predator's but laterally, giving the keeper a field of vision that extends nearly three hundred and forty degrees. There is a blind spot, directly behind and above, and the keepers are aware of this blind spot in the way that they are aware of everything about their environment: they never rest with their back to open air. The eyes themselves are dark, reflecting the bioluminescence of the plant mat as a faint red eyeshine that the flatland observer might mistake for a trick of the darkness. The pupils are horizontal slits that can open wide enough to nearly swallow the iris when the light is at its lowest, and they adjust continuously, not in the stepwise fashion of a cat's pupil but in a smooth dilation that tracks the shifting brightness of the fog.

The mouth is small, the teeth specialized: incisors for severing the stems of the hanging plants, canines for the kill, molars that are not quite molars but something between grinding and shearing, adapted to the particular toughness of floater tissue, which has the consistency of dense cartilage and must be worked thoroughly before swallowing. The keepers do not eat the plant mat. They do not eat the fungi. They eat floaters, and they eat the chitinous insects that live in the mat, and occasionally—very occasionally, when the fog brings them—a kind of aerial crustacean that drifts up from the lower gorge in the same way that the floaters do, its body a delicate lattice of chitin and gas bladder that collapses with a small sad sound when the keeper's jaws close around it.

And the membrane. This is what makes them what they are. Between each pair of legs, stretched from the wrist-equivalent to the ankle-equivalent, is a fold of skin that lies flat against the body when the keeper is climbing, so thin as to be invisible, but that can be unfurled in an instant when the keeper launches itself into the air. The membrane is not a wing. It provides no propulsion. But it provides *control*—the ability to glide, to steer, to arrest a fall, to extend a leap from three meters to thirty, to ride the updrafts that rise from the gorge when the fog begins its evening ascent. A keeper in full glide is a thing of unsettling beauty, its body a dark star against the green luminescence of the cliff walls, its six legs splayed, its membranes catching the faint light, turning and banking with adjustments so small that the mechanism is invisible, dropping toward a floater with an accuracy that suggests it can see the air currents themselves.

They land feet-first, always. The pads absorb the impact. The membrane folds. The hunt is over.

## The Mind They Carry

The keepers are intelligent. This is not a conjecture; it is a fact as evident as the warmth of their foot-pads, as certain as the precision of their strike. But their intelligence is not human intelligence, not the kind that solves abstract problems or builds tools or worries about the future in the way that humans worry. Their intelligence is *spatial*—an intelligence of position and trajectory and surface, an understanding of the vertical world that is as intuitive to them as walking on flat ground is to us.

They know their territories. A keeper can patrol the same section of cliff for a decade and never exhaust its knowledge of that section. Every ledge, every overhang, every handhold, every route from one point to another, every place where the plant mat is thin enough to grip the rock beneath, every place where the quartz vein provides a better purchase than the gneiss—this information is stored not as a map but as a *body memory*, a set of kinesthetic possibilities that the keeper carries in its muscles and its reflexes. A keeper does not think "I will go from here to there by this route." A keeper simply *goes*, its legs finding the path without conscious direction, the way a human hand finds the handle of a familiar door in the dark.

They communicate. The subsonic vibrations that you feel in your chest are their voices, and those voices carry information: the location of a floater aggregation, the presence of a rival on a shared border, the approach of the fog, the shifting of a loose stone that has made a particular route unreliable. The signals are not words but modulations—changes in frequency, in duration, in the pattern of pulses—that convey meaning through the same mechanism that music conveys emotion. A keeper hearing another keeper's signal does not translate it into language. It *feels* the signal, and the feeling contains the information, directly, without the intermediary of symbols.

They have relationships. A male and female will pair for life, their territories overlapping along the shared border, their hunts coordinated in a way that suggests not instinct but choice. They groom each other with those delicate forelimbs, working through the dense fur to remove parasites and debris, and they sleep curled together in the nesting crevice, their warmth shared, their breathing synchronized. When one dies—falling from a ledge, or succumbing to age, or simply failing to wake one morning from the cold stillness of sleep—the other changes. It stops patrolling the border. Its subsonic voice falls silent. It may leave the territory entirely, wandering into adjacent sections where it is not welcome, as if the geography of its home has become unbearable without the presence that gave it meaning. These wanderers do not last long. The cold takes them, or the hunger, or the claws of a territorial neighbor who does not recognize them and does not care.

They have memory. A keeper separated from its territory for any reason—a hunt that goes wrong, a fall that lands it on a lower ledge from which it must climb back—will find its way home with a certainty that is eerie. It does not retrace its steps. It does not follow a scent trail. It simply *knows* the direction, the way a migrating bird knows the direction, and it moves toward home with a determination that overrides hunger and exhaustion and injury. How it knows this is not understood. Perhaps the subsonic voice of its partner carries over long distances, a beacon that the keeper's body can detect even when its conscious mind cannot. Perhaps there is something in the rock itself—a vibration, a magnetic signature, a warmth pattern—that the keeper reads like a map. Perhaps home is not a location but a state, and the keeper's body recognizes its own territory the way a body recognizes its own heartbeat.

They have play. The young keepers—the kits, born in pairs, small and blind and furless, their six legs paddling in the air as if they are already trying to climb—engage in a behavior that can only be called play. They chase each other across the ledges. They practice their glides from low overhangs, landing badly, tumbling, trying again. They stalk the insects in the plant mat with an exaggerated seriousness that is, somehow, comic. They pounce on each other from hiding places, wrestling with a gentleness that belies the claws and teeth they are developing. This play is training, of course—every pounce is a rehearsal for the hunt, every glide a preparation for the strike—but it is also something more, something that has no survival value, that exists only because it is *enjoyable*, because the keepers are not survival machines but living beings who find pleasure in their bodies and their world.

## The Hunt

The floater runs are predictable in their timing but not in their location. The fog dictates where the floaters drift, and the fog is a creature of moods, its currents shifting with the temperature gradients and the rock-face thermals and whatever happens in the upper air, far above the cloud-stratum, where the keepers never go. A keeper must know the fog the way a sailor knows the sea: not as an obstacle but as a medium, a partner, an unreliable collaborator whose behavior can be anticipated but never trusted.

The hunt begins with waiting. The keeper positions itself on an overhang that it has used before—a launch point, a place where the rock juts out far enough to give a clear drop into the air column where the floaters pass. It presses its pads against the stone and becomes part of the overhang, its body invisible against the dark gneiss, its breathing slowed to a rate that barely sustains consciousness. It watches. It listens. It feels the subsonic murmur of the gorge, the low frequencies that carry information about the fog's movement, the floaters' positions, the presence of other hunters.

The floaters appear as shadows in the fog—darker shapes against the dark gray, moving with the slow pulse of their passage, their bodies catching the faint light and holding it briefly before releasing it. They drift in loose formations, their paths determined by air currents that the keeper can read but not see, and when they pass below the overhang, the keeper calculates the trajectory. This calculation is not mathematical. It is not even conscious. It is an integration of wind speed, fog density, floater velocity, and the keeper's own weight and gliding characteristics that happens in the body, not the mind, the way a basketball player calculates a shot—not with numbers but with an instinct honed by ten thousand attempts.

The launch is explosive. The keeper pushes off with all six legs simultaneously, the stored energy of its coiled spine releasing in a single contraction that hurls it outward and downward, its membranes snapping open, its legs spreading wide, its body suddenly transformed from a compact climbing machine into a gliding predator with the surface area of a kite. The air catches it. The keeper tilts, adjusts, banks—tiny movements of the legs and the membrane edges that redirect its fall toward the target. The floater may see it coming, may attempt to evade, but the floater's evasion is slow, a gradual change of direction that the keeper anticipated before it left the overhang.

Impact. The keeper's legs close around the floater's soft body, the pads sealing against the yielding surface, the claws engaging for grip where the pads cannot find purchase. The floater convulses, its internal bladders contracting, its body trying to compress and expel the keeper, but the keeper holds. It rides the floater downward, steering with its body weight, guiding the struggling prey toward the nearest ledge. The floater's pulse slows as its reserves deplete. The keeper waits. It does not kill in the air. It kills on the stone, where it can eat without falling, where the floater's body can be opened and consumed without waste.

The killing bite is precise: the canines entering at the junction of the floater's central bladder and its feeding array, severing the nerve concentration that serves the floater as a brain. Death is instant, or near enough that the floater's final contraction is a release rather than a spasm. The keeper feeds methodically, starting with the nutrient-dense tissue around the nerve cluster, working outward through the bladder walls and the filter membranes and the muscular ring that drives the floater's pulse. It eats everything that is edible. What remains—the chitinous framework that gives the floater its shape, the fragments of bladder that are too tough to chew—falls from the ledge into the gorge, descending through the fog until it reaches the lower levels where other organisms will find it and complete the recycling.

After feeding, the keeper grooms. This is essential; the floater's tissue contains compounds that are corrosive if left on the fur, and the keeper spends as much as an hour cleaning itself, licking its pads, working its forelimbs through the dense fur of its belly and flanks where the floater's fluids have splashed. Grooming is also a return to normalcy, a ritual that transitions the keeper from the intensity of the hunt back to the patient stillness of the wait. When the grooming is done, the keeper finds a crevice or an overhang, curls into a ball with its nose tucked against its belly and its membrane wrapped around its body like a blanket, and sleeps.

## The Knowing

The keepers know things that they should not know.

They know when a ledge is going to fail. Not by visual inspection—they do not examine the stone for cracks or listen for the subsonic groaning that precedes a collapse. They *feel* it, a wrongness in the rock that registers in their pads as a change in temperature, or texture, or some quality that has no name in any human language. A keeper will avoid a ledge for weeks before that ledge shows any sign of instability, and when the ledge finally falls—cracking along a fault line that was invisible to every other observer—the keeper will have already moved its nesting site to a new location, as if it had received advance notice.

They know the fog. Not as a weather pattern but as a presence, an entity with habits and preferences and occasional fits of unpredictability that the keepers navigate with a familiarity that borders on the personal. There are keepers who seem to communicate with the fog—or, rather, who seem to understand what the fog is saying. They stand on their ledges and pulse their subsonic voices into the mist, and the fog responds—not in any way that a human can measure, but in ways that the keepers can read, changing its density or its direction or its timing in response to the signal. Whether this is communication or simply the keepers' exploitation of atmospheric physics that they understand intuitively is a distinction that may not matter.

They know the mountain. This is the deepest knowing, the one that disturbs flatland observers the most. The keepers are aware of the water that moves through the horizontal cracks, the warm seeps that trace their slow paths across the cliff faces. They follow these seeps, not to drink from them (the water is too mineral, too warm, too *alive* with whatever microorganisms thrive in the mountain's circulation) but to monitor them, as if checking the flow of blood through a body they are responsible for. When a seep changes its path—when the water finds a new route through the rock—the keepers in that section of the cliff become agitated, their subsonic voices rising in frequency, their patrol patterns shifting to cover the area where the change occurred. They are not defending territory. They are *attending* to something, the way a nurse attends to a patient whose IV line has kinked.

And they know each other. Not as individuals—though they are individuals, with distinct personalities and preferences and habits—but as *parts of a whole*. The keepers of a given section of cliff are not a colony, not a pack, not a herd. They are something else, something for which the flatland vocabulary has no term. They are independent in their hunting, solitary in their daily routines, fiercely territorial against intruders. But they are also *connected*, their subsonic voices weaving a constant web of communication that covers the cliff face like the plant mat covers the stone, a living network through which information flows and decisions are made and the mountain's condition is monitored and maintained.

When a keeper dies, the network adjusts. The neighboring keepers expand their territories to cover the gap, their patrol routes shifting with a fluidity that suggests coordination rather than opportunism. When a new keeper is born, the network expands to include it, its subsonic voice—thin and reedy at first, gaining strength as it matures—adding its thread to the weave. The network has no center, no hierarchy, no leadership. It is a democracy of presence, a governance by participation, and it has operated continuously since the keepers first evolved, which—given the pace of change in the cloud-stratum—may have been longer than human civilization has existed.

## The Old Ones

There are keepers who have lived on the cliff for a very long time.

You cannot tell them by their size; they do not grow larger with age. You cannot tell them by their fur; it remains as dense and dark as ever, renewed by the constant shedding and regrowth that the cold demands. You cannot tell them by their movement; they are as agile as the young ones, their pads as sure, their glides as precise.

But you can tell them by their territories. The old ones hold the best sections of cliff—the ledges with the most reliable condensation, the overhangs nearest to the richest floater runs, the crevices that are deepest and warmest and most defensible. They have held these territories for years, for decades, and the rock beneath them shows the marks of their occupation: the polishing of their pads, the grooves worn by their claws at the entry to their nesting crevices, the faint discoloration of the plant mat where generations of their urine have altered the chemistry of the growth.

You can tell them by their silence. The old ones pulse less frequently than the young. Their subsonic voices are deeper, slower, more selective in what they choose to communicate. They do not waste their signals on routine information. When an old one speaks, the other keepers in range stop and listen, because whatever an old one is saying is worth hearing.

And you can tell them by what they do not do. The old ones do not hunt as often. They do not patrol as far. They spend more time in their nesting crevices, pressed against the warm rock, their ears tuned to frequencies that the young ones cannot hear. They are listening to the mountain. They are feeling the water move through the cracks. They are monitoring the pulse of the system that sustains them all, and they are waiting—not for death, not for something to happen, but for whatever it is that keepers wait for when they have lived long enough to know that waiting is its own form of knowledge.

The oldest keeper anyone has observed—a female on the eastern face of the northern gorge, in a territory centered on a massive overhang that the younger keepers call nothing at all, because it has always been hers—was identifiable only by the territory she held and the silence she kept. She did not hunt. She did not patrol. She emerged from her crevice each evening when the fog began to rise, moved to the edge of her overhang, and stood there for the entire night, her pads pressed against the stone, her eyes open, her body still, listening to the mountain breathe. In the morning, when the fog retreated, she returned to her crevice and slept.

The younger keepers brought her food. Not every night, not on any schedule that a human observer could discern, but regularly enough that she was sustained. They would kill a floater and carry a portion of it to the edge of her territory, leaving it on a particular ledge that she could reach without entering their space. She would collect it without acknowledgment, eat it without ceremony, and return to her listening. This was not kinship; she had no surviving offspring. This was not obligation; the younger keepers owed her nothing. This was something else—something that looked, from the outside, like reverence.

She died in her crevice, curled in the position she had slept in for however many decades she had lived. The younger keepers did not enter the crevice for a long time. When they finally did, they found the stone where she had lain was different—smoother, warmer, threaded with the same faint luminescence that marked the fungi. The plant mat in her territory had changed too, its blue-green deeper, its glow brighter, its water-holding capacity measurably greater than the mat in adjacent sections.

The territory did not stay empty for long. A young pair—sisters, born in the same litter—expanded their range to include the old one's overhang, and they moved into the crevice, and they slept where she had slept, and the stone was warm beneath them, and they dreamed whatever keepers dream.

## What They Are

The keepers are not the owners of the cloud-stratum. They are its *keepers* in the oldest sense of the word: those who keep, who hold, who maintain. They are the mobile component of a system that is mostly immobile, the part that can move between the fixed elements and carry information and energy and material from one place to another. They are the mountain's eyes and ears and hands, and they take this role not because it has been assigned to them but because the system has evolved to need it, because a vertical ecology this complex requires something that can navigate it, that can respond to changes, that can attend to failures and disruptions before they cascade into collapse.

They do not know this. Or perhaps they do. The distinction between knowing and being is less clear in the cloud-stratum than it is in the flatland world, where minds are imagined as separate from bodies and bodies as separate from environments. Here, in the vertical dark, the keepers are what they do, and what they do is *keep*: keep watch, keep track, keep the ledges monitored and the water flows attended and the fog understood and the network maintained and the mountain alive.

They are not gods. They are not spirits. They are animals—warm-blooded, mortal, hungry, afraid, mating, dying, being born. But they are animals who have become part of something larger than themselves, whose bodies and minds have been shaped by a place that required them to be shaped that way, whose existence is a function of the cloud-stratum's existence in the same way that the plant mat's existence is a function of the stone's existence.

They are the answer to a question that the mountain has been asking for a very long time. The question is: *who will watch?*

And the answer is: *we will.*
