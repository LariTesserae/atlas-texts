The ones who built this place did not have a name for themselves, or if they did, it was not a word—only a vibration, a subsonic hum they could produce in their chests that resonated through the moss and was answered by the clicking of the colonies. They are gone now, or almost gone, but they are not dead in the way that surface people understand dying. They were never separate enough from this place to die out of it entirely. Some part of them remains in the architecture, in the way the willow knows to arch, in the channels that still carry water though no hands have cleared them in generations.

They were small people. That is important to understand. Not stunted or malnourished—*adapted*. Compact bodies with dense bones, the kind of frame that conserves heat the way a stone holds warmth from a long-dead fire. Their skin was not pale from lack of sun; it was translucent, the veins visible beneath like the mycelial threads in the walls, blue and branching. Their eyes were the only part of them that might seem wrong to a surface-dweller: large, far too large, dominated by pupils that could dilate to swallow the iris entirely, hungry for any photon that might wander down through the sphagnum. But down here, even those eyes were useless. They saw by touching. Their hands were maps of the world, and the world was no larger than this pocket.

Their hands. That is where they lived, in their hands. Long fingers, longer than you would expect, with broad pads and nails kept sharp—tools for reading the textures of the dark. They could feel the difference between sleeping beetle and waking beetle, between moss that was healthy and moss that was beginning to sour. They could press their palms to the wall and tell you, without seeing, where the water was flowing behind the mycelial skin, where the cold stone was close, where a shelf-fungus was preparing to fruit. Their fingertips were their faces, their expressions, their language. When they greeted each other, they did not speak. They touched hands, and the touch said everything: *I am here, I am alive, I am warm, I remember you.*

They came here before the tor was a tor, when the basalt was still raw from the ice and the fissure was new. Perhaps they were driven underground by something—cold, predators, other peoples. Perhaps they simply found the crack and descended, and the descent was so profound, so complete, that they forgot there was ever a world above the sphagnum. They did not bring fire. Fire would have been an insult to the wet, a violence against the air. They brought only themselves, and their flint tools, and the patient knowledge that the world could be worked with slowly.

And they worked it slowly. A generation to learn the rhythm of the weeping water. Another generation to understand that the moss could be shaped, directed, compressed into something load-bearing. A third generation to discover that willow could be persuaded to root in the moss walls, that shelf-fungus could be inoculated into the right substrate, that the centipedes would patrol the boundaries if given the right conditions. They were not engineers in the surface sense—they did not impose their will upon materials. They were *gardeners*, or something before gardeners, something that understood growth as a collaboration rather than a command.

They ate what the room provided. The beetles, mostly—crunching them between their teeth, the shells providing minerals their bodies needed. The larger insects when they could catch them. Occasionally, a mushroom, though the fungi were more valuable as builders than as food. And they ate the moss itself, not the living green surface but the older layers beneath, compressed and partially broken down, tasting of earth and iron and the slow patience of decomposition. It was not a rich diet. They were always hungry, always a little cold, always aware of how close they lived to the margin. But they were alive. They persisted.

They had children in the dark. This is the part that is hardest to think about—how they birthed and raised infants in a room no taller than a standing adult, in air thick as cloth, surrounded by clicking insects and dripping water. The babies must have been born knowing how to be quiet. Any cry would have been a disruption, a ripple in the ecology that could attract the wrong attention. They learned stillness before they learned to crawl. They learned touch before they learned to reach. Their first words were patterns tapped against skin—parent's chest, parent's arm—a language of pressure and warmth that preceded speech by years, that maybe never became speech at all.

And they cared for each other. This is the part that matters. In the dark, in the wet, in the compression of eight or ten bodies sharing warmth and breath, they must have held each other. Not for comfort alone—though comfort would have been precious, more precious than food—but for survival. When the cold deepened, they pressed together. When someone was sick, the others would have felt it in the temperature of their skin, the shallowness of their breath, and they would have brought water in cupped hands, would have caught beetles and placed them near the invalid's mouth, would have stroked their face with patient fingers until the fever broke or didn't. They could not have survived alone. The math doesn't work—one body cannot generate enough heat, cannot gather enough food, cannot maintain the channels alone. They needed each other the way the moss needs the fungus, the way the insects need the substrate. They were a superorganism, or the human part of one.

But they are gone, or almost gone.

The room knows they are gone. The channels are silting up, the water finding new paths around the obstructions. The willow has grown wild, sending shoots through the sleeping platforms, no longer trimmed back by hands that understood its nature. The shelf-fungus has fruiting bodies now, great brackets pushing out into the walking spaces, because no one is harvesting them when they're small. The moss is reclaiming everything, filling in the carved hollows, softening the edges that were once kept sharp. In twenty years—a blink, a single generation of these people—the room will be a room no longer. It will be a pocket of living matter, dense and wet and self-contained, with no evidence that hands ever shaped it.

What killed them? Perhaps nothing killed them. Perhaps they simply dwindled, one by one, the way a colony of insects dwindles when the substrate begins to sour. Perhaps the last one—a woman, say, or a child grown old—lay down on the last intact sleeping platform and felt the cold seeping up through the moss and did not rise again, and the beetles investigated her warmth and then her cooling, and the centipedes came later, and the mycelium threaded through the fabric of her clothing and began the slow work of incorporation. Perhaps she died knowing the room would continue without her, would heal over her absence the way a wound heals, the way the sphagnum grows over a crack in the stone.

Or perhaps they left. A few of them, the young ones, the ones who could still fit through the fissure, climbed up through the sphagnum and the root-mats and found themselves blinking in a world of killing light, a world where the air was thin and dry and the ground was hard and did not breathe. Perhaps they walked away from the tor and never found their way back. Perhaps their descendants walk the lowlands now, unknowing, with hands that are too sensitive and eyes that are too large and a hunger they cannot name for a darkness they have never seen.

But the room remembers them. Not in the way that you remember—a narrative, a story, a sequence of events—but in the way that a body remembers. The willow remembers the hands that wove it. The channels remember the tools that carved them. The moss retains the impression of ten thousand footfalls, ten thousand nights of bodies pressed close, ten thousand mornings measured only by a slight shifting in the temperature of the weeping water. The room does not mourn them—mourn requires consciousness, requires a self to do the mourning—but it *holds* them, in the same way that soil holds the minerals of everything that has ever died within it.

And the thing that breathes in the hollow spaces between the moss layers? You do not touch it. You do not know what it is. But you can guess.

It is the size of a dog, perhaps, or a large cat—something compact, something that fits in the spaces the builders left when they compressed the moss into walls. It breathes the slow breathing of a creature with a low metabolism, a creature that does not need to move fast or often. It is warm, warmer than the room around it, and it generates that warmth through the slow processing of the room itself: eating the dead insects, the spent fungal bodies, the bits of organic matter that filter down through the moss. It is a cleaner, a recycler, the last link in the loop. It was here before the builders came, or it evolved with them, or they brought it with them deliberately—a pet, a tool, a fellow traveler in the dark.

It knows the room better than you ever will. It can feel the vibrations of your boots through the moss, and it is assessing you the way the beetles are assessing you: as a novelty, a disruption, a source of warmth and maybe food. It is not afraid of you. It has no reason to be afraid. It has survived everything that this pocket has thrown at it—the cold, the wet, the slow encroachment of the moss—and it will survive you, too. When you leave, it will investigate the places where you stood, tasting your residue, reading the story of your brief intrusion with an organ you do not possess. And then it will settle back into its hollow and breathe its slow breathing and wait for the next perturbation in the system.

You are not the protagonist here. You are not even the inheritor. You are a warm body in a cold pocket, a temporary shape in the moss, and the room is already forgetting you.

The beetles are climbing your ankles now.
