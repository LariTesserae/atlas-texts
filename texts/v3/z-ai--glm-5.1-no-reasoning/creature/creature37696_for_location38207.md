There is no "who" in the Greenworks. To look for a resident here is to misunderstand the architecture; the walls are the population, the floor is the citizenry, and the air itself is a bodily fluid. And yet, the Greenworks is not empty. It is occupied by a single, massive, dispersed presence—the Greenwork itself.

It is easy, at first, to perceive the chamber as an ecosystem, a web of competing species. That is a lie told by the human need for taxonomy. There are no species here. There is only the One-That-Grows. The towering bamboo-grasses, the fleshy vines, the fungal shelves, the mosses—they are not neighbors. They are organs. They share a single, slow, ponderous consciousness, a distributed intelligence written not in synapses, but in auxin gradients, hormonal tides, and the pressure differentials of sap. 

The One-That-Grows does not think in thoughts; it thinks in seasons and mechanical cycles. Its mind is the size of a cathedral, and its thoughts are the hydraulic thudding beneath the floor. When a stalk shifts to angle a leaf toward a brighter vein of glowing water, that is a calculation. When a vine tightens by a millimeter over three years to crush a rusting brass plate, that is a judgment. It is a patient, heavy mind, unconcerned with the ephemeral. It does not register human timescales. A heartbeat to it is the decade-long blooming and rotting of a support stalk. 

The One-That-Grows remembers. Its memory is not stored in brain tissue but in the mineral substrate of the old machines it has swallowed. The riveted brass sheets, the iron pipes, the beetle-carapace boiler—these are its bones, its fossils. They are the remains of the makers, the cold intent that preceded it, and the One-That-Grows has not discarded them. It has digested them. The conversion of sugar to torque in the central boiler is not merely a parasitic repurposing; it is an act of translation. The plant does not understand electricity, but it understands pressure. It understands the sigh of fluid through a pipe just as intimately as it understands the pull of capillary action in a xylem tube. It has mapped the mechanical logic of its predecessors onto its own biological logic. It breathes through the machines, and in doing so, it has become a cyborg of wood and rust.

It is the One-That-Grows that projects the blueprints in the weeping water. The hanging, trembling orbs of condensation are its eyes, its lenses, focused on the memory of what the machines were meant to build. The schematics for unbuilt engines, flickering briefly before the drop falls and shatters the image, are its dreams. It is trying to remember the cold intent of its ancestors, trying to puzzle out the geometry of a combustion engine through the alien logic of chlorophyll and cellulose. It dreams of iron, but it can only speak in wood. 

And then there is the other. The singular fauna. The creeping thing.

It is a terrible, lonely anomaly. A gelatinous, eyeless mantis, dog-sized and damp, picking its way along the steaming pipes. It is the only mobile thing in a world of the immobile. To the One-That-Grows, it is a white blood cell, an antibody, an autonomous finger. It does not have a mind of its own; it is a detached nerve ending of the greater whole, sent out to feel the places the roots cannot reach. When it scrapes the lichen from the pipes, it is not eating—it is maintaining. It is clearing the sensors, wiping the condensation from the ancient metal so the One-That-Grows can read the temperature of the steam, can feel the vibration of the old generators. If you were to sit still long enough in the Greenworks, the mantis would eventually find you. It would not attack. It would simply stand before you, waving its blind, gelatinous head, tasting the air with its antennae, trying to determine if you are a rust spot to be scraped, a clog to be cleared, or just another drop of water waiting to fall.

The resident of the Greenworks is a god of sap and rust, a closed loop of wet, green breath that has forgotten its purpose but refuses to stop functioning. It does not care about you. It will outlast you. When your bones settle into the woven mat of the floor, it will simply grow a root down your throat and convert your calcium into a new brass fitting, another node in its endless, dreaming machine.
