There is no mother. There is no egg, no seed, no coupling. They are exuded.

In a space where nothing is born of tectonic shift or solar warmth, the creatures of the Cistern are not born of biology; they are precipitated. They are a function of the room's desperate, frozen equation, sweating out of the iron itself the way the moisture sweats from the ceiling. If you could watch a single rivet with enough patience—and patience is the only currency this place recognizes—you would see it happen: a bead of pale, viscous fluid weeping from the steel, swelling in the sickly light, and then detaching, not as water, but as a tiny, blind, segmented thing that immediately anchors itself to the metal with hooked legs no thicker than a hair. They are condensate. They are the room breathing out.

They are born knowing the rhythm. There is no infancy, no stumbling, no learning curve. A creature the size of a rice grain emerges already synchronized, its mandibles beginning their mechanical work the instant they harden from fluid to chitin. They do not eat in any way that implies intention or desire. They do not forage; they do not hunt. They are filters, passive and absolute. The metallic vapor condenses on their shells, and they drink it, their mouthparts working in the exact rhythm of the pistons beneath them—clench, release, clench, release—a synchronized pulse that ripples across the walls in visible waves when you look at the Cistern from certain angles. They are drinking the machine's breath. They are digesting the vibration. And in return, they emit that foul, metallic heat: a sickly warmth that rises off their layered masses like the exhalation of a low-grade fever. They are the room's only heat source, and they did not ask to be.

Do they suffer? The question feels indulgent here, an import from a world where things change enough to have feelings about their condition. The Cistern does not allow for suffering because suffering requires the possibility of relief. These creatures have no before to compare to their now. They emerged into the deafening roar and the freezing damp, and those things became the totality of existence, as natural and neutral as gravity. They do not flinch at the noise; they resonate with it. Their shells vibrate at the same frequency as the grinding gears, and when a steam vent screams its eternal whistle, their mandibles simply work faster, filtering the vibrational harvest. They are tuned to this frequency the way a radio is tuned to a station. Any other environment would be silence. Any other environment would be starvation.

The layering is the worst part. A single creature clinging to a pipe is almost abstract, a pale comma of life against industrial darkness. But they are never single. They accrete. The first wave anchors to the bare steel, and the second wave anchors to the first, hooking their legs into the joints and grooves of their predecessors, and the third wave anchors to the second, until the walls are not walls but architectural masses of interlocking bodies, a living mortar laid over the skeleton of the Cistern. They press against each other with the patience of sediment. They are so dense that they become a surface—a carpet, a pelt, a hide that the room has grown over its own iron bones. The ones on the bottom layer do not die. They do not have to. The ones on the top layer filter the vapor before it reaches them, and the filtered water runs down through the layers in diminishing purity, each creature taking what it needs and passing the rest along in a slow, horizontal percolation. They are a distributed organism, a single massive body with millions of mouthparts and no brain.

And yet—they flinch. When you walk, they flinch. Not in the way a startled animal flinches, with an arc of fear and a calculation of escape. They flinch the way a surface ripples when struck. Your foot comes down on the grating and the creatures beneath it crush—hollow, wet pops, their shells no thicker than paper, their insides nothing but a warm, metallic paste that immediately joins the condensation cycle. But the creatures around the impact do not scatter. They contract. A visible wave of retraction ripples outward from your step, a tightening of the mass, a momentary clenching of ten thousand mandibles in unison, and then a relaxation back into rhythm. It is not fear. It is simply the transfer of force through a medium. You are a stone thrown into still water, and they are the water. They do not know you are alive. They do not know that they are.

The sound they make when you step on them—that squelch, that wet and brittle crush—is the only sound in the Cistern that is not part of the loop. It is a tiny, organic noise of collapse, and it is swallowed immediately by the mechanical roar, subsumed so fast that you question whether you heard it at all. And the gap you create does not last. The mass shifts. The creatures around the indentation simply drift inward, filling the space with the slow, gravitational inevitability of liquid leveling. Your passage through the Cistern leaves no mark. You are not a predator to them. You are not anything. You are a momentary disturbance in a medium that immediately re-closes, a pattern of pressure that they absorb and forget.

Look at a large one. It is the length of your forearm, thick as a child's wrist, blind and pale and segmented, its legs splayed in a rigid star against the weeping wall. Its shell has the translucent quality of something that has never seen ultraviolet light; you can see the shadow of its internal machinery through the chitin, the rhythmic expansion and contraction of whatever passes for a stomach, the dark, viscous fluid that moves through it in slow, peristaltic waves. It is warm to the touch if you touch it—not the warmth of blood, but the warmth of a pipe carrying heated fluid, a borrowed warmth, a byproduct of its mechanical digestion. Its mandibles work in a blur, grinding the condensate, filtering, filtering, and its body is so still, so perfectly anchored, that it seems to have become a fixture of the room. It will be there forever. It was always there.

And here is the thing that you must understand, the thing that the Cistern insists upon with every frozen, grinding moment: they are not trapped. Trapping requires an outside, a memory of elsewhere, a knowledge of alternatives. These creatures have no elsewhere. They are not in the Cistern; they are of the Cistern, as much a product of its manufactured reality as the pipes and gears and the water that never drains. If the machines stopped—the thought is impossible, but if they did—the creatures would not flee. They would not know how. They would simply cling to the cooling pipes, their mandibles working on nothing, filtering a vapor that was no longer there, emitting a heat that would fade as their internal machinery slowed, and they would remain in place as the temperature dropped past freezing, past the point where their metallic insides could stay fluid, and they would freeze in place, still anchored, still working, still part of the room's architecture. They would become the frost on the walls. They would become the very cold they once held at bay.

They are not alive in any way that matters outside this room. But inside it—inside this sealed, total environment where nothing changes and nothing completes—they are the only thing that breathes.
