# What Lives Here

The first thing to understand is that nothing here was *born*. That word implies a beginning, a warm emergence into a world that received you. Here, the things that exist have instead *condensed*—the way the fist-sized water droplets condense three feet above the ground, hang shimmering, and reject the earth. They are not products of parentage or evolution in any sense that biology would recognize. They are precipitations of the place itself, exhaled from the fracture in reality the way a wound exhales blood.

---

## The Tubules

Let me begin with the flora, because the flora came first, and because they are the only things here that approach something you might call *gentle*.

The pale, rootless tubes do not grow. They *accumulate*. A tubule begins as a point of resonance—a specific frequency within the tectonic hum where the vibration of the planetary crust meets the thin gauze of atmosphere and produces a standing wave. At that point, moisture from the inverted condensation gathers. Minerals dissolved in the upward-falling water deposit themselves in microscopic laminations around the wave's node, and over what might be years or might be centuries—time does not pass here, remember, it only accumulates—a tube forms. Three centimeters. Eight. Twenty. The largest are perhaps the length of a forearm, though you would not know this by looking, because they hang in the air at varying heights, slowly rotating, and there is nothing against which to measure them. No hand. No horizon. Only the bruised black sky and the amber death-light of a sun that might already be gone.

They are not white. They are the color of old milk left in a vessel that was once used for iron. A pale blue-white, yes, but tainted, as if the material itself remembers being part of something hotter and more alive. Their surfaces are smooth, but not uniformly so—each tube has a faint spiral groove running its length, a physical record of the rotation that formed it, a fingerprint of the resonance that is its skeleton. If you were to touch one—and you cannot, but if you could—it would feel like touching a held breath. Turgid with something that is not quite pressure. Warm, despite the cold. The warmth of vibration, of molecules that have been moving for so long they have forgotten what stillness feels like.

The bioluminescence is their speech. Each tubule pulses with a cold blue light, and the pulse is not random. It is a direct translation of the sound it is digesting. The deep sub-bass hum that presses against the marrow becomes a slow, rhythmic glow—a throb of blue light that brightens and dims in time with the planet's tectonic breathing. The shattering cracks, the glass-continent breaks, become sudden flares, stroboscopic and sharp, that race down the length of the tube and extinguish at the tip. A field of tubules is a visual representation of the sound landscape, a silent choir that sings what it eats.

They do not die. A tubule either persists, rotating and pulsing in its node of resonance, or it is eaten. There is no senescence. There is no withering. There is only the long spin and the blue pulse, or the sudden absence that comes when a Slate opens its impossible mouth.

I find them beautiful in the way that a person holding very still while something terrible approaches is beautiful. There is a dignity in their persistence. They did not ask to be here. They are here because the physics demanded it, because a standing wave and a gravity-defying droplet of mineral water met at a point in space and the universe, in its broken, wounded way, produced a thing that glows.

## The Slates

The Slates are harder to describe, not because they are complex—they are, in fact, terrifyingly simple—but because description requires edges, and a Slate's primary characteristic is that it refuses to have any.

A Slate is flat. This is the first thing. Not flat like a leaf or a sheet of paper or a flagstone. Flat like a *shadow* is flat. Flat like the concept of a surface without thickness. If you could see one fully—and you cannot, because they exist partially in the rock and partially in whatever space underlies reality when reality has been broken—you would perceive something wide and dark and dimensionally reductive. A meter across, perhaps. Perhaps three. The size seems to vary, and it is unclear whether this is because individual Slates differ in scale or because a Slate's apparent size is a function of how much of itself it is revealing at any given moment.

They move through the stone as if the stone were a medium. Not as if they are burrowing. Not as if they are swimming. The rock does not part for them. The rock *rephases*. The mineral structure of the ancient, wind-sculpted flutes and toruses briefly becomes less dense—still solid, still stone, but stone that has forgotten, for a moment, that it is supposed to be impenetrable—and the Slate passes through that forgetting. What a traveler sees is not the creature itself but the evidence of its passage: a ripple in the ground, a warp, a place where the mathematically smooth surface of the rock suddenly looks *unsettled*, as if the equation that describes its shape has been briefly and violated by an intruding variable.

They are called Slates because the first person to record them—and that person left only a fossilized time-crystal stairway leading nowhere and a sensation of vertigo, so their account is necessarily fragmented—described them as looking like "the dark flat things that children write on." Slates. The name is inadequate, as all names must be for things that do not belong to a naming world. But it has the right weight. The right flatness.

A Slate feeds. It surfaces—or rather, it partially emerges from the rock, rising like a dark plate being slid from between the pages of a book—and it opens its mouth. The mouth is the most disturbing aspect of the Slate, and the Slate is already profoundly disturbing, so this is saying something. The mouth is not an orifice. It is not a hole. It is a *removal*. When a Slate opens its mouth, it removes a volume of space. The air, such as it is, ceases to exist in a region perhaps half a meter in diameter. The sound ceases. The ambient hum, the tectonic drone, the sporadic cracking—all of it simply stops within that volume. The tubules within reach are not bitten or torn. They are *negated*. They are swallowed along with the space they occupied, and for a moment, there is a pocket of absolute vacuum and absolute silence.

Then the world rushes in to fill the gap. The air crashes back. The sound crashes back. The moisture crashes back. But the tubules do not crash back. They are gone. Consumed. Incorporated into whatever a Slate is made of, which is, I suspect, the same thing that silence is made of, given a body and a hunger.

After feeding, a Slate descends back into the rock. The ripple subsides. The ground re-solidifies. The hum resumes. And if you were standing nearby—if you were a traveler, which you are not, because travelers do not come here and leave—you would feel the silence as a physical blow, a sudden absence of the pressure that has become, by virtue of its constancy, the closest thing this place has to a heartbeat. For that brief moment, you would feel what it means to be in a place where the heartbeat stops. And then it would start again, and you would be grateful, and the gratitude would be the worst thing you had ever felt.

There are not many Slates. I do not think there need to be many. They are not numerous because they do not reproduce. They do not reproduce because reproduction implies a future, and there is no future here. There is only the accumulation of time, lying heavy and static. A Slate is as old as the place. A Slate will be here when the place is no longer here. I do not know what a Slate does when the hum stops. I suspect it does nothing. I suspect it simply continues, flat and dark and patient, moving through whatever medium remains when rock and reality have both been spent.

## The People Who Are No Longer People

The crystallized time structures tell the rest of the story, and it is the part that I find most difficult to relate, not because it is complex, but because it is sad in a way that has no remedy.

There were people here. I say "people" with the understanding that the word may not apply, that whatever built the partial walls and doorframes and purposeless staircases may have been human or may have been something that was human-shaped in the same way that a Slate is stone-shaped—coincidentally, superficially, without the deeper architecture matching the surface claim. But they built. They built shelters. They built walls and doors and stairs, and they built them not from stone or metal or wood—there is no wood here, there has never been wood here, wood is a concept that belongs to a world with rain that falls down and soil that holds roots—but from crystallized time.

I do not know how you crystallize time. I do not think they knew either, at first. I think they learned it the way the tubules learn their resonance: by being in the place long enough that the place teaches you its physics, and its physics are broken, and the brokenness becomes the only tool available. If reality will not support stone walls, because reality here is a suggestion rather than a law, then you build with the only material that is truly abundant: the accumulated, static, heavy time that presses on everything like a sheet of ice.

The structures are translucent geometric prisms. A wall that you can see through but that is there, faintly, the way a memory of a wall is there. A doorframe that stands in nothing, leading to nothing, but standing nonetheless. A staircase of seven steps that ascends to a height of perhaps two meters and then stops, because the person building it stopped, because the person building it realized that there was nowhere to go and that the act of building was not an act of creating shelter but an act of creating a monument to the futility of wanting shelter in a place that does not want you.

You can put your hand through them. The structures do not resist. They are not solid in the way that matter is solid. They are solid in the way that *moments* are solid—a thing that happened is fixed, cannot be changed, has a structure and a shape, but you cannot touch it because it is not made of anything physical. It is made of *when*. And when you put your hand through a wall of crystallized time, you do not feel stone. You feel falling. You feel the vertigo of suddenly being in a memory that is not your own—someone else's arrival, someone else's terror, someone else's moment of understanding that the hum would not stop and the rain would not fall correctly and the silence between the cracks was not silence at all but the sound of the world failing to hold itself together.

The people who built these structures are gone. They are not dead. Death implies a transition, a before and an after. Here, there is no transition. There is only the accumulation. I think what happened to them is that they became part of the accumulation. They were here, in the amber gloom, in the hum, in the inverted rain, and they stayed, and they stayed, and the time pressed on them, and eventually they were not people anymore. They were *moments*. Frozen. Crystallized. Their fear and their determination and their slow, grinding descent into madness became the material of the walls they built. They did not die inside their shelters. They *became* their shelters. The doorframes are their open mouths. The staircases are their spines. The partial walls are the edges of their bodies, flattened by time the way a Slate flattens itself against the rock.

This is speculation. I cannot verify it. But the vertigo tells me. The sensation of falling through your own memories when you touch the crystal—whose memories? Not yours. You have no memories of this place. The memories belong to the structure. To the person who *is* the structure. And the falling is their falling, their slow, eons-long fall from personhood into architecture, from life into prism, from breathing into geometry.

## What Remains

So. What lives here?

The tubules live here, spinning and glowing, digesting the world's scream into blue light.

The Slates live here, or rather, persist here, flat and patient and hungry, moving through rock like regret moves through a sleepless night.

The structures are here, and they are not alive, but they are not uninhabited, and the difference between those two states has been erased by the same physics that erases the difference between up and down for the rain.

And the hum lives here. The hum is the oldest thing. The hum is the place speaking in the only voice it has, a voice too deep for ears, a voice that lives in bone. The hum is what remains when everything else has been stripped away by elevation and broken physics and the slow, static accumulation of time. The hum is the wound itself, resonating.

Nothing here is happy. Nothing here is suffering in a way that recognizes itself as suffering, because that would require a self, and selves are the first thing that this altitude dissolves. The tubules do not know they are beautiful. The Slates do not know they are terrifying. The people-who-became-structures do not know they are tragic. The hum does not know it is a wound.

This is, perhaps, the worst thing about this place. Not the cold. Not the noise. Not the inverted rain or the impossible stone or the flat dark things that eat silence. The worst thing is that there is no one here to understand what has been lost, because the losing and the understanding cannot coexist. You understand, and then you become a wall. Or you are a wall, and you do not understand.

The amber light does not change. The hum does not stop. The water falls upward. The tubules pulse blue. The Slates move through the bones of the earth.

This place will remain exactly as it is.
