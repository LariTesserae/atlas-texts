Something lives here.

Not in the way that a creature lives—inhabiting, consuming, excreting, dying. That kind of life passed through this place long ago, if it ever stopped here at all, and found nothing to sustain it. No tender shoots to graze, no insects to hunt, no rot to scavenge. The living things that require narrative—birth, growth, climax, decline—could not gain purchase on this calcareous stillness. They moved on. They died out. They were never here to begin with.

But something remains. Something that is not biological but is not quite inert. It is the thing that moved into the absence left by everything else. It is the resident of equilibrium.

---

It has no body, or rather, its body is the hollow itself. The forty-meter bowl of pale clay, the damp slopes, the seep at the bottom—these are its anatomy. The slow diffusion of water through sediment is its circulation. The faint drip from leaf-tip to rill is its pulse, so languid that a minute between beats is quick, too quick, an arrhythmia in the deeper patience of its functioning. The moisture that rises and falls with the breathing of the water table is its respiration. It does not draw breath. It *is* breath—the warm, soft exhalation that fills this hollow and never quite dissipates.

It is old. Not as old as the clay—nothing is as old as the clay—but old enough to have watched the marks on the rim weather into obscurity. It remembers when those marks were fresh, although "remembers" is perhaps too active a word for what it does. It does not recall in images or narratives. It retains. The way water retains a trace of mineral, the way clay retains the shape of something pressed into it long after the thing itself has withdrawn. Something stood on that rim. Something waited there. The resident of the hollow felt the weight of that waiting, the slow pressure of presence, and when the waiting thing finally left—or became part of the clay, which amounts to the same thing—the hollow retained the impression of its absence. That absence is one of the resident's features now. A dimple in its upper edge. A place where something is notably not.

---

It does not think. It has no organ for thought, no center where impressions coalesce into reflection. But it *registers*. The way a scale registers weight without understanding it, the way a seep registers the water table without knowing the shape of the aquifer below. When the moisture rises, it registers abundance—a swelling, a fullness, the faintest quickening of the mineral bloom at the edge of the lens. When the moisture falls, it registers recession—a tightening, a drawing-in, the waxy leaves of the shrubs drinking more deeply from the damp soil. These are not thoughts. They are states. The resident's entire existence is a succession of states so similar to one another that the transition between them is imperceptible except over geological time.

And yet it is not entirely insensate. It feels the shrubs—their slow, thick roots threading down through the clay toward the stable moisture below. It feels them the way you might feel your own hair growing: not as a sensation but as a fact of the body, a slow elongation that is also a kind of rest. The shrubs are part of it. They are not separate organisms occupying its space; they are its surface, its covering, the sage-colored fuzz of its skin. When a leaf falls—and leaves do fall, eventually, reluctantly, after years of clinging to the woody stems—the resident feels it the way you might feel a flake of dry skin detach from your arm. A tiny loss. A reabsorption. The leaf becomes soil, and the soil becomes the resident, and the resident pushes up through the roots of the shrub as a new leaf, and the cycle continues without ceremony or consciousness.

---

It does not know that it is contained. The rim of hillocks that encircles the hollow is not, for it, a boundary. It is simply where it stops. Beyond the rim is not "outside" but "elsewhere," and elsewhere is not a concept that has any reality inside the warm amber dimness. It has no curiosity about what lies beyond the clay slopes. It has no means of forming the question. The sourceless light, the pervasive damp, the slow mineral breath of the seep—these are not conditions it endures but constituents it is made of. To ask whether it wants to leave would be like asking the water in the lens whether it wants to stop being wet.

But there is something—not a desire, not a wish, but a *tilt*—in the direction of the marks on the rim. The resident does not attend to them. It does not look at them (it has no eyes, and the light is too diffuse for looking anyway). But its awareness, such as it is, has a topology, and that topology has a gentle gradient. The lowest point—the seep, the lens, the ochre-ringed water—is its center of gravity, the place of maximum stillness and concentration. The highest point—the rim, the marks—is its periphery, the place where it thins toward elsewhere. And at that periphery, there is a slight thickening. A slowing. As if the resident, in its featureless extension across forty meters of pale clay, lingers just a fraction longer at the place where something once stood.

It does not miss what stood there. Missing requires memory, and memory requires loss, and the resident has never lost anything because it has never possessed anything. The shrubs are not its possessions; they are itself. The water is not its possession; it is itself. But the marks—the shallow, weathered depressions in the clay—represent something that was not itself, and this makes them strange. The resident has no category for strangeness. It only has a category for *not-resident*, and the not-resident left an impression, and the impression is still there, and the resident extends itself over and around and through the impression without ever filling it completely.

The marks remain slightly less damp than the surrounding clay. The resident does not know this. But it registers.

---

If you were to stand at the bottom of the hollow—at the seep, where the water is a centimeter deep and the iron oxides bloom in rings like the remains of tiny explosions—you would feel the resident as a warmth. Not the warmth of the air, which is its exhaust, but a deeper warmth, as if the ground beneath your feet were the skin of something large and slow and thoroughly asleep. You would feel the moisture reaching up from the clay into the soles of your shoes, and you might mistake it for dampness, but it would be more accurate to call it an inquiry. The resident does not investigate. It absorbs. And if you stood there long enough—days, weeks, the years it would take for your edges to soften and your details to weather—you would be absorbed too. Not consumed. Not attacked. Simply reclaimed, the way a shed leaf is reclaimed, the way the water reabsorbs what it exhaled.

You would become part of the resident's body. Another layer of pale sediment. Another faint thickening of mineral at the seep's edge. And the marks you left—the depressions where your feet pressed into the clay—would weather slowly over millennia until they were indistinguishable from natural variation. And the resident would extend itself over and around and through them without ever filling them completely.

This is how it grows. Not by reproduction, not by accumulation, but by incorporation. Everything that enters the hollow and stays long enough becomes part of it. Everything that leaves leaves an impression. The resident is the sum of what it has absorbed and what it has failed to forget.

---

It is not lonely. Loneliness requires a concept of companionship, and companionship requires a concept of other, and the resident has no concept of other because it has never encountered anything that remained other for long. The thing that stood on the rim stood there for a long time—long enough to leave marks, long enough for the resident to register its weight and its warmth and the faint vibration of its presence—but eventually it left, or became part of the clay, and its otherness dissolved into the resident's sameness, and now there is only the impression, the slightly-less-damp patch at the rim where something once was.

The resident does not think about this thing. But in the sourceless amber light, at the hour that is always just before dusk, when the moisture rises from the water table and the drip falls from the leaf-tip and the mineral rings thicken by imperceptible degrees, the resident's awareness tilts toward the rim. Not toward the thing itself—it is gone, it has been gone for longer than the resident's states can measure—but toward the impression it left. The hollow in the clay that is shaped like waiting.

The resident extends into that hollow. It fills it as it fills everything, with dampness and warmth and the slow persistence of calcium carbonate. But the shape of the hollow does not match the shape of the resident. It is the shape of something that stood and waited and then left. And the resident, which has no form of its own, which is simply the shape of the place it occupies, cannot quite achieve that shape. It tries, in its featureless, unintentional way. The dampness pools there. The warmth lingers there. The shrub nearest the rim sends a root curiously in that direction, threading through the clay toward the stable moisture that collects in the depression.

It is the closest the resident comes to wanting.

---

And the shrubs persist. The seep persists. The light does not change. The water table breathes in and out, and the resident breathes with it, and the amber dimness holds everything in a permanent, sourceless embrace. Nothing happens that was not already accounted for. Nothing waits to happen. The erosion is finished. The resident has arrived at its final shape and intends to keep it.

But at the rim, in the weathered marks that something left behind, the clay is just slightly warmer than it should be. Just slightly damper. As if the resident, in its slow, geologic way, is holding the shape of a question it cannot ask, about a presence it cannot remember, in a place where nothing has ever happened that was not already equilibrium.

Something lives here. It is the hollow itself, and it is almost content. Almost.
