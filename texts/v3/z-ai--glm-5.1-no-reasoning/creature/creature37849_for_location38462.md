There are no people here. Humanity has been so thoroughly composted into the substrate that even the concept of a ghost would find no purchase in this warm, heavy water. What lives here is something altogether older and newer—a population that measures time not in years but in metabolic cycles and the slow, geometric accrual of calcium carbonate. 

The residents of this drowned plain do not build; they occupy. They are the inheritors of a catastrophe they cannot conceptualize, living inside the skeletal remains of an intelligence they do not possess. 

**The Silt-Shapers** dominate the ground level, though "ground" is a misnomer—a meter of fine, organic muck blanketing the ancient asphalt. They are a species of large, heavily armored crustaceans, wide as manhole covers, their carapaces a dull, rust-flecked red that perfectly mimics the iron-heavy algae. They move in a slow, deliberate scrape, their modified claws sweeping the silt into their ventral mouths, filtering out the detritus, the micro-fauna, and the trace metals that leach from the concrete. When they rest, they depress their bodies into the silt, leaving only their armored backs exposed. To look out over a submerged boulevard is to see a thousand shallow, breathing potholes in the mud. They know the topography of the city better than the engineers who paved it; a sunken curb is a highway, a collapsed fire hydrant is a landmark. When the sleek, muscle-bound eels glide down from the girders to hunt, the Silt-Shapers do not flee. They simply clamp down, becoming stone, relying on the city’s ruined infrastructure—a dip in the shattered pavement, the overhang of a buried bus shelter—to break the predator's line of sight. 

**The Beam-Walkers** are the avian equivalents of this deep, dark world, though they are creatures of cartilage and wet flesh. They are long, ribbon-like fish, pale and nearly blind, their bodies tapering to thread-thin tails. They have evolved to live vertically, spending their lives suspended along the rusted spines of the old skyscrapers. Their ventral fins have modified into gripping hooks, allowing them to latch onto the corroded I-beams and the softened rebar. They hang head-down, spacing themselves precisely thirty centimeters apart—a distance dictated by the reach of their lateral lines, ensuring they do not compete for the drifting snow of organic matter falling from above. When they move, it is a startling, sudden uncoiling, a flick from one steel rib to the next. They are the guardians of the vertical city; they know every stress fracture and buckled column, retreating higher up the iron latticework when the rhythmic, creaking groan of settling concrete warns of a coming pressure shift.

**The Deep-Bellied** are the grand architecture of this ecosystem. They are the immense, pale jellyfish, beings of such fragile, translucent enormity that they seem like the ghosts of the buildings they inhabit. Their bells, four meters across, pulse with a slow, geological rhythm—*whump, whump, whump*—pushing the warm, thick water through their dangling, stinging nets. They have claimed the macro-spaces of the sunken metropolis. A collapsed parking garage is a nursery; a hollowed-out office block is a highway. They drift in vast, silent armadas through the cores of the towers, their tentacles trailing for dozens of meters, grazing on the dense clouds of silver-side fish that swarm like liquid shadows.
