The people who live in the Works have become what the place requires: slow, dense, and warm.

You do not notice them at first. You come over the rim expecting inhabitants of an industrial settlement—figures defined by their labor, distinct against the machinery, moving with the purposeful energy of workers on a clock. But the Works do not operate on a clock. They operate on geology. And the people have learned to operate on geology too.

They are not many. Two hundred, perhaps. Perhaps fewer. The population shifts like the steam—arriving, condensing, sometimes dissipating back down the mountain. But there is a core, a substantial number, who have been here so long they have forgotten there was ever a question of staying or leaving. They move through the corridors and the open vats with a gait that is distinct to the altitude and the heat—a stride that is not quite slow, but *measured*, as though every step has been assessed for its thermal and metabolic cost. They do not hurry. Hurry eats oxygen. Hurry produces sweat, and sweat cannot dry here. Hurry is an argument with the mountain, and the mountain does not argue; it simply persists.

Their faces are strange. This is the second thing you notice, after their pace. The constant damp and the mineral saturation of the air have done something to their skin. It is not the weathering of sun and wind you see in high-altitude communities elsewhere. It is a kind of mineralization—faces that have absorbed the caldera's chemistry. There is a matte quality to the skin, a lack of the natural oils that lower altitudes produce. The pores seem diminished, the surface almost stonelike, not rough but *sealed*, as though the body has adapted to the wet-dry cycling by developing a cuticle. The dark glass masks they wear during sulfur work leave the skin around their eyes and jaws outlined—pale where the mask blocks the air, dark and mineral-stained where it does not. After a shift, when the masks come off, they look like negative versions of themselves, or like maps of a place you have never been.

Their hands are enormous. Not thick with callus in the way of manual laborers elsewhere, but *hardened*—fingers that have become dense, the knuckles swollen, the skin on the palms and inner fingers almost laminated. They handle basalt and raw sulfur and the dark strange alloys from the forges. They grip pipes that are hot enough to burn visitors. The heat has done something to the tissue—remade it slowly, cell by cell, into something more like leather, or like the pale fibrous growth in the cracks: dense, dry, conducting warmth rather than being consumed by it. When they shake your hand, the grip is firm and radiantly, unnervingly hot.

They do not speak much, and when they do, the language is low and close. The sounds do not carry. They have learned that sound is absorbed by the damp stone and the thick, mineral air, so they speak in registers meant only for the person standing next to them. Conversation happens in the corridors, in the sheltered spaces between the vats, in the low electric hum of the turbine halls. You catch fragments—words in the trade language, words in something older that belongs to the mountain, words that seem to be mostly vowels, rounded sounds that require the mouth to stay half-closed against the sulfur thin air.

They do not explain themselves. This is not hostility, though visitors sometimes read it as hostility. It is the natural diplomacy of people who live in an unfinished geological process. The Works are still becoming, and the people are still becoming with them, and to explain the place would be to claim it is finished. So they do not describe the deep corridors. They do not name the silhouette on the rim. They tell you the rules—do not dig, do not build without checking, do not go deep—but they do not provide the stories behind the rules. The woman who runs the sulfur refinery, a heavy, quiet figure whose mask-off face is a stark white triangle of unexposed skin on a field of umber, does not tell you who went into the lower passages and did not return. She simply says, "It is not a direction."

They are not dour. This is important. The silence and the slowness and the dark glass masks suggest a grimness to visitors, a grimness we associate with endurance and suffering. But the people of the Works are not suffering. They are *attuned*. There is a calm in them that is not resignation but something closer to resonance. They have matched the frequency of the turbines. They move at the speed of the earth's heat because they have discovered that fighting it is exhausting and unnecessary. They have found a kind of stillness inside the continuous, slow-motion catastrophe of the caldera, and that stillness is not unhappiness. It is something else.

They are patient in a way that seems almost geological. A man working the metal forges, feeding a dark alloy gear into a channel of magma-heat, does not check the time. There is no time. There is only the heat and the phase-state of the metal and the slow, steady attention of his hands. He has been making gears for eleven years. He does not know where the gears go. A trader comes twice a year and takes them down the mountain in crates. He does not ask. He is not indifferent; he is *untroubled*. The gear is finished when it is finished, and then there is another gear, and this is the nature of making things in a place that is itself being made.

They have adapted to the damp. Their clothing—heavy fabric and leather, layered, strapped, and buckled in configurations that seem baroque until you realize each strap anchors a layer of insulation against the wet—does not try to keep the water out. It manages the water. It channels it. The leather is dark and supple from permanent saturation. The fabric wicks and breathes and lets the condensation move through it rather than pool against the skin. They do not own anything that rusts or rots. Their personal objects are ceramic, stone, the dark forges-alloy. Their bunks—carved into the caldera walls in rooms that share three walls with other rooms—are spare, warm, and dry only in their immediate hollow, the stone around them beading with moisture.

At night they gather. Not all of them, and not in a formal way, but in the larger chambers where the geothermal heat concentrates and the air is thickest and warmest—they gather and they eat the packed-in food, the dried things, the compressed things, and they talk in their low, close way. Sometimes someone sings. The acoustics are strange in the Works: the damp absorbs high frequencies, and the singing is all low register, all resonance, a sound like the hum of the turbines, like the pulse from below, and it harmonizes with the drip and the hiss, and you stand in the corridor listening and you cannot tell where the singing ends and the machines begin.

And there are the ones who have been here the longest. You can identify them not by age—they are not chronological elders, simply the ones who arrived and never left—but by a quality of presence. They are the ones who are the most still. They sit or stand in the warmest rooms, in the spaces closest to the steam vents, and they do very little. They are not idle. They are *inhabiting*. They have become so patient, so slow, so mineral in their stillness that they verge on the geological. Their eyes are calm and dark. Their hands rest on the warm stone. They do not speak often. They have passed through the work and the labor and the adaptation into a state that looks, from the outside, like a feature of the caldera itself. They are the human equivalent of the pale fibrous growth—conductive, warm, quiet, placed in the margin between forming and formed. To touch them would feel, perhaps, like touching the ground: warm, unmistakably alive, generating heat that is not the heat of the surroundings.

They do not seem lonely. They do not seem trapped. They seem like people who have found the thing that matches them. The caldera is not finished, and they are not finished, and the warmth goes on, and the slow work continues, and the condensation rises and falls, and they are at home in theWorks: in the crater of an ongoing act of creation, doing what the mountain does, which is to persist, and to be warm, and to become.
