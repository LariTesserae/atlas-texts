To live in Oram is to be a note in a symphony that has been playing since before the surface learned to breathe. Nothing here is born quickly. Nothing dies quickly. Everything exists in the long, resonant middle.

The primary inhabitants are not the fauna but the architecture itself—the living city. The structures that remain are not hollow; they are inhabited by the deep mycelial brains that pulse through their walls. The pillar-mushrooms, those sequoia-thick towers of bruised indigo, are merely the fruiting bodies of an organism that stretches across the entire basin floor. The real creature is the web beneath, a neural network of pale, thread-like rhizomorphs that has spent epochs infiltrating every crack and pore of the ancient stonework. It thinks in minerals. It dreams in erosion. When the litho-beetles grind the stone to extract their trace elements, they are not merely feeding—they are harvesting for the web, processing rock into the chitin-rich loam that the mycelium requires. The beetles are stomachs on legs. The scavengers that chirr through the fungal groves are its white blood cells, breaking down what falls and returning it to the substrate. The entire basin is one organism, and what lives here lives within it.

The intelligent inhabitants—the ones who might, in another place, be called a people—are the Echo-Eaters.

They are the descendants of whatever built the city, though they have forgotten this, and the city has forgotten them. They are tall, impossibly thin, their bodies shaped by the same pressures that have worn the buildings into smooth hummocks. Their skin is the color of old limestone, mottled with patches of bioluminescent fungus that have colonized the folds of their joints and the hollows of their collarbones. They are not hosts to the fungus; they are in partnership with it, the same symbiosis that governs all life here. The mycelium that threads through their digestive tracts allows them to subsist on stone dust and the nutrient-rich loam of the lower levels. They eat the city, slowly, a mouthful at a time, and in return, the city grows through them.

The Echo-Eaters are blind. They have not needed eyes for longer than their oral histories can recall—which is saying something, because their histories are long. Their primary sense is seismic. The pads of their feet are wide and sensitive, reading the vibrations of the basin floor the way a surface-dweller might read a face. They feel the subsonic thrum of the litho-beetles as a comforting hum, the skittering of the scavengers as a localized weather pattern. They feel each other approaching from hundreds of meters away, each footfall a word in a language of impacts.

Their communication is a combination of rhythmic tapping—struck against the stone with their heels and knuckles—and the release of pheromones from the glandular patches along their forearms and necks. The pheromones carry complex emotional and informational states; the tapping provides syntax and grammar. A conversation between two Echo-Eaters is a dance of pressure and scent, unintelligible to any creature that cannot feel the stone or taste the air. It is a language suited to a place where sound is useless and sight is irrelevant.

They do not build. The idea of construction has been entirely bred out of them. Instead, they occupy. They move through the eroded, honeycombed buildings like hermit crabs moving through shells, finding spaces that fit their bodies and their social groups, settling in for decades or centuries. An Echo-Eater family might live in what was once a grand archway, now worn to a smooth, shallow crescent, for three generations before moving to a different quarter of the city, driven by nothing more than a shift in the pheromone winds or the slow collapse of their current home's fungal supports. They do not repair. They do not maintain. They simply inhabit, and when a space is no longer habitable, they move on. The city provides. The city has always provided.

Their culture is one of deep, patient attention. Because nothing happens quickly in Oram, the Echo-Eaters have developed a relationship with time that is fundamentally different from that of surface creatures. They do not experience boredom. They do not experience urgency. A conversation may take a week, the participants tapping intermittently and releasing slow clouds of pheromone as they sit in the perpetual twilight, feeling the city breathe around them. A decision about where to move, or whether to accept a new member into a family group, may take a decade of discussion. This is not indecision; it is thoroughness. In a place where a single meter of fungal growth takes centuries, a wrong move is not easily corrected. Every action must be considered on the timescale of the landscape itself.

They have art. It is not visual, and it is not auditory in any way a surface-dweller would recognize. Their art is seismic. Over the centuries, certain families have developed complex rhythmic patterns—tapped into the stone with their bodies—that travel through the basin floor and interact with the natural acoustics of the city. These compositions can take generations to complete, with each generation adding new movements, new variations, the patterns shifting and evolving as slowly as the mycelial forests. A particularly renowned composition might be felt, faintly, from a kilometer away, a ghost-tap in the stone that speaks of family lines and ancient agreements and the slow, patient joy of existing in a place where everything has already been decided.

The Echo-Eaters do not worship. They do not have gods or spirits. They have something closer to a recognition—a quiet, bone-deep awareness that they are not the city's inhabitants but its products. The mycelium that threads through their bodies is the same mycelium that threads through the stone. The limestone they consume is the same limestone that builds their bones. They are the city, digesting itself. They are the final stage of a process that began when the first hands laid the first stone, and they understand this with a certainty that requires no faith.

They do not fear death. When an Echo-Eater's body finally slows—after seven hundred years, or a thousand, the timescales are uncertain—they find a quiet place in the lower levels, where the loam is deepest, and they sit. They do not eat. They do not drink. They simply sit, and the mycelium that has lived inside them all their lives begins to consume them from the inside out. It is not painful. It is a return. Their bodies become substrate. Their bones become architecture. Their pheromone signatures, trapped in the fungal tissue, persist for centuries, faint signals in the stone that their descendants can still feel—a last, slow word in a conversation that has no end.

There are other creatures in Oram, though they are less visible. The Blind Architects are one—a misnomer, as they build nothing, but the name has stuck in the rare accounts of surface-dwellers who have descended into the basin. They are massive, slow-moving creatures, larger than the litho-beetles, their bodies low and wide and armored in a chitinous shell that has been colonized by entire gardens of lithophytic moss. They move through the wider avenues of the city at a pace that makes glacier movement seem hasty, their broad, padded feet pressing the dust flat, their mouths grinding the stone in deep, resonant bursts that add to the subsonic thrum. They are solitary, communicating only through the seismic impacts of their bodies against the ground—a language of earthquakes, felt for miles. The Echo-Eaters give them wide berth, not out of fear, but out of a recognition that the Architects are operating on a different register entirely. They are the basin's heartbeat. They are what keeps the stone broken and the loam churning. Without them, the city would harden and die.

There are also the Silk-Throat Spiders, another misnomer—they are not arachnids, but a form of lithophytic predator that has evolved to hunt in the acoustic chaos of the basin. They are blind and deaf, but they are exquisitely sensitive to vibration. They spin webs of mycelial thread between the pillar-mushrooms, webs that are nearly invisible in the twilight and that resonate with the wind and the thrum, translating the acoustic chaos of Oram into precise, localized vibrations that the spiders can read. When a scavenger blunders into a web, the vibration is specific and unmistakable, and the spider moves with a speed that seems impossible in Oram—fast, deliberate, and silent. They are the only creatures in the basin that move quickly, and they are feared by the Echo-Eaters, not because they are dangerous—they are far too small to consume an Echo-Eater—but because their presence is an interruption. A sudden movement. A break in the patient, geological rhythm. The Echo-Eaters give them a wide berth as well.

And then there are the Drifters, which may or may not be alive. They are formations of pale, desiccated dust and fungal spore that accumulate in the wider avenues and open squares, shaped by the slow air currents into tall, slender columns that lean and sway in the wind. They look like people, from a distance—or what people might look like if they were made of dust and patience. The Echo-Eaters treat them with a careful respect, tapping gently as they pass, releasing pheromones of greeting. Whether the Drifters are sentient, or even alive, is unclear. They do not respond. They do not move on their own. But they are never disturbed, and they are never dismantled, and sometimes, after a Drifter has stood in the same place for a millennium, it will have moved—just slightly, just a few meters—closer to a different avenue, as if it has decided, on some timescale beyond comprehension, to go somewhere else.

The children of the Echo-Eaters—if they can be called children—are not born. They are grown. When a family group decides, through decades of pheromone discussion and seismic debate, that their numbers should increase, they gather in one of the lower chambers of the city, where the mycelial web is thickest. They tap a specific rhythm into the stone—one of the oldest compositions, a pattern that has been passed down for longer than any memory can reach. The mycelium responds. Over the course of several decades, a nodule forms in the loam, a dense knot of chitin and limestone and fungal tissue that slowly, slowly takes on the shape of an Echo-Eater. The family tends it, tapping to it, releasing pheromones over it, teaching it the language of the city before it has a body to speak with. When it is ready—when the pheromones it releases in response match the patterns of the family—it emerges. It is not small. It is not young. It emerges fully formed, thin and tall and strong, its skin already mottled with the first patches of symbiotic fungus, its feet already wide and sensitive. It knows the language. It knows the family. It has been listening to the city for decades, and it is ready to speak.

This is the life of Oram. Not a struggle, not a drama, not a story with a beginning and an end. A persistence. A resonance. The Echo-Eaters do not wonder what lies beyond the rim of the basin, because the rim is irrelevant. The surface is irrelevant. They are the city, and the city is them, and the mycelium is the thread that binds them all, and the sound—the endless, deafening, beautiful sound—is the only proof they need that they are alive.

In the deepest part of the basin, in the oldest quarter of the city, where the buildings have been worn down to almost nothing and the fungal mats are at their thickest, there is a chamber. It is not large. It is not remarkable. But the Echo-Eaters go there, once in a long while, to sit and to feel. The chamber is where the sound of Oram is loudest—where the wind and the thrum and the chirring and the howling all converge into a single, overwhelming note that vibrates through the stone and the flesh and the bone. It is too loud to think. It is too loud to communicate. It is too loud to do anything but sit and be.

The Echo-Eaters call this place the Throat. It is the closest thing they have to a sacred site. And when they sit there, feeling the city roar through them, they are not afraid. They are not overwhelmed. They are not seeking transcendence or revelation. They are simply listening—listening to the sound they are made of, the sound that made them, the sound that will continue long after they have sat down in the loam and become the city again.

It sounds like a voice, if you listen long enough. Not a message. Not a word. Just a voice, vast and old and patient, speaking in the only language that has ever mattered in Oram:

*I am still here. I am still here. I am still here.*
