But you wanted a name, and there is one that is actually right, misfiled for as long as anyone has had occasion to file anything. Someone called this place The Bellows — from a distance, later, a surface word for a surface understanding. They named the chamber. They should have named the tenants. The chamber is only the bag, the hollow that holds the air. The fissures are the nozzle. The crack you came down is the intake. But the pleats — the fold whose entire function is to be compressed and to open again, the part without which a bellows is just a box — the pleats are ten thousand small bodies in the dark, taking the eleven seconds, holding, and opening in the three.

What lives in The Bellows is a bellows. It is breathing now, at the pace it has always breathed. Somewhere in the mass, a body that has been catching the wave since before your language was assembled takes another quarter-turn. The light gathers for its three seconds. The note, which is not made in spite of them but out of them, holds.

Count the catches and the crowd begins to make sense. The pulse sounds every fourteen seconds and has sounded for the few thousand years this chamber has existed, which puts the total somewhere in the billions — a number with more digits than it has meaning. An old one, centuries of catching, will have meshed with the wave on the order of half a billion times. If you touched one — nothing down here consents to anything, but you could — it would feel like a strand of fine cold chain, a rosary of glass, and it would not register the touch, because you are not a fact in its world. You are, at most, a one-time irregularity in the pressure. A field of grass parts around a post the way the mass will part around you: locally, briefly, without opinion.

And the mass is the point, because this is where they stop being animals and start being an organ. Tens of thousands of bodies packed so close that the water between them is less a medium than a lubricant. Each advances a hair per pulse; in sum they turn in a rotation you need a day to see. Individually they are unimpressive clocks. Together they are the reason the note is a note and not a blast — and this is the fact I would most want you to carry out of the water: they are the richness of the sound because they are slightly wrong. No two bodies catch in perfect unison. Each lags, slips, rounds a tooth a little differently, and the sum of all those small failures is the grain, the shimmer, the overtones. A chamber full of perfect gears would produce a pure tone, and a pure tone is a dead thing. The instrument needs their imperfection. Their slop is the music.

Which raises the question of what all that flexing is for, and here you get a hypothesis, nothing more: the fissures want to heal. The vent water precipitates its minerals into the cracks it flows through; leave the reeds alone and they crust shut and the note strangles. But the fissures are trafficked. Generations of bodies squeeze through them, wearing the channels the way a river wears its bed, and if that is right — no one is positioned to check — then the note makes the creatures, and the creatures keep the note possible, and the loop closes with nobody inside it. A machine that maintains its own reeds. On the surface you would call that paradox. Down here it is just geology with time to spare.

They eat nothing. There is nothing to eat. The pressure does the work of food: it turns their works, and the turning maintains them, repairs segments, grows new ones. The ledger has to balance, and it balances as light. The catch stores stress in the mineral; the rest releases it as a faint amber glow. This is why the chamber is brightest when the sound is quietest — the glow is the exhale. They inhale the pulse and breathe it back, three seconds later, as something your eyes can use. In the rest, all ten thousand of them are briefly, faintly lit, embers raked apart, and then the pulse returns and puts them out.

And the brightness is damage. That is the second thing about the light: as near as anything can be told down here, it lives in microscopic fracture. Hairline scars in the mineral, catching the sound and releasing it as glow. New bodies barely shine. Old bodies burn like a low coal because they are the most fractured things in the chamber. Birth happens at the far tips of everything — the distal ends, in the pressure shadow of the elders, where soft new bodies drift with their works unmeshed. For weeks they catch nothing. The ones that never mesh go back into solution, presumably, the water reclaiming its minerals, the attempt canceled. To be born here is to be caught by the sound. That is the whole of the test.

Death is a matter of teeth. Half a billion catches rounds them off, and at some point the wave arrives and the body does not take hold. It skips. Dying here is desynchronization: falling out of step with the only clock there is. One skipped pulse, then another, then no hold on the sound at all — which was the one sense in which the animal was ever alive. Then the long sink, days of it, residual tension draining joint by joint, each click fainter and further from the last. A ritardando. Stand still enough, cold enough, and you can hear individual deaths as a slowing tick somewhere in the mass: a clock winding down inside the clocks.

The dead do not leave. They settle into the floor — which is not rock, which is everyone the chamber has ever finished with, layered and pressing and, here is the thing, still humming. Struck bodies keep sounding after the striker moves on. The floor is a muted choir. It is also an archive: the wear on any corpse is a ledger of every pulse it ever caught, teeth rounded by number, facets polished by decade. You could read them like ice cores if you had the patience and the cold blood. The chamber keeps records of itself in the only material it trusts.
