What lives here is not a community in the ordinary sense. There is no equilibrium, no neat negotiation between species. This is a colonization in progress, a raw occupation of new ground by whatever could reach it and whatever could hold on.

The mosses came first and they know it. They act like it. They cover the basalt in thick, boastful mats, dark green and almost black when wet, drinking directly from the stone's pores with the blind greed of organisms that remember when there was nothing here at all. Their rhizoids have wormed into every micro-fracture, prying apart the young basalt one mineral grain at a time. Underfoot, they are spongy, waterlogged, and they release a smell like clean metal and soil and something older. They do not grow upward; they grow *outward*, merging into one another, colonizing in overlapping waves, each species shouldering the last aside with the slow violence of territorial expansion measured in millimeters. There is no harmony in the moss carpet. It is a continuing argument.

The liverworts are the moss's stranger, more archaic cousins. They sit in the wettest depressions, in the low places where water gathers into shallow, warm pools no deeper than a thumbprint. They are slimy to the touch, dark and gelatinous, their bodies flat and featureless, barely differentiated. They look like something that should be growing on the sea floor, and in a sense they are; this is a marine organism that never left. It found this cathedral of wet stone and simply continued its ancient, submerged project, photosynthesizing in the saturated air with the same biochemical indifference it has maintained since before the lowland scrub existed. It does not care about altitude.

The epiphytes are the true anomaly, and they know it too, in whatever way a plant can know its own wrongness. These are lowland things. Broad-leaved, showy, structurally extravagant, they belong in the steaming river valleys three thousand meters below, not on an alpine spire. They should not be here. But the constant humidity, the warmth of the lower latitude buffered by the thermal mass of weeping water, has created a microclimate that mimics their native lowland, and they have simply taken root. Their seeds arrived somehow, perhaps carried by the rare updraft, perhaps deposited by the occasional moth, and they germinated in the moss and the wet detritus, and they grew. They grow quickly, frantically, as if aware of their precarious position, as if some hormonal alarm tells them to grow fast and seed early before the cold finds them, before the altitude remembers what it is supposed to be. Their roots tangle in dense, floating masses, not anchored in soil but in other roots, in moss, in rotting leaf litter, creating a suspended ecology that has no contact with the actual ground. They are living on top of living on top of stone. The whole shelf is a heap of biomass with no bottom.

Among the roots, in the dark wet spaces between stems, there are things decomposing. The lower layers of the moss carpet are dying, being consumed by fungi and bacteria that are themselves invisible, their presence announced only by the smell of wet peat and by the dark, coffee-colored water that seeps from the base of the moss mats. The nutrient cycle here is fast and closed. Nothing leaves. The dead feed the living, the living crowd and kill each other, the dead feed the living again. It is a compost heap suspended in the sky, running hot and wet.

The fauna is sparse, and what is here is lost.

The moths are large, heavy-bodied, and they should not be flying at this altitude. Their wings are broad and dark, almost black, optimized for heat absorption, and they navigate the saturated air with a clumsy, laborious flight, batting through the mist with the desperate energy of animals that have been blown off course and cannot find their way back. They land on wet leaves and sit, wings spread, shivering. Some of them are drinking. Some of them are dying. It is difficult to tell which.

The tree frog is more established, and it is the closest thing this place has to a permanent resident. There are several, but they are rarely seen; they hide in the deep, wet cavities of the epiphyte root masses, in the dark places where the moss meets the stone. They are small, bright green, with eyes like polished black beads that reflect the flat light. Their skin is always wet. They eat whatever insects blunder into this place, the moths mostly, and the occasional gnat. They call, sometimes, a low, clicking sound that is swallowed immediately by the roar of the water. It is a call that no other frog can hear. They are a population of fewer than a dozen, stranded and inbred, surviving on the margin of survival, and they will either adapt over the next ten thousand generations or they will wink out. They do not know this. They sit in their wet cavities and click into the roar.

There are nematodes in the water films, and tardigrades, and mites. There are things living in the moss that have no common name, visible only under a lens, eight-legged or no-legged, going about their microscopic lives with the same blind persistence as everything else here. The water that weeps from the stone carries them, distributes them, and they colonize each new wet surface with a speed that would be alarming if it were visible. The stone itself is being eaten, slowly, by endolithic bacteria, and they are the only things here that are patient.

The only thing here that is not growing, not adapting, not consuming or being consumed, is the pump-house. It is squatting in the center of the shelf, bolted to the basalt, its corrugated iron walls weeping orange rust. It is automated, electrified, and it is working. The pump inside it draws water from the deep pressure chamber beneath the shelf and pushes it down through the thick hoses. It runs on a rhythm. It was built by hands that are not here, and it serves purposes that are not here, and it operates on a schedule that has nothing to do with the moss, the frogs, the moths, or the slow, argumentative carpet of life that has grown up around it. It was placed here and then abandoned to its task. It will perform that task until it cannot, and then it will stop, and the water will find its own way down the mountain.

The rust is its biology. The oxidation is its aging, its dying. It is being consumed by the same damp that feeds the moss, and the moss is creeping up its foundations, and the liverworts are finding the low places where the iron meets the stone, and in a thousand years, the pump-house will be a fossil, a dark stain in the moss, and the water will still be seeping, and the stone will still be young.
