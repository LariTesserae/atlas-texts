To live here is to have never known that there is anything other than here. The inhabitants of this drowned, hand-wrought cathedral are not visitors; they are the final, soft-bodied conclusions of a mathematical equation written in crude basalt and immense pressure. They do not inhabit this space so much as they are pressed into it, extruded into its crevices like the clay mortar that binds the ancient walls. 

The macro-fauna—the fist-sized crustaceans and the thick, scale-less eels—move through the gelatinous water like thoughts moving through a feverish brain: slow, viscous, and entirely without urgency. Their biology is a masterpiece of reduction. They are the descendants of creatures who might have once possessed eyes, but in a world where light is a 0.4, vision is an expensive waste. Their skulls and carapaces have smoothed over where sockets once were, leaving them with a face of pure, unbroken touch. To navigate is to press against the world. The eels, thick as human arms and writhing with a muscular patience, do not swim; they squeeze. They fill the crevices not out of fear, but because in a place of absolute crushing pressure, the only relief is the counter-pressure of the stone. They lie layered upon one another in the squat archways, a squirming, lukewarm biomass of interlocking curves, sensing each other’s heartbeats not as sound, but as minute shifts in the hydrostatic weight.

The crustaceans are the slow-handed monks of this cathedral, tending to the rust. Their carapaces are not grown, but accrued. Over the decades of their lives, the mineral-laden water deposits a rind of iron ore and calcareous rust upon their backs, fusing them to the aesthetic of the masonry until they appear as living, walking basalt blocks. When they move, it is with the agonizing deliberateness of continental drift. They graze. Their mouthparts—grinding, wet, and rhythmic—are the only true percussion in this silent symphony, and they are constantly chewing. They feed on the bacterial mats that paint the stone in bruised purples and muddy siennas, and in doing so, they scrape the stone clean, only for the bacteria to reclaim it over centuries. It is a sacrament of consumption and reclamation that keeps the architecture in its exhausted, stable state. They do not hunt; they simply scrape, moving from one accretion to the next in a state of perpetual, mindless communion.

But the true inhabitants of this place are not the visible creatures. The eels and the crustaceans are merely the larger knots in a fabric woven entirely of microbial presence. The water itself is alive—a thick, warm slurry of suspended particulates and bacterial slimes. This microbial sea is the ghost of the makers, the primordial soup that has reclaimed the hand-wrought void. The bacteria breathe the iron, excreting the phantom gray-green luminescence that outlines the nearest archway. It is a light not meant for seeing, but a byproduct of a chemical appetite, a faint exhalation of a world eating itself. The inhabitants are coated in this slime; it lines their gills, it lubricates their joints, it fills the spaces between their segments. They are swimming through the digestive tract of a god, and they are entirely at peace within it.

To exist here is to be stripped of the concept of "other." The boundary between creature and cavern is porous. The clay mortar has dissolved into mush, and the bacterial mats have bound the sludge to the stone, creating a continuous medium of life and mineral. The isopods that crawl blindly over the walls are indistinguishable from the bulging, eroded masonry except for the agonizingly slow rhythm of their legs. The eels, packed tightly into the crevices, become load-bearing columns of flesh, their bodies adapting to the weight of the water just as the stone has. They are plugged into the room, their heartbeats syncing with the subsonic groans of the shifting arches, their metabolisms throttled down to a fraction of a beat per minute, matching the glacial expansion and contraction of the basalt. 

There is no narrative here. No birth, no death—only a continuous, suffocating presence. The creatures do not know they are alive; they only know the pressure, the warmth, and the iron taste of the water. They are the offspring of the deep time that has smoothed the stone, the embodiment of the room's final form. In this hand-built abyss, they are the last congregation, clinging to the walls in the crushing silence, waiting for the next geological epoch to pass, grinding their mandibles in the dark.
