The people of the Drowned Steppe-Cities do not move; they pour.

You watch them in the dim emerald light, and the first word that comes to mind is *fluent*. Their bodies have negotiated a permanent treaty with the water. They are not sleek, not in the darting, torpedoes-and-fins way of surface-dwelling marine life. They are *dense*. They move through the warm sea with the unhurried weight of stones that have learned to swim, displacing the water with a deep, silent thrum rather than cutting through it. Their skin—the color of the olive-rust pad-vegetation, shifting to the pale gray of the uncategorized stone—is thick and slack. It folds around them like the felted fabric they make from the ribbon-towers, draped not for modesty but because their mass requires it; without the slow draping of their own flesh, they would look like the compacted, fist-like nodules of chert they dig from the deep matrix.

Their faces are not faces in any strict sense. They are instruments, grown for the world they inhabit. There are no noses. The water they breathe—rich with sulfur, iron, and that uncatalogued mineral—passes directly through broad, gill-like slits set low and wide along their throats, slits that pulse in rhythm with their slow hearts. Their eyes are not eyes, either. They are wide, flat patches of light-sensitive skin that stretch across their cheekbones and temples like a bruise, receptor fields evolved to catch the monochrome green glow that filters through the canopy of fans. They do not focus; they *absorb* the light, navigating the world as an overall condition of dimness and brightness rather than a collection of distinct objects. To look at one of them face-to-face is to look at a landscape of smooth, un-featured topography, a geography of subtle swells and depressions where the only focal points are the dark, thumb-width holes of their ears, tuned to the low, resonant hum of the venting seeps.

And the hands. Their hands are the truth of them. Their arms are short, incredibly heavily muscled, ending in broad, palmate appendations with four thick, thumb-like digits of equal length. You watch a builder working a fresh channel into the soft basalt, and you understand that these are not hands meant for grasping. They are *pressing* tools. *Spreading* tools. The builder places a hand flat against the stone, the webbing between their fingers stretching wide, and they lean their entire dense body weight into it. The stone yields. It accepts the impression of the hand, and the builder holds the pose—frozen, breathing the warm iron-water through their throat-slits—for a full hour, letting the slow pressure do what a chert blade could never do: convincing the stone to remember a new shape.

They have no pockets because they have nothing to carry that cannot be held in a hand or tucked into a fold of their own skin. They possess no sense of hoarding. Their tools—the fifteen distinct forms of cutting, grinding, and scoring stones—are communal, left resting in the niches of the soft walls where the stone has comfortably swallowed their handles up to the blade. When a builder needs a tool, they feel along the wall until their flat palm brushes the familiar shape. The city itself is their toolbox, their larder, their wardrobe. 

Their social fabric is as intimate and un-bounded as the soft stone they inhabit. You feel them before you see them in the dimness. A vibration propagates through the matrix of the rock, a low-frequency pulse that strikes your own bones a second before a shape materializes from the green murk. They greet one another not with words, but by pressing their broad, flat hands against the shared wall of a chamber, feeling the presence of the other through the breathing stone. A wall here is not a barrier; it is a membrane. When two builders work on opposite sides of the same soft partition, they synchronize their pressure, leaning into the rock in alternating rhythms, a slow, tactile conversation of weight and yield. They are never truly alone; the city holds the imprint of their bodies in its walls, and the constant, low hum of the mineral seeps is scored with the subtle vibrations of their movement.

When they speak—when they actually vocalize—it is an event of gravity. You hear it rarely, perhaps once in a long-light cycle. A builder will pause in their work, turn their eyeless face toward the central chambers, and release a sound. It begins as a tremor in their massive chest, a resonance that vibrates the water against your skin, and then it opens into a tone, a sustained, baritone note that finds the harmonic frequency of the chamber they stand in. The walls vibrate in sympathy. The sound is not a shout to the far distance; it is a tuning of the immediate space. A phrase of greeting is a low, reverberating chord that makes the light-filtering fans overhead tremble. A phrase of mourning—sung when a great grazer finally sinks to the soft bottom to be harvested—is a descending minor tone that seems to slow the very water, pulling the warmth inward, making the chambers contract. When they sing, they sing the city into tune. 

They age, but their aging is a process of settling rather than decay. An elder builder is not frail; they are dense. They have spent their long life pressing their hands into the soft stone, and the stone has pressed back, compacting their tissues, calcifying their broad hands and heavy jaws until they resemble the hard chert nodules they chip for tools. The eldest among them no longer work the fresh stone; they simply sit in the favored, beautifully-tuned chambers, where the hum of the seeps is a perfect chord, and their own vibrations have made the walls smooth as glass. They become architectural features. They become the sound they inhabit. Sometimes, a younger builder will press their hand against the wall where an elder rests, and hold it there, feeling the slow, subsonic rhythm of the elder's breathing thrumming through the shared rock, the same rock that is still making itself, the same rock that accepts them both as part of the thought.

They are not builders in the way the surface world understands the term. They do not impose their will on raw materials. They are *collaborators in emergence*. They live in the thinking of the water, and their bodies—dense, patient, unadorned, fluent in pressure—are simply the most articulate pieces of the thought yet to harden.
