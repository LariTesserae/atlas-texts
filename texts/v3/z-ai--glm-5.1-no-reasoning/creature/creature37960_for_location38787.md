What lives here is not a creature. It is a residue. It is the ghost not of a being, but of an action.

They are gone. The hands are gone. The soft, sweating, breathing bodies that pressed through these channels have long since been ground away by the very monotony they created. Flesh cannot survive a stasis this complete; life requires flux, requires a margin in which to change, and this place has no margins. So the makers perished, or departed, or dissolved into the heat they produced. But the *making* did not.

What lives here is the gesture.

If you were to compression-fit your body into one of those narrow channels—if you were to press your cheek against the greasy basalt, to feel the heat bleed into you until you could no longer distinguish the temperature of your skin from the temperature of the stone—you would begin to sense them. Not as presences. Not as apparitions. You would sense them as *intention*. The walls are so dense with inscription that the original impulse of the carving has become embedded in the mineral itself, the way a magnet's field becomes embedded in iron.

There is a rhythm in the gouges. Not a decorative pattern—nothing so deliberate as that—but a muscular rhythm. The strike-and-scrape. The strike-and-scrape. You can feel, in the depth and angle of each chisel-wound, the exact posture of the arm that made it. The way the elbow locked. The way the shoulder braced. The way the wrist turned at the end of each stroke to lever the stone-chip free. These walls are a record of every motion, preserved with a fidelity that no memory could match. The body of the maker is not here, but the body's *decisions* are here, each one frozen in the topology of a tool-mark.

And there are so many. So many that they constitute a kind of population.

The earliest marks—the ones at the deepest strata, visible only where later carving has accidentally exposed them—are different from the later ones. They are broader, more confident. The chisel was larger, or the hand was stronger, or the intention was clearer. These are the marks of makers who believed they were *shaping* something, who had a plan, who were excavating a space for a purpose. You can feel the purpose in the strike—each one efficient, directed, economical. These makers knew what they wanted.

But above them—over them, through them, obliterating them—the later marks become something else.

They become desperate.

Not the desperation of panic—there is no hurry in these marks, no frantic energy. This is the desperation of compulsion. The chisel-strokes grow smaller, finer, more numerous. The hands that made them were working closer to the surface, re-working what had already been worked, carving not to change the shape of the chamber but simply to *continue carving*. The gesture has become its own purpose. The hand moves because the hand moves. The chisel strikes because the chisel strikes. The makers are no longer building; they are maintaining a loop, caught in the same recursive obsession that later generations will recognize in the walls—carving over carving over carving, each layer identical in its insistence.

And in the very latest marks—the topmost palimpsest, the final pass of the chisel before the hands stopped forever—there is something terrible. There is *accuracy*. The strokes are so precise, so uniform, so perfectly matched to the strokes beneath them that they might have been made by the same hand, in the same hour, across an impossible gulf of time. The makers have become indistinguishable from their own tool-marks. They have been doing this for so long that there is no longer any difference between the hand and the chisel and the wall. They are a single system. A single process. A single gesture, repeated until the repeater evaporated and only the repetition remained.

That is what lives here.

It is not alive in any way that life is understood. It does not eat. It does not breathe—how could it, in a lung that never exhales? It does not reproduce, or grow, or respond to stimuli. It has no interiority, no consciousness, no will. But it persists. It persists the way a river persists: not because it is the same water, but because the channel endures. The gesture endures. The strike-and-scrape endures, locked into the mineral memory of the walls, reverberating in the endless sound, radiating in the changeless heat.

If you stayed here long enough—if you pressed your body into the channel and held yourself there, against the stone, in the dark, in the roar, in the heat—something would happen. Your own warmth would bleed into the basalt. Your own pulse would begin to synchronize with the standing wave of sound. Your own breathing would slow, stop, become irrelevant. And you would begin to feel it: the impulse. The *necessity*. The need to take up something hard and strike it against the wall, to add your own gouge to the million million gouges already there, to continue the work that was begun by hands you will never see, in a time you cannot imagine, for a purpose that has been forgotten by the very stone that preserves it.

You would want to carve.

And you would not know why.

That is the creature of this place. It is the ghost of a gesture. It is the corpse of an action. It haunts this chamber, not with sounds or apparitions, but with the most infectious thing a maker can leave behind: the unfinished completion of a task that was never truly begun.
