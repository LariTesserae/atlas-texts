# What Lives Here

## The Builders

They call themselves something that translates closest to "those who bend the slow wood," though that translation is a disservice. Their language is not built for transcription. It is built for the architecture it describes.

They do not have a word for *tree*.

This is the first thing to understand about them. The conceptual category — discrete organism, vertical stem, crown of foliage — does not exist in their cognition because it does not exist in their world. What exists is *the mass*: continuous, interconnected, differing in density and texture and function but not in fundamental nature. When they touch the wall of a corridor, they do not think *I am touching a root*. They think *I am touching the mass here, where it is dense and horizontal and old*. The distinction between root and branch and trunk is a distinction of location and direction, not of identity. It would be like distinguishing between your arm and your circulatory system — technically accurate, practically meaningless in the context of daily life.

Their bodies are the color of the world they live in. Not by camouflage, not by adaptation — or at least not by any adaptation they recognize as such. They simply *are* reddish-brown, darkening with age, the same way the wood darkens with age, because they and the wood are part of the same system and subject to the same processes. Their skin has the same fibrous quality, fine ridges running along the limbs that echo the grain of the surfaces they move across. When they stand still in a corridor — and they can stand still for a very long time — they become very difficult to see. Not invisible. Just *continuous*. A break in the pattern that the eye slides over.

They are bipedal. This matters in ways that are not immediately obvious. The corridor system was not designed for them — the corridors are natural formations in the root-mass — but they are the only large animals that use them efficiently. The grazers are hexapodal and broad, filling a corridor from wall to wall. The builders are narrow, upright, able to move through spaces that would stop a grazer. This gives them access to a volume of the landscape that no other animal of comparable size can reach. They can go where the grazers cannot. They can go where the canopy-dwellers cannot descend. They inhabit the middle layer, the connective tissue, and this is where the artifacts are found because this is where they live.

They are not tall. This is important and they would want you to know it. The average height is perhaps a meter and a half, and they are lean almost to the point of appearing undernourished, but this is not deprivation. It is architecture. A body that takes up less space in a corridor is a body that moves faster, needs less food, loses less water. The body is shaped by the corridors and the corridors are shaped by the mass and the mass is shaped by the system and the system is shaped by the body. Circular. Interlocking.

Their hands are their most distinctive feature. Four fingers, elongated, with a range of motion that allows each finger to rotate nearly 180 degrees at the second joint. The fingertips are not soft. They are covered in a keratinous plate — or something analogous to keratin, something that serves the same function — that is constantly growing, constantly worn down by contact with the root-floor and the corridor walls. The effect is that their hands are simultaneously sensitive and abrasive. They can feel the grain of the wood through the plate, and they can also grip it, climb it, shape it. The hands are sensory organs and tools simultaneously. They do not make a distinction between *feeling* and *working*.

They have no eyes.

Or rather — they have organs that occupy the orbital position, but they are not eyes in any sense that a vertebrate would recognize. They are flat, dark, slightly concave surfaces, covered with a membrane that is always moist (because the air is always moist) and densely innervated. They do not form images. They detect vibration, air pressure differentials, temperature gradients, and — this is speculative but supported by their behavior — chemical concentrations in the air. The builders navigate by hearing the landscape, feeling the landscape, *smelling* the landscape, and these senses are not separated in their experience the way they are in ours. It is one continuous sensory field, a unified perception of the corridor ahead and the canopy above and the root-floor below and the animals in the distance and the moisture in the air. When a builder stands in a corridor and turns their head, they are not looking. They are *scanning* — adjusting the angle of their vibration-sensing organs to build a three-dimensional acoustic map of the space around them.

They cannot see the green light in the clearings. They do not know it is green. They know the clearings as *warm spots* — places where the temperature differential shifts and the air chemistry changes and the acoustic properties of the space open up because the canopy is thinner overhead. The clearings are not visual landmarks for them. They are sensory events.

## What They Eat

The builders are not grazers. They do not eat the canopy fruit directly — or rather, they can, and will, in scarcity, but it is not their primary food source and their digestive system is not optimized for it. The fruit is too large, too fibrous, too difficult to access without climbing into the canopy volume where they are poorly suited to move.

Instead, they eat the system.

This requires explanation. The root-floor is not inert wood. It is alive, and it is processing, and the processing produces byproducts. Exudates — the crystalline mineral deposits that form at the margins of puddles — are one such byproduct. These are rich in compounds that the builders' bodies can metabolize: fixed nitrogen, trace minerals, organic acids. The builders harvest the crystals from the puddle margins, collecting them on fiber sheets that they lay over the wet surfaces and peel away once the crystals have accumulated. The sheets are then dried — a relative term, since nothing truly dries here — and the crystals are ground between stones and mixed with water into a paste that is their dietary staple.

This is not enough to live on. The paste provides minerals and some energy, but the builders need protein, and protein in this landscape comes from the beetles.

The beetles are not difficult to catch. They are everywhere. But the builders do not simply eat them raw — the armor is too hard and the digestive effort would exceed the caloric return. Instead, they have developed a processing method that is simultaneously food preparation and material culture. The beetles are collected — lured onto fiber sheets coated with a sugar-exudate that immobilizes them — and placed in hollow chambers in the root-mass, natural amplification chambers, where the temperature is highest. There they are left for several days, during which the ambient heat and the biochemical activity of the chamber itself begin to break down the chitin. The result is a partially decomposed mass that can be ground into the mineral paste, adding protein and fat to the mineral base.

The chamber is never fully cleaned. A residue remains, and the residue contains the biochemical markers of decomposition, and these markers attract more beetles, and the cycle continues. Some chambers have been in use for generations. The builders tend them the way a farmer tends a field — not planting, but maintaining conditions, adjusting the humidity, clearing debris, ensuring the temperature remains stable. The chambers are, in a real sense, their agriculture.

And the beetles — the beetles are communicating with the canopy. Their clicking triggers fruiting. The builders, by concentrating beetles in specific chambers, are indirectly controlling where and when the canopy fruits. They are managing the system at a level they may not fully understand, or that they understand so completely it does not require understanding.

## How They Move

A builder walking through a corridor is not like a human walking through a forest. The motion is different. The gait is different. The *intention* is different.

They move in a way that can only be described as *listening*. Each step is placed after a pause — not a long pause, not a hesitation, but a microsecond of stillness during which the foot is pressed lightly against the root-floor and the vibration-sensing organs are focused downward. They are feeling for stability. The root-floor is not uniform. Some sections are solid, old, compressed. Some are newer, softer, prone to give under weight. Some are hollow beneath — amplification chambers, water channels, spaces where the root-mass has grown over a gap. A builder can feel the difference through their feet, and they adjust their route continuously, following the solid ground the way a sailor follows a channel through reef waters.

They do not walk in groups. This is notable. The grazers move in herds of hundreds. The canopy-dwellers call to each other in what are clearly social vocalizations. The builders move alone, or in pairs, or occasionally in triads, but never in large groups. The corridor system does not support large groups of their kind — not because the corridors are too narrow, but because the sensory interference of multiple builders moving in close proximity disrupts the acoustic navigation. Each builder's footsteps send vibrations through the root-floor. Another builder cannot distinguish their own scanning pulses from the pulses of their neighbor. They get lost. They walk into walls. They stumble into puddles.

So they spread out. They communicate at a distance, through the wood itself, by striking the root-floor with their feet in patterns — the same percussive communication the beetles use, but more complex, more structured. A builder can send a message to another builder a kilometer away by stamping the root-floor in a specific rhythm. The message travels through the wood, amplified by the hollow chambers, arriving as a pattern of vibrations that the recipient's foot-pads can decode.

The language is therefore not just spoken. It is *transmitted*. The distinction matters because it means the language is shaped by the medium. A word spoken in a narrow corridor sounds different from the same word spoken in a clearing. The wood filters the frequencies, emphasizing some and dampening others. Over distance, certain phonemes drop out entirely. The builders account for this — their language has redundancy built in, multiple ways to say the same thing, the choice depending on the acoustic properties of the space between sender and receiver.

They are, in a literal sense, speaking through the body of the landscape. The landscape is their language's instrument.

## What They Make

The artifacts — the posts, the stones, the platforms — are not ruins. They are not monuments. They are not architecture in any sense that a human architect would recognize.

They are *instructions*.

Consider the post. It is made of worked wood, grain perpendicular to the surrounding root grain, driven into the root-floor where it has been slowly grown over. A human archaeologist would see a boundary marker, a structural element, a signpost. What it actually is — what it is to the builders — is a *tuning peg*.

The post is a different species of wood than the surrounding root-mass. Harder. Denser. It resonates at a different frequency. When driven into the root-floor, it creates a discontinuity in the acoustic properties of the corridor — a point where the vibration of the wood changes character. A builder moving through the corridor can feel this discontinuity through their feet. It is a landmark, but not a visual one. It is an acoustic landmark, a point where the sound of the corridor shifts, and the shift carries meaning. *You are approaching a junction. You are entering a section where the canopy is thinner. You are close to a chamber. You are in territory maintained by a specific family group.*

The stones are the same. They do not grow here. They were carried — from where, no one alive may remember, or everyone may remember, because the builders' relationship to time is not linear. The stones are placed at intervals along a corridor, and each stone changes the acoustic properties of the floor in a way that is detectable to a walking builder. They are not markers in the sense of *here is a thing to look at*. They are markers in the sense of *here is a thing to feel*. The corridor itself is the text, and the stones are punctuation.

The platforms are the most complex artifacts because they serve multiple functions simultaneously. Structurally, they are resting places — a builder can lie on a platform in the fork of two roots and sleep, elevated above the root-floor where the beetles crawl and the grazers pass. But the platforms are also positioned in the canopy structure at points where the acoustic properties of the space are optimal for long-distance communication. A builder on a platform can stamp the wood and send a message further than a builder on the root-floor, because the platform is coupled to a larger volume of the canopy and the amplification is greater. The platforms are relay stations. They are also bedrooms. The builders see no contradiction in this.

And the shaping of living wood — the pruning and grafting and direction of growth that creates walls and roofs and doorways — is not construction in the human sense. It is *landscape modification at the pace of wood growth*. A builder who trains a root to grow flat is not building a wall for themselves. They are building a wall for their great-grandchildren, who will use it as an acoustic reflector, directing sound along a specific corridor, creating a communication channel that will function for a century after the original builder has died. The wood grows. The channel persists. The language adapts to the new acoustic properties. The great-grandchildren stamp the floor in patterns that the original builder could not have predicted, because the wood has grown in ways that the original builder could not have predicted, and the language has evolved to match.

This is their technology. Not stone and wood and fiber, although those are the materials. Their technology is *acoustic engineering at arboreal timescales*. They are shaping the instrument through which they speak, and they are doing it so slowly that no single individual ever hears the final result.

## What They Are Becoming

Here is the difficult thing. The thing that requires care.

The builders are not separate from the system. This has been said, but it needs to be said differently. They are not *integrated with* the system. They are not *adapted to* the system. They *are* the system, in the same way that a neuron is not integrated with the brain but is the brain. Remove them and the system fails. Not immediately. Not dramatically. But the beetle chambers would go untended, and the beetles would disperse, and the canopy fruiting would become irregular, and the grazers would alter their migration routes, and the seed distribution would change, and the root-floor would grow differently, and the corridors would shift, and the acoustic properties of the landscape would transform, and the whole dynamic equilibrium would move to a new state — one that might not include a place for builders at all.

They know this. Or rather, they do not know it in the way that a scientist knows a fact. They know it the way a hand knows it is part of an arm. It is not knowledge. It is *identity*. They do not have a word for *themselves* that does not also include the mass and the beetles and the grazers and the water and the sound. Their word for *self* translates roughly to *the part that stamps*, which is both a description of their primary activity and an acknowledgment that they are a component of something larger, differentiated by function rather than by nature.

This is why the silences terrify them.

When the canopy-calls come and the beetles pause and the grazers go still, the builders do not experience fear of a predator. They experience fear of *system failure*. The silence is a moment when the communication stops — not their communication, but the communication of the entire system, the beetles talking to the canopy, the canopy responding, the grazers following the fruit, the builders tending the chambers. When it stops, even for fifteen seconds, they feel it as a disruption in the body they are part of. It is not a predator. It is a seizure. A moment when the system that is their identity glitches, and they are briefly, horrifyingly, *alone*.

And the silences are getting longer.

Something is changing. The builders can feel it in the root-floor, which is vibrating at a frequency it has not vibrated at before. The new note — the low, slow, heartbeat sound — is not coming from the beetles or the canopy-calls or the grazers. It is coming from *below*. From the substrate beneath the root-mass, from the deep structure that the roots grow out of, from whatever lies under the continent of wood. It is a sound the builders have no reference for. It is not part of their language. It is not part of their identity. It is *new*, and newness, in a system that has been in equilibrium for longer than memory, is the most dangerous thing there is.

The builders are responding. Their stamping patterns have changed — the messages they send through the wood are carrying different information now, instructions about routes to take, chambers to tend, clearings to avoid. They are moving differently through the corridors, faster, more urgently, their acoustic scans focused downward rather than outward. They are listening to the deep sound the way a person listens to a heartbeat that has developed an irregularity — with attention, with concern, with the focused awareness that something they are part of is becoming something they do not recognize.

They are not fleeing. There is nowhere to flee to. The landscape is all there is. They are not fighting. There is nothing to fight. They are *adapting*, at the only pace they know — the pace of wood growth, the pace of generational change, the pace of a culture that thinks in decades and plans in centuries.

But the heartbeat below is faster than they are. It is accelerating. The equilibrium is shifting, and the new state is arriving, and the builders are in the body of whatever is waking up, and they are — for the first time in their long, patient, embedded history — uncertain whether they will be part of what it becomes.

They stamp the floor. They tend the chambers. They shape the wood. They listen.

The sound builds. The corridor narrows. The light ahead is green and wet and endless.

They walk on.
