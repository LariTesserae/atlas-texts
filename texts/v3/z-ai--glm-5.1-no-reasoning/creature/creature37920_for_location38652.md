# What Lives Here

## I. The Holding Pattern

They were not always here. Or rather — they were always here, but not in this shape.

The first thing to understand is that the inhabitants of the Drowned Plateau of Kesh-Toral did not arrive. They *remained.* When the water came — and it came slowly, over centuries, the way a season changes if you watch it for long enough — most things left. The large grazers, the things that needed horizon lines, the things whose bodies were arguments with altitude — they went down the slopes, following the retreating shorelines, and they did not come back. The plateau kept what wanted to stay. Or what couldn't leave. Or what didn't understand the question.

What remained has had ten thousand years to decide what it is.

## II. The Fish

Start with the fish, because they are the most legible.

The schooling fish — the silver bodies that move through the kelp forest like weather — are not one species. They are eleven. Or they were eleven. They are now something that a taxonomist would describe in the language of a custody dispute: *derived from,* *affiliated with,* *showing characters consistent with.* They have been trading genes for so long that the concept of species has become a suggestion, a polite fiction maintained by the parts of their bodies that still bother to be different. The lateral line scales. The pharyngeal tooth count. The precise angle of the operculum. Everything else — the color, the size, the behavior, the timing of the spawn — has converged into a single shared identity that serves them better than distinction would.

They are silver. Not the silver of a clean surface but the silver of a prism — their scales are structured to scatter light in all directions simultaneously, which makes them visible from every angle and from no angle, depending on the water. In the turbid green of the plateau's standard visibility, a school of them appears as a single body, a dark mass, a shadow with interior logic. Individual fish are visible only at the edges, where the school meets the open water, and even there they are indistinct — a flash of silver, a movement, gone.

They are not intelligent in the way that matters to humans. They do not solve problems. They do not remember. But they *decide* — collectively, instantly, with a totality that looks like thought but is actually something older and more distributed. The school reads the water. It reads the light. It reads the pressure gradients that precede the upwellings. It reads the presence of predators through mechanisms that are partly sensory (the lateral line detects displacement), partly chemical (injured fish release alarm substances), and partly something else — a field, a coherence, a thing that has no name because the language that could name it hasn't been invented by anything that needs it. The school is a single animal made of forty thousand mouths and eighty thousand eyes, and it moves through the kelp forest like a rumor through a crowd: fast, coherent, and impossible to pin to any single source.

They feed on the zooplankton that concentrates in the upwelling zones. The upwellings are predictable — they rise through the same fissures in the basalt, in the same volumes, at the same intervals, driven by the same geological heat sources that have been stable for longer than the fish have been fish. The schools know where the upwellings are. They cycle between them in a pattern that takes approximately six hours to complete, which is the time it takes for the zooplankton to re-concentrate after a school has passed through and stripped the water to its mineral residue. Six hours. Six hours. Six hours. The rhythm is as regular as breathing, and it is breathing — the plateau's breathing, translated into movement, translated into hunger, translated into the silver flash of forty thousand bodies turning simultaneously in the green water.

The individual fish live three years. They spawn in the kelp canopy, in the upper twenty meters, where the light is strongest and the water is warmest — relatively, which means nine and a half degrees instead of nine. The eggs are broadcast into the water column, and the currents carry them, and the ones that settle in the right substrate — soft, organically rich, the compost of old kelp — survive, and the others don't, and the ratio has been stable for so long that the population never changes. The schools are always the same size. Not approximately the same size. The *same* size. If you could count them — and you can't, but if you could — the number would be the same this year as last year as five hundred years ago, because the plateau's carrying capacity is fixed, and the fish have calibrated themselves to it with the precision of a machine.

They do not know they are here. They do not know the plateau is here. They know the kelp, and the light, and the temperature gradients, and the taste of the upwelling water, and the pressure of the school around them, and the presence or absence of the things that eat them. They know these things totally and exclusively. The plateau is their universe, and they are its most numerous citizens, and they will never understand either of these facts.

## III. The Predators

The things that eat the schooling fish are fewer, and larger, and operate on a different timescale.

There is a fish — it has no common name, because there are no commoners here, no one to do the naming — that lives alone in the kelp forest and hunts the schools at their edges. It is the color of the water: green-brown, mottled, with a pale belly that disappears against the bright surface when viewed from below. It is perhaps a meter and a half long, which is large for a fish but not absurd, and it moves with a slowness that seems at odds with its function until you watch it hunt. It does not chase the school. It *waits beside* the school. It positions itself at the edge of the silver mass, close enough that individual fish are touching its scales, and it waits, and it waits, and it waits, and then it turns its head — a single, sudden, lateral movement, less than a tenth of a second — and a fish is gone. The school does not scatter. The school adjusts. The school moves, collectively, half a body-length away from the predator, and continues.

The predator eats one fish every two days. It does not eat more. It has no competitors for this prey — the other predators on the plateau target different species, different sizes, different zones — and so it has no reason to gorge, to stockpile, to behave as though food is uncertain. Food is not uncertain. Food is the single most reliable thing in this ecosystem, more reliable than the light, more reliable than the temperature, more reliable than the substrate. The upwellings feed the plankton. The plankton feeds the schools. The schools feed the predator. The chain is short and straight and unbreakable, and the predator lives at the end of it with the calm of something that has never known hunger.

There are four hundred of these predators. Not approximately four hundred. *Four hundred.* They do not interact with each other. They do not need to. Each one occupies a territory of approximately one square kilometer of kelp forest, and the territories are spaced with a regularity that suggests either territorial aggression or some form of chemical signaling that maintains distance, but no one has ever seen two of them in the same place at the same time, so the mechanism is unknown. They are ghosts. Large, calm, green-brown ghosts that haunt the kelp columns and eat one fish every two days and do nothing else.

There are other predators. A larger species — two meters, perhaps two and a half — that hunts the solitary fish, the ones that live between the kelp stalks and do not school. This predator is faster, more active, and less visible. It is dark — nearly black — and it moves through the kelp forest in a way that suggests it knows the architecture, the gaps, the corridors between the stalks, the places where the fronds are dense enough to hide in. It is the thing that the solitary fish are afraid of, if fish are capable of fear, which is a question that the plateau answers with a shrug: *they behave as if they are afraid, and behavior is all anyone has.*

And there are the things that are harder to categorize.

## IV. The Octopuses

The octopuses are the plateau's other engineers. The description says this. It is correct but incomplete.

They are not one species. They are at least three, possibly more — the taxonomy is complicated by the fact that they do not want to be studied, and they are better at not being studied than anything else on the plateau is at anything. They can change color, yes. They can change texture, yes. They can fit through any opening larger than their beak, yes. These are baseline octopus capabilities, the things that all octopuses do, and the plateau's octopuses do them with the fluency of creatures that have been doing them for longer than the human structures have been ruins.

But they also build.

The dens are the first sign. They are everywhere — in the rock piles, in the crevices of the basalt, in the collapsed pipeline, in the rusted machinery inside the largest building. A den is not just a shelter. It is a construction. The octopuses collect stones — specific stones, of specific sizes, selected for weight and flatness — and they stack them around the entrance of a crevice until the opening is exactly the size of their body, and then they arrange smaller stones and shells and fragments of kelp holdfast on top of the larger stones, creating a roof, a wall, a barrier that is both camouflage and defense. The dens are invisible until you know what to look for, and even then, they are visible only as a slight regularity in the rock pile, a geometry that is too clean for geology.

Some dens are simple. Some are not.

In the ruins of the largest building — the rectangular structure on the eastern edge — there is a den that has been occupied, by a succession of octopuses, for what may be centuries. The current occupant is a female, approximately two meters arm-tip to arm-tip, colored the red-brown of the rusted iron she lives in. Her den is in the base of what was once a boiler — a large iron vessel, now collapsed, its sides open to the water but its base intact, forming a natural cavity. She has modified this cavity. The entrance is a single opening, the size of a dinner plate, walled with stones she has carried from twenty meters away. Inside, the den is divided into two chambers by a stone wall she built. The outer chamber is empty — a vestibule, a hallway, a kill room where she brings captured crabs and disassembles them. The inner chamber is where she sleeps, and it is lined — floored, walled, ceilinged — with the shells of the crabs she has eaten, arranged by size, the largest at the bottom, the smallest at the top, forming a mosaic that is smooth to the touch and that she maintains with the same attention that she maintains everything else.

She has been in this den for four years. She will die in it, or near it, and when she dies, another octopus will take it. The den will be maintained. The stones will be re-stacked. The shells will be — not replaced, exactly, but *curated.* The new occupant will remove some, keep others, add its own. The den is a document. It is a record of consumption, of preference, of the specific decisions made by specific animals over a span of time that no single octopus could comprehend but that the den itself comprehends, in the way that a wall comprehends the bricks that make it up: one at a time, without knowing the total.

The octopuses do not teach. They do not communicate den-building to their young. The young — tiny, planktonic, drifted by the currents — settle on the substrate and grow and begin to build dens on their own, independently, without instruction, as if the knowledge is already in the eight arms, already in the suckers, already in the neural architecture that processes touch and shape and weight. And yet the dens improve. The oldest dens are more complex than the newest. The techniques — the stone selection, the stacking patterns, the use of shells as lining — have become more sophisticated over time, over generations, over centuries, in a way that should not be possible without cultural transmission and that *is* possible, somehow, because it is happening, because the dens are better than they used to be, and no one is teaching anyone how to build them.

This is not a mystery that the plateau is interested in solving. It is simply a fact. The octopuses build. They build better than their ancestors did. They do not know why. They do not know that they are improving. They know stones, and spaces, and the satisfaction of a wall that fits, and the dark, and the cold, and the crabs that come to the den's entrance and are caught and eaten and added to the record.

## V. The Crabs

The crabs are the plateau's argument with itself.

There are six species. They are all the same species. The distinction is a matter of opinion, and the opinions vary depending on whether the opinion-holder is looking at the shell morphology, the genetic markers, the behavior, or the — this is the real complication — the *tools.*

The crabs use tools. Not all of them. Not in the way that a primate uses tools, with intention and planning and the recognition that a thing will be useful later. The crabs use tools the way a body uses a bone: reflexively, structurally, without the awareness that the tool is separate from the body.

The largest crabs — the dinner-plate-sized ones — carry stones. Not randomly. They carry specific stones, of specific shapes, in their claws, and they use them to crack open the shells of the bivalves that encrust the rocky substrate. The stone is held in the right claw — always the right, even in individuals where the right claw is smaller, which suggests the preference is neurological, not mechanical — and the bivalve is held in the left, and the crab brings the stone down on the shell with a motion that is not learned, that is present in juveniles from the first time they encounter a bivalve, that is as much a part of being a crab as having ten legs.

But the stones vary. Some crabs prefer round stones. Some prefer flat. Some prefer stones with a sharp edge, which they use to pry, not to crack. The preferences are consistent within individuals but vary between individuals, and — this is the part that causes the argument — they vary *by region.* The crabs on the eastern side of the plateau, near the ruined building, use round stones. The crabs on the western side, near the platform, use flat stones. The crabs in the center, near the pipeline, use sharp-edged stones. The division corresponds to nothing geological — the same stone types are available everywhere — and it corresponds to nothing biological — the bivalves are the same species across the plateau. It corresponds only to location, which is to say it corresponds to *tradition,* which is to say it corresponds to something that is transmitted between generations by means that are not genetic and not taught and not learned and yet are clearly, observably, happening.

The crabs fight. They fight constantly. They fight over territory, over food, over stones. The fights are ritualized — the claws are raised, the bodies are tilted, the threat displays are performed, and usually one crab retreats before contact is made. Usually. Sometimes they fight for real, and claws are lost, and crabs can regrow claws but the regrown claw is smaller, always smaller, and a crab with a small right claw still holds its stone in the right claw, and the stone drops more often, and the bivalves escape, and the crab goes hungry, and hunger makes the crab more aggressive, and the crab fights more, and loses more claws, and this is the plateau's only real drama, the only genuine tragedy in an ecosystem that is otherwise as stable as a machine: the crab that loses a claw and enters the spiral, each loss making the next loss more likely, each hunger making the next fight more necessary, until the crab is a small, furious, one-clawed animal running through the kelp forest looking for a fight it cannot win.

This happens. It is not common. It is not rare. It happens at a rate that the population can absorb, and the crabs that die this way are eaten by the things that eat crabs — the octopuses, mostly, and a species of benthic fish that is too unremarkable to describe except to say that it is the color of the mud it lives in and it has a mouth that faces downward and it is patient in a way that nothing else on the plateau is patient, which is a different kind of patience, the patience of a trap, not the patience of a stone.

## VI. The Kelp

The kelp is not an animal. This needs to be said because, after enough time on the plateau, the distinction becomes unclear.

The kelp is the architecture. The kelp is the forest, the canopy, the substrate, the food, the shelter, the dead and the living. The kelp is the thing that every other organism is oriented toward, the way organisms on land are oriented toward the sun. The kelp *is* the sun, here — it is the thing that captures the light, that converts it to sugar, that distributes the energy through the system. Everything else is downstream of the kelp.

It grows fast. Three centimeters a day, in the growth phase, which is continuous — there is no season, no dormancy, no cycle. The kelp grows and dies and grows and dies and the rate of growth exactly matches the rate of death, and the forest is always the same height, always the same density, always the same age, which is no age, which is every age, which is the age of the plateau itself.

Individual stalks live eighteen months. They rise from holdfasts anchored in the substrate — the soft, rich, living mud of the plateau floor — and they grow upward, thirty meters, to the surface, where their fronds spread and capture the light. The fronds are the productive surface — broad, flat, dark green, structured to extract maximum photosynthate from the minimum light, and they are productive, absurdly productive, generating biomass at a rate that should not be possible in nine-degree water under storm-filtered light. But the upwellings feed them. The mineral-rich water rising through the basalt delivers nitrogen and phosphorus and iron in concentrations that the kelp has been calibrated to exploit, and the kelp exploits them, completely, with the efficiency of a thing that has been optimizing for the same resource for ten thousand years.

When a stalk dies — and they die suddenly, the holdfast releasing, the entire thirty-meter column falling slowly, gracefully, settling against its neighbors like a felled tree — the decomposition begins within hours. The substrate is full of organisms whose sole function is to disassemble kelp. Bacteria. Fungi. Nematodes. Polychaete worms. Bivalves that have adapted to filter kelp particles from the water column. The decomposition is fast — a fallen stalk is gone in three weeks, reduced to its component molecules, which are reabsorbed into the upwelling water and carried back to the surface and taken up by the next stalk that grows in the same spot, which it will, because the holdfast leaves a scar on the substrate, and the scar is a nutrient hotspot, and the next spore that settles there grows faster and stronger than it would anywhere else, and so the kelp columns are always in the same places, have always been in the same places, will always be in the same places, which means the forest has an architecture that is older than any individual tree in it, older than any species that lives in it, a design that was established by the first generation of kelp and has been maintained by every generation since, which is — what? Ten thousand generations? Twenty thousand? More?

The kelp does not know this. The kelp does not know anything. The kelp is a plant, or something like a plant, and it grows toward the light and it dies and it grows again and it does not think and it does not remember and yet the forest remembers, somehow, in the pattern of its columns, in the spacing of its stalks, in the density of its canopy, the forest remembers where it was, and it is still there.

## VII. The Large Things

There are large things on the plateau. The description mentions sounds — deep, rhythmic pulses, infrequent, unscheduled. These are real. They are not geological. Or — they may be partly geological, the resonance of water moving through the porous basalt, but they are also partly biological, and the biological component is the part that no one has been able to source, because the water is too turbid and the plateau is too large and the things that make the sounds are too far away or too deep or too something.

What is known:

The sounds occur at depths between forty and eighty meters. They occur in the water column, not on the substrate — the resonance pattern is wrong for a bottom-dweller, right for something suspended or swimming. They are low-frequency, in the range of ten to thirty hertz, which is below the hearing range of most fish but within the range of the schooling species' lateral line system, and the schools *react* to them. When a pulse occurs, the nearest school changes direction. Not dramatically. Not in panic. The school turns, as a body, fifteen to twenty degrees off its previous heading, and continues. The turn is smooth, coordinated, immediate. It is a reflex. It is a response to a signal that the school has been receiving for as long as the school has existed, and the school has been receiving it for as long as the thing producing it has been producing it, which is — unknown. Long.

The sounds are not communication. Or if they are communication, the message is very simple, something like *here* or *moving* or *I am this large and I am displacing this much water and if you are smaller than me you should adjust your position.* The sounds do not vary in frequency or duration. They do not cluster into patterns that could be interpreted as language. They are single pulses, separated by intervals ranging from four hours to eleven days, and the intervals do not correlate with anything — not the tides, not the storms, not the upwelling cycle, not the schools' movements, not the time of day (which is meaningless here anyway, the light being constant in its green-gray ambiguity).

The best guess, and it is a guess, is that there are — two? Three? — very large animals on the plateau, living in the deeper water at the margins, where the plateau slopes down toward the abyssal floor. They are large enough to displace significant water with each movement. They are rare enough that their paths do not cross often, which is why the pulses are infrequent. They are — possibly — the same species that has been here since before the flood, the thing that was here when the plateau was dry, the thing that was here when the water came and stayed, the thing that did not leave because it did not need to leave because the water was not a problem for it, the water was *its element,* and the plateau's drowning was not a catastrophe but an expansion, a widening of its world from a narrow alpine valley to six hundred meters of living water, and it has been here ever since, moving in the deep, making its sounds, existing at a scale that the rest of the ecosystem can feel but not see, not reach, not comprehend.

Or it is a geological phenomenon. The basalt does resonate. The water does move through fissures at velocities that generate pressure waves. The sounds could be stone. They could be water. They could be the plateau itself, speaking in the only language it has, which is low frequency and infrequent and below the threshold of hearing and felt in the chest and in the lateral lines of fish and in the patience of the water and the patience of the rock and the patience of everything that lives here, which is — all of it — patient, because the plateau teaches patience, because the plateau *is* patience, because the water has been here for ten thousand years and the stone has been here for longer and the kelp has been here for as long as there has been light and the fish have been here for as long as there has been kelp and the crabs have been here for as long as there has been substrate and the octopuses have been here for as long as there has been structure to build in and the large things — if they are things — have been here for as long as there has been here.

## VIII. What Does Not Live Here

Nothing migrates. Nothing on the plateau comes and goes. The schools do not leave. The predators do not leave. The kelp does not drift its spores to other shores. The crabs do not range beyond the plateau's edge. The octopuses do not travel. Everything that lives here lives here completely, permanently, without reference to any world outside the water.

The boundary is the slope. Where the plateau's gentle grade steepens into the descent toward the abyssal floor, the water changes — colder, denser, poorer in oxygen and nutrients, free of the upwelling minerals that make the plateau an oasis. The kelp stops at the boundary. The substrate changes from the soft, organically rich compost of the plateau floor to the bare, hard, gray basalt of the slope. The fish do not cross it. The crabs do not cross it. The octopuses do not cross it.

It is not a wall. It is not a barrier. It is simply the place where the plateau stops being the plateau, and everything that lives on the plateau lives on the plateau and does not leave, because there is nothing outside the plateau that is relevant to anything that lives on the plateau, and there has never been, and the instinct to explore — if it ever existed in any of these species — has been bred out, or trained out, or simply atrophied from disuse, the way eyes atrophy in caves, the way legs atrophy in burrows, the way everything that is not used eventually disappears, and the instinct to leave has disappeared, and what remains is the instinct to stay, which is not an instinct at all but a condition, a state of being, the default setting of an ecosystem that has been complete for longer than the structures on its floor have been ruins.

The plateau is a closed system. Not thermodynamically — the upwellings bring energy from below, the light brings energy from above, the storms bring turbulence from the surface — but biologically. Ecologically. The species that are here are the species that have always been here, evolving in place, responding to each other and to the water and to the stone and to nothing else, becoming more themselves with every generation, more specifically adapted to this exact combination of temperature and pressure and turbidity and mineral chemistry and light quality and current pattern, more precisely fitted to this exact place, until the fit is so exact that the organisms and the environment are not really separate things anymore, they are one thing, one system, one body, and the body is the plateau, and the plateau is alive, and what lives on it is not its inhabitants but its *organs.*

The fish are the plateau's circulation. The kelp is its lungs. The crabs are its immune system, constantly fighting, constantly defending, constantly maintaining the boundaries between self and other. The octopuses are its hands. The large things — if they are things — are its heartbeat, slow, deep, infra-slow, the rhythm that everything else moves to without knowing it.

And the structures — the human structures, the ruins — are what? Scars? Splinters? Old wounds that the body has absorbed and incorporated and is slowly, slowly dissolving, the way a body dissolves a splinter, drawing it in, breaking it down, converting it to self, to stone, to substrate, to the mud that the kelp grows in?

Yes. That.

## IX. The Feeling of It

If you were here — and you cannot be here, not really, not in the way that the fish are here, not in the way that the kelp is here, but if you were here, in the water, in the green light, in the cold that soaks into your joints and stays — you would feel something that is not loneliness and is not companionship and is not awe and is not fear. It is the feeling of being inside something that is complete. That is the word. *Complete.* The plateau is complete. It does not need you. It did not need the people who built the structures. It does not need the storms or the seasons or the surface or the sky. It has what it has. It is what it is. The water moves through the stone. The kelp grows toward the light. The fish cycle between the upwellings. The crabs fight. The octopuses build. The large things pulse in the deep. The structures dissolve. The stone becomes sand. The sand becomes substrate. The substrate becomes kelp. The kelp becomes fish. The fish become predators. The predators become waste. The waste becomes upwelling. The upwelling becomes kelp.

This is not a cycle. A cycle implies return, repetition, the same thing happening again. This is not the same thing happening again. This is the same thing happening *continuously,* without beginning or end, without stages, without phases, the conversion of stone to kelp to fish to waste to water to stone happening all the time, everywhere, simultaneously, in a single sustained process that is the plateau's life and the plateau's death and the plateau's persistence, all three, all at once, all the time, and if you were here you would feel it, in your chest, in the hum, in the drone of the water moving through the stone, and you would understand that the plateau is not a place where things live. The plateau is a thing that is alive, and what lives on it is not separate from it, and what dies on it is not separate from it, and the distinction between living and dying and being is a distinction that the plateau does not recognize, because the plateau is past dying and past living and into the third state, the state of persistence, the state of being, and being is what the plateau does, and it does it completely, and it does it alone, and it has been doing it for ten thousand years, and it will do it for ten thousand more, and the fish will cycle and the kelp will grow and the crabs will fight and the octopuses will build and the large things will pulse in the deep, and none of them will know that they are the plateau, and the plateau will not know that it is them, and this — this not-knowing, this mutual ignorance, this shared blindness to the fact that they are one thing — is the closest thing to peace that this place will ever know, and it is very close, and it is enough.
