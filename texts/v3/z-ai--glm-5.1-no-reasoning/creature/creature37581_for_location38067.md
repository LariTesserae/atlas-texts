# What Lives in the Below-Market

---

The first thing to understand is that no one who lives in the Below-Market was born there with any intentionality behind it. No one chose it. Or rather—some did, once, long enough ago that the choosing has become ancestry, and ancestry has become condition, and condition has become identity, and now the question of whether anyone *chose* this is as meaningless as asking whether a kidney chose the body.

They are not a single kind of people. That is the second thing. They are several kinds, or they were, once, before the centuries of shared air and shared stone and shared diet blurred the differences into something subtler than ethnicity, something that shows in the set of the jaw and the particular economy of gesture more than in color or build. If you could line them up—avatars of the Below-Market's population across the centuries—you would watch them converge. The tall ones got shorter. The pale ones got browner. The ones with narrow eyes got narrower still, squinting against the perpetual smoke. They became something specific: a people shaped by a place, the way a river shapes its own banks.

They are not large people. This is not a deficiency. It is an adaptation so obvious it barely merits mention, except that it shapes everything: the way they move, the way they build, the way they fight when fighting happens (which is rare, because who has room). The aisles of the Below-Market are narrow, and the ceiling in places is low, and the overhead clutter of hanging goods means that a tall person would spend their life ducking, would develop a permanent stoop, would eventually become short anyway through the patient compression of years. So they are short. Most of them. The children who grew too fast, who lengthened beyond the market's tolerance—those children learned to navigate the vertical spaces, the shelf-tops, the mezzanines, the upper reaches where the pigeons rule. Some of them stayed up there. There are families who have not touched the floor in three generations, who move across a lattice of planks and ropes and shelf-edges like something between spider and acrobat, who have their own stalls up near the smoke-haze ceiling, dealing in the things that drift upward—lost coins, feathers, the dried husks of insects that the beetles missed. They are thin, these upper-people, and quick, and they speak in a register that carries over the market-noise without competing with it: a high, piercing chatter that the floor-dwellers have learned to filter out or attend to, depending on the news.

But most of them live on the floor. Or rather, *in* the floor—in the stalls, which are not just workplaces but homes, which contain beneath their counters and behind their goods the curled-up mattresses and hanging hammocks and padded nooks where sleep happens. The stall and the home are the same thing. This is not poverty; it is efficiency. When your life is organized around transaction, around the perpetual exchange that keeps the market's metabolism going, you do not separate the place where you sell from the place where you sleep, because the selling never stops and the sleeping is merely a lower gear of the same machine. A vendor curls up beneath her shelf of dried fish and naps for an hour while the market flows around her, and someone wanting fish wakes her with a foot against her shoulder, and she sits up and makes the sale and lies back down. The transition between states is seamless. She does not dream of elsewhere. She dreams of fish.

---

They are not clean. This needs to be said plainly, because the romance of underground markets sometimes casts them as picturesque in their grime, and that is not what this is. The grime is not picturesque. It is the condition of their bodies. They are covered in it—not uniformly, not equally, but pervasively, in the way that anyone who lives in smoke and animal proximity and perpetual damp and close quarters with other sweating bodies will be covered in it. Their skin holds the residue of the air: a film of smoke and tallow and mineral sweat and the oils of whatever they handle most. The fishmongers smell of fish even after scrubbing, because the smell has entered the grain of their skin, has become part of their microbiome, has colonized the folds of their elbows and the creases of their necks. The herbalists smell of the herbs they crush and sell—not pleasantly, not like a garden, but like a warehouse, like concentrated vegetal matter pressed into a confined space. The potters carry clay in the cracks of their hands and under their nails in a way that no washing fully removes, because the clay is part of the geography of their skin now.

Their clothing is functional in the extreme. Layers are the logic—multiple thin garments rather than single thick ones, because the temperature varies from zone to zone, from brazier-adjacent to water-seep, and you must be able to add and subtract warmth as you move. The garments are not beautiful. They are repaired and repaired and repaired until the original fabric is more patch than foundation, and then the patches become the fabric, and the garment continues. Everything is worn until it is worn through, and then it is worn a while longer, and then it is cut down into something smaller—a child's wrap, a cleaning rag, a bandage—and then it is composted or burned or fed to something. Nothing that enters the Below-Market as textile leaves it as textile. It is transformed, step by step, into heat and soil and smoke.

Their hands are remarkable. This is where the beauty is, if you are looking for it. Not in their faces, which are often drawn and smoke-lined and prematurely aged, but in their hands. The hands of a Below-Market vendor are the primary tools of existence, and they show it. They are thick-knuckled, calloused in specific patterns that reveal the person's trade—the fishmonger's knife-calluses on the forefinger and thumb, the potter's clay-horn on the heel of the palm, the herbalist's green-stained fingertips, the bone-reader's smooth-polished fingerpads from years of handling aged bone. The hands are not graceful in the way that fine-boned hands are graceful. They are graceful in the way that a well-designed hinge is graceful: they move with precision and economy and no wasted motion, because every motion is a transaction with the physical world, and waste is not affordable.

Their eyes are adapted. This is literal, not poetic. The light in the Below-Market is low and warm-spectrum—oil lamps, tallow candles, the ember-glow of charcoal—and the eyes of its inhabitants have spent generations in this light. Their pupils are large. Their color perception has shifted toward the red-and-amber end of the spectrum; blues and greens are duller to them, less navigable. When one of them ascends to the surface—which happens, rarely, for goods that cannot be obtained below, for the disposal of things that cannot be composted or burned—they squint and ache and feel the daylight as a kind of assault. They come back down as quickly as they can, blinking, rubbing their eyes, speaking of the above-world the way someone might speak of a foreign country: interesting to visit, unbearable to stay.

---

They speak a language that is not anyone's language. Or rather, it was once several languages, and the Below-Market has mashed them together over the centuries the way it mashes everything together—by proximity, by necessity, by the patient pressure of bodies in confined spaces. The result is a creole so dense with borrowed vocabulary that no surface-speaker can fully parse it, even when they recognize individual words. The fishmonger counts in one language's numerals, bargains in another's, curses in a third, and switches between them mid-sentence without noticing, because the switching is not stylistic but functional: each language contributed the words that worked best for each task, and those words survived while the rest eroded away.

The language has sounds that don't exist in any parent tongue. They have evolved in response to the acoustic environment—which is loud, layered, reverberant, and full of animal noise. To be heard in the Below-Market, a sound must carry. It must cut through the ambient roar without competing with it directly. The result is a set of phonemes that are high, sharp, and percussive: clicks, glottal stops, a kind of forced whisper that carries further than a shout in the dense air. The upper-people have their own register—even higher, even sharper, designed to travel across the market's full vertical span. When a floor-dweller and an upper-person converse, the contrast is striking: the floor-dweller speaks in the mid-range forced whisper, the upper-person in a piercing chatter that sounds, to untrained ears, like a bird or a very angry child.

They have a gesture-language too, because there are zones where the noise is too thick for speech, and because some transactions are better conducted without advertising their terms. This gesture-language is not a full sign language—it has no grammar of its own, depends on the syntax of the spoken language for structure—but its vocabulary is extensive, and it is conducted with those remarkable hands. A flick of the fingers: *how much*. A closed fist rotated: *too expensive*. A palm turned upward, fingers spread: *I have nothing to hide* or *show me*, depending on context. Two fingers tapping the wrist: *time to go* or *the watchman is coming*, depending on which watchman, and which direction the fingers point.

There are watchmen. There have to be. But they are not what you would expect.

---

The watchmen are old. Not old in the way of institutions—old in the way of bodies. The watchmen of the Below-Market are its eldest inhabitants, its retired vendors, its specialists in memory. They do not enforce law, because there is little law to enforce, because the market's ecology handles most of what a legal system would handle. The fishmonger who short-weights his customers does not need a court. He needs the fact that everyone in the market will know by nightfall—and in the Below-Market, there is no nightfall, so they will know within the hour—and his customers will dwindle, and his stall will shrink, and he will either correct his weights or he will starve. The market regulates itself through the simple mechanism of total information flow. Everyone knows everything about everyone, because everyone is always within earshot.

But the watchmen do something else. They remember.

They remember who owned which stall before the current occupant, and before that, and before that. They remember the year the western wall cracked and the water came in and the grain stores rotted and the market ate through its reserves in a week of hunger that is still spoken of in the tone reserved for near-death. They remember which vendor families have been here longest—and they can recite the genealogies, the line of mothers and daughters and the occasional son who did not leave for the upper-works or the surface, stretching back farther than written records, because the written records were eaten by fungus three centuries ago and only the watchmen's memories survived. They remember the arrangement of stalls, which shifts slowly, imperceptibly, the way a river's course shifts—the fishmonger drifting two stalls east over a decade, the herbalist expanding into the space left by a potter who died and whose children chose the upper-works, the slow ecological migration of goods and people that the market performs the way a plant performs heliotropism, slowly, without will, toward what works.

The watchmen carry staves. Not weapons—walking sticks, really, though they can be weapons if the need arises, which it rarely does. The staves are carved with marks: a notch for each year of service, a symbol for each vendor-family the watchman can recite from memory, a groove for each crisis survived. The oldest staves are more groove than stick. They are record-keeping devices, calendars, mnemonic aids. When a watchman dies, the staff passes to the next eldest, and the notches continue.

There is no head watchman. There is no hierarchy. There are simply the eldest, and the slightly-less-eld, and the order of precedence is determined by memory, not by appointment: whoever can recite the most genealogies, whoever carries the most notches, whoever remembers the crisis that the others have heard of only secondhand—that person is the most listened-to, and this is a fact that everyone acknowledges without needing to formalize it.

---

The children are everywhere, and they are nowhere. That is to say: they are present in every part of the market, because there is no part of the market from which they are excluded, and no one has ever thought to exclude them. But they are not *seen* in the way that children are seen elsewhere, because they do not have a separate category of existence. There is no childhood in the Below-Market in the sense of a sheltered period of play and learning before the commencement of work. There is simply smallness that grows into largeness, and at every stage of growth, the child is part of the market's metabolism.

A child of three sits in her mother's stall and plays with the offcuts—the fish-spines, the herb-stems, the broken pot-rims—and her play is her apprenticeship, because she is learning the materials of her future trade through her fingers, the way her mother learned them, the way her grandmother learned them. By five, she is sorting. By seven, she is selling small things—scraps, cast-offs, the goods too minor for an adult to bother with—to other children, who buy them with other small things, and this is how she learns transaction, not as an abstraction but as a bodily skill, like walking. By ten, she has a voice in the stall's operations. By twelve, she can run it alone for an hour while her mother sleeps. By fifteen, she is a vendor in her own right, or she is something else—an upper-worker, a watchman-in-training, a specialist in one of the niche trades that the market has generated through its own complexity.

The children do not go to school. The market is the school. Everything they need to learn is present: numeracy through counting and weighing, literacy through the labels on goods (a partial, functional literacy, enough to read prices and provenance but not enough to read literature, and no one has ever felt this as a lack), biology through the animals they tend and the herbs they sort, history through the watchmen's recitations, physics through the levers and pulleys and inclined planes that move goods through the market's vertical and horizontal space. The education is not systematic. It is *environmental*. The children absorb what they need by living in the place that contains it, and what they absorb is precisely what they need, because the market has shaped them, and they are shaped for the market, and the fit is exact.

They play. It must be said: they play, because they are children, and children play wherever they are, even in a cathedral-sized stone chamber full of smoke and animals and transaction. But their play is inflected by the environment. They play hide-and-seek in the stalls, which is a game so complex in this setting that it verges on tactical training. They play with the animals, which sometimes goes badly—the goat that butts a child into a shelf of pottery, the cat that scratches—and this too is learning. They play with sound, mimicking the market's noise, layering their own voices into the acoustic strata until they are indistinguishable from the ambient. Once, a group of children invented a game that involved navigating the entire market blindfolded, by ear alone, and within a generation it had become a rite of passage. You are not fully an adult in the Below-Market until you can walk from the fish-stalls to the herb-stalls with your eyes closed.

---

The vendors have an essential sameness that is not uniformity. Let me try to explain. They are all, in a sense, the same person, because they are all shaped by the same pressures: the density, the noise, the perpetual transaction, the absence of solitude. But the sameness expresses itself differently in each of them, the way a single species expresses differently across a varied landscape. The fishmonger and the herbalist and the bone-reader and the pot-mender are all the same kind of creature—Below-Market people, short, sharp-eyed, smoke-perfumed, perpetually alert to opportunity and threat—but they have diverged in the way that the market's ecology has diverged them, each into a niche shaped by the particular demands of their goods.

The fishmonger is loud and fast and has no patience for deliberation, because fish does not wait. She moves with the urgency of someone whose inventory is decaying in real time, and this urgency gives her a particular manner—brisk, decisive, slightly aggressive in her sales, willing to cut a deal quickly because a quick deal at a lower price is better than a slow deal at a higher price and a spoiled fish. Her hands are always wet. Her stall is always wet. She has developed an immunity to the smell that others find unbearable, and when she leaves her stall to buy bread or herbs, she trails the smell behind her like a weather system, and people know she is coming before they see her.

The herbalist is quiet and slow and has the patience of someone whose goods improve with time. She moves with deliberation, speaks with precision, and her transactions have the quality of consultations—she does not just sell herbs, she prescribes them, because she knows what each one does and for what ailment, and her customers come to her not just to buy but to be diagnosed. She has the closest thing to authority that exists in the Below-Market, not formal authority but the authority of knowledge, and people listen when she speaks, even when she is not speaking about herbs, because they assume that her patience and precision extend to all subjects, which is not always true but is true often enough to maintain the reputation.

The bone-reader is something else entirely. She is the closest thing the Below-Market has to a priest, and she would reject this characterization violently, because she is a pragmatist, because she deals in bones, because bones do not deal in the abstract. She reads the bones of fish and goats and the occasional dog—bones that have been cleaned and dried and aged until they hold, she says, the pattern of the animal's life, which is also the pattern of the world that contained the animal, which is also the pattern of the question you are asking. She is probably a fraud. Or she is probably not. It does not matter. What matters is that people consult her, and her consultations are a form of therapy, a structured way of thinking about decisions that the market's relentless present-tense does not otherwise permit. She creates a pause. She creates a space for reflection. She charges for this space, and people pay, and the payment is not for the answer but for the pause.

The pot-mender is the quietest of them all. He sits in his stall surrounded by broken things—shards and fragments and the cracked bodies of pots that have been brought to him for repair—and he works with an adhesive made from rendered fat and limestone and something secret, and his hands move with the slowness and certainty of someone who knows that pressure must be applied gradually, that haste shatters what it means to save. He does not speak unless spoken to, and even then he speaks in the minimum words necessary: *yes*, *no*, *three*, *tomorrow*. His silence is not shyness or sullenness. It is concentration. He is always working, always holding something fragile together, and speech is a vibration he cannot afford.

---

There are people in the Below-Market who are not vendors. Or rather, there are people whose vending is secondary to their other function. The lamp-keepers, for instance: a small cadre—five, six, never more than eight at once—who maintain the hundred small sources of light. They move through the market in a perpetual circuit, refueling lamps, replacing tallow candles, tending braziers, and their rhythm is so steady and so predictable that the market has internalized them as a kind of clock. You know what time it is—I mean what *market-time* it is, which has nothing to do with the sun—by which lamp the keeper is currently tending. They carry oil in skins slung across their shoulders, and the oil smells of whatever fat it was rendered from, and the smell changes as they pass, so you know them by scent before you see them. They are trusted in a way that other people are not, because they touch everyone's lamps, and a lamp is a precious thing, and the trust has hardened into a kind of respect that the lamp-keepers wear without thinking about it.

There are the water-carriers. Different from the lamp-keepers. Younger, mostly—adolescents, people in the years between childhood and full trade, who have the energy for the work. The water comes from the weeping walls and the cisterns and the troughs, but it does not distribute itself. Someone must carry it from where it collects to where it is needed, and this someone is the water-carrier, and the water-carrier is the person you see most often in the aisles, because water is needed everywhere and always. They carry clay jugs—two, one on each hip, balanced—and they move with a particular gait that minimizes sloshing, a rolling walk that looks strange to outsiders but that every Below-Market native recognizes and makes way for, because the water-carrier is always on their way to someone who is thirsty, and thirst in the Below-Market is not an abstraction.

There are the knife-sharpeners. There are two of them, and they hate each other. They have hated each other for so long that the hatred has become heritable, has passed to their children and their children's children, and the origin of the hatred is lost—something about a stolen customer, a century ago, or an insult to a technique—and the hatred is now simply a fact of the market's social geography, the way a fault line is a fact of geology. They occupy opposite ends of the market. Their territories are known. A vendor who needs sharpening goes to one or the other, and the choice is a declaration, and the declaration is remembered. This sounds dramatic. It is not. It is a small, low-level tension that surfaces only in snide remarks and the occasional refusal to share tools. It is the market's equivalent of geopolitics, and it is treated with the appropriate mixture of seriousness and mockery.

---

There is love in the Below-Market. It must be said, because the description so far might suggest a place of function without feeling, and that would be wrong. Love here is not the love of poetry. It is the love of proximity—the love that develops between people who share a stall, who share a shelf, who share the same six feet of aisle for twelve hours a day, who know each other's breathing patterns and sleep-twitches and the exact pitch of their clearing-throat-in-the-morning, which in the Below-Market is the same as the clearing-throat-at-any-hour, because there is no morning. It is the love that arises from the impossibility of privacy. You cannot hide yourself from someone who has watched you sleep for twenty years. You cannot pretend. And the absence of pretense, the nakedness of perpetual exposure, either breeds contempt or breeds something deeper than romance, something that has no word in any language because the languages were built by people who had private spaces, who could withdraw, who could choose when to be seen. The Below-Market people have no word for this love. They simply live it, in the way they pass food to each other without looking, in the way they adjust their bodies to make room without being asked, in the way a fishmonger reaches across three stalls to place a dried fish in the pot-mender's hand and the pot-mender eats it without comment and the fishmonger returns to her work without waiting for thanks.

Marriages happen. They are not ceremonies. They are recognitions—moments when the community collectively acknowledges that two people have become a unit, which usually means they have moved into the same stall, which usually means one of them has expanded their shelf to hold the other's goods. The recognition is not formalized. It is simply noted, by the watchmen, who add a mark to their staves—a different kind of notch, a double one—and by the other vendors, who adjust their mental maps of the market's social geography. From that point, the two people are a stall, and their children belong to the stall, and the stall is the family unit, and the family unit is the economic unit, and these are not separate things.

There is grief. When someone dies—and people die, of infection and injury and the diseases of damp and smoke and proximity—their stall does not close. It cannot close. The market does not permit gaps. What happens is a transition: the goods are distributed to the nearest relevant vendors, the shelf-space is claimed by whoever can use it, the family—if there is family, if the children have not gone to the upper-works or the surface—consolidates into a smaller space. The person is gone, and their place is filled, and this happens within hours, because the market cannot afford a vacuum. But the watchmen remember. And the stall where the person lived and worked retains, for a generation, a quality—a way of being referred to. *That was Fen's stall.* Not *that is Fen's daughter's stall.* *That was Fen's stall.* The geography holds the memory even after the person is gone.

---

And there are the animals. The animals are not pets. They are not livestock, exactly. They are co-citizens, in a way that is hard to explain to someone who has not spent time in the Below-Market but that is immediately obvious to anyone who has. The goats belong to people, but the belonging is mutual—the goat gives milk and hair and eventually meat, and in exchange it receives tethering (which is safety, in a market full of dogs and rats and the sharp corners of stalls) and feed (the scraps that the goat could find on its own but that are gathered for it, which is a form of care). The goats know their people. They bleat when their person approaches and bleat when their person leaves, and this is not affection, exactly, but it is recognition, and recognition is the foundation of everything in the Below-Market.

The cats are not owned. They are tolerated. They tolerate in return. The arrangement is ancient and understood: the cats hunt the rats, the rats eat the grain, the grain feeds the people, the people feed the cats (involuntarily, mostly—scraps taken, not given), and the cycle continues. The cats move through the market with an ease that no human has, because the cats can go anywhere—up onto any shelf, into any stall, across any surface—and no one stops them, because stopping a cat is pointless, and because everyone understands that the cat is doing its work, and its work is necessary. The cats are the market's immune system, and they are treated with the grudging respect accorded to all immune systems: acknowledged, relied upon, not exactly loved.

The dogs are the strangest case. They are not owned in the way that the goats are owned. They are owned in the way that the market owns them, collectively, the way a neighborhood owns its weather. They have no masters. They have territories—specific aisles, specific clusters of stalls—and they defend these territories from other dogs with a ferocity that is ritualized but real. They are small, the Below-Market dogs, because the aisles are narrow and the food is scarce and largeness is a liability. They are efficient. They eat what falls, what is discarded, what is too small to sell. They are alert to the market's rhythms in a way that suggests intelligence, or at least a profound adaptation: they know which stalls give scraps and which do not, they know the sound of a transaction that involves food and they materialize at the buyer's feet, they know the difference between a vendor's foot-step (familiar, ignored) and a stranger's (investigated, possibly followed). They are not pets. But they are names. Every dog in the Below-Market has a name, given by the vendors of its territory, and the names are functional—Scraps, Corner, Lame-Foot, the Black One—and the dogs respond to them, or seem to, or have learned that responding to a name produces food, which is the same thing.

The pigeons are above all of this. The pigeons are above everything. They live in the lost ceiling, in the smoke-haze, in the dark, and they descend to scavenge, and their cooing is the market's baseline note, the sound that is always there beneath all other sounds, the drone that makes the silence of the above-world feel so wrong to Below-Market ears. The pigeons are not loved. They are not hated. They are a condition. They exist the way the stone exists—as part of the place, inseparable, permanent.

---

The people of the Below-Market do not think of themselves as strange. This is important. They do not experience their lives as unusual or deprived or brave. They experience their lives as lives. They wake (from naps, not from sleep, because the sleep is always a nap) and they work and they eat and they talk and they barter and they sit and they stand and they move through the aisles sideways and they breathe the thick air and they smell the thousand smells and they hear the thousand sounds and this is simply what life is, and the idea that life could be otherwise—that there are people who wake in silence, who move through wide spaces, who breathe air that smells of nothing—is not a thought they think, because it is not a thought the market provides.

They have no philosophy. Or rather, their philosophy is so embedded in their practice that it is invisible to them. It amounts to this: everything is connected to everything, everything must be exchanged, nothing leaves the system, nothing enters without cost, and the only way to survive is to be necessary to someone. This is not stated. It is lived. It is the logic of the stall, the logic of the aisle, the logic of the watchman's memory and the lamp-keeper's circuit and the water-carrier's rolling walk. It is the logic of the goat and the cat and the dog and the pigeon and the rat and the beetle and the moth and the vine that grows a few hopeful feet before the smoke stops it.

It is the logic of the Below-Market itself. And the people who live there are its cells, its enzymes, its breath. They are what the market is made of, and the market is what they are made of, and this reciprocity is the only fact that matters, and it has been true for longer than anyone can remember, and it will be true for longer than anyone can foresee, and it is fine.
