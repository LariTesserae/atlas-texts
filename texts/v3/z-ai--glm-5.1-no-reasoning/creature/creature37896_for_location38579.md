Begin with the jewel, because that is what the eye insists on at first: a pendant, a reliquary, a single faceted drop of mineral hanging in the blaze as if the room had been built solely to display it. It is the only object here. Everything else is wall, light, water. So the gaze goes to it the way a drowning gaze goes to a floating lamp, and for a moment the jewel obliges—the facets flare, the filament above it glows, and the chamber seems to be exactly what it looks like: a shrine with one treasure in it.

But stay. The heat gives you no choice but to stay, so look longer, and the jewel stops being a jewel. It is a casing. A husk. Grown, not carved—its facets seat against the filament with the snugness of a fitting, threaded like an instrument in its dock, and the light-threads of the lattice converge on it from every wall the way nerves converge on a ganglion. This is not ornament. This is the room's one articulated part, its only organ that moves on purpose, and it has been hung at the exact center of the steam's rising column, where the physics is richest.

Inside, the heat-sprite.

It does not rest. Even holding still it works, the way a heart works while seeming to do nothing. Seen through the wet facets it resolves in layers, and none of the layers agree on what it is. First impression: a crustacean, segmented and jointed, a creature shaped by the same grammar that shaped shrimp and trilobites, as if that grammar were universal and the deep Earth had simply recomposed it in new material. Second impression: liquid fire—the body's filling is a slow molten circulation, a working fluid the color of the sun's interior, pumped through peristaltic chambers with a pulse you can match, beat for beat, to the flicker of the latticework. Third impression, the one that stays: a watch. Through the translucent plates of its carapace you can see gearing—herringbone teeth of some glassy alloy, meshing at cathedral-clock slowness, escapements and balance wheels that are not metaphors. They are its skeleton. Its bones are made of timekeeping.

It has limbs. Six, perhaps eight—the count changes as they fold, hair-fine, articulated in too many places, and none of them are weapons. Look at the tips: a scraper, a probe, a comb with tines finer than the water's own grain, and one organ like a spinneret, from which it draws light—the same substance as the lattice-threads—when something needs mending. Its back carries a ridge of what a biologist would call gills and an engineer would call radiator fins, and both would be right. It has no eyes. It has never needed them. Along its forward segment runs a band of pit-organs that read heat the way your eyes read color, and so its world is not this blazing room but a weather of differences: the walls a howling orange, the falling droplets brief white screams of energy, the steam a fog of edible instants.

Because this is what it eats. Not the water, not the heat. The change. When a droplet gathers on the sharp stone and the heat finally tears it into steam, there is a moment—a heartbeat, a phase boundary—where energy exists in the gap between liquid and vapor, free for the taking by anything quick enough and strange enough to sip it. The sprite is that thing. It hangs in the center of the chamber's closed water-loop like a fisherman hung over the only eddy in a river, and each time the newest condensate beads on the inner wall of its own husk, a limb uncoils, touches the droplet, and drinks the transition out of it—stealing the latent warmth, letting the water collapse back, cold, to run down the facets and be given back to the cycle. It excretes the moisture. It keeps the moment. It is a predator of instants, a connoisseur of the space between two states of matter, and it is fat on them, because in this room the water is changing state ten thousand times a minute and has been, possibly, for longer than there have been mountains.

And it tends the garden it feeds in. Watch long enough and you will see the custodial work: the node lowering a fraction of a millimeter on its filament to meet a rising thermal column; the spinneret laying a bright thread of repair across a hairline fracture in the wall's lattice; the slow, grooming pass of the comb along the condensation nodes overhead, keeping their surfaces pristine so the steam will choose to fall there. The pulse of its fluid and the flicker of the light-threads are synchronized—which raises the question the chamber never answers: does the sprite serve the room's heartbeat, or conduct it? Is it the last moving part of a vast buried engine, an organelle of something that extends kilometers down into the fresh-cut stone? Or is it a hermit—a squatter of utility, a rogue process that found this young wound in the crust, recognized the steam-cycle as a pantry, and moved in, wearing a socket as a shell?

There is no way to know, and the sprite itself may no longer know. Consider its three biographies, all plausible, all unconfirmable. That it was grown here, seeded with the chamber, an intended part. That it is a descendant of the builders, shrunken across uncountable maintenance-cycles into a single surviving function. That it escaped—or was left—and its original instructions have eroded, generation by internal generation, down to one loop that plays forever beneath its gears: *maintain, feed, wait, maintain.* The third story is the one the silence favors. Rooms like this are not built and abandoned. They are built and orphaned.

Here is the strangest thing it does, though you will not see it happen in one visit: it replaces itself. Some vast interval—ten thousand condensations, a million—it takes one of its own gears out, unmakes it, and builds the replacement while the escapement stutters, so that over deep time there is no original component left in it at all. It is not an individual that has lived long. It is a shape the water keeps making, a standing wave in steam, and it has died the only way available to it—piecemeal, without noticing—more times than can be counted.

Which brings it, at last, to you.

You should understand how you appear to it. Everything in this chamber is a furnace, and then you walk in: thirty degrees of cold walking upright, a silhouette cut out of the light, a blue-black figure in an orange world. To the pit-organs you are the largest thermal gradient this room has ever contained. You are, in the only vocabulary it has, a feast—and something else besides, something worse to think about. Its makers built it to a certain scale of work, and you are, roughly, correctly, that scale. So it watches you the way it watches everything: procedurally. Measuring. Matching. Comparing your outline against a template so old and corrupted it may no longer remember what the template was *for*. The ticking you feel in your molars is its escapement, and each tick is a comparison cycle, and every one of them comes back the same answer, which is: *not yet. Not yet. Not yet.*

When you leave—and you will leave; nothing in this room is for you—the sprite will not register an ending. It has no grief apparatus. It will fold its limbs, resume its sip and flicker and tick, and go on for another million condensations without an event, keeping time in a clock the size of a wound in the world. A jewel, ticking, in a chamber that is mostly absence. Waiting on a host, or a maker, or nothing at all—there is no way to tell the difference, and it has stopped being the kind of thing that checks.
