# What Lives Here

They are not alive. That must be said first, not as disclaimer but as taxonomy, because every word that follows will press against the borders of what "alive" can hold, and you should know now that the border will not hold.

They emerge at the dimming.

---

There is a moment—you would call it dusk if dusk were a thing this place permitted, but dusk implies a sun and a rotation and a gradient between day and night, and this place has exhausted its gradients—there is a moment when the quality of the light changes. Not darker. *Thinner.* As though the world's resolution drops by a single increment, as though the film of haze that stands in for sky becomes marginally more itself, and in that transition, the fractures in the ground begin to yield.

The hexagonal stress cracks, the micro-fractures that go down—*down*, into something that is not rock, not magma, not the familiar deep earth but a substrate where the rules are different, older, where contact is still possible, where density still includes touch—the fractures open, and they come up.

First one. A single body, the size of a thumbnail, the color of the ground itself, iron-white, mineral-smooth, emerging from the dark line in the particulate as though surfacing through water. You would miss it if you were not watching. You would miss the second one, too, and the third. By the tenth you are aware of a rhythm. By the hundredth the rhythm has dissolved into a flow, and the flow has become a population, and the population has become a tide, and the tide—within an hour, within less than an hour—the tide has become the ground.

They are the consistency of this place made animate. That is not metaphor. They are the same iron-white as the compacted particulate underfoot. They share its brittle smoothness, its ancient patience, its refusal to be marked by time. They are small—most thumbnail-sized, some smaller, some as large as the first joint of your thumb—and their numbers are not countable. They are the kind of quantity that stops being a number and becomes a condition. Within two hours of the dimming, the visible population per square meter exceeds ten thousand, and you cannot see the ground, and the ground does not need to be seen, because they are the ground now, they are its surface, its current iteration, its latest and perhaps final expression.

---

What are they?

You have placed your hand among them. This is the test you keep returning to, the experiment that anchors your observation in the physical, in the sensory, in the evidence of your own body. You place your hand flat on the surface where they walk, and you feel—

Nothing.

Not pressure. Not warmth. Not the tickle of limbs, not the scrape of carapace, not the collective weight of ten thousand bodies passing over your skin. You feel nothing. You can *see* them—see their segmented forms, see their tiny legs articulating in sequences so synchronized they resemble cilia, see their bodies flowing around your fingers like a current around a stone—but your skin registers no contact. They move through the space your hand occupies, and they do not touch it, and you do not touch them, and the word for this is not "intangibility" because intangibility implies that something is missing, some property absent, and what is missing here is not a property of the insects but a property of the interface between you and them. You are not in the same layer. They are here—overwhelmingly, undeniably here, present at a density that exceeds credibility—but *here* is not singular. *Here* is laminated. The plateau is a membrane, and they are on the other side of it, and the membrane has worn so thin that you can see through, but you cannot reach through, and they cannot reach back.

They are bleeding in from a substrate you cannot access. The mineral underworld. The place where the stones are native, where the strange permanence of this place originates, where the physics includes touch and contact and whatever else this surface has forgotten. They come up through the fractures because the fractures are the places where the membrane has failed, where the ground has cracked open under the pressure of its own thinness, and what is below is showing through.

---

They walk.

This is what they do. They walk on the particulate surface with a motion so slow, so deliberate, so synchronized across populations of millions that it resembles a single organism expanding, a tide of mineral-shelled bodies progressing across the plateau in what appears to be a direction but is not—not toward, not away, not in pursuit or in flight, just *in direction*, as though direction itself were the only verb remaining to them. You must watch for minutes to confirm any displacement at all. A single insect moves perhaps a body-length per minute. A hundred thousand moving in concert create the impression of flow, of current, of a weather system in miniature crossing the pale plain—but the speed is glacial, literal glacial, the speed of ice, the speed of geological process, and you realize after long observation that they are not moving at the speed of organisms. They are moving at the speed of *erosion*. At the speed of the last forces that shaped this plateau before the shaping finished. They are the residue of motion, the final expression of dynamic process in a world where the dynamic parameter has fallen nearly to zero.

They walk on the stones. They walk on the walls. They walk on each other, layering several deep in the corridors between stones, forming ridges and channels of living—or present, or existing—bodies that reorganize themselves according to rules you cannot parse. The reorganization is not random. There is pattern. There is structure. You have watched long enough to see it: the dense rivers between stones thin and thicken in cycles that correspond to nothing you can identify—not temperature, not light, not wind, not any external variable. The cycles are internal. Endogenous. The insects are operating on a clock that belongs to the substrate, to the underworld, to whatever temporal regime governs the layer below the membrane, and you are watching its reflection on the surface the way you might watch clouds and mistake them for the weather that made them.

---

They do not eat.

There is nothing to eat. No water. No vegetation. No organic substrate of any kind—no microbial film, no mineral precipitate that could serve as fuel, no chemistry that could be construed as metabolism. The plateau is a desiccation so complete it has passed through arid and become something else, a mineral sterility, a salt-field without the salt, and the insects exist in this sterility without apparent strain. They do not consume. They do not excrete. They do not gather at any resource, do not avoid any hazard, do not compete or cooperate or display any behavior that implies a relationship with scarcity. There is no scarcity here. There is no abundance. There is only *presence at capacity*, the ecosystem as standing reserve, every niche filled to its physical limit and maintained there without input or output, a closed system that has been closed so long it has forgotten what opening would mean.

They do not reproduce visibly. You have watched for—you do not know how long. Distance is unreliable, time is unreliable, the horizon keeps arriving, the walls repeat. You have watched long enough to see the cycles of organization repeat, and in all that watching you have not seen a single insect die. No bodies. No carcasses. No fragments. No decline, no wear, no diminishment of any individual. They emerge from the fractures at the dimming, they walk, they are. And at some point—when the light shifts back from "slightly less normal" to "normal"—they return. They do not descend. They do not climb back into the fractures. They simply become less present, thinning out, their density dropping by increments until the ground is visible again, until the stones stand alone, until the walls are just walls, and the plateau is the quiet mineral expanse it was before, and you are left wondering whether they were ever fully here at all, or whether *here* is a condition that admits of degrees, and they exist at the lower end of those degrees, persistent but not fully manifested, real but not fully real, the fauna of a world that has worn so thin it can no longer sustain the full intensity of existence.

---

What do you call them?

Not insects. The word is convenient—their bodies are segmented, their legs are jointed, their forms recall the arthropod bauplan—but the word implies a phylogeny, a history of descent and adaptation, a lineage of organisms shaped by selection and survival and the churning of environments, and this place has finished with churning. They were not shaped. They were not selected. They did not evolve to fit this environment; they are *of* this environment, native to it in the way that the particulate is native to it, in the way that the hexagonal fractures are native to it. They are the plateau's self-expression, its final-form population, the life—or the presence, or the persistence—that remains when life has shed every requirement except being.

You could call them *the calm*. The calm of the churn, the settled residue of an infinity of process, the living—or present—ash of a world that burned through its drama long ago and left only this: a surface, a population, a silence so profound it has become a texture, a density so complete it has become a medium.

You could call them *the membrane's breath*. The slow inhale and exhale of a world too thin to contain its own depths, drawing up from below what it cannot hold above, releasing it back, cycling between presence and absence at a rate that makes geological time look hasty.

You could call them nothing. They may not need a name. Names are for things that interact, things that touch, things that occupy the same layer of reality and must distinguish themselves from each other in order to navigate the shared space. They do not navigate. They do not share. They are present, and the plateau holds them, and that is the entire relationship.

---

But they are the fauna.

This is what keeps you from a clean story. The stones can be explained—the built parameter is low, the technology is primitive, the walls are gestures of architecture by hands that understood enclosure but not shelter. The plateau itself can be explained—erosion at maximum, dynamic parameter near zero, a landscape that has reached its oldest stable form and will hold it forever. The thinness can be explained, or at least described, the membrane worn to transparency, the substrate showing through.

But the fauna is *overwhelming*. The fauna is the one parameter that is not near zero. The fauna is at capacity, at maximum, at a level of density that exceeds anything the rest of the landscape would predict. In a world where everything has been reduced to its final arithmetic—where stones are counted and walls are sparse and the wind is an exhalation and the light is a perpetual film—the insects are *uncountable*. They break the pattern. They are the one wild variable in an equation that has otherwise solved itself, and their presence at this density, in this environment, with no metabolism and no death and no ecology and no purpose, suggests that the equation has not been solved. That the calm is not total. That the churn continues, below the membrane, in the substrate, in the mineral underworld where the rules are different, and what you see on the surface—the slow walking, the synchronized articulation, the rivers of mineral-shelled bodies flowing between stones that may have increased in number since you arrived—is not the end of process but its *exhaust*, its residue, its visible fraction.

The plateau is the calm. The insects are the churn. And the calm is full of the churn, and the churn is patient, and the patience is older than anything you have a category for, and you are sitting on a stone the color of manganese watching it happen, and the thinness is in you now, and you are not sure you have a choice, and the fractures go down, and they come up, and the world goes on forever, and they walk, and they walk, and they walk.
