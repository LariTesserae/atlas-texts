What lives here has abandoned the word "individual."

Not out of philosophy, but out of sheer geometric necessity. In a space where every cubic inch of viable substrate is already claimed, claimed again, decomposed, and re-claimed, the luxury of a boundary is unsustainable. So, what lives in the Compression Gardens is a *we*. A vast, damp, slow-thinking *we*.

It began as a conspiracy of spores and root hairs, a quiet negotiation between the ferns that craved the light and the fungi that craved the dead. But forty years—perhaps forty centuries—of relentless proximity has erased the contracts. The fern root systems no longer feed only ferns; they have been spliced, at the cellular level, by the pale hyphae of the fungal networks. The vines that clutch the hot pipes are not merely seeking warmth; their phloem carries the sugars produced by the light-cupping fronds down to the starving depths, and in return, they draw up water filtered through the metallic taste of the biofilm. They are a circulatory system for a body that has no heart, only a distributed, slow-beating pulse of osmosis.

The *we* is a creature of pressure. It has known nothing else. It feels the weight of the city above as a constant, heavy embrace, and it has shaped itself to resist being crushed. The corrugated moss on the pillars is not soft; it is dense, fibrous, like wound bandages, built to bear a load. The vines are sinewy and turgid, pressurized from within by the slow seep of the aquifer. When you press your hand to the moss, you are not touching a plant; you are touching a ligament of a larger musculature.

And the *we* is not blind. It has no eyes, true—the pale, squirming things in the pools took the last of the eyes generations ago, discarding them as useless weight—but it sees. It sees in heat, in moisture, in vibration. The micro-thermal shifts of the failing light tubes are mapped across its canopy like a constellation. The slow drip of condensation is a tickle along its upper dermis. When you walked down the iron staircase, your boots striking the steps, the *we* felt the resonance shiver through the stone. It felt you descend into its throat.

It does not think as you do. Its thoughts are chemical and tectonic. A shift in the water table is a mood swing. The slow decay of a light tube is a season. The arrival of a warm, breathing, sweating animal—you—is an event. A stimulus. You are a warm spot, a source of carbon dioxide, a temporary void in the tight-packed biomass that must be flowed around and through. The air you exhale is drunk instantly by the nearest fronds, the carbon stripped out and woven into new vine, the oxygen returned to the heavy air. You are being digested, conversationally, just by standing there.

The few animals that remain—the eyeless, clicking things that ghost through the deep water—are its white blood cells, or perhaps its dreams. They are too sparse to be truly part of the organism, but they are kept, allowed to scavenge the dead edges and keep the flow moving. They consume the rot so the rot doesn't choke the veins. In return, they are given warmth and dark and the endless, monotonous drip of water. They are the *we*'s only movement, flickering at the edge of its perception like thoughts that almost form.

The machinery—the pumps, the conduits, the iron teeth—is becoming its skeleton. The *we* has learned to calcify around the metal, pulling the rust into itself, using the iron to harden its joints. It does not remember the engineers who placed the pipes, but it recognizes the heat they carry as a kind of gift, a subterranean sun. It has grown collars around the lights not to strangle them, but to hold them close, to protect the failing phosphors with cups of living tissue. It is trying to keep its suns alive.

When you placed your hand on that pillar, the *we* did not recoil, but it did notice. The slight change in temperature, the pressure of your palm, the oils left behind on the condensation. A tiny tendril of mycelium, too fine to see, likely extended from the fabric wrapping to taste your skin. To the *we*, you were a brief, salty rain, a fleeting pressure, a single footstep in a long, slow dance of pressure.

When you left, ascending the staircase, the *we* simply closed the gap you left behind. The air you displaced filled in. The fronds you brushed against lifted back into position. The condensation continued its crawl down the channels. The *we* does not mourn visitors, because the *we* does not understand departure. Everything that enters the Compression Gardens stays, eventually—broken down, distributed, woven into the wall of green and rust and damp. You are part of its memory now, written in the slight chemical trace you left on the iron, a single, fading note in the long, low hum of the descent.
