The people of Firstgrove do not think of themselves as inhabitants; they think of themselves as the stuff that grows. They are the third kingdom after the machines that bore and the roots that finish, and they bear the same traits as everything else in the Long Room: they are dry, they are deliberate, and they do not erode. In a place where the ambient humidity is a threat to be consumed by crystal lungs, they have become wax-works. Their skin is like old paper, tight over the knuckles, slightly shiny at the elbows and knees with a natural slick of resinous sweat. Their hair grows in slow, dark mats, and they tend it the way they tend the streets—pruning it, training it, sometimes grafting small dried blossoms or chips of bright stone into it, an architecture of the scalp. They smell of turpentine and minerals, of cold stone and warm sap. When they bleed, they bleed amber. 

They move slowly. Not out of lethargy, but because speed in a dry, still place is friction, and friction is heat, and heat—outside the sealed ceramic bellies of the forges—is danger. They walk with a pacing that syncs to the thirty-second pulse of the sap mains, a faint pressure in the soles of their soft, root-mat boots that dictates the rhythm of their days. Their voices are low and unhurried, matching the acoustics of a town where the loudest sound is often the paper-rasp of moth wings in another room. Laughter here is a quiet, wheezing thing, warm and internal, expelled through the nose rather than shouted into the dark. 

Because the town is sparse, spaced, and connected by lines of light, the people are spores living on those bright threads. To stand off the path is to be fallow, to be subject to whatever presses heavily through the dark miles. So they travel the lamp roads with a solemn reverence for the dosers and the lampmoths. Out there, a walker becomes an ember in the black, an isolated point of green and wax. On the lamp roads, they travel in pairs or in family knots, their shoulders slightly hunched against the immense vacancy of the unlit country. They do not linger between groves. 

Inside the groves, they are gregarious in the still, slow way of trees. Social life happens in the warm crowns around the heat-trees. They sit on the grafted benches, pressed close to the fat, slow-burning trunks, absorbing the blood-warmth of the internally combusting wood. The family unit is a cluster of dry limbs and waxy skin arranged around a single, biological stove. They share food here—hard biscuits of compressed sap-feed, dried moth-pupae cracked between resin-stained teeth, slivers of sweet pitch dissolved under the tongue. They rarely drink. Their bodies are closed systems; they get what little moisture they need from the solvent sap, and they vent it as slowly as the salt lungs vent the cavern’s damp. 

Children in Firstgrove are uniquely precious because they are the only truly soft things for miles. They are herders of the red mites, running along the lamp roads with wax-tipped wands, their small, quick feet patting the warm root-mats. But even their play is a kind of tending. A child tying a wax-tipped wand to a low-hanging vine is simultaneously inventing a game and pruning a sap line. They grow up learning that a building is a sibling, that a street is a slow creature that agreed to leave a gap, and that the town itself is just a larger body. They develop a tacit, deep grammar with the borer-oxen, leaning against the six-legged beasts under the heat-trees, a mutual domestication so old that a child will absentmindedly scratch the bark-hide of an ox the way they would scratch their own scaling scalp. 

The adults have the frontier cheerfulness of people building a world, but it is a cheerfulness tempered by the gravitas of resin. They know that in Firstgrove, a mistake does not wash away; it is preserved. A bad graft is a permanent scar on a wall. A dropped torch could consume the dry, rich air in a flash of sweet, lethal smoke. So their happiness is a quiet, steady thing, like the tick of cooling wood after dusk. They are meticulous. The dosers who walk the feed-lines at dawn are solemn as priests, and their hands are steady as they knock the valves that bring the manufactured sun. The tappers walking the dark before night carry their buckets with the quiet reverence of dairymen, knowing that the slow amber drip is the town's heartbeat. 

And they age not by wrinkling or stooping, but by girth. As they get older, they slow down further, their movements becoming like the geological time of the cavern itself. An elder in Firstgrove is not frail; they are dense. Their skin is thick with preserved layers, their joints stiffened with deposits of the very pitch they have spent their lives harvesting. They become indistinguishable from the architecture. The oldest among them sit so still under the heat-trees that you might mistake them for grafted furniture, or for the founders standing at the crossroads—dark, amber lanterns of wax and bone, slowly becoming one with the rooms they grew up in. 

When they die, they are not buried. They are set. The resin that was their blood becomes their coffin. They are fixed in pitch and wax in the main squares, their arms often trained around a new sapling or fused to the root-mat of a street. It is not a grim fate, because it is what the material wants to do. To the people of Firstgrove, a human body is just another kind of stillwood sapling, born of the same dry dust and amber blood, meant to be planted, trained, and eventually outgrown by the town that consumes and keeps them. They are living inside a beginning, and they are happy to be its foundation.
