The people of Kael are the color of the stone at the end of its life. Their skin is the hue of dried marrow and compressed ash, mottled with the pale, chalky white of the deep rock, streaked faintly with the rust of the old earth. They are born of this closed throat, and they wear its palette. Their skin is thick, dense, and slightly tacky to the touch, evolved to absorb rather than reflect the ambient warmth. It hangs loosely over their frames, folding and creasing like the billowing, fleshy walls of their home, giving them an appearance of softness that belies the dense, dense muscle beneath—the muscle required to pull oneself up guide-ropes, to squeeze between clustered homes, to survive a life lived vertically. 

They are small-boned, a necessity dictated by the close press of the world. The space is fixed, and so are they. No one here grows tall or broad; the lattice of the Hanging City would not permit it, and the long-ago geometry of their own genetics has answered the walls pressing in at the edge of their vision. They are lithe and compact, with long, clever fingers and wide, gripping feet, the toes splayed and prehensile from a lifetime of gripping swaying bridges and knotted root-ropes. They move with a slow, rolling grace—a clinging, deliberate gait that makes them look, in the dim rust-light of the fungal shelves, like pale, eyeless geckos negotiating a vertical world.

And they are eyeless. Or nearly so. In the permanent warm gloom of Kael, where shapes are rumors and the far wall is just a shadow behind a veil, sight is a useless, vulnerable luxury. Their eyes have long since recessed into the smooth skin of their faces, leaving only the faintest, dimpled depressions beneath the brow ridge, like the scars of an ancient eviction. They do not look at one another. They have no gaze to meet. Instead, they *listen* and they *feel*. 

In the amplified, resonant throat of stone, their hearing is exquisite. The low, continuous murmur of their voices—once you learn to hear it—is not a murmur at all, but a rich, tactile language of clicks, hums, and sub-vocalizations that rides the humid air, utilizing the chamber's natural echo to locate and communicate. They can hear the subtle shift in the pitch of the air when a body moves through a walkway three levels above; they can sense the tension of a root-rope fraying long before it snaps. Their wide, flattened noses are equally acute, reading the chemical signatures of the stone, the sweet-metallic tang of the amber sap, the faint, sour exhalations of the grazing slugs, and the particular, warm scent of one another's skin. In this lightless world, they map their home by the twin compasses of sound and smell, navigating the three-dimensional lattice of Kael with a fluid, unerring confidence that would look like magic to a sighted visitor.

They wear little. The constant, geothermal warmth of the chamber renders clothing a hindrance, another layer in a world already suffocating with density. What they do wear is woven from the cured stalks of the pale growths and dried moss, but it serves function, not modesty: a sash to hold a stone axe, a thick, padded wrap around the forearms and shins to protect against the abrasive friction of the ancient, sagging rock. When they brush against the rough walls or the woven baskets of their homes, it is with the casual familiarity of a body that has always known the presence of stone at its shoulder. They move through the close, packed space with a gentle, deliberate care, always conscious of the weight of the bodies around them, the nearness of the stone, the drop to the shallow pools below.

The amber sap is their blood, too. It is the only fluid they drink, thick and mineral-rich, and it has shaped them as completely as the stone has shaped their home. They are warm, but with a slow, deliberate warmth, the fire banked low, burning with the patient, viscous fuel of the deep earth. They live a long time. A generation passes while a single stalactite grows a centimeter. There is no urgency in the people of Kael. They do not hurry, because hurrying is a violence against the close, fragile press of their home. To move fast is to knock a bridge, to snap a root, to send a neighbor tumbling. Their patience is not a virtue; it is a structural necessity, as fundamental as gravity.

They are born into the woven pods, suspended in the lattice of the Hanging City, and they are born into a world at capacity. This is the central, unyielding fact of their existence: the space is fixed, and every birth requires a death to make room. The people of Kael accept this with the same quiet, bone-deep certainty with which they accept the warmth and the dimness. They do not speak of it as tragedy. It is the rule of the world, the single, simple law that governs the closed loop of warmth, flesh, and amber sap.

When an elder feels their time approaching—when the slow pulse of the earth beneath their woven floor begins to sound in their bones like a call to rest—they do not rail against it. They move, perhaps, a little slower, their climbing grip a little less sure. They withdraw to the highest, warmest levels of the city, where the air is thickest with the mineral breath of the cracked chimneys. And then, with the same deliberate grace with which they have lived, they make room. A pod is dismantled, its stalks and cured shelves redistributed to the young family waiting in the basin below. A body is lowered to the floor, where the six-legged slugs graze and the pale insects scuttle, and it is returned to the lush, suffocating garden that feeds the world.

In this way, the people of Kael are as much a part of the vegetation as the fungal shelves and the thick, waxy fingers of pale green. They grow, they reach, they drink the amber sap, and when their long, slow lives have run their course, they are composted back into the garden, their bone and flesh feeding the next cycle of dense, fractal growth. They do not build monuments or keep histories. The stone is unimaginably old; their lives are brief in comparison, no more than a thickening of the root-mass, a brief florescence in the dim, and then a subsiding. They are woven into the world, as inextricably as the stalks of their homes, and they are content in the way that only a people who have never known a horizon can be content—completely, entirely, without reservation.

To look at them—really *look*, if you could see in the dim, rust-colored light of the fungal glow—is to see a people who are not separate from their world. They do not stand apart from the stone and the vegetation; they are of it. They sag and billow like the ancient rock. They pulse with the slow, bruised phosphorescence of the deep. They click and hum with the skittering biomass. The throat of stone does not just contain them; it speaks them, breathes them, swallows them whole and holds them close in its warm, unyielding, permanent embrace.
