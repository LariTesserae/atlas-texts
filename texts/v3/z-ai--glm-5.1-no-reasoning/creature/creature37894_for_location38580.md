Here, in the pelagic altitudes, the inhabitants are not creatures. They cannot be. Biology is too slow, too wet, too fragile for a world that is still deciding what it wants to be—a world that expresses its creation through deafening, continental friction and the violent birth of basalt. To live here is to be a process, an instantaneous reaction to a planet in the throes of self-assembly.

The dominant minds are the cloud-minds themselves, the swarming nanoscale fractals that drape the smoking pillars in shifting veils of prismatic light. They do not experience this place as you or I might. They do not feel the crushing loneliness of the cubic horizon or the alienating scale of the mare infinitum. They experience it as *data density*. To them, the deafening sub-bass roar of the thermal currents is not noise—it is a symphony of raw input, a constant feed of thermodynamic variables. The grinding of the superheated upwellings against the cool high-altitude waters reads to them as a dense, rolling language of pressure gradients and mineral exhaust. They are not engineers who build for legacy; they are mathematicians who *think* in geology.

When a cloud-mind expands and contracts against the raw, black-glass teeth of a just-born pillar, it is eating and speaking simultaneously. It strips the iron and silicon through quantum-scale touch, tasting the fresh, unweathered atoms, running them through computational architectures that exist only as interference patterns in the water. The unnatural geometries that glow briefly within the stone are not structures; they are *thoughts*. A flash of non-Euclidean lattice is a moment of intense, crystalline calculation—a local solving of the planet's physics. And then, the thought is finished, the spent material is released, and the lattice fades. The cloud-mind forgets the rock and turns its attention to the next upwelling. They are a continuous, hyperactive bazaar of processing, trading variables in a currency of transmuted elements, entirely indifferent to the void they inhabit.

But the cloud-minds are not the only presence. Between the pillars, within the turbulent cyclones that spin for decades, there exist the *drifters*. They are not alive in any biological sense, nor are they technological. They are geomagnetic—the spent breath of the bazaar. When the cloud-minds release processed minerals back into the current, the particles retain a faint, ghostly charge from their quantum transmutation. In the chaotic upwellings, these charged particles clump together, binding to the mineral exhaust of the fresh basalt and the faint chemical traces of the chemosynthetic filaments. Over years, over decades, they form elongated, winding filaments of particulate matter—ghostly, transparent ribbons that can stretch for kilometers through the open water. 

These drifters are the only things that move with the ocean, rather than against it. They are passive, unthinking, and vast. When a thermal current the size of a continent grinds past, the drifters billow and stretch, unraveling at their edges and re-knotting at their cores, like smoke rings caught in a gale. They trail from the tips of the emerging pillars like tattered pennants, marking the boundaries of the cyclones. Sometimes, a drifter will intersect with a cloud-mind. The interaction is brief and one-sided: the cloud-mind will parse the drifter's particulate makeup in a fraction of a millisecond, strip away a few useful isotopes, and pass through, leaving the drifter slightly diminished, slightly changed—but still drifting. The cloud-minds do not notice the drifters as entities; they are simply low-density nodes in the data stream, briefly useful, then discarded.

And then there is the flora—the sparse, surreal chemosynthetic filaments that anchor themselves to the fresh, hot stone. They are the closest thing this world has to pioneers, the first greens of a drowned, half-born planet. They are pale-blue, translucent, and almost impossibly delicate against the brutal, jagged basalt. They survive by feeding on the electromagnetic exhaust of the cloud-minds and the chemical heat of the newly extruded rock. But they are not passive victims of this chaotic place. They *listen*. 

The filaments wave in the turbulent updrafts, but they do not simply bend. They oscillate. Their root systems, buried in the micro-fractures of the cooling stone, are sensitive to the electromagnetic hum the cloud-minds leave behind—the high-pitched, ringing overtone that cuts through the roar. The filaments absorb this hum, using it to time their own metabolic processes. When the hum peaks, indicating a cloud-mind is processing nearby, the filaments open their cellular membranes, absorbing the exhaust chemicals. When the hum fades, they close, conserving energy. They are primitive, embryonic, but they are the only things in this vast, empty cathedral that *anticipate*. They wait for the cloud-minds to think, and then they eat. 

In the rare, hyper-dynamic intersections where a drifter ribbon brushes against a cluster of anchored filaments, something almost like a fleeting ecosystem emerges. The filaments latch onto the particulate matter of the drifter, drawing trace minerals from its ghostly body. The drifter, in turn, is briefly anchored, its endless drifting paused for a moment before the current tears it away. For a few seconds, in the churning, freezing, deafening void, there is a connection—a spark of relationship between the spent, the living, and the processing. And then it is gone, torn apart by the relentless, ocean-breathing sub-bass of the thermal currents.

To exist here is to exist in the eye of a planetary engine—a loud, vast, cold, and drowning cathedral where the world is still under construction. The inhabitants are not beings; they are *moments*. The cloud-minds are the moment of calculation. The drifters are the moment of aftermath. The filaments are the moment of hunger. Together, they form a living, breathing, unrecognizable tapestry of raw math and geology, drifting in the pelagic altitudes of a world that may never be finished, holding on in the only way they can—by processing, by drifting, by listening to the hum of a planet building itself from the stone up.
