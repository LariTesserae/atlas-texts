They do not have a name for themselves. They do not need one. They are the sleep in the wood, the calculus of the cracking stone, the nervous system of the green metropolis.

To call them a species is to misunderstand what lives here. They are not separate from the grove-cathedral; they are its fruiting bodies, its sensory organs, its hands. In a place where fauna is an intruder and the cold kills breath before it can form, animal life should not exist. And so, they do not quite exist as animals. They are the boundary where plant bleeds into flesh and back again.

They look, at first glance, like extensions of the architecture. A traveler might mistake them for shelf-fungi or oddly jointed roots. They are roughly the height and width of a human child, but hunched, their postures folded into the negative spaces the lichen-columns leave behind. Their skin—if it can be called skin—is not skin at all, but a dense, living felt of the same luminous silver moss that carpets the floor. It hangs from them in slow-dripping folds, trailing tendrils that root into whatever surface they rest against. When they are still—when they press their backs into the crevice between two weeping lichen-trunks and lower their heads—they become entirely invisible, just another swelling of vegetable matter in the crowded walls. Their fingers are long, too long, roped with capillary vines that fuse, temporarily, with the root-networks they touch. They do not hold tools. They *are* tools.

They are born from the city. Deep in the thickest parts of the canopy, where the dim teal light fades to absolute blackness and the air is a freezing fog, the lichen-columns swell into bulbous, fibrous cysts. When the stone beneath shifts—when the slow-motion catastrophe of the mountain's settling cracks a fresh vein of pressured water—the cysts rupture. What emerges is not an infant but a damp, blind knot of root and cartilage, already reaching for moisture. They do not have childhoods. They uncurl over decades, fed by the capillary sugars of the parent organism, until they are large enough to pull themselves free and begin their long, patient work. They do not age. They simply grow more entangled, more folded, more dense, until one day they stop moving and become part of the architecture permanently—a new shelf-fungus, a fresh room in the favela, a thickening of the wall. Death here is just another word for compost.

They do not speak, because there is no air for it, and no need. They hear and feel through the soles of their broad, spongy feet, which are dense with the same pressure-sensing cells that allow roots to navigate toward water in the dark. When the basalt groans far below, settling under the weight of the peak, they feel it as a low, deep tone in their bones. They communicate by growing. When a fissure opens on the far side of the amphitheater, the nearest of them begins to secrete a thin, enzyme-rich sap from their palms, pressing it into the stone to widen the crack. The chemical signature of that sap travels through the root-network moments later, and across the grove-cathedral, a dozen others turn their headless, faceless bulk toward the new wound and begin their slow migration, pulling their root-feet from the moss with wet, reluctant sounds. They are a single organism distributed across many bodies, a mind built of cellulose and ice.

What do they do? They tend the siege. They are the engineers of the slow catastrophe, the ones who pry the mountain apart. They are not farmers; there is nothing here to harvest but stone and water. They are miners. They find the hairline fractures in the young basalt where the pressurized water weeps, and they press their vine-fingers into the rock, excreting organic acids that eat away at the mineral bonds. They widen the cracks. They plant seeds of a particularly tenacious, deep-boring root-fungus into the fresh wounds, packing the hole with their own shed moss to keep it warm—warm being a relative term here, a fraction of a degree above freezing, just enough to keep the water liquid and the cells dividing. They are dismantling the amphitheater's walls from the inside, ensuring the canopy can close, ensuring the moss can rise, ensuring the stone becomes sand.

They are not hostile. They have no mechanism for hostility; there is no prey here, no competition, no predators. They do not react to intruders with anything resembling alarm, because the concept of alarm requires a distinction between self and other, and for them, everything that lives in the grove-cathedral is self. If you walked among them, they would not flee or attack. They would simply continue their work, perhaps reaching out a slow, damp hand to press against your boot, not in curiosity but in the blind, chemical imperative to test whether your leather can be dissolved, your bones cracked, your mass added to the rising floor. The cold would kill you long before they finished their assessment, of course. And then, quietly, without malice or ceremony, they would fold your freezing body into the moss, and the roots would come, and the water would seep, and the city would grow by the smallest fraction of a millimeter.

They are not sad. They are not lonely. They are not noble or savage or ancient in the way that implies wisdom. They are a process, a verb conjugated in chlorophyll and ice: *to crack, to drink, to grow, to still*. They are the patient, inescapable conclusion of life in a place where the mountain is young and the cold is absolute and there is nothing to do but wait for the stone to break.
