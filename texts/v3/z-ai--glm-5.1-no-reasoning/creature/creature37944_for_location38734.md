To live in the Green Altitude is to be an apology made flesh.

The inhabitants call themselves the Hush-Bound, though they have no voice loud enough to use the name over the thunderous hum of the swarm. They are the caretakers of a cathedral that did not ask for them, living in the hollow spaces between the ribs of a god that does not know they exist. They are a people defined entirely by their smallness, their quietness, their desperate, frantic attempt to not disrupt the perfection of a completed world.

They are slight. Generation after generation of nutrition drawn from the same dense, root-woven substrate has made them lean and dense, their bones heavy with minerals leached from the ancient detritus, their muscles hard from the constant push through the thick, humid air. Their skin has taken on the hue of the world they inhabit: not the bright green of sunlit leaves, but the deep, shadowed jade of the rhizome layer beneath their feet, the color of things that live without seeing the sky. They move through the colossal, echoing aisles of the forest like breath through a throat, instinctively flowing around the massive trunks, never touching the bark, never disturbing the six-legged grazers that browse the root-hairs in hound-sized clusters.

The Hush-Bound have no spoken language. In a world where the air itself vibrates with the deafening, rolling thunder of biological urgency, to shout is to waste breath, and to speak is to invite the attention of the swarm. Instead, they speak in the language of the space itself: in pressure and stillness, in the subtle shifting of weight, in the faintest pattern of footfalls on the springy root-mass. A child learns early that a single sharp impact on the ground can ripple through the miles of interconnected root-hair mat and draw a tide of chitin. To communicate across the vast, empty aisles, they use the canopy's own breath: they strike the living wood of the trunk with stone hand-axes, low and resonant, tapping out messages that are swallowed by the greater hum within seconds. They are a people who have learned to be heard only by those who are listening.

They are naked, but not exposed. Their skin is their clothing, and it is enough. The humidity hangs at a constant, breathless warmth, and the weeping of the canopy provides all the water they need. They drink from the localized rivers that flow silently through the root-mass, cupping the warm, sweet-tangy water in their hands, tasting the faint cellulose and ancient decay that is the flavor of the world. They eat the swarm. This is their greatest act of survival, their most intimate communion with the Green Altitude. When the fat, heavy drops fall from the ceiling without a cloud in the sky, the hound-sized grazers rise up from the root-hairs in startled, clicking masses, and the Hush-Bound are waiting. They move with silent, frantic precision, snatching the six-legged things from the air with bare hands, crushing the chitin between their fingers, feeling the hot pulse of life cease in their grip. The flesh is rich, the shells are ground into meal for their crude breads. They do not hunt. They harvest. They take only what the space itself offers up in moments of disruption, and they give thanks for the brief, electric spark of the insect's life against their palms.

Their shelters are the faintest geometric anomalies in the endless green: the 1.4 of construction. They do not build homes; they grow suggestions of them. A family will find the place where two mega-angiosperm roots diverge, creating a shallow bowl in the living substrate, and they will weave flexed saplings into the gap, hardening them with the copper they smelt in tiny, breath-held fires. The fire is the most dangerous act. The heat is held close, buried in pits dug into the root-mass, fed with the driest detritus they can find. The smoke is a violation of the pure-plant ecosystem, a sharp, acrid protest against the damp chlorophyll air. They smelt only enough copper to tip their tools, to sharpen the edges of the stone hand-axes that they use to scar the bark of the ancient trees in ritualistic semi-circles. The scarring is their only art, their only mark upon the world. They carve patterns into the living wood: spirals that suggest the weeping of the canopy, lines that echo the flow of the temporary rivers, shapes that honor the frantic, silent pace of the swarm. The scars heal within a generation, grown over by the endless, patient green, and the Hush-Bound accept this. They do not build to last. They build to be *in* the world, briefly.

Their inner lives are as vast and hollow as the space they inhabit. They are a people of profound contemplation, not because they are philosophers, but because there is nothing else to do in the emptiness between the trunks. They walk for days without speaking, without touching another being, moving through the warm, luminous air in a state of trance-like absorption. They feel the vibration of the swarm in their teeth, in their bones, in the heavy, rhythmic pulse of their own blood. They know that they are surrounded by an uncountable biomass, that the world is teeming with life at absolute capacity, and yet they are alone in the echoing aisles. This paradox defines them: they are the loneliest creatures in the most crowded world, ghosts in the lungs of a completed planet.

They do not fear death. They fear disruption. To die is to fall to the root-mass and be consumed, returned to the green, digested back into the endless, contiguous mass of hyper-aged flora. This is not a tragedy; it is the natural order. But to die *disruptively*—to be crushed by a falling branch, to be overwhelmed by a swarm of grazers, to collapse in a place where the decay of the body would upset the delicate balance of the ecosystem—this is the deepest shame. The Hush-Bound tend to their dying with the same careful, quiet attention they give to the weaving of their sapling shelters. When an elder feels the end approaching, they walk alone into the furthest aisles, seeking a place where the root-hairs are thin and the temporary rivers run strong, a place where their body will be absorbed quickly and cleanly. They lie down on the living substrate and close their eyes, and the green takes them, and the hum of the swarm continues, unchanged.

They have no leaders, no hierarchy. They organize themselves by proximity and by need, coming together in the rare instances when the ecosystem demands it: when a section of the canopy weeps too heavily and the rivers flood, when a new trunk begins to emerge from the root-mass and threatens to reshape the aisles, when the swarm's rhythm falters and the humming quiet reveals the terrifying, breathless silence beneath. In these moments, they gather in the shallow bowls between the roots, their dark jade skin blending with the shadows, their stone and copper tools held tight, and they wait for the world to settle again. They do not act upon the world. They endure it.

The Hush-Bound know that they are an afterthought. They are the 1.4, the faintest scratch on the surface of a world that has already decided what it wants to be. They are not the architects of the Green Altitude; they are its tenants, its guests, its quiet, apologetic inhabitants. They live in the echo of a biological god's heartbeat, and they have made peace with their own insignificance. 

And yet, they persist. They move through the vast, hollow hall with a grace that the plants, for all their ancient patience, will never possess. They feel the warmth of the air, the weight of the humidity, the texture of the sound against their skin. They love, in their silent, pressure-based way, reaching for each other in the dark spaces between the trunks, their hands finding the familiar contours of another's small, dense body. They bear children in the root-bowls, easing them out into the humid air, pressing them to the breast, teaching them the first and only lesson: *be small, be quiet, be part of the green.* 

They are the last whisper of the animal kingdom in a world that has moved beyond animals, the final, faint breath of a lineage that will one day be swallowed entirely by the endless, patient, hyper-aged flora. And until that day, they walk the Green Altitude, deafened by the swarm, warmed by the weeping canopy, hollowed by the vastness of the space, and filled with a quiet, fierce, unutterable love for the cathedral that did not ask for them but holds them nonetheless.
