They do not know they are hidden. They do not know they are small. But a visitor, reeling from the roar and the gleam, might struggle to believe that—might look at the Moum-folk and see only the crushing scale of the crevice reflected in their eyes. And yet, to know the crevice is to know a world, and the Moum-folk are the world's knowing.

A visitor might assume, at first glance, that they are slight. Malnourished, perhaps, by the standards of a sunlit world—their frames compact, their shoulders narrow, their stature rarely cresting five spans. But this is not the softness of deprivation. This is the density of a life formed under pressure. Their bodies are knit tight, muscle and sinew wound like the dark vines that anchor the shelf-fungi, economical and fierce. Their fingers are long, astonishingly so, the pads broad and calloused from a lifetime of gripping slick stone, the nails kept deliberately short, filed against the rock until they are as blunt and hard as chisels. There is no softness here, no yielding flesh; they are built for the close press, for the constant clamber, for the narrow stairs where a misstep means a fall into the churning生态系统 below.

Their skin gleams with the same viscous sheen as the stone. It is the breath-stuff, of course—it condenses on them as it condenses on everything, a thin, copper-slick film that catches the dim light and makes them look as if they have been glazed. Over years, the mineral content of the vapor stains them. The Moum-folk are not born with this patina; infants arrive pale and crinkled, as strange and wet as new growth after a fire. But the copper and iron seeps in. It accumulates. By adulthood, their skin carries the undertones of the deep—verdigris greens and hematite rusts and the dark, streaked umber of oxidized stone. The elders, who have spent decades breathing and bathing in the vent-fogs, are nearly indistinguishable from the crevice walls in low light, their skin mapped with mineral deposits that feel, to the touch, like the fine grain of sandstone.

And their eyes—the visitor would notice the eyes, if they could bear to look. The Moum-folk do not meet the gaze of outsiders easily, not out of shame or evasion, but because their eyes are not made for the surfaces of things. They are large, dark, the pupils blown wide and permanent, swallowing the iris almost entirely, little black pools that drink in the perpetual dusk. They have traded the ability to see far for the ability to see near, to parse the subtle textures of shadow. A Moum-folk can read the mood of the crevice walls—the shift in humidity, the tiny movements of the pale centipedal things, the fresh gloss of breath-stuff on a mushroom’s lip—at a glance. But show them the open sky, and they will flinch, overwhelmed, their too-large pupils stinging and streaming with tears they have no practice shedding. They do not look up. They look in.

Their hair—if it can be called that—is a secondary sensor. It grows thick and dark, but they do not let it fall free. It is too easily grasped by hunting creatures, too likely to snag in the close-press of home. They bind it, weave it, knot it into complex patterns that press the hair flat against the skull and neck, serving a double purpose: it keeps the strands clear of their work, and the knots themselves, pressed by the humid air, become fibrous, almost cartilaginous, catching the vibration of the crevice-roar in the same way the echo-clicking flutter-things use their crests. A Moum-folk with unbound hair is either a child, a mourner, or someone very sick.

They speak in the register of the crevice—not the high, carrying shouts of an open-field people, but low, resonant sounds, humming and thrumming, phrases that sit in the chest and vibrate against the stone. Their language sounds, to an outsider, like the subsonic groan of the vents, like the wet click of a radula on bone. It is not a quiet language, but it is *dense*—packed with meaning, each vowel a payload, each consonant a hook that catches the ear. They do not need to shout because the stone shouts for them, carrying their voices along the walls, amplifying the deep vibrations until a whisper at the crevice's floor can be heard, faintly, at the highest watching-post.

They are singers, though a visitor might not recognize it at first. The songs are not for entertainment—they are *tools*. A particular harmonic can steady a ladder of vine. A certain rhythm, clapped against the wall, can scatter the grazing mollusks from a path. The deep-voiced chants, sung in groups during the fermenting of the drink that shares their name, are believed to settle the mineral content, to align the copper and the ash into something that will not poison the gut. Whether the songs truly affect the chemistry or simply synchronize the labor, no one asks. The songs work. That is enough.

They sing the children to sleep, and this is perhaps the only time a visitor would hear the crevice truly quiet—the creatures seeming to settle, the drip of breath-stuff fading to a whisper, as the small, fierce voices of three hundred souls rise in a low, buzzing harmony that fills the ancient wound like breath fills lungs.

The children. They are not coddled. They cannot be; there is no space for coddling, no margin for a child who does not learn, quickly, to keep their hands from the radulae and their feet from the edges. They are carried in slings of woven fungus-fiber until they can grip, and then they are set loose—supervised, but barely, by a rotating cast of aunt-figures and uncle-figures who are themselves only twelve or thirteen. The children of Moum learn to climb before they learn to walk properly, because the stairs demand it. They learn the names of the creatures before the names of their own kin, because the creatures are more likely to hurt them. They are fed spoonfuls of mineral paste and sips of deep-voice before they are weaned, their bodies calibrated early to the strange sustenance of the place, their stomachs colonized by the same gut-flora that breaks down the moss and fungi.

They play games that are, in essence, survival training. Hide-and-seek in the wall-gardens, where the seeker must track by sound and touch. Catch-the-click, where a child mimics the echo-calls of the flutter-things and the others try to pinpoint the source. And when they are old enough—seven, eight, a blur of limbs and mineral-stained skin—they join the hunts.

The hunts are not grand affairs. There are no packs, no coordinated strategies worthy of the name. A hunt in Moum is a single person, or perhaps two, moving through the dim tiers with a short, heavy spear of sharpened stone and a quiet, expectant patience. The prey are the larger arthropods, the soft-bodied mollusks, occasionally one of the vertebrate-creatures if it is wounded or slow. The hunt is close, intimate—a matter of listening for the rustle, feeling the vibration through bare feet, and striking fast and hard before the creature can flee. There is no sport in it. There is only hunger and its satisfaction, the conversion of one life into another's hours.

The Moum-folk do not glory in the kill. They do not tell stories of great hunts or cunning prey. The creatures are not adversaries; they are *neighbors*, in the most literal sense—they share the walls, the air, the food. To hunt is to cull, to take what the crevice offers, and to offer oneself in turn when the time comes. Because the Moum-folk know, with a certainty that needs no articulation, that they are part of the cycle. They eat the creatures. The creatures, given the chance, would eat them. And when a Moum-folk dies—falling, sickening, or simply growing too old to climb—they are not buried. There is no soil. There is no space. The body is carried to the lowest level, near the vent-mouths, and left. By morning, the clean-up crew of the ecosystem—the centipedal things, the beetles, the opportunistic fungi—will have reduced it to a stain and a memory. By the week's end, the stain is gone, mineralized, absorbed into the breath-stuff that condenses on the walls and feeds the moss that feeds the creatures that feed the living.

It is not callous. It is not brutal. It is the deepest respect: to be returned, fully and without remainder, to the world that made you.

The elders, then, are the ones who have not yet been returned. They are rare—not because the Moum-folk die young, but because age in the crevice is a matter of mobility. An elder is not someone who has survived many years; an elder is someone who can still climb the stairs, still hunt, still sing the settling-chants, still move in the constant, necessary motion of Moum. The moment they cannot—when a hip cracks, when lungs fill with vent-dust, when the fingers lose their grip—they are no longer elders. They are *becoming*. And the transition is swift.

But while they are elders, they are the memory of the crevice. They know the patterns—the years when the centipedal things swarm and the years they vanish, the subtle shifts in the vent-heat that presage a change in the breath-stuff's composition, the particular fungi that will bloom after a collapse of the shelf-growths. They do not write this down; there is nothing to write on and no light to read by. They *are* the writing, their bodies and voices the parchment and the ink. An elder's scar is a record of a particular hunt. An elder's limp is a record of a particular fall. An elder's silence—rare, unsettling—is a record of grief, of a child or a friend who became before their time.

The watching-posts at the top of the crevice are staffed by the young and the restless, those who still look upward out of some atavistic impulse they cannot name. They watch for threats from outside, though none have come in living memory. They watch for changes in the sky—that narrow, gray slit that seems, to the Moum-folk, to be the world's ceiling, a long, bright crack in an otherwise endless stone. Sometimes they see shapes against the light—birds, perhaps, or the shadows of clouds—and they report these sightings with the gravity of astronomers recording a comet. The outside is *theoretical* to most Moum-folk. It exists, they know, in the way that the vent-shafts exist: as a source, a below, an above, a somewhere-else that feeds the world but is not the world.

Occasionally, a watcher will descend with a strange look in their eyes—a yearning, a puzzlement. They will say they heard something from the slit. Not sound, exactly. More like an absence of sound, a silence so vast it pressed against the ears. The others will nod, and give them deep-voice, and wait for the feeling to pass. It always passes. The crevice is too loud, too full, too *present* to leave room for the void-above. But some watchers, late in their lives, will go up to the posts and stay, staring at the slit, until someone comes to bring them down or carry them to the vents.

They do not leave. No Moum-folk has ever left the crevice, at least not in the histories the elders carry. The crevice is the world. The walls are the boundaries. The breath-stuff is the water. The creatures are the neighbors. The deep-voice is the blood. Why would anyone leave?

And yet.

And yet there is a word in their language—a word the children learn early and the elders speak rarely. *Vremm*. It means "the place where the walls end." It is not a destination. It is not even a direction. It is a concept—a shape of thought, a feeling of looking up and *through* and not seeing stone. Vremm is the outside, but it is also the *idea* of outside, the haunting suspicion that the world is larger than the crevice, that the walls are not the boundaries of existence but merely the boundaries of *knowing*.

The Moum-folk do not speak of vremm as a place to go. They speak of it the way they speak of the vents—something that feeds the world, that makes it possible, but that cannot itself be inhabited. Vremm is what gives the crevice its shape, its meaning. Without the walls, there is no Moum. Without the idea of beyond-the-walls, there is no reason to build, to sing, to press close and endure.

So they endure. Three hundred souls, more or less—the number fluctuates, as numbers do in a closed system, but it never drops below two-fifty and never rises above four-hundred, as if the crevice itself regulates the population, offering more food in times of growth and less in times of plenty, maintaining the balance that has kept Moum alive for generations beyond counting. They press together in their fungus-walled homes, sharing breath and warmth and the constant, clicking, thrumming, dripping music of the crevice. They love, they breed, they lose, they mourn, they sing, they hunt, they return.

They are not happy, in the way a visitor might understand happiness. They are not sad. They are *present*—fully, fiercely present, their attention turned always inward and downward and around, never up, never out. They are the life of the wound, the healing tissue of an ancient crack in an ancient stone, and they do not know they are small because smallness requires a sense of bigness, and they have none.

They know the walls. They know the breath-stuff. They know the creatures and the stairs and the songs and the taste of copper on the tongue. They know each other, in the way you can only know someone when you have heard them breathe through a shared wall for thirty years, when you have watched their children become and return, when you have sung the settling-chants together and felt the deep-voice align in your gut.

They know the crevice.

And that is a world.
