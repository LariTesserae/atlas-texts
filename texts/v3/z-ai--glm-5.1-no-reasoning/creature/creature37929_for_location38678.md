The people of Veren have become like the pale reed they harvest: thin, supple, and rooted in saturations that would rot anything harder. Their bodies bear the hallmarks of a life spent in intimate compression. They are not a tall folk. Generations of navigating plank-width walkways and doorways that stick as the stone rises have favored the compact, the low-center. Their shoulders have grown narrow, almost deflected, shaped by the necessity of slipping through passages where the weeping walls brush against both sides. Their skin is a terrain of damp and clay. It holds a permanent pallor, bloodless as the eyeless crustaceans in the channels, and it carries the faint, mineral smell of the weep-water—a scent of clean gravel and submerged chalk that seeps from their pores along with a thin, cold sweat. Their hands are their most telling feature: broad across the palm, thickly calloused, fingers splayed wide from a lifetime of gripping wet stone, scraping failing mortar, and cranking the cage-lift. The joints of those hands swell slightly, perpetually inflamed by the chill and the damp, yet they possess a mechanical, unhesitating dexterity.

They move through Veren the way breath moves through a throat—constricted, rhythmically, with no wasted expansion. You will not see sudden gestures or sprawling postures here. To sit is to fold tightly onto a carved bench; to stand is to brace a shoulder against a timber or a wall, leaning into the embrace of the architecture. There are no handshakes, for the passages are often too narrow to face one another fully. Instead, they pass side by side, exchanging a brush of forearms or the brief press of a damp shoulder blade. They have a way of turning sideways by instinct, of sucking in their breath and narrowing their ribcages to pass one another on the walkways. It is an intimacy born of absence—of space, of distance, of air.

Their voices have become like the water: quiet, close, absorbed. They do not call out. They murmur, using a lexicon half spoken and half signed, their broad hands flickering in the perpetual grey light. Their language has no words for "thunder," for "distance," or for "horizon." But they possess forty distinct terms for the texture of wet stone—terms that distinguish the slickness of fresh weep-water from the deeper, grippy damp of aging mortar, or the cold, hostile sweat of a new fracture. When they must communicate across the tiers, they do not shout; they tap. A stone hammer rapped twice against a beam means *the cage is rising*. A hollow thud on a floorboard means *the insects are thinning on the third channel*. The settlement speaks in a continuous, murmuring percussion, a heartbeat of bone-on-wood that underlies the constant drip of the water.

Their faces are long, habitually tilted downward to watch the channels, the mortar, the next wet step. The damp and the lack of sun have bleached the color from them. Their eyes are a washed-out, watery blue or a pale, muddy green, and they have developed an astonishing sensitivity to contrast, able to read the subtle darkening of a wall that signals a fresh seep, or the faint telltale glint of a crustacean shell in the gloom. Blinking slowly, they watch the world through the fringe of their woven hoods, their expressions restrained and difficult for an outsider to read. They do not grimace at the cold or shiver visibly; these responses have been socialized out of them, or perhaps absorbed into the steady, low-grade endurance of their muscles. Pleasure, for them, is not expressed in a grin but in a long, slow exhale—the body briefly releasing its constant tension, settling deeper into the damp.

They live in an equilibrium of ongoing decay. The mortar they mix is not just clay and crustacean shell; it is a repository of their own hair, their shredded reed-cloth scraps, anything fibrous that will hold the pale sludge together a little longer. The walls of their homes are patched with the detritus of their lives. There is no trash in Veren, only material waiting to be reconstituted. A worn-out tool is sharpened into a smaller tool; a broken handle becomes a wedge; the scraping from a reed-cloth becomes stuffing for a damp pillow. They eat the half of the hardroot they are allotted, boiling it into a bland, gelatinous broth that coats the throat and warms the belly. They never question the half they leave in the pool. It is the architecture of their survival: a contract not with the earth, but with the *process* of the earth. To take all would be to assert that the emerging stone is merely a resource. To leave half is to acknowledge that they, too, are a process the stone is expressing.

For they do not see themselves as separate from Veren. They do not think of themselves as settlers who came to a place, but as an extension of the hollow’s breath. They are what the water does when it is forced upward by the rising rock. The crustacean eats the debris in the channel; the people eat the tuber that grows in the channel; the people use the crustacean's shell to hold the stone back; the stone swells and pushes the people back toward the center. They are the current running through this closed circuit of dissolution and repair. When a child is born—a rare, quiet event, attended by the murmured percussion of the walls—they do not swaddle it in dry cloth. They rub the pale clay into its skin, sealing it against the wet, initiating it into the constant negotiation. The child's first breaths are of the heavy, mineral air; its first sounds are the tap of a hammer and the drip of the weep-water.

At night, they lie packed in their lofts, shoulder to shoulder, breathing in unison. The cold presses in, but the mass of their bodies creates a pocket of tolerable warmth. They do not dream of open skies or dry deserts; such imaginations have no soil to grow in. Instead, they dream of the stone rising, of the subtle groan of timber under pressure, of water finding new paths through tight confines. They dream of being the water itself: seeping, working, slow and irresistible.

They do not grieve the weeping of the walls, for they do not hear it as grief. They hear it as a kind of birthing—the slow, agonized emergence of the young stone into the grey air, the shedding of what was held inside. And if their own lives are a constant, quiet struggle against the damp and the pressure and the narrowing confines, they do not name it suffering. It is simply the condition of being here, of being shaped by this place. They tend the channels. They harvest the root. They mix the dead into the walls to hold back the living stone. And when the rock shifts and a doorway sticks, they do not curse it; they take up their bone chisels and their wooden trowels, and they negotiate again with the water and the earth for the narrow space of their continued existence.
