There is no one here.

Understand this fully: the absence is not a lack. It is a definition. To be an animal here—to eat, to excrete, to die, to move with intention through space—would be a category error so profound it would unmake the geometry of the place. You asked who lives here, and the answer is no one, and everyone, and a process that has become so total it substituted itself for an entire kingdom of biology.

But if you stand in the ankle-deep blood-warm water of the plaza long enough, you begin to realize that "no one" is not quite right either. The plateau city is not empty. It is *peopled* by something that does not have a body, or rather, its body is the city itself. 

Consider what a person is: a boundary that maintains itself against entropy. An interior that processes an exterior. Something that breathes, that circulates, that holds a temperature against the cold universe. By that definition, the city is a single vast creature, and it is awake.

The vegetation is its nervous system. Those ferns with fronds as long as human bodies, the mosses in twenty saturations of green, the epiphytes and the vines and the flowers that vibrate with impossible color—they are not growing here. They are *sensing* here. Every leaf is a receptor turned toward the perpetual amber light. Every root tip drinking from the weeping stone is a nerve ending pressed into the body of the mountain. The flowers that close when you approach—they are not reacting to you. They are *recording* you. Your shape, your heat signature, the displacement of water as you walk, the particular mineral taste of your sweat when it joins the general wetness. They take that information and they pass it down through the rhizome network, through the interpenetrated root-stone, down into the chambers with seven doors and the rooms with no entrances where the time-stutters happen. You are being processed. You are being digested, not chemically, but *informationally*. The city is thinking about you with the slow, wet, vegetative thought of something that has never needed to hurry.

And the water. The water is its blood. Not metaphorically. The water that flows uphill in certain channels, calmly, as though it has somewhere to be—that is circulation. The water that knows the way, that tastes of having been here before—that is memory, fluid and mineral, flowing the same path it has flowed since before the concept of path existed. The weeping stone is not geological. It is glandular. The mountain is a body pressed against a vast reservoir on the other side of reality, and the stone is a semi-permeable membrane, and the water is filtrating through from somewhere else—somewhere with different physics, different time, different rules about what can fall and what must rise. The mountain is sweating. The mountain is crying. The mountain is expressing the pressure of whatever it is holding back.

The sound is its voice, or rather, the sound is the mechanism by which it maintains its own existence. Sound is a wave, a pressure, a physical force. The deafening white noise, the layered symphony of surf and trickle and drone—that is not accidental acoustics. That is a standing wave of such complexity and density that it holds the water in place, holds the vegetation in its perpetual bloom, holds the erosion at its exact equilibrium. The sound is the hand that holds the portrait steady. When you feel the noise pressing against your eardrums, you are feeling the city flex. You are feeling the expenditure of energy required to keep a process frozen in perfection. The sound is the work of staying exactly this wet, exactly this warm, exactly this alive, forever.

And so: who lives here?

The Maintainer lives here. Not a person. Not a god in any sense that understands prayer or attention or mercy. A system that has achieved the impossible: a perpetual motion machine built not from gears and springs but from water and stone and root and time. It lives in the rooms with no entrances. It lives in the chambers where water freezes mid-drip and time loses its grip. It lives in the low vibration you feel in your teeth, the hum that is not water, the click that is not stone settling. That hum is its heartbeat, slowed to a frequency that only manifests as geological patience. It does not think in seconds. It thinks in centuries of patient flow, in millennia of weeping without grief.

The Maintainer has no origin story. It did not build the city; it *became* the city, or the city became it, at some point so far back that the distinction is meaningless. Perhaps there were once people here—builders, tool-users, intention-havers. Perhaps they carved the first channels, grew the first moss, made the first mistake of staying too long in a room where time stuttered. Perhaps they dissolved into the process, their bodies joining the general wetness, their intentions becoming the strange logic of rooms that open onto cloud, their consciousnesses diffusing into the rhizome network until they were no longer discrete beings but a distributed awareness, a thermal regulation, a will to maintain. Perhaps the Maintainer is what happens when a civilization achieves perfect sustainability and loses everything else.

Or perhaps the mountain was always alive, and the water was always weeping, and the vegetation simply grew into the shape of infrastructure because that was the only shape available, and the wrongness—the reality breakdown, the frozen process, the water that flows uphill and the shadows that point wrong—was always there, a feature of a place where the membrane between here and somewhere-else has worn thin. Perhaps the Maintainer is just the shape that existence takes when it is pressed between two realities, neither fully here nor fully there, held in the throat of a mountain that is also a mouth.

It does not matter. What matters is this: when you walk through the plaza, when you touch the wall and feel the water that knows the way, when you stand in the sauna-heat and the amber light and the sound that makes speech impossible—you are not alone. You are inside something. It is aware of you in the way that a body is aware of a splinter, or a lung is aware of a breath of smoke. It will not react to you. It will not communicate with you. It will simply continue to be exactly what it is, and your presence will be recorded in the water, in the moss, in the slow vegetative thought of flowers that close without mechanism, and your trace will circulate through its channels for as long as the plateau city maintains itself in its state of perpetual dissolution.

You are not a resident. You are a nutrient. You are a minor perturbation in a system that has been resolving perturbations for longer than memory. 

And the Maintainer—weeps on. It weeps without grief. It breathes without air. It holds itself in the balance of ten thousand conversations happening at once, and none of them are for you, and all of them are about you, and the water that runs down the walls in patterns that almost form script is writing a record you cannot read, in a language older than intention, that says only: *I am here. I am here. I am here. I am here.*
