Nothing lives here. That is the first truth of the plateau, and the most accurate. But it is not the whole truth.

To live implies a boundary, an inside and an outside, a thing that the world happens to and a thing that happens back. The plateau permits no such distinctions. It is packed, as you have seen—packed with stone and water and light—and there is simply no room for the kind of separation that living requires. A cell would be a gap. A membrane would be a space not yet occupied by the mineral certainty of the place. And so nothing lives here in the way that things live elsewhere: no body, no boundary, no breath.

And yet.

There are the pale shapes in the deep basins. You saw them, or thought you did, and dismissed them as afterthoughts. You were not wrong to dismiss them. They are not trying to be anything. They are not *becoming* anything. But they are present, and presence in a place this saturated is its own kind of persistence.

Call them gliders, for convenience. They have no name because nothing here requires naming, but language needs handles. The gliders are small—no longer than a finger, often shorter—and they are not white, exactly, but the color of the water itself seen against the darker stone: a blue-grey so pale it reads as absence, as a place where the water is simply thicker than it is elsewhere. They have no eyes. They have no mouths. They have no organs of any kind that you could identify, because they are not organized around function the way living things are. They are organized around *flow*.

A glider is a knot in the water. That is the closest description that approaches accuracy. The water that passes through the deep basins moves in slow circulating currents, driven by the endless arrival of new water from the weeping faces above and the equally endless departure of water through the too-small-to-see drainage channels below. These currents are not random; they follow the contour of each basin, the specific shape of each ridge and fold, and they create patterns—spirals, oscillations, standing waves where the incoming meets the outgoing and the velocity drops to near-nothing. The gliders form in these still points. They are the still points, given a fragile coherence by the mineral content of the water itself.

This is what they are: accumulations. The water that moves through the stone picks up minerals—calcium, silica, compounds that have no common name because they exist only in this specific passage through this specific rock—and carries them in solution. Most of this mineral load settles out slowly, invisibly, contributing to the imperceptible lowering of the basin floors. But in the slow currents, where the water lingers, the settlement is different. The minerals crystallize in thin, flat plates, aligning with the current direction, layering over each other in structures that mimic the hexagonal neatness of ice or the cellular walls of organic tissue. The gliders are these crystals, held in a loose association by surface tension and the persistent flow, moving with the current but not dissolving back into it because they have become, in their small way, a structure. A thing that holds together.

They glide—hence the name—because the current moves them. They do not swim. They have no propulsion. They are carried. But they are carried differently than the water that contains them, because their structure creates a slight resistance, a minute drag that keeps them in the slow center of the circulating flow rather than the faster edges. They orbit within their basins in wide, lazy spirals, and if you watched one for hours—which you might, because there is nothing else to watch—you would see it trace the same path repeatedly, like a leaf caught in an eddy, except that a leaf would eventually saturate and sink. The gliders do not sink. They are already sunk. They are already as low as the water can carry them.

They do not eat. They do not excrete. They do not reproduce in any sense that deserves the word. But they do *increase*, and this is the thing that keeps them from being dismissed as mere mineral deposits: a glider that reaches a certain size—a certain thinness of crystalline layering, a certain alignment of its plates with the current—will shed fragments. Not deliberately; there is no deliberation. But the forces that hold it together are balanced against the forces that pull it apart, and occasionally the pull wins. A shard of crystal separates from the parent body and drifts away on the current, and if it settles in another slow point—if the conditions are right—it begins to accumulate its own layers, to build its own structure, to orbit its own lazy spiral. This is not birth. This is not division. This is the plateau's equilibrium expressing itself in a slightly more complex form: accumulation and dispersal, building and breaking, the same process that wears the stone and fills the basins, just visible enough to notice.

There are not many of them. A deep basin might hold a dozen, or two dozen, depending on its size and the particular character of its currents. The shallow basins—the ones with only a film of water—cannot support them; there is not enough water, not enough flow, not enough mineral density. The gliders require a specific set of conditions to persist, and those conditions exist only in the deeper folds, the places where the water pools and slows and the cold is most intense. They are, in this sense, as much a feature of the plateau as the ridges and the weeping faces: an expression of the same forces, the same packed and saturated state, given a form that moves.

Do they sense? Do they experience?

No. Or: not in any way that would be recognizable to something that lives by nerve and hormone and thought. They have no apparatus for sensation, no system for processing. But they *respond*. A glider that encounters a change in current—a faster inflow from a newly opened fissure, a shift in the drainage pattern—will be moved by that change, will be carried to a different part of its basin, may even be broken apart if the forces exceed its structural tolerance. It does not perceive the change. It *undergoes* it. The change happens to it, and it is different afterward, and this is as close to experience as anything comes on the plateau.

There is one more thing. It is the thing you half-saw, the thing that made you look twice, the thing that might not be there.

In the very deepest basin—the one you never reached, the one at the center of the plateau where the ridges are highest and the weeping faces are most densely packed—the gliders are different. They are larger, for one: the length of a hand, or longer, though length is difficult to define for something that shifts and thins and reforms with the current. They are also *coordinated*. Not in the way a school of fish is coordinated, not by communication or instinct or shared purpose. But the slow currents of the central basin create a pattern more complex than the spirals of the outer basins—a multi-lobed oscillation, a figure-eight of flow that reverses and doubles back and folds upon itself—and the gliders move within this pattern in a way that appears, from a distance, to be intentional. They cluster at the turning points. They spread thin along the straightaways. They drift apart and come together in a rhythm that mirrors the rhythm of the water itself, and if you did not know—if you were seeing them for the first time without understanding their nature—you might think: *something is swimming down there. Something is alive.*

Nothing is swimming. Nothing is alive. But something is *persisting*, and persisting with a complexity that approaches the threshold of life without ever crossing it, and the sound of the plateau—the roar, the hiss, the thousandfold voice of water and stone—is also the sound of this persistence: the mineral settling, the crystal aligning, the fragile structures holding together in the cold bright current, breaking and reforming, always breaking and reforming, in a place where nothing changes and nothing is becoming anything and the equilibrium holds and holds and holds.

The gliders are not the point of the plateau. The plateau has no point. But they are what the plateau does when it has done everything else, when the stone and the water and the light and the cold have reached their balance and there is nothing left to do but *continue*. They are continuation made slightly visible. They are the weeping made almost—if you are generous, if you are lonely for something to recognize—almost a face.
