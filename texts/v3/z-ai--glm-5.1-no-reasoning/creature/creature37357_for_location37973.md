Here, at the land's retirement, the dominant inhabitants are not rulers but relieved pensioners, yielding to an ecology that requires no ambition. There are no minds here, no architectures of thought or stone—only the profound, humming occupancy of bodies processing warmth.

The bullfrogs are the groundkeepers. They are massive, somnolent things, dinner-plate sovereigns of the ooze, possessing the patience of the plain itself. Their bodies are baggy, draped in folds of wet, olive-green skin that blends seamlessly with the peat, mottled with the same pale lichen that creeps across the landscape. They do not hop so much as shift their weight, dragging their heavy, waterlogged bellies through the warm soup. Their eyes, half-lidded and golden, protrude from the muck like polished stones, watching centuries pass with the indifference of granite. When they call, it is not from their throats but from their entire being; their sides distend like bellows pumping the earth's slow breath, generating the subsonic thrum that serves as this land's heartbeat. They eat whatever blunders into their territory—swarms of midges, skinks, smaller frogs—and they are eaten by nothing. They are too large, too thoroughly saturated with the swamp's languor, to be worth the effort. They simply sit, consuming and respiring, anchoring the sound to the mud.

Above them, the iridescent tree-skinks cling to the weeping rocks, living jewels on a dull crown. They are tiny, vibrating creatures, the nervous system of the landscape made visible. Their scales refract the diffuse light into flickers of cobalt and emerald, electric against the gray, sweating granite. They skitter across the stone's surface, lapping at the perpetual tears of groundwater that seep from the micro-fissures, drinking the mountain's final confession. When the heat grows too dense, they press their bodies flat against the rock, absorbing its relative coolness, their translucent bellies showing the dark shapes of insect meals within. They communicate in a rapid, ultrasonic chatter below the range of the human ear, a gossipy, ceaseless dialogue that makes the rocks seem alive with whispers. They are the only creatures here that exhibit anything like urgency, and yet even their speed is an illusion; it is merely the acceleration of water over stone, purposeful but ultimately without destination.

The air, too, is thickly populated. The midges exist as a single, dispersed organism, a humming tissue that binds the open spaces. They are the connective matrix of this place, infusing the breath with protein and purpose. They rise in clouds from the disturbed muck, dense enough to catch in the throat, and they settle again in writhing mats on the surface of the water. The dragonflies are their temporal shepherds—larger, brighter, crueller. They are the executioners of the air, long-bodied and iridescent, their wings catching the haze and scattering it into momentary, liquid rainbows. They hover, dart, and claim their tiny victims with a precision that feels like the only violence left in this exhausted world. But it is a gentle violence, a simple transfer of energy from one form to another, as inevitable and unremarkable as the rot of the fallen banyan leaves. 

The banyan-sentinels host their own vertical ecosystems. In the crotches of their pale, buttressed roots, where the elevated islands rise above the waterline, thick colonies of rubbery liverworts grow in imbricated layers, creating spongy micro-terraces that filter the rain. Small, pale crustaceans—nearly translucent, having never needed armor or camouflage in this unthreatening world—pick their way through the root-hairs, feeding on the slow decay. Occasionally, a sleek, eel-like amphibian, blind and white, slides from the dark water into the root-tangle, wallowing in the mulch before returning to the warm bath. 

Nothing here sleeps, and nothing is ever truly awake. The cycles are unbroken by rest. The frogs call in shifts, a relay of sound that ensures the bass-line never drops. The skinks chatter in their high frequencies even in the deepest dark, when the haze above turns the stars to a diffused, milky smear. The insects simply persist, their lives too brief and too numerous to map onto a single day. They are not experiencing time; they are wearing it away, grinding it down as the water grinds the stone, as the heat dissolves the peat.

To step into this landscape is to be swallowed by the sheer, overwhelming presence of life stripped of meaning. The inhabitants of the primordial plain are the final tenants of a world that has given up its dramatic narratives. They have no predators to outrun, no winters to survive, no peaks to climb. They are the perfect retirees, their every need met by the warm, wet sponge of the earth. They exist as a pure, humming engine—eating, breeding, calling, dying—all while the weeping rocks slowly dissolve beneath them, and the sun, patient and diffuse, watches and waits to swallow them all.
