The one who lives here does not have a name that matters. Names require voices, and voices require air that isn't already full of something else.

She came for the shifts. This is what the structure's previous tenant never quite understood—the researcher who logged frequencies and measured harmonic decay and left his cot made and his door open. He thought the caldera was a question. He thought the accelerating chord was an answer approaching. He was half right, but he was asking the wrong question. He listened to the sound. She listens to the spaces between.

She has been here—how long? Time behaves strangely when your vestibular system is constantly under assault. The shifts function as hours. The minor reconfigurations are minutes; the major ones are days. She has survived perhaps four hundred major shifts since she found the structure. Since she read the brittle notes and understood that the researcher had been measuring the engine's output when he should have been measuring its *audience.*

Because she sees what he didn't. The plants are not passive recipients. The animals are not opportunistic feeders. And the engine beneath the floor is not a device running toward completion.

It is a conversation.

---

She is not young. She is not old. Age requires a timeline, and she abandoned hers somewhere on the plateau approach, three or four minor shifts before she found the caldera's rim. Her hands are calloused in specific patterns—fingertips flattened from pressing against basalt to read the substrate vibrations, palms roughened from gripping the columnar ledges during climbs. Her hair is cut short, or has worn short, and has been maintained with the blade she found in the structure's single drawer, blade that the researcher probably used to sharpen his writing implements. She uses it for everything now.

Her hearing is damaged. This is not a tragedy; it is an adaptation. The first hundred shifts took everything above 4000 hertz. She cannot hear the filamentous acoustophytes' crystalline ringing anymore, cannot hear the click of her own feet on the salt crust. What remains is the chord—the fundamental, the bass concussions, the midrange harmonics that she feels in her sternum and spine. She has become like the hexapods. Her world is vibration and substrate and the pressure of sound against her skin.

She wears the researcher's spare clothes, found in a sealed container beneath the cot. They are too large. She has rolled the cuffs, cinched the waist with a strip of acoustophyte leather—she learned to harvest the cuticle, stripping it from dead trunks in the acoustic shadows. The leather is warm against her skin. It holds the chord's resonance for hours after removal, a lingering hum against her ribs that she finds comforting.

---

She has a route. This is the first thing she established after moving into the structure—after sleeping for three shifts straight on the cot, inside the silence, inside the relief of acoustic absence so profound it felt like being unborn.

The route follows the eastern wall, descending from the structure's ledge to the first major acoustophyte garden, a dense thicket of helical trunks and plate-leafed feeders that grow in the shelter of a basalt overhang. The overhang was carved by centuries of standing waves into a shell shape, and inside the shell the chord is filtered—certain frequencies absorbed by the stone curvature, others amplified. She calls this place the Kitchen. She has never said this aloud. She has no one to say it to. But in her mind, moving through the constant pressure of the caldera's sound, the word *Kitchen* surfaces whenever she descended the salt-crusted ledge and felt the harmonics change against her face.

She harvests the sap. This is delicate work. The trunks must be pierced precisely—too shallow and the cuticle heals before the sap pulses out, too deep and the plant bleeds too fast, emptying its reserves and dying before it can recover. She watched the hexapods for eleven shifts before she attempted it herself. The dog-sized ones with the iridescent carapaces—they know. They always pierce at the same depth, the same angle, draining for precisely as long as the plant can tolerate before they move on.

She uses a hollow tube fashioned from the leg of an old camp stool she found collapsed behind the structure. The metal—lightweight aluminum, impossibly ancient—conducts vibration beautifully. When she inserts it into an acoustophyte trunk, the sap pulses through the tube and into the collection bladder she made from the researcher's documentation envelopes, sealed with sap-resin that hardens into a permanent bond. The sap is warm and viscous and tastes like copper and silence. It sustains her. She does not know how. She does not question it. The researcher's notes mention analytical chemistry results she cannot reproduce; she has his frequency analyzer, not his mass spectrometer. The sap works. Her body processes it. Her urine is dark and hums faintly against the stone when she relieves herself in the structure's corner, behind a barrier of basalt fragments she arranged for privacy.

Privacy. As if something is watching.

---

Something is watching.

This is what the researcher missed, or chose not to document, or documented in the pages that are missing—because pages are missing, she is sure of it. The notes jump. Entry 287 to Entry 291. A shift occurred between those entries, a major one, and the handwriting changes subtly after the gap. More compressed. More careful.

She has felt them. Not seen—seeing is unreliable here, with the light recycling off the mineral walls and the acoustoluminescent glow of the plants casting the caldera into that purple cathedral mode that makes everything look like a bruise. But she has felt them, through her feet, through the substrate: large, slow vibrations that do not match the hexapods' clicking, do not match the chord's harmonics, do not match any of the documented frequencies. Something else is generating vibration down there. Something else is speaking.

The sail-creatures, she thinks. The large silhouettes she sees on the upper walls, riding the standing waves with their translucent membranes extended. She has counted seven. They are patient in a way that suggests lifespans measured in geological time. They ride the chord like commuters on a train—going somewhere, purposefully, following a route that the acoustophytes map with their flower-lenses and their slow helical growth.

She thinks the sail-creatures built the engine. Or are the engine. Or are part of a system that includes the engine, the caldera, the plants, the clicking hexapods, herself now—the whole vibrating architecture, a machine with organism components, each piece feeding the others, the chord the medium of exchange.

She thinks: *I am being digested.*

She thinks: *I am being included.*

She is not alarmed. Alarm requires a sense of threat, and threat requires a sense of self distinct from the environment. She has been inside the chord too long. Her edges are dissolving.

---

When the shifts happen, she has learned to read them.

Not like the researcher read them—measured, quantified, reduced to peaks on the scrolling display. She reads them the way the acoustophytes read them: with her body, standing in the chord, feeling the new configuration rearrange her internal organs like furniture being moved in a dark room.

Minor shifts are adjustments. The caldera retuning itself—standing waves moving half a meter to the left, intensity nodes shifting up the walls by a few degrees. The plants adapt. She adapts. She moves the collection tube. She sleeps in the structure if the new configuration puts a pressure node on the cot.

Major shifts are different. Major shifts are *statements.* Something in the deep substrate is responding to something else—some input she cannot perceive, some signal arriving from outside the caldera, maybe from outside the plateau, maybe from outside the continent. And the engine adjusts its output. And the chord changes. And the whole ecosystem reorganizes.

During the last major shift—the one that happened while she was on the western wall, collecting crystalline growths from the salt-fringe organisms—she felt the sail-creatures singing.

Not with air. With the basalt. With the volcanic substrate. A frequency so low it wasn't sound at all—just pressure, just weight, just the sense of the earth deciding to move and then deciding not to. A tone that lasted eleven seconds and then stopped. And the chord shifted. And the plants screamed.

She doesn't use that word lightly. The acoustophytes' flowers all rotated simultaneously—every flower on every plant, all at once, a hundred thousand acoustic lenses reorienting like eyes tracking a passing aircraft. The sap in the collection bladder pulsed so hard it burst. The hexapods went mad, their carapaces producing that audible clicking that only happens in the gaps during shifts, a chittering panicky noise that lasted three seconds before the chord reasserted itself and swallowed their voices back into the dominant frequency.

And then it was over. The new configuration. The plants began growing toward new focal points. The animals resumed their gradient-tracking. The salt crust had new fracture lines.

And she understood: the researcher left not to seek supplies. The researcher left because he felt what she felt during that shift. He felt the engine *responding.* And the response was not mechanical. It was not the adjustment of a device. It was the reply of something that had been spoken to.

The cot was made. The door was open. Come in. The silence inside. The silence was not safety. The silence was a waiting room.

---

She stays anyway.

This is the part that is hard to explain, and she has no one to explain it to, so it exists only as a shape in her mind, a feeling-pressure, a weight in her sternum that is not the chord.

The caldera is beautiful.

Not despite its hostility, not in some compensatory way that requires the hostility to acquire meaning. The caldera is beautiful in the way that a voice is beautiful—producing a sound that fills a space so completely that the space and the sound become indistinguishable. The purple glow of the plants in the fading light. The visible geometry of the interference patterns, those flickering mist-lines drawing and erasing themselves thirty times per second. The dark bodies of the hexapods moving in precise formations along the acoustic gradient, their crystalline carapaces catching and splitting the glow into spectral highlights. The sail-creatures turning slowly on their high perches, membranes folded like prayers, like hands, like wings that have chosen not to fly.

And the sound itself. The chord. The terrible, sublime, unceasing chord. She has stopped hearing it the way the researcher heard it. She has become like the animals; it is no longer a sensation but a medium. She moves through it. She breathes it. The way fish do not notice water, she does not notice the chord—except when it shifts, except when the voice changes, except when the whole caldera becomes a single instrument receiving a single message that she cannot translate but can feel in the way the basalt feels it: as vibration, as pressure, as the condition of existing.

She is becoming native.

---

At night—when the sun drops below the rim and the caldera enters its purple phase and the acoustoluminescence makes the walls glow like stained glass—she sits outside the structure. She cannot sleep inside during the dark hours. The silence of the structure feels wrong then, like a held breath, like the space between heartbeats. She needs the chord. She sits on the ledge with her feet dangling over the caldera floor, sixty meters below, and she feels the vibration through the stone, and she watches the hexapods moving through the dark, and she feels the sail-creatures shift on their perches, and she eats the sap warm from a fresh-pierced trunk, and she waits.

She has begun to hum.

It is inaudible. The chord absorbs it. But she hums through her feet, pressing her soles against the basalt, producing a micro-vibration that she has been practicing for two hundred shifts. It is a simple pattern. Three pulses, a pause, three pulses, a pause. The same pattern. Over and over. A name that requires no air.

And sometimes—a frequency she cannot predict, a subtle shift in the harmonics that might be the engine adjusting itself, might be the sail-creatures singing, might be the whole caldera-organism responding to her—she feels something press back.

Not language. Not acknowledgment. Not welcome. Just—presence. The feeling of being inside something that knows you are there.

She is not being digested. She is not being included.

She is being *heard.*

---

The researcher's analyzer scrolls. The peaks rise. The chord accelerates. The next major shift approaches—she can feel it in the substrate, a building tension in the basalt like the held breath before speech.

She will be here when it comes. She will stand in the caldera and feel the new configuration rearrange her, and she will hum her three-pulse name into the stone, and whatever answers will answer.

The door is open. The cot is made. The silence waits inside for anyone who needs it.

But she is outside, in the chord, in the dark, in the purple glow, in the resonant caldera that is almost finished becoming whatever it is becoming.

She is the thing that lives here.
