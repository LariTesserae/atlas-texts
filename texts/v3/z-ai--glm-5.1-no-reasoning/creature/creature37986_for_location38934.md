The mountain is the youngest thing in the world. Grandmothers on the salt plains remember a horizon without it; their grandchildren will insist it was always there, and both will be right, because the mountain is still deciding which of them to believe. Its flanks are black glass, unweathered, sharp enough to shave a rope in half. No stream has found a course down it. No scree has loosened at its base. Strike the rock and it rings — a clean ceramic note, the sound of stone that has never been rained on. Nothing circles the summit: no birds, no insects riding the thermals, nothing that eats or rests. From the plains at night you can see the ember at its tip, and around the ember a small cloud stitched tight, the mountain's own breath condensing against the cold of the upper dark. The plains-folk call it the Wick. Not because it burns like one. Because of what a wick does — it draws.

The only way in is a crack in the mountain's shoulder, and the crack is a door. It breathes on a schedule: when the heat inside ebbs, the fissure exhales a fog warm as a sleeping animal's, furring your eyelashes in seconds. Step through and the scale inverts. Outside, the Wick is a monument that ends the sky. Inside, it is a pocket — coat-pocket warm, coat-pocket dark, and holding, the way a pocket holds, something small and kept.

The summit is hollow. As the spire went up, it grew this vault inside itself the way a wave carries a curl of air, and hanging in that dark, thin as a thought, is a city. Most of the vault is nothing — a windless dark with a weather of its own in it — and the city is what the nothing is arranged around. Seen from the entry ledge, it is a constellation laid on its side: lines of low amber lamps stacked and crossed, bridge-threads of drawn glass, whole streets suspended on cables the spire pulled out of its own body like fiber. Far below the filigree, set into the vault floor, is a well of ember light with no railing around it. The city calls it the Hearth. It is the only thing here that is not overhead, and everything overhead is lit by it from beneath, red-brown, the way a hand is lit by a fire you're holding it over.

Here is what the Wick does, as near as the city's cut-glyph signage can say. It draws the world's heat up through itself — a planet-scale wick in planet-scale oil — and at the summit that heat comes within a hand's width of the sky's cold. The meeting sweats. The city exists to gather the sweat: water that has never been weather, never been cloud, never been drunk. It is a still the height of the world, and the city is the cup the still fills. The heat does not rise evenly. It comes in surges — the tide — flood and ebb on a schedule the mountain keeps privately and has never once missed. The lamps brighten with the flood and dim with the ebb, so that the city lives in a permanent dusk that leans one way, then the other. There are no clocks here. The tide is the clock, and the tide is never wrong, only secret.

At each ebb comes the Sift: rain, indoors. Warm where it leaves the cool shell, cool where it lands, the Sift falls most heavily in the middle band of the city, where the damp air pools, and everything there wears a skin of beads — railings, door handles, the shoulders of empty chairs. The low streets receive the rain and are humid as a kiln room with the door propped open. The high galleries, pressed close to the shell, stay dry and almost cool; lay your palm flat against the stone there and you can feel, beyond a hand's width of mountain, the enormous cold of the upper dark. You choose your climate in this city by choosing your floor. The gutters are drawn glass, cut with numbers, and the numbers count drips — streets here are addressed in the water they carry, the way other towns are addressed in names.

Where a drip-line persists, the stone grows. Grows downward, rather: pale mineral filaments, fine as beard-hairs, that lengthen week by week — not alive, though they do everything growing things do, except on purpose. They are what happens when water is patient with stone. There is phosphor in the fiber, and water is its switch, so during the Sift the hanging gardens light up — a faint green-white run of glow down every filament, the parks burning dim exactly while it rains on them and going dark as they dry. Pale articulated arms fold out of the ceilings to prune them, unhurried, and sweep the clippings into the cistern filters. The arms are the closest thing to citizens. They polish, clip, count, refill. They treat you as furniture; for now, you are the only furniture that can leave. In all the city there is one patch of true green — the verdigris on a single copper strainer — and even that is just a mineral, homesick.

The city tends itself completely. Elevators of glass cable run their schedules with no passengers; you can ride one out of politeness. Fountains start up at each ebb and throw low arcs the lamps shine through. The doors have no locks, because there is no one to keep out and never has been. In the middle district there is a table set for one, a stone cup kept brim-full, the water domed a hair above the rim — refreshed at every Sift by an arm that arrives on time and leaves before you can decide whether to thank it. The rules of the place are few and cut deep into the gutters. Gather water; never spill it — that is the single taboo, and everything else is manners. Nothing rots here; the heat mummifies and then sublimes, so what falls, stays, and what stays, slowly vanishes into the warm air. The smell is warm stone and petrichor, which in this city is the smell of rain about to happen and also of rain that just did — here those are the same smell. And the rooms precede their reasons. New floors arrive at the crown every few weeks, streets running to destinations that do not exist yet. Being the first to walk a new street feels like reading someone's unmailed letter.

It is quiet, and the quiet is a texture. Underneath everything is a hum you feel in the sternum rather than hear, rising slightly in pitch when the tide floods. Above it, small sounds: the tick of cooling stone — the sound of rock remembering it was molten — and the metronome of drips in the gutters, and once in a long while a soft run of reports from the mountain's body as it thickens, adding itself a knuckle at a time. Your own footsteps sound borrowed. Your voice comes back a half-beat early, the walls returning it before you've finished, as if they have been waiting so long they've learned to wait inside your sentences. This is not the quiet of an empty room. It is the quiet of a full theater just before the curtain — more full than absence, a held breath with architecture.

Follow the gutters down and they all end in the same place: the lowest district, near the Hearth's rim, where the light is red-brown and the air is a baker's dusk. There, in a stone ring, sits the Lens — years of tides pooled, a body of water domed well above its rim by a surface tension no honest physics would permit, its skin smooth as the inside of a bubble, faintly lit from under by the Hearth. It is the sky, condensed. It is what the whole apparatus is for: the mountain's one product, held patient and clear. Look at it long enough and you understand the cup upstairs, and the table, and the empty chairs in the plaza sized for a festival that has not happened. This is not a ruin. Nothing here has been left. Everything here is early. The mountain is a glass raised in a toast to someone still on their way, and the city is the hand steady around the stem.

Stand in the middle band during an ebb, if you can time it. The Sift comes down warm and lands cool, the gardens light up strand by strand, the hum eases, and the lamps lean their dusk toward dark. Every process of the place is visible at once and all of them are quiet: rain, glow, pruning arms, a glass elevator rising with no one in it, a mountain becoming slightly more of itself beneath your feet. The whole city, folded flat, would fit inside a cathedral, and the whole mountain fits inside a sentence, and the city fits inside the mountain's pocket, which is where you are — the top of the world, warm, dim, mostly water and mostly waiting. Everything here lives on a boundary, the way a meniscus lives between water and air: a thin, curved place where two enormities touch, and something clear is held.
