# What Lives Here

Something lives here that has never been named.

Not the frogs. The frogs are obvious—jewel-bright, deliberate, their throats pulsing like small engines set to idle. They sit on leaves that should not hold their weight and seem untroubled by this. They are not what I mean. Not the snakes either, patient and fluid, dissolving into the bark they rest on until the moment of contraction. Not the arthropods, black and jointed, their mechanical progress across wet stone so steady it could be clockwork. These are the surface life, the visible machinery, and they belong here as obviously as the moss belongs on the rock.

What lives here *properly*—what this place belongs to—is something you become aware of slowly, the way you become aware that someone is standing behind you.

---

It begins with the breathing.

The steam vents chuff on their rhythm—*pssshhh… pssshhh…*—and after a while, you stop hearing it as a mechanical process. The intervals are not quite regular. They lag, then catch, then lag again, the way a sleeping body breathes. You catch yourself syncing to it. Your own lungs begin to match the mountain's, and that is when you understand that you are standing on something that is not, in the strictest sense, asleep.

The heat is its exhalation. Not metaphor. The heat comes from below, from the geothermal veins, from the pressurized water moving through 1.8 billion years of stone, and it rises through the rock's pores the way warmth rises through a body's tissue. The condensation on every surface is the same process that forms on skin in a humid room. The mountain sweats. The mountain breathes. The weeping streaks of mineral runoff down the basalt faces are not oxidation. They are the slow, patient leakage of something very large maintaining its temperature.

You did not notice at first because the vegetation is so aggressive, so consumptive, that it disguises the shape of what is underneath. The moss, the ferns, the lithophytes, the roots prying into micro-fractures—all of it is a pelt. A living pelt laid over the stone, drinking from the moisture that seeps through. The cliff faces, weathered into smooth, monumental bulges and deep recesses, have the topology of a torso seen at impossible scale. The staggered descent of basalt palisades is the architecture of ribs. The plunging valley below, that fist of green and grey extending dozens of miles, is the open cavity of a body laid on its back.

But it is not dead. It is not even dormant. It is resting. The steam is proof of this. The heat is proof. The water cycling through it—stone to steam, steam to condensation, condensation to drip, drip back to stone—is its circulation, its respiration, its metabolism. It is working in the dark, in the wet, in the heat, perfectly content to lie here on the shoulder of the escarpment with the sun beating down on its pelt and the fog pooling in its hollows.

---

The fauna know this.

The frogs do not sit on leaves. They sit on its skin. The leaves are a convenience, a surface layer, but the frogs face inward, their throats pulsing in time with the venting, as though monitoring. The black arthropods do not pick at algae. They move along the weeping stone in steady, deliberate lines, tracing the mineral runoff the way ants trace a chemical trail, and their path always leads to the fissures, the vents, the places where the steam escapes. They are tending something. Or feeding on something. Or simply *attending*, the way a small bird attends a hippopotamus—picking at the surface, useful in its minor way, tolerated.

The snake coiled on the mossy branch is not hunting. It is draped. It lies along a limb of the body the way a vine lies along it, in the posture of something that belongs to the larger shape. Its scales match the wet bark because the wet bark and the snake's scales are part of the same system, the same color palette, the same patient saturation. It does not strike at the birds that come near. It does not need to. The birds land on the body, briefly, and then leave. They do not stay long. They seem to understand something about the body that the snake does not need to enforce.

The insects are different. The insects are the body's white blood cells, or its digestive system, or its nervous system—you cannot tell which. They swarm in the humid pockets, in the greenhouse-green shadows under the overhangs, and their movement is not random. They cluster where the heat is strongest. They cluster where the steam vents. They cluster where the rock is warmest, where the body's internal temperature is closest to the surface. They are measuring something. Or drawn to something. Or *are* the something, the way a single neuron is not the mind but participates in it.

---

And then there is the wrongness.

It is slight. You almost miss it. But the proportions are not right for a mountain that breathes. The steam vents are too well-placed, too evenly distributed across the cliff face, like the respiratory pores of an insect. The erosion—the slow, settled negotiation with gravity that has worn the stone into smooth bulges and echoing recesses—has produced forms that are too symmetrical for geology. Too intentional. The deep, echoing recesses are not random. They are spaced. They have the regularity of a structure, not a collapse.

And the roots. The roots that grip the sheer faces like sinew, prying into micro-fractures, drinking from the water that perpetually weeps from the stone's pores—they are not growing into the rock. They are growing *out of* it. They are part of the body. They are the body's vascular system, reaching outward, branching into finer and finer capillaries, drawing moisture from the air the way the body draws moisture from below. The moss is the same. The ferns are the same. The entire carpet of vegetation, that aggressive, nearly malignant green that consumes every available inch, is not consuming at all. It is *growing*. It is the body extending itself, putting out leaves, photosynthesizing, turning the diffuse, blinding glare of the refracted sun into energy that it pulls back down into the stone.

The mountain is not covered in plants. The mountain *is* the plants. The mountain is not beneath the vegetation. The mountain and the vegetation are one continuous organism, one vast, lithic-botanical body, lying on its back in the equatorial heat, breathing steam, sweating condensation, tending its small attendants, and waiting.

---

For what?

Nothing. For nothing. It is not waiting. It is simply *being*, the way a crocodile lies in the sun and does not wait for anything because the sun is enough, the heat is enough, the wet is enough. It has been here for 1.8 billion years. It was stone before it was this. It was magma before it was stone. It was something else before it was magma. The heat drove the engine then and the heat drives the engine now. The water cycled through it then and the water cycles through it now. The small creatures that live on its surface have changed shape many times—the amphibians were not always amphibians, the insects were not always insects, the snakes were not always snakes—but the body has not changed. It has only grown its pelt thicker. It has only refined its vents. It has only learned, over millennia, to breathe a little more steadily, to sweat a little more efficiently, to support a little more life on its surface.

It is active, locked in its slow, wet rotation. It is aware, in the way that a body is aware of itself without thinking. It does not know you are standing on its shoulder. You are too small. You are the size of a mite on a hippopotamus. You are standing in its pelt, breathing its breath, sweating its sweat, and it has not noticed you because it has never had reason to notice anything that small.

And then you leave, or you don't. And the mountain breathes, and the steam vents, and the frogs pulse, and the roots pry, and the water cycles, and the heat holds, and the green engine turns in the dark, and nothing changes, because nothing needs to.
