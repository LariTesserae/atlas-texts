# What Lives Here

## The Inheritors

Let me not begin with the root. The root is the place. The root is the world. To describe what lives in the Great Ferment by starting with the root would be like describing the life of a city by starting with the bedrock, or the life of a reef by starting with the calcium carbonate. The root is the condition. The root is the given. What lives *in* the Ferment, what lives *on* it, what lives *because* of it — that is a different question, and the answer has a different texture.

Let me begin instead with the broth itself, because the broth is where everything that lives here begins and ends, and because the broth is not what you think it is.

You think it is water. You think it is a medium. You think it is the thing that fills the space between the interesting parts.

The broth is alive.

Not alive in the way a pond is alive — not a collection of microorganisms suspended in a liquid. Alive in the way blood is alive. The broth is the root-organism's circulatory fluid, its interstitial space, its externalized metabolism. Every molecule of dissolved sugar in it was placed there by the root. Every molecule of dissolved oxygen was produced by the root. Every enzyme, every tannin, every trace mineral — the broth is a constructed solution, maintained at a precise concentration by an organism that has been maintaining it for longer than "longer than you can imagine" comfortably covers. The broth is not where the root lives. The broth is part of the root. The broth is the root, externalized, dispersed, made fluid.

Which means that everything that lives in the broth lives in the root.

Which means that everything that lives here is, in a sense that becomes more uncomfortable the longer you think about it, a parasite.

Or a symbiont.

Or a thought.

## The Mobile Filterers

Call them the ribbons, because they move like ribbons, because their bodies are flat and long and slightly translucent and they undulate through the broth with a motion that suggests silk in a slow current. They are the most visible fauna of the Ferment's upper zones, the ones you see first when the amber light reveals the column-forest, the ones that give the landscape its sense of being inhabited rather than merely occupied.

A ribbon is not a fish. Let me be clear about this, because the temptation to describe it as a fish is strong, and the temptation is wrong. A fish is a vertebrate. A fish has a skull, a spine, a jaw, paired fins, a brain encased in cartilage or bone. A ribbon has none of these things. A ribbon is a membrane. It is, structurally, a flat tube — an inner channel through which broth flows and an outer wall that is, at its thinnest point, only six cells thick. Six cells. You can see through a ribbon. You can see its internal structure, which is: the tube, the filter apparatus (a series of folded membranes that increase surface area, like the pages of a book), the muscular wall (a single layer of contractile cells that runs the length of the body and produces the undulation), and the reproductive organs, which are two small dense masses located near the anterior end and look, when you see them through the translucent body wall, like seeds inside a pod.

They range from ten centimeters to three meters. The small ones move in groups — not schools, not flocks, but aggregations, loose clusters that drift through the same current and seem to benefit from proximity without any detectable social structure. The large ones are solitary. They move slowly. Their undulation is so gradual that at any given moment a large ribbon appears to be holding still, curved into a shape that looks deliberate, like a letter in an alphabet you cannot read, and only by watching for several minutes do you realize that the shape has changed, that the ribbon has traveled, that what seemed like a pose was a frame in a motion too slow to parse as motion.

They feed by swimming. This is the simplest way to describe it, and it is almost accurate. The ribbon moves forward, drawing broth in through an anterior opening, passing it through the folded filter membranes, extracting the dissolved sugars and enzymes, and expelling the depleted fluid through a posterior vent. The feeding is continuous. The swimming is continuous. For a ribbon, swimming and eating and breathing are the same act. There is no distinction. There is no moment when a ribbon is not doing all three.

This means that a ribbon's entire life is a single uninterrupted gesture. It began swimming when it was born — when it separated from its parent's body, a bud that broke free and immediately began to move, because not moving meant not feeding meant not living — and it will continue swimming until it dies, and the only thing that will change is its size, and its size changes so slowly that you would need to watch a ribbon for weeks to detect any growth at all.

How long do they live? I do not know. No one knows. The Ferment does not track its fauna the way a terrestrial ecosystem tracks its fauna, because the Ferment does not track anything. But consider: the swimmers do not alter their paths. The population does not change. The number of ribbons in the upper Ferment has been the same for so long that the number is a property of the system, like the temperature. If births exactly match deaths, and the population is stable, then either the lifespan is very short (and the turnover is rapid but invisible because the dead are immediately dissolved) or the lifespan is very long (and the turnover is so slow that no one has been watching long enough to see it).

I think it is the latter. I think the ribbons live for a very long time. I think a ribbon that is three meters long has been swimming for decades, possibly centuries, and that its path through the column-forest is not random but established — a route it has followed so many times that the route is now part of its body, part of its neural equivalent, a habit so deep it has become physiology. But I cannot prove this. No one can. The Ferment does not cooperate with observation, because observation requires change, and change does not happen here.

## The Sessile Gardeners

The things that grow on the columns are harder to describe, because they are harder to see as individuals. They blend. They merge. A cluster of sessile filterers on a column wall looks, at first glance, like a texture — like the surface of the column itself has developed a rash, a bloom, a crop of protuberances. It is only when you look closely that you realize the protuberances are separate organisms, each anchored to the root-column at a single point, each expanding outward into the broth in shapes that reflect, above all else, the need to maximize surface area.

They are not coral. Coral builds. These do not build. They grow, and they die, and their dead tissue remains attached to the column as a structure on which new organisms grow, and over time — over long time — this layering produces something that looks like a reef but is actually a cemetery that is also a garden that is also alive.

The most common form is the shelf. A flat, slightly curved plate of tissue, anchored at one edge to the column, extending outward like a balcony. The upper surface is furred with cilia — not the microscopic cilia of a cell, but macroscopic structures, millimeters long, that wave in synchronized waves visible to the naked eye. The synchronization is not neural. It is mechanical — each cilium is connected to its neighbors by a flexible band of tissue, and the wave is a physical consequence of that connection, the way a row of dominoes falls. The cilia create a current. The current moves broth across the shelf's upper surface. The upper surface filters the broth. The shelf feeds.

The shelves layer. A column that has been colonized by shelves develops a series of horizontal projections, one above the other, like the floors of a building. The oldest shelves are at the bottom — dense, dark, compressed, barely alive, their cilia long since fallen away, their filter surfaces covered with a fine deposit of sediment that the broth carries. The youngest shelves are at the top — thin, bright, their cilia waving vigorously, their tissue almost transparent. Between them, a gradient of age and function, a vertical timeline of colonization and growth and slow decline.

But here is the thing: the oldest shelves are not dead. They are barely alive, but they are alive. Their tissue is still connected to the root-column, still drawing a minimal supply of nutrients from the contact point, still metabolizing at a rate so slow that it is almost undetectable. They are in a state that has no analog in the world you know — not alive, not dead, not dormant, but *persisting*. They have been persisting for so long that they have become part of the column's surface, part of the texture, and if you were to run your hand over them (you cannot, you should not, but if you did) you would feel the difference between root-wood and shelf-tissue as a change in warmth, not in texture. The root-wood is slightly warmer. The shelf-tissue is slightly cooler. The difference is one degree. It is the difference between something that is actively metabolizing and something that is almost not.

There are other forms. Globular filterers, anchored by thin stalks, their bodies pulsing — expanding and contracting, expanding and contracting — as they pump broth through their internal cavities. Their pulse rate is slow. One cycle every four minutes. The pulse is visible. It is rhythmic. It is, of all the things in the Great Ferment, the thing most likely to induce a trance state in an observer, because the rhythm is close to the rhythm of slow breathing, and the broth is warm, and the light is amber, and the sound of the pumps is a drone, and the globular filterers pulse, pulse, pulse in the amber, and you could watch them for a very long time and not notice time passing, because time is not passing, because time does not pass here.

And the sheets. The translucent membranes that drift between columns. These are the hardest to describe because they are the hardest to see. They are one cell thick in places. They are meters across. They move — not under their own power, but with the currents of the broth, catching particulate matter that the root-organism has shed, dissolving it, incorporating it. They are not quite organisms. They are not quite extensions of the root. They exist in a taxonomic gray zone that the Ferment seems to specialize in — the zone between "part of the environment" and "alive in its own right." The sheets are the Ferment's most honest expression of its own philosophy, which is: the distinction between living and non-living is a convention, and conventions do not apply here.

## The Deep Dwellers

Now. The things in the deep.

I have to be less certain about these, because the deep Ferment is not a place where observation is easy. The light fades. The broth thickens. The root-columns grow closer together, larger, denser, and the spaces between them become corridors, become chambers, become a architecture that the root-organism did not design but produced anyway, as a consequence of its growth patterns, as a side effect of its need to structure itself.

The deep dwellers live in this architecture.

They are large. The smallest one I have ever detected — and "detected" is the right word, because I did not see it, I felt it, as a pressure wave that arrived before the displacement that caused it — was approximately five meters long. The largest was something I cannot assign a number to. It moved through a corridor between two root-columns that were twenty meters apart, and the pressure wave it produced filled the entire space, and the corridor darkened — not because the thing blocked the light, there was no light to block, but because the broth it displaced pushed the faint amber glow away, and for a moment, the space was completely black, and then the thing passed, and the amber returned, and I was left with the sense that I had been in the presence of something that occupied space the way a cloud occupies space — diffusely, without a clear edge, with a body that was more of a region than a shape.

What are they? I do not know. They are animals. They move under their own power. They displace broth. They produce pressure waves. They are alive. Beyond that, inference.

They feed on the broth. This is almost certain. There is nothing else to feed on. The broth is the only resource, and the deep broth is richer than the surface broth — more saturated, more dense, carrying a higher concentration of dissolved organics because it is closer to the root-organism's active metabolic zones. The deep dwellers live where the food is thickest.

They are slow. This is also almost certain. The pressure waves they produce arrive gradually, build gradually, fade gradually. A thing that moves quickly produces sharp pressure fronts. A thing that moves slowly produces rolling ones. The deep dwellers produce rolling ones. They are not in a hurry. They have never been in a hurry. Hurry requires a reason, and in the deep Ferment, there are no reasons. There is only the broth, and the corridors, and the passage of bodies through them at a speed that makes the ribbons look frantic by comparison.

Do they see? Almost certainly not. There is no light in the deep Ferment. Do they sense pressure? Yes — this is how they navigate, how they find the corridors, how they avoid the columns. Their bodies are, I suspect, entirely covered in pressure-sensitive cells, so that they experience their environment the way a blind person experiences a room by sound — not as a picture, but as a field, a topology of near and far, of open and closed, of moving and still.

Do they interact with each other? I do not know. The pressure waves I have detected have all been singular — one wave, one source, one body moving through one corridor. I have never detected two deep dwellers in proximity. This could mean they are solitary. It could mean they are so sparse that encounters are rare. It could mean they are so large that their individual territories span kilometers of root-corridor, and they never need to come close to each other because the Ferment is vast and they are few.

How few? I cannot say. But consider: the deep Ferment is the size of a continent's deep geology. The root-corridors extend for thousands of kilometers. If the deep dwellers are as large as I believe they are, and if they require as much broth as their size suggests, then the carrying capacity of the deep Ferment is not large. There may be hundreds. There may be dozens. There may be fewer than that. There may be a number so small that each deep dweller is, in a sense, a nation — a single organism occupying a territory so vast that it has never encountered another of its kind, has never needed to, has never had reason to.

They reproduce. This must be true, because they are not immortal, and the population is stable, and stability requires replacement. But I have never seen a young deep dweller. I have never seen a dead one. The dead are dissolved by the root-organism's surface tissue before they reach the floor, or they are dissolved in the deep, consumed by the broth itself, which is, remember, alive, which is part of the root, which does not waste.

The deep dwellers are the Ferment's largest fauna, and they are also its most unknowable. They live where observation fails. They live in the black, in the pressure, in the thirty-hertz drone that is not loud here but is everything, is the entire sensory world, is the only sound and the only sensation and the only fact. They live in the Ferment's body, in the literal interior of the root-organism's structure, and they are tolerated there, or they are unaware there, or they are, like the sheets on the surface, in a gray zone between resident and tissue, between guest and organ, between alive-in-the-Ferment and alive-as-the-Ferment.

## The Broth Itself (Revisited)

I said the broth was alive. I said it was part of the root. Let me now say something more difficult.

The broth contains organisms that are not the root.

They are small. They are very small. They are the size of the dissolved sugar molecules they feed on, which is to say they are molecular, which is to say the distinction between "organism" and "chemistry" is, at this scale, a matter of opinion. They are not bacteria, though they fill the bacterial niche. They are not viruses, though they are close to viral in their simplicity. They are, perhaps, best described as self-replicating fragments of the root-organism's own biochemistry — loose pieces of its metabolism that have become autonomous, that float in the broth and catalyze reactions and reproduce and die, and that the root-organism does not reclaim and does not destroy and does not seem to notice.

Or does not seem to notice. The root-organism notices everything in its broth. The broth is its blood. It monitors its blood the way your body monitors yours — not consciously, not deliberately, but through feedback loops, through concentration gradients, through the simple physics of chemical equilibrium. If the molecular organisms in the broth were a problem, the root-organism would solve the problem. It would alter the broth's composition. It would introduce a counteragent. It would adjust.

It has not adjusted. The molecular organisms have been in the broth for as long as the broth has been the broth. They are part of the equilibrium. They are part of the equation. They are, in a sense that the root-organism did not choose and cannot change, part of it.

This is the Ferment's deepest truth: the boundary between the organism and its environment is not a boundary. The root is the place. The broth is the root. The organisms in the broth are the broth. The fauna that feed on the broth are feeding on the root. The deep dwellers live in the root's corridors. The ribbons swim through the root's blood. The sessile gardeners grow on the root's skin. Everything here is inside the root. Everything here is the root, expressed in forms that the root did not design but that the root's existence makes inevitable, the way your body makes mites inevitable, the way a forest makes deer inevitable, the way a continent makes rivers inevitable.

The Great Ferment is one organism. It is also a world. These statements are not contradictory. They are the same statement.

## What It Is Like to Be a Ribbon

I cannot know this. But I can imagine it, and the imagining has a quality that feels true, and in the absence of certainty, quality is what I have.

Imagine: you are moving. You have always been moving. You do not remember a time when you were not moving, because you do not remember, because remembering would require stopping, and stopping would require not feeding, and not feeding would require not living, and you are living. You are moving through a medium that is warm and thick and sweet. It enters you at the front. It feeds you as it passes through. It leaves you at the back, and the leaving produces a small thrust, and the thrust moves you forward, and the forward motion brings new medium to the front, and the cycle continues. You are a tube. You are a process. You are a verb, not a noun.

You do not see. The amber light is there — it is everywhere, it is a property of the medium — but you do not see it, because you have no eyes, because seeing is not necessary, because the medium is the same everywhere. You do not hear. The thirty-hertz drone is there — it is everywhere, it is a property of the place — but you do not hear it, because you do not have ears, because hearing is not necessary, because the sound is the same everywhere. You sense pressure. You sense chemical gradients — the faint, enormous, slow variations in the broth's composition that tell you, without words, without concepts, that you are near a column (the broth is richer there, the dissolved sugars more concentrated) or that you are crossing a plain (the broth is thinner here, the oxygen slightly lower, the taste flatter). You follow the gradients. You do not choose to follow them. You follow them the way water follows gravity — because your body is built to follow them, because following them is what your body does, because your body is a following machine and the gradients are what it follows.

Sometimes you encounter another ribbon. You sense it as a pressure displacement, a slight turbulence in the medium ahead of you. You adjust. Not deliberately — your body adjusts, your undulation shifts by a fraction of a degree, your path deviates by centimeters, and the other ribbon does the same, and you pass each other at a distance that is neither close nor far but is, precisely, the distance that two bodies of your size and speed pass each other when both are following the same gradient and neither has a reason to be closer or farther. The encounter has no significance. It will happen again. It has happened before. It is a feature of the system.

Sometimes you pass a column. The column is enormous. You sense it as a vast pressure shadow, a region where the medium is still, where the gradients shift, where the broth is richer and warmer and slightly more viscous. You brush against its surface. The velvet tissue of the column touches your body wall, and for a moment — a moment that lasts perhaps ten seconds, which is a long time for you, because your entire sensory experience is the continuous flow of medium through your body, and a stillness in that flow is an event — you feel the column's warmth, its texture, the faint pulse of its osmotic breathing. And then you are past it, and the flow resumes, and the event is over, and you do not remember it, because you do not remember, because you are a verb, and verbs do not have memory. Verbs have only the present tense, which is the same as the always tense, which is the only tense the Ferment has.

You will die. Someday. Your body will slow — not suddenly, not catastrophically, but by degrees, by the accumulation of small inefficiencies in your filter membranes, by the gradual thickening of your body wall, by the slow decline in the contractility of your muscular layer. You will slow. You will feed less. You will move less. You will sink — slowly, gently, settling toward the root-floor like a leaf settling toward the ground in air so still that the leaf falls straight down. You will reach the floor. The velvet will touch you. And the velvet will grow over you, and your body will dissolve, and the sugars and enzymes and minerals that were you will return to the broth, and the broth will carry them to the next ribbon, or the next shelf, or the next globular filterer, or the deep dwellers in their corridors, and you will be part of them, and they will be part of you, and the distinction was always a convention, and conventions do not apply here.

## What It Is Like to Be a Deep Dweller

I cannot imagine this as confidently. But I will try.

Imagine: you are large. You are so large that your body does not end where your body ends — your body extends into the pressure field you create, into the displacement you produce in the medium, into the corridor of disturbed broth that trails behind you for hundreds of meters. You are a moving region. You are a weather system.

You are in the dark. You have always been in the dark. The dark is not a deprivation. The dark is the world. The dark is the corridors, the chambers, the vast interior spaces of the root-organism's structure, and you know them the way you know your own body — by pressure, by displacement, by the way the medium moves differently in a wide chamber than in a narrow corridor, the way the broth's viscosity changes near a root-wall, the way the thirty-hertz drone resonates differently in a dome than in a tube.

The drone. The drone is your world. It is not background. It is not noise. It is information. The pumps that produce the drone are distributed throughout the root-organism, and each one has a characteristic frequency, a characteristic rhythm, and you know them — not by name, not by concept, but by the way they feel in your body, which is covered, every centimeter of it, in pressure-sensitive cells that parse the drone the way an ear parses a chord. You can tell where you are by the drone. You can tell how far you are from Pump 4,071 (which has a rhythm of forty-one minutes and a frequency of 28.3 hertz and a resonance pattern that suggests it is located in a compressed root-stratum of moderate density). You can tell that Pump 4,071 has been operating at this rhythm for as long as you have been alive, which is long, which is very long, and that it will continue operating at this rhythm for as long as you will be alive, which is also long, which is also very long, and that neither of you will change, because change does not happen here.

You feed. The broth enters your body — not through a single opening, but through your skin, through a million microscopic pores that cover your surface and that are, each of them, a tiny mouth. The broth seeps in. The dissolved sugars are extracted. The depleted fluid seeps out. You are a sponge. You are a lung. You are a vast, slow, patient absorber, and the broth flows past you and through you and you take what you need from it and you give back what you do not need, and the exchange is so slow, so gradual, so perfectly matched to the medium's flow rate that you do not disturb the broth. You are transparent to the system. You are part of the equilibrium.

You move. Not fast. Not far. You move through the corridors at a speed that is, in human terms, barely perceptible — a few meters per hour, perhaps less. But the corridors are long, and the chambers are vast, and you have been moving through them for so long that you have a territory, a range, a set of corridors and chambers that you know and that know you, in the sense that the broth in them has passed through your body so many times that it carries your chemical signature, your waste products, your metabolic residue, and the root-organism's walls have absorbed these signatures, and the walls are, in a sense, marked with your presence, and the marking is the closest thing to memory that the Ferment possesses.

You are alone. You have always been alone. You do not experience this as loneliness, because loneliness requires a concept of company, and company requires a concept of other, and other requires a boundary between self and not-self, and in the deep Ferment, where the broth is the root and the root is the place and the place is the medium and the medium is in you and through you and around you, the boundary is not clear. You are in the root. The root is in you. You are not alone. You are not accompanied. You are simply here, in the dark, in the drone, in the broth, moving at a speed that makes geological time look hasty, and you have been here for a very long time, and you will be here for a very long time, and the "here" is not a location. The "here" is a state. The "here" is the Ferment. The "here" is the only thing that exists.

## The Things I Have Not Described

There are things I have not described.

There are organisms in the Ferment that I have not described because I do not understand them. There are structures on the root-columns that do not match any functional model I can construct — growths that do not filter, do not feed, do not appear to serve any purpose, and yet are present, and are consistent, and are part of the system. There are chemical signatures in the broth that do not correspond to any known metabolic process — compounds that the root-organism produces and that nothing consumes, compounds that accumulate at a rate so slow that they would take millennia to reach detectable levels and that have been at detectable levels for as long as anyone has been looking, which means they have been accumulating for longer than that, which means the root-organism has been producing them for longer than that, which means they have a purpose, or they are a purpose, or they are the side effect of a purpose, and the purpose is something I do not have the framework to identify.

There are sounds in the Ferment that are not the pumps. Faint, irregular, high-frequency sounds — clicks, chirps, brief tonal events — that occur at intervals too variable to be mechanical and too consistent to be random. Something is producing them. Something is listening for them, or something is producing them without listening, or they are a byproduct of a process I cannot observe. They are rare. You might hear one in an hour of listening. You might hear three. You might hear none. They do not correlate with any observable feature of the environment. They are, as far as I can tell, the Ferment's only concession to novelty, and they are so small and so brief and so inexplicable that they function, for an observer, as a reminder that the Ferment is not a machine. A machine does not produce unexplained sounds. A living thing does. A living thing that is the size of a continent and has been alive for longer than the concept of "longer" can convey produces sounds that are not for you, that are not about you, that are not about anything you can understand, and the sounds are part of it, and the sounds are it, and the it is the Great Ferment, and the Great Ferment is alive.

## What Lives Here

What lives in the Great Ferment?

The root lives here. The root is the Ferment. The root is the floor and the walls and the columns and the corridors and the pumps and the broth and the light-filter and the heat-source and the reason. The root is the reason.

The ribbons live here. They swim. They feed. They die. They are the Ferment's circulation, externalized — the way the broth is the Ferment's blood, externalized. They are the moving parts of a body that does not have moving parts.

The gardeners live here. The shelves and the globes and the sheets. They grow. They persist. They layer into reefs that are cemeteries that are gardens. They are the Ferment's skin, its texture, its surface, its interface with its own blood.

The deep dwellers live here. They move in the dark. They feed through their skin. They are the Ferment's interior, its deep tissue, its private spaces, its gut fauna, its dreams, if a continent-sized root-organism can be said to dream, and I think it can, and I think the dreams are the clicks and the chirps, and I think the dreams are the unexplained sounds, and I think the dreams are the only thing about the Ferment that I cannot describe, and that is fitting, because dreams are the part of a living thing that resists description, and the Ferment is a living thing, and it resists.

The broth lives here. The molecular organisms live here. The chemistry lives here. The equilibrium lives here. The thirty-hertz drone lives here. The amber light lives here. The twelve degrees lives here.

Everything lives here. Everything is the Ferment, and the Ferment is everything, and the question "what lives in the Great Ferment" has the same answer as the question "what lives in a body," which is: the body lives in the body. The organs live in the body. The cells live in the body. The blood lives in the body. The bacteria live in the body. The mites live in the body. The chemistry lives in the body. The electricity lives in the body. The body lives in the body.

The Great Ferment is a body. It is the size of Australia. It is submerged in its own blood. It has been alive for longer than "longer" can convey. It is in equilibrium. It is finished. It is still here.

It will be here for a very long time.
