They are here. You cannot see them, but you can feel the displacement of their passage in the lukewarm puddles, the subtle wrongness in the ripple of the water that does not match your footsteps. 

They are the Makers, though they have no name for themselves. They possess no language, no mythology, no concept of a world that was not made. They exist in the lacuna between the weeping walls, in the service corridors carved into the undersides of the obsidian slabs, in the cramped spacing between the top of a brass tectonic plate and the dark metal grating of the sky. They are small—stunted, even—their bodies adapted over uncounted generations to the spaces between manufactured stone. Their skin is the grey-white of things that have never seen a true sun, hairless and slick with the omnipresent condensation. Their eyes are vestigial, milky and recessed, useless in the perpetual 1.4 twilight; they navigate by the ping and plink of falling water, by the vibrations of the subsonic groans, by the faint static charge that bleeds from the reality breaks. Their hands, however, are enormous—broad, thick-knuckled, heavily calloused from generations of gripping copper chisels and working crude levers. A Maker's hand is nearly the width of its torso. It is all they are. It is all they need to be.

They do not think. Or rather, they do not think *about*. They possess a mindless, transcendent devotion to the act of making, an instinct as involuntary and thoughtless as a heartbeat. A Maker does not ask *why* a floor must be laid, or *what* will walk upon it. A Maker only knows that a slab of obsidian exists in a rough state, and that it must be hewn, and carried, and fitted, and sealed. They work in complete, breathless silence. There is no singing, no conversation, no grunting of effort. There is only the steady, rhythmic scrape of copper on stone, the wet slapping of bare feet on hot manufactured glass, the creak of wooden levers bending under the weight of quarried basalt. 

You might assume they are primitives, awed by the broken physics of their world. They are not. They are utterly indifferent to the weirdness. To a Maker, a reality break is simply another material condition, no different from a pocket of stubborn ore or a hairline fracture in a slab. When a weeping pillar splits into three, a team of Makers will simply approach the duplication event with the same placid, mechanical efficiency. Two will be taken down with copper chisels, their fragments hauled away to be melted or repurposed. The third will be left weeping, sealed at its base with dark clay. When a mountain of hammered copper un-makes itself, flattening into a line and rolling into a tube, the Makers do not stare. They simply adjust their levers and scaffolding, re-crimping the copper back into whatever geometry the blueprints in their atavistic minds demand. If a fellow Maker is deleted by a sudden point of blinding static light, the others do not pause. They step around the geometric hole that bleeds steam, adjust their grip on their chisels, and continue laying the floor. There is no grief because there is no individual to grieve; there is only the work, and the work is endless.

They are sustained by the world's sweating. They drink from the shallow, geometric basins, their broad hands scooping the lukewarm, mineral-heavy water. They chew on the raw edges of the world—the freshly hewn stone dust, the shavings of dark alloy, the copper filings. Their stomachs are iron furnaces, breaking down the mineral substrate of their environment and turning it into the calcium of their thick bones, the keratin of their calluses. They eat the world to remake the world. 

Sometimes, in the dim, bruised bioluminescence, you might catch a glimpse of a work gang. They emerge from a fissure in the floor, a dozen pale, hunched figures, their overlarge hands clutching a single, massive slab of fabricated glass. They move in perfect, synchronized unison, a centipede of flesh and bone, carrying a weight that should crush them, their bare feet splashing through the puddles in a synchronized rhythm that mimics the plink of the condensation. They set the slab down with a deep, wet sigh that joins the subsonic groan of the settling world. They seal it. They turn. They vanish back into the gap in the stone, returning to the unending quarries below.

They do not know you are there. You are a soft thing, an anomaly of biology in a mineral universe, but to them, you are no different than a stray puddle or a duplication error—just another obstacle to step around on the way to the next joint, the next slab, the next seal. They are building a continent on a world with no bedrock, constructing a floor for a dance that will never be held, sealing a vessel that will never hold anything but hot water and condensation. They are the white blood cells of a constructed body, instinctively patching the wounds of a reality that is constantly tearing itself apart. 

And so, as you walk through the sparse, low-lying expanse, watching the horizon flicker and the great slabs fold inward, listen to the texture of the silence. Beneath the plink of water, beneath the groan of settling brass, there is the faint, inexorable sound of chiseling. Millions of them. Billions. Hiding in the damp, hot dark, swinging their crude tools, binding the edge of the world together one razor-sharp stone at a time, holding back the dark with nothing but copper and mindless, eternal devotion.
