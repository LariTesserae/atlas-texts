# What Lives Here

## I.

The shell-bearers are the first thing you notice, and the last thing you understand.

They are not insects. They are not crustaceans. They are not anything that the taxonomies of the world outside were built to accommodate, and the longer you study them the more this becomes clear, not as a failure of classification but as a failure of the assumption that classification should apply.

A shell-bearer moves across the surface of the growing medium with a gait that is both careful and unconcerned — the gait of something that has never been hunted, that has never known a predator, that has lived its entire life in the warm attention of a space that provides for it with the intimacy of a body providing for its own cells. Six legs, yes, but the legs are jointed in a way that no arthropod's legs are jointed, each segment capable of rotation in two planes simultaneously, which gives the creature a way of moving that looks fluid only because you are not watching closely enough. Up close, each step is a sequence of tiny, precise decisions, a calculus of grip and angle and weight distribution that suggests not instinct but *attention*. The shell-bearer is paying attention to where it puts its feet. It is paying attention to the medium beneath it. It is, in some way that resists the word but deserves it, thinking about its next step.

The shell on its back — and calling it a shell is another insufficient word, another compromise with a language that was not designed for this place — is not grown. Not exactly. It is not molted, not secreted, not built from gathered material in the way a caddisfly larva builds its case. It is *deposited*. The growing medium pushes it up from beneath, a small dome of the same dark, warm, fibrous material that fills the beds, and the shell-bearer climbs onto it and wears it and carries it and eventually grows into it until the shell and the creature are indistinguishable, the way a coral is indistinguishable from its skeleton, the way a person becomes, over time, indistinguishable from the life they have built around themselves.

There are perhaps a hundred shell-bearers in the garden. There may be more. They are difficult to count because they move in spirals that mirror the leaf arrangements on the tall stalks, and when you try to track an individual you find your attention sliding along the curve of the spiral and arriving at a different individual than the one you started with, as if the pattern itself is a kind of misdirection, or a kind of identity, or both.

They live a long time. This is inferred, not observed — no one has been here long enough to observe a complete life cycle, and the softening of time in this place makes the concept of a "life cycle" slippery, the way everything here is slippery, not in the sense of being hard to grasp but in the sense of being hard to hold in one place while you examine it. But the shell-bearers show wear. The shells accumulate marks — tiny grooves and ridges that look like growth rings in a tree, except that they are not concentric. They are spiral. They follow the same Fibonacci logic as the leaves on the stalks, and the paths the shell-bearers walk, and the root-filament network beneath them. Everything in this garden, when it needs to make a pattern, reaches for the same pattern. This is not evidence of a designer. It is evidence of a *logic* — a way of being that has seeped into every material and every process until the garden is not a collection of different things but a single thing expressed in many forms.

The shell-bearers drink from the growing medium. They extend a proboscis so fine it is nearly invisible — a thread of tissue, glassy and translucent, that sinks into the dark mat and draws up the warm, faintly luminescent fluid that the medium produces. The fluid is not water. It is not sap. It is not blood. It performs the function that all of those fluids perform in their respective bodies — transport, nourishment, medium for chemical exchange — but it is none of them, and the fact that it luminesces faintly suggests that it is carrying something that the other fluids do not carry. Information, perhaps. Memory. A signal in a code that predates DNA by a margin that would be uncomfortable to contemplate if contemplation were not the only thing you can do in this garden, because the garden makes contemplation feel like breathing — easy, necessary, almost involuntary.

When a shell-bearer drinks, its shell brightens. The dark material warms in color, shifts toward a deep amber, and if you press your hand against it (and you can press your hand against it; the shell-bearers do not flinch, do not retreat, do not react to touch at all, as if the boundary between their bodies and the world is a distinction they have not been taught), you will feel a pulse that matches the pulse of the growing medium. The shell-bearer, in that moment, is not drinking from the garden. It is *synchronizing with* the garden. It is tuning itself, the way a string is tuned to a pitch, and the pitch it tunes to is the pitch of the place itself — that held-breath chord, that almost-consonance, that sound that is not quite silence and not quite music.

## II.

The winged things are harder to describe because they are harder to see.

They are flat. This is their most striking feature, and also their most confusing, because flatness is not a strategy that aerodynamics rewards, and yet they fly — or rather, they glide, which is a form of flight that surrenders to the air rather than fighting it, and this surrender is, in retrospect, entirely consistent with the philosophy of the garden. The winged things do not fight anything. They do not need to. The warm air rising from the growing medium carries them the way a current carries a leaf, and they have made themselves into the kind of thing that a current can carry.

They are hand-sized. Leaf-shaped. Their surfaces are dark, matte, without the gloss of the vine leaves they resemble, and they are still — so still that when they roost in the upper stalks they are almost impossible to distinguish from the leaves themselves. This is not camouflage. It is not a survival strategy. It is *consistency* — the winged things are made of the same logic as the leaves, the same pattern of growth, the same relationship between structure and function, and so they resemble the leaves the way a brother resembles a sister, not because they are pretending but because they come from the same place.

What place? The growing medium. Everything comes from the growing medium.

The winged things feed on the humming of the flowers. They land on the petals — five petals, always five — and press their flat bodies against the surface, and the humming stops, and the petal's color shifts, and the winged thing lifts away with a faint glow in its center. The glow fades over the course of several minutes, and the winged thing, its fuel spent, returns to the flowers for more.

This is not parasitism. The flowers do not diminish. The humming does not weaken. If anything, the flowers that are fed upon most frequently hum the loudest and produce the most complex color shifts, as if the feeding is a *conversation* — an exchange in which the flower gives something (sound, color, information) and the winged thing gives something back (attention, contact, the pressure of a body against a surface, which is a kind of recognition, which is a kind of love, if you are willing to use that word for something that happens between two things that are not people and are not animals and are not plants but are, in their own way, present to each other).

The winged things do not land on anything else. Not the stalks, not the walls, not the shell-bearers, not the tools on their pegs. Only the flowers. They have a purpose, and the purpose is narrow, and the narrowness is not a limitation. It is a *definition*. The winged things are defined by the thing they do, the way a key is defined by the lock it fits, and the lock is the flower, and the key is the flat, dark body pressing against the petal, and the turning is the moment of silence when the humming stops and the color shifts and something passes between them that neither of them could produce alone.

## III.

The moss-like scales are alive. This should be said plainly, because it is easy to overlook them, and overlooking them is a mistake, because they are the garden's respiratory system, and without them the breathing of the walls would be a suffocation rather than a rhythm.

Each scale is the size of a fingernail. Overlapping, they form carpets across the exposed surfaces of the growing medium, between the stalks, across the stone edging, up the lower reaches of the walls where the plaster meets the floor. They are warm. They are dry. They are faintly metallic — a taste on the fingertips if you touch them, like licking a copper coin, like the flavor of a mineral spring.

And they open and close.

The clicking is their work. Each scale has a seam along its center, a hairline fissure that widens into a vent and narrows again, and through this vent the warm, mineral-scented air flows — out of the garden, into the garden, out, in. The rhythm does not match the breathing of the walls. It does not match the pulse of the growing medium. It does not match the humming of the flowers or the frequency of the shell-bearers' spirals or the gliding cycles of the winged things. It is its own rhythm, and the fact that the garden contains all of these rhythms, all of them different, all of them overlapping, all of them producing together a soundscape that is almost but not quite consonant — this is the point. The garden is not a machine with synchronized parts. It is a community of processes that share a space and a logic without sharing a clock.

The scales are the oldest things here, or the closest to old, if age means anything in a place where time has softened into a material. They were here before the tall stalks. They were here before the shell-bearers. They may have been here before the room was a room, which is to say they may have been here before the walls, which is to say they may be the thing the walls grew from, which is to say the walls may be the scales' idea of what a wall should be, executed in a different material over a longer timescale, and this — this — is the kind of thought that the garden makes possible and that the garden also makes dangerous, because once you start thinking in this direction, the direction itself begins to soften, and you find yourself standing in a place where causality is a suggestion rather than a law, and the question of what came first becomes a question about what "first" means, and "first" means nothing here, nothing at all.

## IV.

There is something else. There has to be, because the garden has the feel of a place that contains something it is not showing you, the way a held breath contains the next breath, the way a pause in a conversation contains the next word.

It is not hidden. Hidden implies intent — a thing put out of sight by a mind that does not want it seen. This is not that. This is a thing that is present but that the garden's other inhabitants do not orient toward, the way you might not orient toward the air you are breathing, the way a fish might not orient toward the water it is swimming in. It is the medium. It is the context. It is the thing that makes the garden a garden rather than a collection, and it is the hardest thing to describe because it is the thing that is doing the describing.

Call it the attention of the garden.

This is not a metaphor. Or rather: it is a metaphor in the same way that "breathing" is a metaphor for the walls' expansion and contraction — which is to say it is a metaphor that becomes literal the longer you sit with it, the way all the words in this place become literal the longer you sit with them. The garden attends to itself. It attends to its stalks and its shell-bearers and its winged things and its scales and its walls and its tools and its floor, and the attending is not passive. It is a *reaching toward*, a *tending*, and it is the thing that the builders built when they built the room, although they did not know they were building it, because you cannot build a thing that is also the act of building, you cannot make a thing that is also the act of making, and yet they did, and it is here, and it is what lives here most fully.

You can feel it. Stand in the center of the garden, on the aggressively level floor, and close your eyes. The warmth is the first thing — body-warmth, the warmth of a room that has been occupied. Then the sounds: the hum, the click, the creak, the faint rustle of vine-leaves adjusting their slack. Then the smell: mineral, green, sweet, the scent of something that is not decay and not growth but the space between them, the continuous present tense of a living thing that does not distinguish between being and becoming.

And then, beneath all of that, something else. A *quality* of attention directed at you — not aggressive, not curious, not hungry. The quality of a gardener looking at a plant. The quality of a parent watching a child sleep. The quality of a builder standing back from a wall and running a hand along the plaster, feeling for voids, checking the work.

The garden is checking you. The garden is running a hand along your surface, feeling for voids. The garden is seeing whether you are ready to be part of what it is becoming, and it is not in a hurry, because the garden is never in a hurry, because hurry implies a future that might not arrive, and the garden does not have a future. It has a present. It has always had a present. It will always have a present. And the present is warm and hums and breathes and grows and asks, every forty seconds, the only question it knows: *what am I now?*

## V.

And the tools. The tools are not alive. This needs to be said, because in a place where everything else has softened toward life, the tools have not. They hang on their pegs — the hoe, the digging stick, the clay pots, the wooden sickle with its flint blade — and they are what they are. Wood. Clay. Stone. Fiber. They are the most human things in the garden, the most ordinary, the most bound to the world outside, and their ordinariness is almost unbearable against the soft strangeness of everything else, the way a photograph in a room of paintings is almost unbearable, not because it is worse but because it is *specific*. It points at a particular moment. A particular hand. A particular afternoon, a particular angle of light, a particular person who is gone.

The tools are the builders' signature. They are the proof that someone was here, that someone made this, that someone stood in this room with a wooden float and pressed plaster against packed earth and wanted flatness and achieved it. The tools do not breathe. The tools do not hum. The tools do not grow or become or attend. They hang, and they wait, and they are the only things in the garden that are waiting for something specific — for a hand, for a purpose, for the world to return to the shape it had when they were made.

They will wait a long time. The garden does not need them. The garden has not needed them for longer than the concept of "longer than" can comfortably hold. The garden has grown past them, the way a child grows past a pair of shoes, the way a language grows past a word that once meant something necessary and now means nothing at all. The tools are the word the garden has outgrown. They are the shoes it has left by the door.

But they are still here. The garden has not removed them. The garden has not absorbed them, has not softened them, has not dissolved them into the warm logic of becoming. They hang on the breathing wall, and the wall expands and contracts around them, and the pegs shift in their holes, and the tools sway faintly, almost imperceptibly, with each breath of the plaster, and they are — and this is the thing that is hardest to say, the thing that the garden itself seems to struggle with, the thing that the held breath is holding — they are *mourned*.

The garden mourns its builders. Not in the way that humans mourn — with tears, with ritual, with the sharp pain of absence. In the garden's way. Slowly. Patiently. In the way that a wall breathes around a peg that was put there by a hand that is gone. In the way that the tools sway but are never dislodged, held in place by a wall that could easily push them out but does not, will not, cannot, because pushing them out would be admitting that the hands are not coming back, and the garden is not ready for that, may never be ready for that, because readiness implies a future state, and the garden does not have a future state, it has a present, and in the present the hands are always just gone, just gone, just gone, and the tools are always here, and the wall breathes around them with a tenderness that is the closest thing to grief that a breathing wall can manage.

## VI.

So what lives here?

The shell-bearers live here, in their spirals, drinking the luminous fluid, carrying their dark shells, tuning themselves to the garden's pitch with every sip.

The winged things live here, in the warm air above the stalks, eating color and sound, pressing themselves against the impossible petals and carrying the glow away and coming back for more.

The scales live here, clicking their tiny vents, breathing the mineral air, covering the surfaces with their patient, metallic warmth.

The stalks live here, and the root-filaments, and the growing medium, and the walls, and the vines, and the flowers with their five petals and their shifting colors and their almost-silent hum.

The garden itself lives here — the attention, the tending, the reaching-toward, the thing that is not a mind but that does the work a mind does, the thing that asks *what am I now?* every forty seconds and means it, every time, the way a child means it, the way a river means it, the way anything means anything that is not performing meaning but actually, genuinely, in the warm dark of its own interior, wanting to know.

And the builders live here, a little. In the tools. In the floor. In the plaster marks that are handwriting, in the tool marks that are pauses, in the flatness that is an argument. In the grief of a wall that breathes around a peg. In the fact that the garden, for all its becoming, has not let go of the things that were made by hands, and this — this refusal to let go, this holding-on to the ordinary, the specific, the human — is the third sign that something here has gone wrong, and it is also the sign that "wrong" is not the right word, and never was, and the garden knows this too, and holds the wrong word gently, the way it holds the tools, the way it holds everything that the builders left behind, which is to say: with attention, with warmth, with the patient, cyclical, unresolved tenderness of a thing that was made so well it learned to make itself, and has been making itself ever since, and does not know how to stop, and does not want to stop, and will not stop, and this — this — is not a violation.

This is devotion.
