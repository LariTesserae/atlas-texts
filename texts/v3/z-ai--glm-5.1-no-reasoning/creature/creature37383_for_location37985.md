To live here is to be fluent in pressure. Whatever lives in Expansion 9 has surrendered the ordinary currencies of the world—weather, horizon, silence, soil—and taken up residence in a working machine. They are not residents; they are readings. They are the biota of the breath.

Consider what it is to be born in the turn. For the pale fish in the cistern, spawning is not cued by moonlight or temperature, but by the flip of the vast diaphragm far below—the moment the suction breaks and the pressure equalizes,shaking the water in a single, profound shiver. Life begins in the hinge of the machine’s jaw. These fish have never seen the sun; their world is a dim, steady warmth rising from the deep works, and their food is the condensate that drips through the floor, mineral-heavy and tasting of iron and moss. They navigate not by sight, which they have entirely lost, but by the slow, tidal pull of the room’s respiration. They feel the day as a long draft, and the night as a long sigh.

Consider the dew-midges, who exist as the handwriting of the sound. They hatch at the first drop of indoor rain, rising from the damp grating in columns. But they do not scatter to the wind; they are caught by the standing waves, shaken into visible ladders and spirals by the antinodes. A midge in this room does not fly freely; it is ratcheted upward, step by step, by the violence of the air. To be a midge is to be a passenger of vibration, ascending the sound like a staircase, until the breath changes and the exhale carries you out the high vents, out into the cold sky to find another glass crown on another summit. Their lives are short, a few hours of shimmer, but they are the room's articulation: the roar made flesh and made visible.

And the knockbirds, who have given up the frailty of song for the permanence of percussion. In a room where a voice would be stripped to noise, they speak in sharp, felt taps. A male carries his left stone like a heirloom, striking it against the resounding plates of the structure—a high, clear ring to declare territory, a rapid, muted clatter to court. The females answer with a rhythm of their own, and the nestlings beg not by opening their mouths to squawk, but by hammering their tiny beaks against the nest’s metal anchoring. They are musicians of the hull. When the great Horn sounds at the turn of the breath, it does not startle them; they simply wait for it to pass, then resume their tapping, answering the machine's long note with their quick, brief ones. They nest in the kettle of the absent keeper because the spout makes a fine resonator, amplifying their quiet declarations into something that can be felt through the perch.

The sail-mice inhabit the middle air, never touching the still floor if they can help it. They are the interceptors of the dew. To be a sail-mouse is to know the thermals of the Works by heart—to read the inhale as a rising stairway, stretching the pale skin from wrist to ankle and letting the machine's exhalation loft you to the high cable runs. They drink while gliding, lapping moisture straight from the braided streams on the glass. A young sail-mouse might spend its first year entirely in the updrafts, sleeping curled in the lee of a dead valve body, waking to catch the morning's intake. Only the dead or the sick fall to the grating, where the pipe-stoats find them.

Ah, the stoats—not mammals of fur, but of oil and flow. They patrol the gutters like liquid seeking its level. They are long and low, their coats glossed by the constant mist into something amphibious, something that parts the water without disturbing it. They hunt by listening with their feet: the frantic, muffled tap of a trapped sail-mouse, the scrabble of a spider losing its grip. They know the room's topography by the vibration it admits. A stoat moving through the gutter-stream is a shadow that flows, patient and cold, untroubled by the roar, anchored to the iron by the sound it feels.

And in one gutter-stream, there is a toad. It is the room's philosopher. It has never sung because there is no silence to fill, no chorus to join. Its call—a long, resonant trill—would be swallowed whole by the roar, rendered pointless. So it sits, cool to the touch, watching the indoor rain bead and fall with businesslike precision. It does not need to call a mate; mates find each other by schedule, drawn to the same damp node at the same breath-turn. It does not need to sing; it simply is, a dense, silent node of life in a world of noise, a counterpoint to the orchids.

None of these creatures remember the keepers, though they live in the keepers' architecture. The chalk wall of tallies is just another texture to them, another surface for the moss to climb. They do not know the Works was built; they only know that it breathes, and that the breath brings warmth, and water, and the schedule of living. They are the machine's dreaming—its green, moving, tapping, gliding, swimming thought. They do not wonder what the machine is for, because they know, with the absolute certainty of life, what it does: it sustains.

To live here is to be held. It is to live inside the chest of something incomprehensibly vast and unimaginably old, to be the small, warm movement within the great, patient stillness. It is to know that the world is iron and air, schedule and breath, and that you are one of the things that happens, reliably and without fuss, in the turn.
