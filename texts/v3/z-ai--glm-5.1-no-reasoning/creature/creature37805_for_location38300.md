What lives here is not truly separate from the stone. That is the first thing to understand. In a place where the cycle closes so tightly, where every calorie originates in the mountain's own warmth and the weeping of its walls, the boundary between organism and habitat becomes a polite fiction—a distinction that matters less with every millennium.

The isopods—call them that, though they have forgotten whatever name biology gave them, forgotten the shape of their ancestors, forgotten that there is such a thing as sight—are the foundation. Not the bottom of a chain, but the *substance* of it. They are not individuals in any way that would make sense above. They are a *tissue*. A chitinous integument that breathes and pulses as one across square meters of granite. When you press your hand to the wall—and you would, eventually, because the dark makes you hungry for touch—you would feel them shift beneath your palm like a single muscle contracting, a slow peristaltic wave moving through the colony as they graze the fungal felt. Each one is the size of a human thumb, pale as the inside of a wrist, blind in the way that stones are blind—not lacking, but *beyond* the need. Their mandibles are meticulous instruments, evolved to shave fungal hyphae from rock without scraping the granite itself. They have been doing this for longer than the cirque has been a cirque. They will be doing it when the walls finally slump inward and the throat closes forever.

They do not compete. There is no competition here because there is no scarcity—not in the terms that matter. The mountain weeps; the fungi bloom; the isopods graze. A human would call this stasis, but it is more like *song*—a single sustained note held for ten thousand years, the chord of a closed loop humming in the dark.

And in the crevices between the isopod armor, the grubs.

They are not larval. They are not *becoming* anything. This is their final form: finger-thick, segmented, soft as organ tissue, pulsing with that sickly luminescence as if their bodies were windows into some deeper, greener darkness. The glow is not for seeing. Nothing here sees. The glow is *metabolism* made visible—a chemical exhalation, the way their particular digestion of fungal matter produces light as a byproduct, the way humans produce carbon dioxide. They do not control it. They do not *use* it. It simply happens, and in happening, it grants the cirque its only illumination—a weak, wet green that makes the stone seem to swim, that turns the isopod colonies into shadowed continents on a granite sea, that reveals the grubs' own clustering density as a kind of living constellation, stars pressed cheek to jowl in the crevices, throbbing in asynchronous rhythms that never quite sync and never quite cease.

If you stared at them long enough—and you could, because there is nothing else to look at, and the dark makes you desperate for pattern—you would start to feel that the pulsing *means* something. It doesn't. Or if it does, the meaning is not for you.

The moths are the only things here that move with what you would recognize as *intent*.

They navigate the warm updrafts with a precision that seems deliberate, but their brains are not brains in any sense you'd understand—merely thickened knots of nerve that process heat gradients and the chemical signatures of fungal spores. They are the size of fists, and their wings are leather and dust, and when they brush past your face in the dark—which they will, because they do not know to avoid you, and the updrafts carry your own body heat like a flare—you will flinch, and your flinch will mean nothing to them. They are seeking the fungal blooms that fruit in the highest crevices, where condensation is thickest. They are seeking places to lay their eggs, which will hatch into more grubs, which will pupate into more moths, which will die and be eaten by the fungi, which will fruit and release spores, which will be eaten by the moths.

The cycle does not have an entrance. It does not have an exit. It simply *is*, the way the rivulet simply flows, the way the stone simply weeps.

And the spiders.

You would not see them, even with the grubs' miserly light. You would walk into their webs first—feel them across your face like silk and glue, thick as fishing line, strung across the narrower passages with an architectural precision that no sightless creature should possess. But they are not sightless, not exactly. They feel the warm bodies moving through the dark—the moths, mostly, but also you, if you are foolish enough to enter the throat. They feel the vibrations of the isopod clicking, the grub pulsing, the drip of condensation. Their webs are not merely traps. They are *instruments*, strung to catch not just prey but *information*—a vast, distributed sensory network that lets the spiders map the chamber in pressures and frequencies. They are the size of dinner plates, and they are blind, and they know the shape of this place more intimately than you will ever know anything.

They do not hunt the moths so much as *receive* them. The moths come to the warm updrafts; the webs span the updrafts; the collision is inevitable, a geometric certainty rather than a pursuit. When a moth strikes the silk, the spider does not rush. It *waits*, feeling the struggle through the vibrations of the web, reading the moth's exhaustion like a script, and only when the thrashing subsides does it move—slowly, inevitably, with the patience of the stone itself—to feed.

There are other things here. Smaller things. Nematodes in the fungal mats. Mites that ride the isopods like passengers on a living continent. Blind, legless amphipods that swim in the thin rivulet, their bodies translucent, their hearts visible and beating. Springtails that drift through the warm mist like aerial plankton, billions of them, so numerous that their collective mass exceeds all the moths and spiders and isopods combined, though you would never know it because they are beneath notice, beneath scale, beneath the threshold of any sense you possess.

And beneath all of that—beneath the springtails and the mites and the nematodes, beneath the fungal hyphae that thread the porous stone like slow lightning—there is the mountain itself.

Not as metaphor. Not as backdrop. As *participant*.

The mountain exhales warmth. The mountain weeps water. The mountain provides the stone on which all of this grows and the bowl in which all of this is contained. In the terms of this ecosystem, the mountain is not *environment*; it is *host*. The cirque is its body cavity, and the life within it is not residing here but *inhabiting* it, the way gut flora inhabits a digestive tract—symbiotic, dependent, essential. The fungi break down the mountain's minerals. The isopods graze the fungi and return waste to the stone, waste that feeds the next bloom of fungal felt. The rivulet carries away what is not needed, sluicing it into the abyss, a slow excretion that has been flowing for longer than human language.

This is what lives here: a single, vast, *slow* organism composed of stone and chitin and hyphae and silk and the warm, wet breath of the earth. It has no brain. It has no name. It has existed in this configuration, in this exact balance of species and substrate, for longer than the Himalayas have been tall. It will exist until the geothermal breath cools, or the throat collapses, or some catastrophe from above intrudes—though nothing from above would survive the descent into this heat and density and dark.

The place is not hostile. That is important to understand. It is not *for* you, but neither is it *against* you. If you entered the cirque—if you squeezed through the throat and descended into the warm, wet bowl—you would not be attacked. You would not be hunted. The spiders would not target you; the isopods would not swarm you. You would simply be *absorbed*. Your body heat would join the updrafts. Your condensation—because you would condense, in this wet warmth, your skin filming over with moisture that was once the mountain's tears—would join the rivulet. Your exhalations would feed the fungi. Your dead skin cells would graze the isopod mandibles. And when you died—of thirst, perhaps, or of the slow suffocation of being surrounded by so much *breathing*, or simply of the inability to find purchase in a world that does not accommodate your shape or your senses or your timescale—you would become substrate, and the cycle would close over you like water over a stone, and you would be gone, and the cirque would not notice, because the cirque has been processing the dead for longer than your species has been processing the living.

What lives here is patience. What lives here is the slow, grinding metabolism of a closed system that has achieved the closest thing to permanence that biology permits. What lives here is an ancient, blind, *saturated* persistence that does not know you exist and would not care if it did, because you are too fast, too small, too brief to register against the timescale of its breathing.

The darkness breathes.

And what breathes in the darkness is *old*, and it is *enough*, and it does not need you at all.
