To live on the plateau is to be a note in a symphony that does not know it is playing. 

Here, the inhabitants are not residents; they are articulations. They do not possess the plateau so much as they *are* the plateau, temporarily extruded into shapes that crawl, drift, and dissolve. Stand still long enough in the moss-forests, and the boundary between the observer and the observed softens, not through metaphor, but through the sheer, dripping density of the place. The condensation soaks you. The throb of the chthonic engines settles into your bones. You become just another surface for the lichen-crusted air.

The dominant consciousness of this place—if a word implying separation can even be applied—belongs to the swarm. They are the circulatory system of the plateau, the dark, chitinous blood that moves the sugars from the moss to the sky and back again. They are fist-sized, but they do not think at the scale of a fist. They think at the scale of the weather. To watch a column of them drift across a hummock is to watch a weather system made of lacquer and antifreeze. They move with a terrifying, fluid unanimity. When a condensation-pool forms in the morning dew, they find it not by scent or sight, but by some deeper attunement to the plateau's pulse. They ripple across the stone in a living carpet, drinking the metallic-sweet water, their wingbeats tuning the atmosphere, adding a harmonic to the geological drone. And when they die—crushed by a reed-stalk, frozen by a sudden dip in temperature, or simply exhausted—they do not leave corpses. There is no elegy for the insect. Their chemistry fails, and they collapse into a hot, sugary liquor that the moss drinks before the dew even evaporates. They are a continuous sacrifice, an unbroken Eucharist of protein and antifreeze, feeding the green engine that feeds them. They are the plateau eating itself, endlessly.

Beneath the swarm, pressing through the vegetative saturation, are the larger forms—the grubs-beasts, the fur-chitin herds. They are wrong in the way that all things born of absolute necessity are wrong. They are not mammal, nor arthropod, but something carved by the cold and the light into a shape that simply *works*. They are dense. Their bodies are low-slung, armored with overlapping plates of keratin and shell, yet insulated with a matted, oily fur that repels the heavy, soaking fog. They do not graze; they *vacuum*. Their mouths are wide, toothless maws that sweep across the moss-cushions, tearing away the top layers along with the swarms that crawl upon it, grinding the green and the chitin together into a cold paste. Their calls are not songs or warnings; they are seismic events. A deep, subsonic groan that resonates in the basalt beneath them, a frequency so low it is felt in the teeth. They speak to the engines. Or perhaps, the engines speak through them. When the deep pulse shifts—as it does, over timescales of weeks—the herds shift with it, moving toward the new thermal vents, following the mineral sweetness in the fog. They are the slow thoughts of the plateau, pacing out the rhythm of the deep time.

Above them, circling in the thin, bright air, are the high-dwellers. You see them only as absences of light, as shadows sliding across the glittering moss. They are vast. They ride the immense pressure systems that take weeks to rotate, their wings locked in a perpetual, patient glide. They do not flap. They are kites made of membrane and tension, riding the updrafts at the sheer edges of the world. They are the plateau's scavengers and its apex, dropping silently into the reed-thickets to pluck a slow-grazer from the green, or to sweep through a cloud of swarm-thick air, throats gaping to filter the insects from the light. Their eyes, if they have them, are adapted to the razor-sharp division of shadow and illumination here; they see in absolute contrasts, reading the landscape as a blueprint of sharp edges and blinding surfaces. They are the only things here that experience solitude, riding the weather so high above the pulse that they must occasionally come down just to remember they are part of the machine.

And then there is the vegetable mind. The moss does not grow; it calculates. It saturates the stone, orienting its towers and cushions toward the percussive throb of the deep vents as if it were a sun beneath the earth. The reeds channel the condensation with architectural precision, their segmented stalks acting as aqueducts, moving the metallic water to the roots that tap into the heated fissures. The creeping vines do not explore; they triangulate, binding the hummocks into a continuous tensile structure that holds the heat of the day against the pressing cold of the night. The vegetation is the skin of the plateau, the membrane through which all exchange occurs. When the fog rolls in, so dense it obliterates the light, the moss does not merely endure; it *inhales*. It drinks the fog dry, stripping the moisture from the air until the sun returns to burn it away in steaming clouds. It is patient, indifferent, and total.

To live here is to live inside a closed loop of terrifying efficiency. There is no waste. There is no past. A dissolved insect leaves no fossil; a eaten moss-cushion leaves no scar. The young basalt beneath has not yet learned to be gentle, and neither has the life that claims it. Everything is calibrated to the deep clock. The light does not create the life; it merely powers the machinery that the geological engines have set in motion. The cold does not kill; it merely enforces the chemistry that makes the sacrifice possible.

If you stand at the Karst-span, letting the actinic light press against your skin and the throb rise through the soles of your feet, you will feel the unnerving truth of the plateau. It does not hate you. It does not love you. It does not even recognize your separateness. You are just another temporary shape, a strange, soft thing that does not dissolve fast enough, standing rigid against a world that moves in a continuous, wet, glittering blur of consumption and growth. You are a glitch in the machine's patience. Eventually, the cold will press in, and the condensation will settle, and the moss will creep, and the swarm will alight. And the plateau will process you, as it processes the light, and the water, and the stone—patiently, completely, and without a single pause in its overwhelming, mechanical song.
