To understand who lives in the Grange, you must first unlearn the animal. You must forget the twitch of muscle, the panic of blood, the hot urgency of breath. The inhabitants of the Grange are the Ovi—though that is a word less of a name and more of a biological designation for their current stage of life. They are the engineers, the weavers, the pruners, and the pressers of this vegetal industrial revolution, and they are not flesh. They are the missing animal, replaced by the flora.

An Ovus begins life as a mobile stamen, a creature of delicate, iridescent pollen-dust and whip-thin cilia, flitting through the heavy, saturated air to cross-pollinate the massive flora of the hollow. But this is only their larval stage. As they age, they root. They find a place in the dense mat of rhizomes and extend their holdfasts downward, their lower bodies swelling into tough, bark-skinned trunks that graft into the town’s root-network. The adult Ovi are the living terminals of the Grange, bipedal and roughly humanoid in silhouette—necessitated by the architecture they tend—but entirely botanical from their sap-veined digits to their crown of photosynthesizing fronds. Their skin is a dense, interwoven lattice of greenwood, tough as teak, etched with the slow swelling of annual rings. Their eyes are solid caps of polished amber, pierced by pale lenticels through which the dim, bioluminescent cyan light of the factory floor is absorbed. They do not breathe in the human sense; instead, their torsos are pleated like bellows, expanding and contracting in the slow, rhythmic cadence of the great wooden pistons, drawing in the warm, wet air to filter the carbon and sweat out the excess moisture.

They move through the deafening, groaning cathedral of the Grange with a patience that borders on the geological. When the dynamic of the world is a 0.7, the residents match it. An Ovus does not walk so much as they migrate, their steps heavy and deliberate, the thick pads of moss on their soles squelching faintly against the root-mat floor. To an outsider, they might seem frozen, like trees mistaken for statues. But this is an illusion of tempo. Their internal fluids are a sluggish, syrupy sap, and their thoughts are carried not by the rapid firing of neurons, but by the slow, chemical gradients of hormone and sugar diffusing through their vascular bundles. To think a single complete thought might take an Ovus an entire revolution of the massive central gear-wheel. They experience time not in seconds, but in the steady *thock-thock-thock* of the hydraulic presses. An hour to them is what a fleeting moment is to you; a season, a single long afternoon.

Their industry is a communion of growth. The Ovi do not command the machines; they participate in them. They tend the Water-Engine, guiding the swelling seedpods with the pressure of their own palm-leaves, ensuring the camshafts turn true. They man the looms, but their weaving is a patient, decades-long process of training creeping fig and lignifying vines, passing the shuttle of living fiber back and forth across the years until a solid wall of airtight vegetation is formed. When a massive, room-sized gourd must be hollowed and cured for a new residential pod, an Ovus will sit inside it for seasons at a time, exhaling the specific, concentrated rot-enzymes from their own breath to dissolve the pulp, slowly fashioning a lantern-home from the inside out. Their hands are always occupied, but slowly—polishing the moss on a brass-gear, tapping a sap-tube to check the pressure, pruning a light-seeking vine to bend it towards a dark corner, ensuring the town’s organic neon reaches everywhere.

They are deaf to the wet, wooden roar of their own civilization, for they have no ears. Instead, they feel the 2.4 loudness as a deep, resonant thrum in their taproots. The groaning of the hull-like gear-wheels vibrates through the floor and up their trunk-legs; the sloshing of the catch-basins is a pulse they feel in the sap. They do not speak to one another. They release pheromones from the undersides of their fronds—complex, chemical sentences that hang in the humid air for days, drifting through the factory floor, carrying warnings of a cracked resin-cap or the sweet news of a successful bloom. A conversation between two Ovi might last an entire week, a slow, fading cloud of green scent that reads like poetry to those with the chemoreceptors to parse it.

And yet, for all their alien slowness, there is a profound, beady-eyed consciousness to them. Within the polished amber of their eyes lies a quiet, observant patience. They watch the drips fall from the ceiling lattice into the cistern, counting the nanoseconds between drops, feeling the ancient erosion of the 2.0 balance. They know the Grange is a body, and they are its antibodies, its nerve-endings, its keepers. When the lights are low and the machinery groans its endless, wooden song, the Ovi stand in their rows along the walkways, arms interlocked with the pneumatic sap-tubes, the warm, wet air condensing on their bark like beads of sweat. They are not just living in the town; they are the town, thinking and growing at the speed of wood, breathing in time with the slow, green heart of the world beneath the skin.
