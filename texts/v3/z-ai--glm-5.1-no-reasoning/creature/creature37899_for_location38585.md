The ones who stayed are not the makers. The makers left their tools sitting where they set them down—flat pressing-stones, sharpened sticks, frayed brushes—and the structure has been absorbing them ever since. The pale paste has swelled around the bases of the sticks. The fungal mats have crept over the stones, thin gray skin occluding the smooth faces. The makers were taller. You can tell by the ceiling height, by the way the ramps are spaced for a stride longer than yours, by the placement of the channels at a height that would catch the light for someone standing upright.

The ones who stayed are shorter. They have to be. The ceilings here permit no pride.

They came later—or they were already here, already small, already living in the gaps left by the makers' departure. They do not make things. They maintain. The difference is important.

There is a word for what they are but it belongs to them and it is not spoken aloud above a whisper, and only in the parts of the structure where the water-roar is loud enough to cover it. They have no need of the word most of the time. They know what they are.

To look at: they are the color of wet ceramic, a dark wet gray that lightens to the color of the paste when they are still for a long time. The fruiting bodies glow amber; they do not. They are dark, and they move through the dark places between the lit basins like something being careful not to be seen, which is not the same as hiding. They are careful. They are always careful. The structure does not forgive clumsiness. A wrong step can crack a curing seam; a shoulder brushed against a fragile tube can split it. They know the structure's tolerances. They have lived within them for as long as they have been here.

Their bodies are soft, in the way that things born from damp places are soft. Not gelatinous, but compressible. If you pressed your hand against one of them—gently, because they are easily hurt—it would give, and then slowly fill back in, like pressing your thumb into the thick fungal mat in a long-established basin. They are segmented, though the segments are not rigid. The divisions run horizontally across their torsos and vertically down their limbs, and when they move, the segments slide against each other with a faint wet sound, like someone rubbing their thumb across a damp leaf.

They have limbs. Four, possibly more—the counting is difficult, because the segments sometimes look like limbs and the limbs sometimes look like segments, and they can extend and retract parts of themselves with a fluidity that suggests they are not strongly committed to a single body plan. The default configuration seems to be: two weight-bearing limbs, thick and short, for moving across surfaces; two manipulatory limbs, thinner, for touching and holding; and a head-region, which is not a head in the sense of having a skull or a face but is rather the end of the body that has the most sensors.

The sensors are the hardest part to describe. They do not have eyes. They have something instead that does what eyes do, but differently. Across the head-region, where a face would be, there is a smooth, slightly concave surface, like the inside of a shallow bowl, and it is covered in fine filaments—pale, almost transparent, constantly moving, like the cilia on something unicellular. These filaments pick up something, though it is not light. When they are near a fruiting body, the filaments orient toward it, but not toward its glow. They orient toward its warmth. They orient toward its *activity*—the fact of it being alive, the metabolic hum of it. The filaments read the room in a way that has nothing to do with seeing. They read vibration, air pressure, the subsonic shifting of the structure, the pulse of air through the tubes. They read the humidity gradient, bending toward the wetter parts of the space. They read each other—when two of them are close, their filaments reach toward each other and interlace briefly, exchanging something that is neither greeting nor information but something between the two, a recognition, an update, a confirmation that the other is still present and still the same.

They do not speak in the way that you would recognize. The structure is too loud for speech. They speak in pressure: they press against the ceramic surfaces, the paste seams, the columns of stacked rings, and the structure carries the vibration. A tap here becomes a pulse there; a sustained contact becomes a hum that travels through the walls and floors and is felt by the filaments of whoever is near enough. They have conversations through the architecture. The structure is their medium. The fact that it is settling, that it creaks and groans and shifts, means that their language is always being interfered with—always losing clarity to the noise of the building becoming itself. They are used to this. They repeat themselves. They speak simply. They say things like: *water rising here* and *paste crack in the fourth seam* and *I am at the basin where the light is strong* and *I am not coming back to that place* and *come* and *come* and *come*.

What they eat is the fungal mats. They do not eat the fruiting bodies, though they are drawn to them; they eat the pale gray film, the spreading layer, the body of the growth rather than its light. They harvest carefully, never stripping a basin entirely, always leaving enough to regrow. They know the rate of growth in each basin and how long it takes to recover, and they rotate their feeding accordingly. Some basins are young—new mats, thin, not yet fruiting—and they leave these entirely alone. Some are old—thick with layered growth, glowing with amber clusters—and they feed from these more freely. They have a relationship with the fungal mats that might be called cultivation, except that they did not plant the mats and they do not control their spread. They tend. They clear channels that have clogged with overgrowth. They redirect small flows of water toward basins that are drying out. They break up mats that have grown too thick and are suffocating themselves, peeling away the dead lower layers and leaving the living surface exposed. They do this with the makers' tools—the pressing-stones, the sharpened sticks—or with their hands, which are nearly as effective.

They also tend the small dark things. The moving things. The thumb-length segmented crawlers that feed on the mats and contract into spirals when disturbed. They do not eat the crawlers. They seem to value them, though it is not clear why. They move crawlers from basin to basin, carrying them curled and motionless in their manipulatory limbs, placing them on mats that are growing too dense. The crawlers feed. The mats are thinned. The cycle continues. It might be agriculture. It might be worship. It might be something for which no word exists because no one has needed one.

They live in the basins—not in the water, but near it. They build shelters, though *build* is perhaps too strong a word. They arrange. They find places where the structure provides shelter naturally—an overhang of ceramic, a gap between two columns, a place where the ceiling dips low and the paste has extruded into stalactite forms that block the drips. They modify these places incrementally: a flat stone placed to redirect a flow of water away from where they sleep, a bundle of fibrous material—stripped from the tubes overhead, never the functioning ones, always the dead ones—piled into a rough cushion, a channel scratched into the ceramic with a sharpened stick to drain a pool that keeps forming. They do not make structures of their own. They inhabit the structure. They are tenants.

They do not sleep all at once. The structure does not sleep, and neither do they. Instead, they rest in rotation—some moving, some still, some feeding, some tending—so that there is always someone awake, always someone listening for the sounds of the building, the shifts in the hum, the groans of settling that might mean a seam is about to crack or a column is shifting. When they hear these sounds, they respond. They go to the place. They assess. If a crack is forming in the paste, they press more paste into it—from where? They make paste. This is the only manufacturing they do. They have a process for making the pale mineral paste, and it involves the collection basins, the mineral residue the water leaves behind, and something else—something organic, something they contribute, something that comes from their bodies, a secretion that mixes with the minerals and makes them set and cure. The paste they make is not identical to the makers' paste. It is less strong, less durable. But it fills the cracks. It keeps the water in the channels. It slows the structure's slow dissolution long enough for the structure to finish becoming whatever it is becoming.

They do not know what it is becoming. They do not think in those terms.

They are not curious about the makers in the way that someone finding abandoned tools might be curious. They know the makers existed. They know the makers were here before them. They know the makers made the structure. They do not wonder why the makers left, or where they went, or whether they will return. The tools are tools. They use them. The makers' absence is not a question; it is a condition, like the humidity, like the noise, like the low ceiling. It is part of the place. It is part of what they live inside.

They are not numerous. You could walk through the Incession Floors for days—stooped, in the dark, in the noise—and never see one. They avoid the open stretches, the places where the structure is widest and the ceiling highest. They prefer the dense parts, the crowded sections where columns stand close together and the tube bundles press low and the water sounds are loudest. They prefer these places because the noise covers their movement, and because the dampness is higher there, and because the fungal mats grow thickest where the structure is most enclosed, and because they are, in their soft way, fragile things that need the close press of surfaces around them to feel safe. When they are in open space—with nothing within arm's reach, with surfaces distant—they become agitated. Their filaments wave erratically. They move toward the nearest wall, column, overhang. They press themselves against it and stay there until they have calmed.

When one of them dies—their bodies do fail, slowly, segment by segment, the soft tissue drying and hardening until it can no longer flex, can no longer move water through itself, can no longer sustain the processes that keep it alive—the others come. They come to the place where the body is. They touch it with their filaments. They press their manipulatory limbs against the segments that have hardened. They stay for a time. Then they carry the body to the nearest collection basin with an established mat, and they place it on the surface of the mat, and they leave it there. The mat grows over it. The fruiting bodies eventually rise from that place, and their amber glow is perhaps slightly brighter, or perhaps it is not—it is difficult to tell, and the ones who are alive do not seem to notice or to mark the difference. They do not return to that basin more than any other. They do not avoid it. They feed from it when it is their turn, the same as any other.

They do not count their dead. They do not count themselves. They do not track time in any way that corresponds to the calendars of other places. They track moisture—the rise and fall of water in the basins, the thickening and thininning of the mats, the cycles of fruiting and glowing and dimming. They track the structure's sounds—the rhythm of the settling, the pitch of the hum, the intervals between cracks and groans. They track each other—the presence and absence of particular filaments, particular pressures, particular patterns of speech-through-surface. These are their measures. These are enough.

They have been here long enough that the structure's sounds have become their language's grammar. The hum of the tubes determines the tone of their speech: when the hum is low, they speak in low vibrations; when it rises, their speech rises. The settling of the paste determines the rhythm: when the structure creaks frequently, they speak in short, rapid bursts; when it is still for a stretch, they speak in long, slow pressures. They do not control this. They grew into it. Their language is a dialect of the building's voice. To understand them, you would have to understand the structure first, and the structure is not done speaking.

They do not name the parts of the structure. They do not name the basins or the columns or the channels. They know them by their feel—the texture of the ceramic at that spot, the thickness of the mat, the temperature of the water, the quality of the sound the surface makes when pressed. A basin is not "the basin where the light is strong." A basin is a specific pressure against a specific filament, a vibration traveling through a particular path, a place that is known by the way it feels to stand in it, stooped, in the dark, with the water roaring and the hum vibrating in your chest and the faint amber glow warming the filaments on your head-region, and the knowledge—carried not in thought but in body—that you are inside something that is still being born, and that you were born inside it too, and that you will live inside it until you are placed on a mat and grown over, and that this is not sad, and that this is not happy, and that this is simply what it is to be this thing in this place at this time.

They are not waiting for the makers to return. They are not waiting for anything. They are maintaining.

They are maintaining something that was made to be maintained. That is what they were for. That is what they are for. The makers, in setting the processes in motion, may have set them in motion too—may have made them, or seeded them, or designed the conditions that would produce them from the same wet, mineral, organic processes that produced the fungal mats. No one knows. They do not know. It does not matter.

What matters is the crack in the seventh seam. What matters is the water finding a new path around the third column cluster. What matters is the mat in the deep basin that is growing too thick and needs thinning. What matters is the crawler that has been still too long and needs to be moved to a wetter place. What matters is the hum, which has changed pitch, which means the air pressure has shifted, which means the water will flow differently soon, which means the channels will need checking, which means someone needs to go.

They go. They always go. Stooped, in the dark, in the noise, with their filaments reaching toward the warmth of the nearest fruiting body, their segments sliding against each other with a sound like a damp leaf, their hands—soft, careful, knowledgeable—carrying a pressing-stone or a sharpened stick or nothing at all, moving through the dense, close, made world that is still being born, and maintaining it, and maintaining themselves within it, and being, in their small and soft and damp and simple way, alive.
