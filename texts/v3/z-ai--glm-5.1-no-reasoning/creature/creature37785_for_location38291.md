The ones who live here do not live. They persist.

They are the Slake-Masses, and they have no word for themselves because they have no language, no memory, and no interior life. They are the only breath in a body that has forgotten it is dead.

To understand them, you must first understand that they are not organisms. They are events. A Slake-Mass begins where the heat is most extreme—where superheated, mineral-heavy water forces itself through a fissure in the ancient brickwork and meets the cooler, chemical soup of the runoff pools. In that collision, something precipitates. Not life, not as any carbon-based system would recognize it, but a localized turbulence, a self-sustaining reaction that draws iron from the water and sulfur from the stone and spins them into a matrix that holds itself together. The process is slow. A mass takes decades to form, layering itself like a stain, building density in the warmest corners of the city's gutters and cisterns. It has no parent. It has no ancestor. It simply accumulates until it becomes too heavy for the surface it clings to, and then it detaches.

When a Slake-Mass pulls free, it does not move with purpose. It is a gelatinous weight, a slumping, viscous pillow the size of a dog or a barrel or occasionally a cart, depending on how long it grew before gravity claimed it. Its surface is a skin of oxidation—rust-red and black, crusted with mineral deposits that crack and reform as it shifts. Inside, it is striated like rotten ore, veined with metallic compounds that catch the flat amber light when the skin tears. It is warm to the touch, sometimes actively hot, carrying the mantle's breath inside itself like a coal that refuses to die. And it moves by the simplest mechanism available: gas. The chemo-synthetic processes at its core produce bubbles of hydrogen sulfide and other, less identifiable vapors. These gather in cavities within the mass, expanding until the pressure forces a rupture, and the escaping gas propels the whole clumsy body in one direction or another. The motion is a series of lurching flops, each one slightly different from the last, governed not by intention but by the internal geometry of the gas pockets. A mass might travel ten meters in a day, or it might spend a week inching along the same rusted gutter, depending on the heat of the water and the mineral content of its feed.

There is no brain. There is no nervous system. There are no organs. The Slake-Mass is a single, continuous reaction, a tubeless gut that absorbs heat and dissolved metals through its surface and excretes a dark, sulfurous slurry from its lowest point. It does not eat in any meaningful sense. It simply exists in contact with what sustains it, and the boundary between itself and the water it sits in is more a gradient than a wall. The outermost layers of a mass are always dissolving, always bleeding back into the runoff, while new material precipitates on the inner face of the skin. It is a standing wave of chemistry, a shape that maintains itself not through repair but through the constant, mindless pressure of the conditions that created it.

They congregate in the low places. The city's drainage system—those ancient, automated channels that the grinding pump-houses still clear—is their highway and their home. The water flows there, warm and mineral-rich, and the masses follow it, or rather, they are pushed by gas and gravity into the same paths the water takes. In the deepest cisterns, where the heat is constant and the runoff pools are thick with dissolved iron, dozens of masses accumulate, sliding over and under one another in a slow, oozing pile. Sometimes they merge. Two masses pressed together in a tight channel will gradually lose their separate skins, their matrices interpenetrating until they become a single, larger body. Sometimes they split. A mass that grows too large for its internal cohesion will tear apart under its own weight, each fragment developing a new skin and continuing its blind, purposeless drift. There is no death in this process, not exactly. A mass that loses too much of itself to dissolution simply ceases to be a mass, its material rejoining the mineral soup from which it came. But there is no moment of ending, no border between life and not-life. It is a continuum.

They do not perceive the city. They do not perceive anything. There is no mechanism for sensation, no structure that could translate the vibration of a distant stamp-mill into an internal state. The fog, the amber light, the vast, empty avenues—all of it is invisible to them, not because they are blind but because there is nothing in them that could see. They exist in a state of pure, reflexive chemistry, responding to heat and moisture the way a candle flame responds to air. When the geothermal vents surge and the water temperature rises, the masses grow more active, their internal gas production increasing, their flops becoming more frequent and more violent. When the heat dips, they slow, settling into the lowest pockets of the drainage channels and growing still, their surfaces hardening into a thicker, more crust-like skin. It is not sleep. It is not waiting. It is simply a reduction in the rate of a reaction.

And yet.

There is something in the way they gather. Something in the pattern of their movement that resists pure randomness. The masses do not simply disperse evenly through the runoff system. They cluster. They accumulate in the warmest pools, yes, but they also accumulate near the oldest, most deeply corroded infrastructure—the pumps that have been cycling for millennia, the vents that breathe the planet's heat into the wet air. It is possible that this is mere chemistry, a simple thermal gradient pulling them toward the heat sources. But standing in the fog, watching a mass lurch and settle, lurch and settle, inching its way toward the shuddering heart of a pump-house, it is difficult not to feel that something is being sought. Not consciously. Not with intent. But with the same blind, gravitational pull that draws a root toward water.

They are the city's last inhabitants, and they do not know the city exists.

They do not know that the iron they absorb was shaped by hands, that the channels they slide through were carved by tools, that the heat that sustains them was tapped by minds that understood geology and pressure and the behavior of steam. They do not know that the pump-houses that draw them are the last gasp of an industry that once served a purpose, or that the vast, vacant avenues above their heads were built for creatures who walked on two legs and saw the world through eyes. The Slake-Masses have no relationship to the city's history. They are a consequence of it, an afterthought of chemistry, a mold growing on a corpse that does not know it is a corpse.

But they are also, in a sense, the city's children. They are made of the city. Their iron is the iron of the walkways and the pipe bridges. Their sulfur is the sulfur of the furnace residue and the vent deposits. Their water is the water that the builders dragged up from the mantle, the water that was meant to power engines and cool retorts and serve a hundred industrial purposes. The city spent millennia creating the conditions for the Slake-Masses to exist—the heat, the minerals, the constant wet—and now, in its long, empty twilight, it has produced something that lives in the ruins of its infrastructure, feeding on the exhaust of its final, purposeless maintenance.

They will outlast the city. Not because they are durable—they are not; individual masses dissolve and reform constantly—but because they are adaptable in a way the city is not. As the brickwork softens and the iron fuses into unrecognizable lumps, as the drainage channels clog and the automated pumps finally seize and fall silent, the masses will simply find new paths. They will accumulate in the pools that form in the hollows of collapsed buildings, in the bowls of rusted machinery, in the cracks where the deep heat still reaches the surface. They will follow the water, and the water will follow the heat, and the heat will be there long after the last human-built structure has dissolved into sandstone and rust. And in the hot, wet dark, the masses will continue their blind, purposeless flopping, their slow, chemical accumulation, their endless cycling of iron and sulfur and gas. Not alive. Not dead. Just continuing.

If you stood in the deepest cistern, where the water is hottest and the masses are thickest, you would feel them before you saw them. The water would be viscous, thicker than it should be, and warm—almost too warm, the heat of a bath left on the stove too long. The fog would be dense, opaque, and the amber light would not reach here. In the darkness, you would hear a soft, rhythmic plopping—the sound of gas escaping, of masses shifting, of the slow, oozing churn of a chemistry that has found its equilibrium. And if you reached into the water, you would feel them—smooth, heavy, slick with mineral slime, moving against your hand with a gentle, insistent pressure, like a heartbeat that does not know it is beating.

They are the closest thing this world has to an ecosystem. And they are enough.
