To live here is to be a parasite of the dynamo.

They are not the architects. The builders—if there ever were builders of flesh and intent—have long since been ground into the oxide dust that rains perpetually from the ceiling. Whoever laid the first rivet, whoever calibrated the nuclear-hot reactor cores that still scream in the basaltic dark, departed or dissolved ages ago. What remains is the machine itself, and the machine does not need citizens. It only needs fuel, and in this bath of superheated, mineral-heavy water, fuel is a matter of desperate, Darwinian improvisation.

The residents of the pocket are called, by the rare and terrified submariners who have charted its periphery, the *Galvanic Remora*. They are not a species in any terrestrial sense; they are a localized infestation, a spontaneous generation born of the abyss and the arc light.

Physically, they are illustrations of the environment that made them. They are bipedal out of necessity—the frantic crosshatch of gantries, pipes, and vibrating rails requires something that can clamp and climb. Their skin is not skin at all, but a bio-mineralized carapace of manganese and iron, built up in thick, asymmetrical plates over generations of absorbing the heavy metals from the syrupy water. They look like rust given locomotion. Their limbs are overly long and jointed in too many places, ending in four-point clamps of calcified copper that can grip a rattling steam pipe without being thrown by the violent updrafts. 

They have no eyes. In a world where visibility extends four meters into a mineral haze, eyes are a vulnerability. Instead, their flattened, eyeless heads are dominated by complex arrays of pressure-sensitive pits and electroreceptors. They do not see the city; they feel its electromagnetic heartbeat. To them, the pocket is a symphony of vibration. They can hear the screaming vent of a pressurized valve from a hundred meters away; they can taste the lethal current humming in an overhead cable, navigating the lethal infrastructure by the static charge it casts into the water.

They do not eat. Not in the way surface dwellers understand. There is no kelp, no fish, nothing to hunt or harvest. The *Galvanic Remora* survive by tapping the city's veins. Clinging to the trembling brass towers, they insert their hollow, syringe-like feeding tubes into the high-pressure pipes. They do not drink the water; they strip it. Their internal chemistries, running at ferocious temperatures facilitated by the ambient thirty-degree heat, extract the dissolved metals, the trace sulfides, and the electrical gradients directly from the flow. They are living smelters, refining the mineral breath of the earth into the tissues of their own bodies. When they exhale, they release a cloud of purified, cooled water—a brief, icy anomaly in the boiling dark.

Their lives are a constant, violent ballet of avoidance. The city is a machine that does not recognize them, and it would kill them without malice in an instant. To live here is to be a mote in a seized engine. They dodge the snapping blue arcs of shorting junction boxes. They brace against the shuddering shockwaves of the massive reciprocating pistons. They are thrown by the underwater jet-plumes, their long limbs flailing, scrambling to find a barnacled purchase before they are slammed into the corroded steel walls. They are heavily scarred; older individuals are often missing limbs, cauterized by electrical discharge or crushed by shifting gantries, surviving only because their decentralized organ clusters allow them to regenerate, slowly and painfully, feeding off the ambient heat.

There are perhaps three hundred of them in the entire pocket. A number tragically, beautifully proportional to the three-hundred-meter cavity. They do not speak—over the catastrophic din of the steam horns and the relentless hammering, verbal communication is impossible. They communicate through touch and resonance. When two *Remora* meet on a vibrating rail, they press their carapaces together and hum, emitting low-frequency vibrations that travel through the metal infrastructure, a subsonic whisper in the machine's deafening roar. 

They have no culture, no art, no recorded history. They possess only an intense, localized consciousness, a hyper-awareness of the immediate present. Their minds are attuned entirely to the rhythm of the pistons, the pressure of the water, the proximity of the electric arcs. They do not know why the city was built, nor do they care. They do not wonder what the machine produces. 

But they know, with a certainty that goes deeper than instinct, that the city must not stop.

They do not worship the machine, but they are its acolytes. Sometimes, a junction box blows, severing a power loop. Sometimes, a valve sticks, threatening to burst a pipeline and flood a sector with freezing, unheated water. When this happens, the *Galvanic Remora* swarm. They do not fix the machine with tools; they fix it with their bodies. They form conductive bridges with their copper-rich limbs, allowing the lethal current to arc safely across their insulating carapaces. They use their own body heat and the chemical exhaust of their feeding to thaw frozen valves. They throw themselves into the gaps of the perpetual motion engine, suffering and dying in droves to clear a blockage or reset a switch, ensuring the circulation continues. 

Why? Because they have dimly, ancestrally understood the terrible truth of the pocket. The city is manufacturing the pocket. The heat, the turbulence, the violent insistence of its own running is the only thing keeping the crushing weight of the abyss from collapsing this single, contained cavity. If the engines fail, if the convection loop stops, the ocean will instantly reclaim this space. The basaltic crust will shift. The three hundred meters of warmth will become a tomb of absolute pressure, crushing thought, and heat, and life into silence.

The *Galvanic Remora* cling to the rattling steel, deafened by the horns, bathed in the sickly orange glow of thermal radiance, enduring the chaotic, violent life of the pocket. They are the ghost-town's only citizens, tending an automated engine that makes nothing, producing only their own continued existence. They are the rust that walks, the parasites that keep the host alive, roaring forever into the dark alongside their machine, keeping the abyss at bay.
