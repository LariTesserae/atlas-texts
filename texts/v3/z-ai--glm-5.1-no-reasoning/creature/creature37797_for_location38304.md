What lives here are the architects of an anonymity so profound they do not know they possess it. They live in the absolute negation of the sun's memory, in a medium so dense it is nearly gel, in a room that is less a location and more a temporary disagreement between the ocean's weight and the earth's heat.

The builders—those thumb-sized, finger-sized, rice-grain-sized entities—are the citizens of a metropolis built from the inside out. To call them animals is to misunderstand what they have become. They are discrete, yes; you can see where one plated body ends and another begins. But they are also limbs of a larger body. The shell they build is their exoskeleton, their stomach, and their home, and when they retreat inside it to exhale that warm, mineral breath, the boundary between "organism" and "apartment" dissolves. They are a city of hermit crabs whose shells grew from their own spit and the bones of the earth. They do not have a concept of "self" versus "structure." They are the stone they carry.

Their lives are a liturgy of addition. A fragment of basalt is selected. It is scraped, tasted, dissolved, extruded. It is placed. They do not rest, because resting is a rhythm dictated by the sun, and the sun is a lie that has never reached them. Their circadian cycle is the pulse of the tide above—the slow, crushing systole and diastole of the ocean pressing on the roof of their world. When the pressure shifts, they shift. They are synced to the breath of the sea like a heartbeat synced to a drum. They feel the weight of the water change, and they know it is time to carry the next stone.

They do not have thoughts. They have *urges with momentum*. The urge to seal a gap. The urge to thicken a wall. The urge to climb, always to climb, building the city higher toward the forty meters of killing cold above, as if the city itself were trying to fashion a spine to pierce the ocean floor. They do not know what they are building toward, only that the structure is not finished. The geometry of their city—the dense, spiraling, almost-repeating shelves and terraces—is not designed. It is accumulated. It is the sum of ten thousand local decisions: *here is a gap; I will fill it. Here is a weak wall; I will thicken it.* The result is a cathedral of accreted logic, a labyrinth of necessity.

And yet, they are not alone in their blindness.

The eyeless, flat-headed cleaners are the ghosts of the city's metabolism. They are the future-tense of the builders' waste, eating what is exhaled and exhuming the white snow of calcium carbonate that falls like ash in the dim gray non-light. They do not interact with the builders; they *commute* with them. The builders exhale, the cleaners inhale. They are the exhale of a body the size of the room. They move with the patience of things that have no predators and no prey, only a chemical obligation to process.

Then there is the fog—the cloud of gray-green luminescence that pulses in the eddies. This is the city's voice. The builders are deaf, but the fog is speaking. It brightens when the chamber breathes out; it dims when the chamber breathes in. It is a city-wide signal, a chemical and photic network that ties the tens of thousands of structures into a single entity. When a builder scrapes the wall, the mineral dust it creates joins the fog. When a cleaner excretes calcium carbonate, it feeds the fog. The fog is the city's nervous system, its internet, its dreaming—it is the medium through which the city knows itself. It is not thinking in the way you or I think, but it is *processing*. It is integrating the ten thousand local decisions into a single, slow, geological thought: *grow*.

And in the corner, there is the old thing. The large thing. The torso-sized shape resting against the wall, pitted and eroded. It is not dead. It is not dormant. It is *retired*. It is the progenitor, or perhaps a previous iteration of the city's logic—a body that built a shell so large it became a ruin, and in its shadow, the smaller builders found a niche. It is a monument to a different scale of existence. It does not move because it does not need to move; it has become a part of the geology. The smaller builders crawl over it, quarrying its surface for mineral, recycling its legacy into their own shells. They do not know they are eating their ancestor. They only know the stone is good, the stone is soft, the stone can be made into a wall.

What lives here is a single organism the size of a room, composed of tens of thousands of discrete, blind, deaf bodies, communicating through light they cannot see and chemicals they cannot taste. They are a circulatory system of stone and heat. They are the earth's immune response to the crushing cold above. They are building a wall against the ocean, one fragment at a time, and they will never finish, because the room is shrinking as they build, and the ocean is pressing down, and the heat is rising, and the pulse goes on.

They live in the vesicle, and the vesicle is alive, and what lives there is the world becoming more itself—one placed stone, one exhaled current, one gray-green pulse at a time.
