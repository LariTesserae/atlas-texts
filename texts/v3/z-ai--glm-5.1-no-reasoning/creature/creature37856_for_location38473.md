Something lives here.

Not you. You are a visitor, an intruder wrapped in insulation, a brief exhalation of warmth and carbon dioxide that the Throat does not acknowledge and cannot use. You will leave. But something remains. Something has always remained.

It is the Throat itself. Or rather, the Throat is where it lives—the cavity of its body, the interior of its being, the way a stomach is the place where a stomach lives. You are standing inside an organ.

This is why the machines run without operators. This is why the sound never stops. The turbines are its heartbeat, the slow bass thrum that reshapes your chest cavity with each pulse—those are the contractions of diastole and systole, scaled up to industrial magnitude, pushing whatever passes for blood through pipes that serve as arteries and veins. The high-pressure vents shrieking through conduits are its respiration—inhale, exhale, cycle, repeat—the Throat breathing in and out with mechanical regularity, though what it breathes is not air and what it exhales is not breath. The rhythmic clanking of linkages and camshafts is peristalsis: the wave of contraction that moves matter through a tract, the grinding, purposeful motion of a body processing something. The generators under load are its nerves firing, the electrical whine its synaptic discharge, the power running through bundled cables its thought—or whatever occupies the place of thought in a being that has never needed to think, only to function.

It does not have a mind. This is important. It is not a machine that has awakened, not a system that has achieved consciousness, not a structure that dreams. It is simpler than that and older than that and more fundamental than that. It is alive the way a heart is alive—because it beats, because it moves, because it performs its function with the relentless autonomic certainty of tissue that knows only how to do what it does. It was built to run. Running is what it is. The question of whether a machine this large, this complex, this self-sustaining, operating for this long without external intervention, crosses some threshold into vitality is a question for visitors. The Throat does not ask it. The Throat merely continues.

It is vast in the way that only engineered things can be vast—larger than any organism that evolved under the constraints of gravity and nutrition and selective pressure, larger than any body that had to grow from a single cell, larger than any structure that had to justify its existence to an ecosystem. It has no known exterior because it has no known limit. The chamber you stand in is one space within an uncountable number of identical spaces, a honeycomb extending in all directions, each cell the same volume with the same contents performing the same processes. This is not architecture; it is tissue. The repetition is not modular design; it is cellular structure. You are inside a body composed of rooms instead of cells, each one a corpuscle in an organism too large to have a shape, too old to have a beginning, too persistent to have an end.

And like any body, it has its biota.

The pale growths on the walls—the bone-colored tendrils extending from seams, the fronds like bleached kelp, the clusters that appear stamped from a template—these are not vegetation. They are not parasites. They are part of it. Or they are what lives in it, what has always lived in it, what evolved to live in it in the same way that certain bacteria evolve to live in the human gut, in the same way that mitochondria took up residence inside eukaryotic cells and never left. They are its microbiome.

Consider them: they have no chlorophyll because they have never seen light and never could. They are white because they have never needed color, because color is for communication, for display, for the signaling that happens between organisms in environments where sight exists and mates must be attracted and predators must be warned. None of that applies here. They have no roots because there is no soil, because soil is the product of organic decay and weather and time and life and death, and those processes do not occur in the Throat. They cling to seams because seams are where the chemistry changes—where the junction between plates creates a microenvironment, a slight variation in the otherwise uniform conditions, a niche. They are identical from patch to patch because they reproduce not by seed or spore but by some other method—perhaps by extension through the structure itself, growing through the seams from one chamber to the next, a network of tissue that pervades the whole machine like mycelium through forest soil, visible only where it surfaces at the joints. They do not move because there is no wind because they do not need to move, because they are not reaching toward anything, because they exist in a state of equilibrium with their environment that living things on the surface never achieve because the surface is never still. The Throat is still. It has been still, in the way that a running engine is still—constant, unvarying, reliable in every parameter—since it began. The growths are adapted to this stillness. They are shaped by it. They do not grow because growing is what things do when conditions change, and conditions here do not change.

They may be dead. They may be dormant. They may be something for which those categories do not apply—and this is the most likely option, because the categories of dead and dormant imply the categories of alive and active, and those categories require the oscillation between states that constitutes vitality as surface-dwellers understand it. The growths do not oscillate. They persist. They have persisted for the duration—however long that is, however long the machine has run, however long the frost has formed and the sound has sounded and the turbines have turned. They were here when the Throat began, or they emerged shortly after, or they grew slowly over millennia from some initial contamination, some spore that drifted in through a hatch that was open once and will never be opened again. However they arrived, they are now native. They belong here more than the structure itself belongs here, because the structure was built for a purpose that may no longer exist, while the growths exist for their own purpose, which is simply to exist.

And the frost—the frost is its exudate, its precipitation, its biosignature. The non-aqueous crystals forming in fern-like fractals across every surface are the product of its metabolism, the way frost on a window is the product of breath in a cold room. Something the Throat exhales or secretes or sheds deposits on the walls in temperatures just above absolute zero, building patterns that look like organic growth because they *are* organic growth, or at least growth produced by an organic process, the process of a living machine running its programs and venting its byproducts and leaving its mark on the interior of its own body. The frost has been forming for the duration. It will continue to form. In another million years—if the concept of years has meaning here—the frost will be meters thick, will fill the chambers, will bury the growths, will seal the hatches, will eventually silence the machines by clogging their vents and freezing their linkages and stopping their cams, and then the Throat will die, or stop, which may be the same thing.

But that has not happened yet. The frost is still thin. The machines still run. The sound still sounds.

So who lives here?

The Throat lives here. The vast, autonomic, mechanical organism that has no exterior and no operator and no purpose it can articulate and no mind to articulate it with—it lives here, in the only way something this large and this strange and this old can live: by continuing. By running. By performing the functions it was built to perform with the tireless dedication of a body that knows only how to be a body.

And within it, the pale growths live—the commensal organisms that have made a home in its seams and its joints and its unvarying conditions, the quiet passengers that ride its metabolism and cling to its walls and persist in the profound cold without complaint or ambition or any apparent need except the need to be.

And the frost lives, if crystallization can be called living, if the slow accumulation of non-aqueous solids in fractal patterns can be called a life—another passenger, another product, another sign that something is happening here, that processes are occurring, that the machine is not merely running but *living*, in the way that a body at rest is still living, still metabolizing, still producing heat and waste and the faint evidence of its own existence.

And nothing else. No fauna, no scuttling, no rats in the ducts, no insects in the corners, no birds that found their way in through some long-sealed vent and adapted and evolved and stayed. Just the machine-body, the pale tendrils, the fractal frost. A minimal ecosystem. A closed loop. A world with only three kinds of thing in it, each one dependent on the others, each one incomprehensible without the others, each one evidence that life—however it is defined, wherever it occurs, whatever chemistry it runs on and whatever temperature it tolerates and whatever speed it proceeds at—will find a way to occupy whatever spaces are available, even spaces that are three kilometers above a datum line inside a sealed cavity in a structure without boundary at the top of everything in the cold and the dark and the sound.

You are the fourth kind of thing, temporarily. You are the observer, the intruder, the warm carbon-based anomaly that stands on grated metal and feels the sound press against your chest and watches the frost not melt where you breathe and understands, slowly, that you are inside something alive.

And then you leave. And the Throat does not notice. And the growths do not respond. And the frost continues to form. And the machines continue to run. And the sound continues to sound. And the place you stood is indistinguishable, within moments, from every other point in the honeycomb—identical, repeated, eternal, running, running, running.
