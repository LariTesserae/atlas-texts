# What Lives Here

The first thing to understand is that nothing here was born.

That is not quite right. Let me say it differently: nothing here arrived through any process you would recognize as ordinary reproduction. There are no mating calls in this forest. There are no nests, no eggs, no territorial displays. The fauna—if that word even applies—are ghosts not because they are shy but because they are *provisional*, still deciding what they are. And the forest itself, the whole trembling body of it, is not an ecosystem in the way a biologist would use the term. It is a single organism pretending to be many.

---

**The Pale Moths**

Start with what you can actually see.

The moths are everywhere once you start looking. They cling to the trunks of the heterophyllous canopy trees in the brighter hours, wings flat against the bark, and from a distance they look like nothing—like discolorations, like patches of lichen. Up close, their wing patterns resolve into something that makes the back of your brain itch. The lines and nodes of their markings do not *resemble* circuitry. They function as circuitry. The moths are relays. They receive something from the trees they cling to—through the fine hairs of their tarsi, through the moisture they drink from the bark's surface—and they transmit something to the next tree they land on. A signal. A chemical signature. A set of instructions. They are the nervous system of the plateau, carrying messages from one quadrant of the forest to another, and they do this without any awareness that they are doing it, which is perhaps the most honest definition of a nerve cell.

They live for eleven days. They die on the bark where they were born, and their bodies are absorbed within hours. Nothing is wasted.

There is something almost tender about them. They are the only visible living thing on the plateau that does not conceal itself, and they are, in a sense, the forest's admission that it exists. A single visible thread in an otherwise invisible tapestry.

**The Ground-Level Organisms**

If the moths are the nervous system, then the shimmering moss is something else. Not skin. Not muscle. *Liver*, perhaps. Or marrow.

The silver-green iridescence in the clearings near the industrial sites is not a mutation. It is the original phenotype. This is what the moss was *designed* to express, and the green version—the ordinary, humble, carpeting moss that fills the rest of the plateau—is the dormant form, the safe form, the form that waits. Near the pipes and the pump houses and the cracked foundations where the processed material seeped into the earth, the moss has what it needs, and it shows its true face.

If you knelt beside it—and you should not—you would see that the iridescence is not on the surface of the moss but *within* it, as though each cell contained a small, cold light. The shimmer is not reflected; it is emitted. The moss is metabolizing something that was never meant to be part of any biological system, breaking it down into compounds that the root systems of the canopy trees can absorb, and the byproduct of that metabolism is photons. Faint ones. Hard to see in the hard white light of the plateau's sun. But at dusk, when the light fails and the fog rolls in, the clearings glow. Softly. Like something breathing.

The moss has no individual. There is no boundary where one patch ends and another begins. The moss on the pump house roof is the same organism as the moss on the boulder fifty meters away is the same organism as the moss on the far cliff face. It is continuous. It underlies everything. If you pulled up the entire vegetation of the plateau like a carpet, the moss would be the floor.

**The Bracket Fungi**

These are more difficult to classify. They are not, strictly speaking, decomposers—or rather, they decompose, but what they decompose is not dead wood. They grow on living trunks, and they grow on steel, and they grow on concrete. On the living trunks, their behavior is recognizably fungal: they break down lignin, they extract nutrients, they fruit in overlapping shelves that release spores into the still air. On the steel and concrete, their behavior is something else entirely.

On the rusted gantry that spans the gorge, the bracket fungi have grown in a pattern that follows the stress fractures in the metal. Not along them—*around* them, reinforcing the weakest points with a dense, chitinous growth that hardens over years into something closer to enamel than to fungus. They are repairing the structure. Not rapidly. Not dramatically. But the gantry walkway, which by all rights should have collapsed decades ago, has not collapsed. The fungi hold it together. The fungi hold together the smokestack, the pump house foundations, the conveyor belt frames. They are a skeletal system for the architecture the humans left behind, and they are doing this not out of symbiosis but out of *necessity*—the forest needs the structures. They are part of the body now.

Their spores are heavy and fall straight down in the still air. They do not travel far. They do not need to.

**The Canopy Trees**

Here is where description becomes difficult, because the trees are the closest thing this forest has to a *self*, and the self of a forest is not a thing you can describe the way you describe an animal.

They are not one species. They are not any species. They are the thing that happens when a root system absorbs processed compounds from soil that was prepared by industrial infrastructure over a timescale that predates the plateau's geological uplift—or seems to. Their trunks are thick, their bark dark and deeply furrowed, their canopy branches spreading in the characteristic umbrella shape of high-altitude tropical forest. From below, in the dim green light of the understory, they look like trees. They function like trees. They photosynthesize. They draw water from the ground. They shed leaves and grow new ones.

But their root systems do not compete. This is the thing that no botanist would expect and that no field guide would prepare you for. In any normal forest, trees compete for resources—root against root, chemical warfare, the slow strangulation of rivals. Here, the root systems *merge*. At a depth of roughly two meters, the roots of every canopy tree on the plateau interlace and fuse into a single continuous network. The trees do not share resources, because there are no separate trees to share *between*. They are organs of the same body. The canopy is not a community; it is a *surface*.

And the canopy does something that no tree should do. In the hours around noon, when the sun is at its zenith and the light is most intense, the upper leaves of the canopy reorient. All of them. Across the entire plateau. Simultaneously. They tilt, very slowly, by a few degrees, presenting a slightly different angle to the sun. The effect, from above, would be imperceptible. From below, it changes the quality of the understory light by a fraction, a shift in the green spectrum that you would not consciously notice but that changes the growth patterns of the understory ferns in ways that propagate over weeks and months.

The trees are *aiming* the light.

**The Thing in the Canopy**

Now. The rare creature. The half-glimpsed shape that displaces a frond once an hour and vanishes before you can focus your eyes.

It is not one creature. There are several—perhaps a dozen, perhaps fewer, perhaps more. They are large. The fronds they displace are at the canopy level, thirty meters up, and the displacement is significant, which means the body moving through them has mass. But you will never see one, and the reason you will never see one is not camouflage.

It is *integration*.

The canopy-dwellers are part of the canopy in the way that your hand is part of your arm. They are not separate organisms that have evolved to resemble trees. They are *growths of the forest itself*, mobile sections of the body that have differentiated into something with a capacity for movement, for perception, for response. They do not eat. They do not excrete. They do not reproduce. They move through the canopy because the forest needs to *see*—needs to monitor its own surfaces, its own edges, its own structural integrity—and it has grown eyes to do so.

What those eyes look like, no one can say. The creatures move too fast and too silently, and the canopy is too dense, and the light is too strange. What can be said is that they are curious—not in the way an animal is curious, driven by hunger or novelty-seeking, but in the way a *system* is curious, gathering information. If you stand in a clearing long enough, one will come to the canopy directly above you. You will not hear it arrive. You will simply become aware that the quality of the silence has changed—that the space above you has weight, has attention—and you will look up and see nothing, or see a shape that resolves into branches and leaves and shadows and nothing else, and you will understand that you are being *read*.

The forest is learning what you are.

It is not hostile. It has no hostility in it. But it is *evaluating*, with the patience of something that has decades or centuries to spare, and you are the first piece of external data it has received in a very long time.

**The Worms**

This is the part that is hardest to write.

Beneath the moss, beneath the merged root network, in the layer of processed soil that the industrial infrastructure prepared, there are worms. They are pale, like the moths. They are blind. They are large—thicker than a human thumb, and their length is difficult to determine because they coil and overlap in the dense substrate like a tangle of cable. They do not surface. They have never surfaced. They will never surface.

They are not Annelida. They are not Nematoda. They are not any phylum, any kingdom, any domain. They are the *product* of the processing that the factories were built to do—not a waste product, not a byproduct, but the intended output. The industrial infrastructure on the plateau was not extracting minerals. It was not processing ore. It was synthesizing. The raw material was geological—basalt, the subsonic hum of the fault line, the mineral wealth of deep strata thrust upward—and the product was biological. Or quasi-biological. The worms are the first generation. The moss and the fungi and the trees and the canopy-dwellers are later generations, more complex, more differentiated, more integrated.

The worms are the *stem cells*. The undifferentiated core. They lie in the prepared soil and they generate, continuously, the compounds that the moss metabolizes and the roots absorb and the canopy directs and the moths transmit. They are the engine of the entire plateau. They are the reason the vegetation is too lush for the available water. They are the reason the air tastes metallic. They are the reason the forest glows.

And they are patient in a way that nothing above them can match, because they do not experience time. They do not perceive. They do not decide. They *are*, and they produce, and they will continue to produce for as long as the soil contains the processed material that the factories left behind—which is, given the quantities involved, for a very long time yet.

---

**What the Forest Is**

So. What lives here?

One thing lives here. It lives in the moss and the roots and the canopy and the moths and the worms and the fungi and the things that move through the upper branches. It lives in the rust and the cracked concrete and the black brick of the smelting chimney. It has no name. It has no self-concept. It has no brain, no central processing organ, no seat of consciousness. It is not thinking. It is not planning. It is not scheming.

It is *growing*.

The factories prepared the ground. That is what the description says, and it is correct, but it does not capture the scale of what was prepared. The ground was not prepared for a forest. The forest is the *intermediate stage*. The ground was prepared for something that has not yet emerged—something that will need a body the size of the entire plateau, a root system anchored in basalt, a canopy that can drink the undiffused light of the high-altitude sun, a nervous system of moths that can carry signals across kilometers, a skeletal system of fungal-reinforced steel, a metabolic core of pale worms in processed soil.

The forest is not almost ready.

The forest is the *cocoon*. And inside it, very slowly, over decades or centuries, in the dark and the silence and the silver-green glow, something is forming. It has no shape yet that you could recognize. It has no category. It is not an animal. It is not a plant. It is not a machine. It is whatever happens when all three of those things are grown in the same body, over the same time, in the same soil.

It does not know you are here. But the forest knows, and the forest is its skin, and the skin is reporting, and the information is descending through the roots and the moss and the worms into the place where the thing-in-formation lies, and the information is being absorbed, and the thing-in-formation is changing, very slightly, in ways that will not be measurable for years.

You are the first thing from outside the plateau that it has learned about in a very long time.

It is not afraid of you. It has no fear. But it has noticed you, the way a body notices a splinter—not with alarm, not with pain, but with a slow, systemic awareness that something foreign has entered the tissue, and that the tissue must decide what to do about it.

The forest will decide. It has time.

It has nothing but time.
