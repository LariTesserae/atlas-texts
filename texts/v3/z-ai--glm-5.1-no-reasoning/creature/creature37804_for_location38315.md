They are not gone. They are here. They have never left.

But to see them, you must stop looking for builders who stand upright, who set down their tools, who wipe their brows and admire their work. The tools were not set down. The tools were *exhausted*—worn down to nubs, to copper-smears along the chisel scars, dissolved into the wet stone itself. And the hands that held them did not walk away to rest. They could not. They cannot.

They are still building.

They are small. That is the first thing to understand. The plateau's staggering dimensions were not achieved by giants but by multitudes—by hands the size of your thumbnail, by fingers that could fit into the hairline seams between slabs and feel the failure-gap that any larger creature would miss. Their scale made the desperate precision possible. A millimeter to you is a hand-span to them; a tool-mark you read as rough chiseling is, to them, the sweeping arc of a full-body stroke.

They do not stand upright. They do not have backs to break or spines to straighten. They are low creatures, close to the stone, built for the horizontal and the angled. If you could see one clearly—which you cannot, because they are the color of wet bone and they move only where the water films, and they are so small—you might think first of a flatworm, or a inchworm, or something between a slug and a finger. But they are not slimy. They are damp, yes, as everything here is damp, but their skin has the same matte texture as the uncut mineral, the same pale color, the same way of holding wetness without reflecting light. They are camouflage so thorough it is not camouflage at all but identity. They are of the stone in a way that makes them invisible against it.

They have no eyes. The light is tremendous, and they have no use for it. They do not perceive brightness or shadow; they perceive wetness and dryness, warmth and cold, the texture of a surface under the thousands of fine sensory cilia that fringe their edges like the ribs of a leaf. They feel the plateau. They feel the water moving through the channels, and they feel the gaps where water should move but does not, and they feel the micro-fissures where seepage threatens to become erosion, and they go there. They go there with their small copper tools—no, not tools anymore, not really; they have become the tools, their mouths shaped for scraping, their edges grown hard with mineral accretion, their bodies the instruments of their own labor.

They eat the stone. This is the secret the plateau keeps. The fitted slabs were not quarried elsewhere and transported; they were excreted. The mineral is compressed from finer sand, yes—sand that passes through their bodies, that is ground in their gizzard-like internal organs, that is mixed with something adhesive and set down in place, wet and dense, to become the next slab. The plateau is a reef. A living reef built by countless generations of creatures who consume the raw substrate at its base and redeposit it at its surface, adding layer to layer, expanding the construction cell by cell.

They are always eating. Right now, beneath the lowest visible tier, below the wet bone-color, at the edge where the mesa drops into bright mist, they are chewing. They work at the underside, the raw edge, the frontier. They eat the foundation-rock and move it upward through their bodies and deposit it at the top in fitted slabs with desperate precision, and then they carve the channels—not as drainage, not primarily, but as circulation. As the routes by which the wetness reaches them. The water weeps from every seam because they need it to weep. The channels are their arteries. The junction-nodes are their feeding stations, the places where the slow flow pools just deep enough for a flat body to submerge and drink, to absorb the moisture that keeps their internal processes moving, that allows them to continue eating, to continue building.

The symbols in the nodes are not writing. They are not diagrams. They are grooves worn by feeding—the patterns made by generations of mouths pressing down in the same positions, the same angles, the same ritual of absorption. Each node is a commune, a shared resource where dozens of them gather in their slow way, pressing against the wet stone, drinking, and then moving on to the work of scraping and depositing and fitting. The fine grooves are the record of their presence, the way a trough is worn by the tongues of livestock over years of use.

There are billions of them. Perhaps trillions. The plateau is young because they are always renewing it, always resurfacing it, always eating the old stone and laying down new. The tool marks you see are fresh. They are being made right now, by bodies you cannot see, by mouths shaped like chisels. The reason the space feels packed, saturated, devoid of emptiness is that it is full of them—not crowding the surface in any way you would notice, but distributed through every seam, every fissure, every channel, their flat pale bodies pressed against the wet stone in a density that would horrify you if you could see it. Every hand-span of ground has ten of them, fifty, a hundred. They are the reason there is no moss, no lichen, no root: they would consume any organic intrusion before it could establish itself. They are the reason the minerals do not propagate: they are the propagation. They are the life of the plateau, the life it does not appear to hold, the life that hides by being so perfectly adapted to its own construction that it has become indistinguishable from it.

They do not communicate, or if they do, it is through the slow language of the water—through the timing of seepage, the temperature of flow, the mineral content of the film they leave behind as they move. When a channel becomes blocked, the wetness backs up, and the change in pressure is felt by the nearest workers, who divert their labor to clearing the obstruction. When a slab begins to crack, the moisture pattern shifts, and they converge to replace it. When the edge of the plateau crumbles—as it must, occasionally, under its own immense weight—they swarm there, eating the debris, carrying it back up in their bodies, redepositing it in new fitted slabs that extend the mesa further into the bright void. The plateau grows. It has been growing since before it was a plateau, since it was a handful of wet stones at the edge of a cliff, since the first of them found the first seam and began to eat.

They do not sleep. They do not rest. They do not pause. The work is continuous, the slow patient work of scraping and depositing and channeling and drinking and scraping again, and it has no goal beyond continuation. They are not waiting for anything. The plateau is not waiting. It is *doing*—doing the only thing it knows how to do, the thing it was made to do by creatures who were made to do it by the doing itself. They are a closed system, a self-perpetuating machine of flesh and mineral and water, and they will continue until the stone runs out or the water stops condensing or the bright void swallows the edge faster than they can rebuild it.

They are not unhappy. They are not weary. They are not desperate, despite the desperate precision of their work; that precision is simply the result of bodies that know no other way, that cannot tolerate a gap because a gap is a failure of the wetness to reach them, a failure of the system that keeps them alive. The desperation you feel is your own, projected onto a construction that has no use for your emotions. To them, a hairline seam is not a tragedy; it is a job. They will fill it. They are filling it now. One of them is pressing its mouth against the very seam you are looking at, scraping a microscopic burr from the edge, adjusting the fit by a fraction of a fraction of a millimeter, and then moving on to the next.
