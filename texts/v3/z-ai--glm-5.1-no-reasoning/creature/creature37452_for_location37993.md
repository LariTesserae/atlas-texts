They come when the light angles a certain way.

Not at dawn—dawn is too sharp, the shaft too narrow, the chord too thin and reedy, weighted toward the high terraces where the first sun strikes. They prefer the late morning, when the column has widened to cover the central platform and the bass-note breathing of the walls syncopates with the lower plant tones to produce what they call the Root Sound. They have a word for it. Their language has words for seventeen distinct chord-states, each corresponding to a time of day, a season, a condition of the walls. The same word also means *safe*. The same word also means *home*.

---

There are nine of them. Or there were nine, last count. Counting is difficult because they do not stay.

They are small. Not stunted—*calibrated*. Generations of subsistence on what the chamber provides has drawn them inward, compacted them, made them efficient in the way that island fauna become efficient. The tallest reaches your collarbone. Their skin is the color of the pale stone, which is the color of bone, which is the color of the sky at midday when the heat erases all hue. They wear nothing woven. Why would they? The chamber is warm as skin. Above, the salt will kill exposed flesh in hours, and they do not linger above. When they travel the surface—between chambers, for there are others, you did not find the others—they wrap themselves in the pressed fiber of the reedy plants, which insulates and also, when dampened with the viscous plant-fluid, produces a chemical barrier against the desiccation. They move across the salt like ghosts. You would not see them if they did not wish you to.

Their feet are broad and calloused. The soles are leather from a lifetime of the carved steps. Their toes splay wide, gripping the stone, and they descend the fissure not by sight—they do not see well in the way you see well, their eyes adapted to the diffuse glow of the chamber, not the blazing surface—but by feel. By sound. They know the chord's architecture the way you know your own house in the dark. They could find this place deaf and blind, one hand on the warming stone.

---

The oldest is named something you cannot pronounce. The consonant is a click produced by placing the tongue behind the upper teeth and drawing breath inward sharply. It sounds like one of the plant tones—a mid-register note, the one that corresponds to the seventh terrace from the floor, where a cluster of fern-things produces a sustained A-flat. She is old. Perhaps sixty years, perhaps more. Time is measured differently here; the chord is the clock, and the chord does not lie. She was born during a period the younger ones call the Great Thinning, when a section of the living wall sloughed its outer layer and the bass pulse weakened for three seasons. She remembers the sound of the chamber when it was wounded. She says it was like a throat trying to swallow. She says the plants on the wounded wall screamed for a full cycle of the sun before they accepted the new contour and re-tuned.

She is the one who decides when a plant is removed.

This is the harvest, and it is sacred in the way that only survival can be sacred. The plants in the alcoves are not decorative. They are not even primarily musical, though the music is their function and their function is their music and the people do not separate these things. The plants produce the viscous fluid you touched—the non-aqueous solvent that fills their stems and leaves and roots. The fluid is their blood. It is also the people's water, their medicine, their food, and their means of entering the chord.

The oldest walks the terraces each morning. She touches each plant—each one, thousands of them, a circuit that takes hours—and feels its vibration. She knows by touch whether a plant is healthy, whether its tone is true, whether it needs pruning or feeding or removal. When a plant is ready, she removes it. She cuts the stem with a chert blade—quick, practiced, the way you might cut a ripe fruit—and catches the fluid that drains in a ceramic vessel. The plant is then laid on the moss at the base of the wall, where the living stone will slowly absorb it, metabolize it, convert it back to the mineral gas that feeds the next generation. Nothing is wasted. Nothing has ever been wasted.

The fluid, once collected, is portioned. Some is drunk immediately—it is thick, slightly sweet, mineral-rich, and it coats the throat in a way that protects against the dry surface air. Some is fermented in the ceramic vessels, a process the people do not oversee but simply wait for; the chamber itself, the gas, the warmth, does the work. The fermented fluid is thick and mildly psychoactive. It does not make them hallucinate. It makes them *resonate*. After drinking it, they report—and you would not have believed this, but you felt it yourself, standing in the light column—that they can feel the chord in their bones. Not as sound. As structure. As if the music were a building and they were walking through its rooms.

This is how they navigate the chord. This is how they compose.

---

The younger ones tend the terraces.

There are three whose work is the green. A young man, barely twenty, with arms scarred from handling the sharper plants—the succulent ones have thorns, not for defense, for *tuning*; the thorns vibrate at specific frequencies and alter the resonance of the stem. A woman his age, quieter, who specializes in the moss, who knows every patch by texture and color, who can tell by the glow whether the light-shaft is occluded before she looks up. And a child—ten years old, perhaps—who is learning the terraces by crawling along them, who cannot yet reach the higher alcoves but who studies the lower ones with an intensity that the oldest watches and approves.

The child is being taught the tones. Not their names—the names are feelings, not words, and the child is learning to feel. When the oldest removes a plant, the child is the one who places the new cutting in the alcove. The cutting comes from the parent plant, which has been allowed to send runners along the terrace walls; the child selects the runner, severs it, sets it in the prepared niche, and then waits. The new plant takes days to root, weeks to produce its first tone. The child visits it hourly, adjusting the soil—a powder made from ground pale stone and dried moss—whispering to it, though the whisper is inaudible beneath the chord and is not for the plant. It is for the child. It is a practice of attention. Of devotion.

The younger ones do not remember the Great Thinning. They know the chamber only as it is: full, roaring, alive. They trust it the way you trusted the sun when you were small—completely, without reservation. The oldest worries about this. She knows the walls can sicken. She knows the chord can break. She is teaching them to listen for the cracks, the micro-shifts, the moments when a single plant's tone wavers and the whole structure tilts toward something wrong.

She is teaching them because she will not always be here.

---

Two of the nine never enter the chamber.

They live above, in the salt, in shelters built from basalt slabs and sealed with the plant-fiber wrap. They are the watchers. Their eyes are adapted to the surface glare, their skin darker, tougher, and they spend their days monitoring the sky and the ground. They track the sun's path for the light-shaft. They track the weather—which is always dry, always empty, but sometimes, once a decade, not. They track the other chambers.

You did not find the other chambers. The watchers know where they are. There are seven, spread across the plateau, each with its own fissure, its own living walls, its own chord. The chords are different. Each chamber sings a different song, determined by the shape of the room, the species of the plants, the breathing cycle of the walls. The watchers know all seven songs. They travel between them on paths marked by stones so small you would not see them, carrying cuttings and fluid and information. The chambers are not independent. They are a network. A body, with seven organs, each producing its own sound, and the watchers are the nerve that connects them.

They also watch for people like you.

They saw you coming. They let you pass. This was a decision, made by the oldest after consultation with the watchers, and you will never know why they made it. Perhaps the chord told them. Perhaps the light. Perhaps they simply wanted to see what you would do when you stood in the music. Whether you would try to take something. Whether you would try to break something. Whether you would understand.

You understood enough to leave. That was the test, and you passed it.

---

The remaining three are the ones you did not see.

They were in the chamber when you arrived. Not on the floor, not among the terraces—*in* the walls. The living stone, the pale concretion that breathes, is not merely a wall. It is a medium. The people who first built this chamber—ancestors so distant that even the oldest does not know their number—discovered that the breathing stone could receive impressions. That sound, sustained long enough and loud enough, could be *recorded* in the mineral layers as they were laid down. The walls are archives. Every tone the chamber has produced since the first plant sang is embedded in the stone, in the slow accretion of calcium and gas and time.

The three are the readers. They press their bodies against the wall—torso, cheek, palms flat—and they listen. Not to the present chord, which is deafening, but to the past. The stone plays back. Slowly. A single cycle of the wall's breath might contain ten thousand years of music, compressed into a mineral memory that takes a lifetime to hear.

The readers do not speak. They have not spoken in generations. Their vocal cords, the oldest explains without words—she draws in the stone dust on the floor, a diagram you almost understand—have atrophied. They do not need them. They communicate through touch, through the stone, through the vibrations the wall carries from body to body. They live in the walls. They sleep in shallow depressions worn into the breathing stone by centuries of contact, and when they dream, they dream the chamber's ancient songs.

They know what the chamber sang before there were plants. Before there were terraces. When it was only the living stone and the mineral gas and a sound so low and so deep that it was not heard but *felt*, a pulse in the earth, the heartbeat of something so large and so slow that its existence could only be inferred from the vibrations it left in the rock.

They know what the chamber will sing when the last plant is gone and the walls have sealed themselves and the chord resolves into silence. They have heard it. It is the same sound.

---

You asked—did you ask? You stood in the light, and the question was in your posture, in the angle of your head, and the oldest saw it and answered—*who are they?*

They do not have a name for themselves. Names are sound, and sound is the chamber's domain, and to name themselves would be to compete with the chord, which is not their way. When the oldest must refer to her people, she touches the floor—the living floor, the moss, the stone beneath—and then touches her chest. *The ones who tend.* That is the closest translation. *The ones who keep the song going.*

They are not ancient. They are not mystical. They are gardeners. They are tuners. They are the staff of an instrument so large it takes seven chambers to contain it, and they have been at work for longer than any oral history can recall, and they will continue until the salt above gives way or the walls stop breathing or the last cutting fails to root.

The child is learning. The oldest is teaching. The readers are remembering. The watchers are scanning the horizon. The tenders are pruning. The chamber is singing.

And you are climbing out, back into the silent world, carrying a sound you did not ask for and cannot forget.

They will not follow you. They have no reason to leave. But the chord will follow—the memory of it, the way it pressed against your sternum, the way the moss pulsed beneath your feet, the way the light shifted and the whole vast structure *mourned* for ten seconds before resolving.

You will hear it in quiet rooms. You will feel it in your sleep. You will stand in sunlight and wait for it to dim, and when it does, you will listen for the change in the air, the shift in the register, the moment when the world goes minor.

You have been inside the instrument. You have heard what it sounds like to be *kept*.

They are still there. They are tending. They are listening. The chamber is singing.
