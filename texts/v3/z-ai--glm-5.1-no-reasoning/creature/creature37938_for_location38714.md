# What Lives in the Weeping Terraces

---

## The People

They have become something specific. Not a separate species — nothing so dramatic — but a population shaped so long by pressure and wet and noise that they read, to a visitor, as a variant of human. Their posture gives it away first. The shoulders ride high, not from tension exactly, but from a constant low-grade brace against the vibration that comes up through the boots and into the long bones. The neck thickens. The jaw sets forward, slightly, giving even the gentlest of them an aspect of grim endurance. They carry their heads tilted — down and to the side, an unconscious orientation toward the nearest water-channel, because the sound is information and the information is survival and you cannot stop listening even when you cannot stop hearing.

Their skin has the pallor you'd expect from a life lived in mist and twilight. But it is a particular pallor — not the soft, even fairness of northern climates but something greyer, more mineral, as if the dissolved compounds in the water have been depositing themselves in the pores for generations. The complexions have a faint opalescence in certain lights, a sheen like the wet stone around them. Older residents develop a darkening at the knuckles, the nail beds, the skin around the eyes — a staining that looks almost like bruising but is just the deep accumulation of the same minerals that stripe the canyon walls. They call it *water-marking*. It is neither feared nor celebrated. It is simply what happens.

Their hands are remarkable. The ledge-way shuffle demands a certain footwork, but the real adaptation is manual. They grip constantly — iron railings, stone ledges, rope lines, each other's forearms. The grip is wide-fingered and inward-rolled, a clutching hand-shape that looks slightly clawed to outsiders but that can hold a slick iron bar indefinitely, that can feel the difference between a surface that will bear weight and a surface that will shear away under it. The fingertips broaden and flatten over years. The calluses run across the palm in transverse ridges rather than the usual spots. They can pick up a wet needle from a wet stone. They can tie knots in saturated rope without looking.

Their voices carry the signature of the place. You hear them before you understand them — a shout that cuts through the roar not by volume alone but by pitch, a trained placement of sound in the higher registers where the water-roar has natural gaps. They speak in a clipped, consonant-heavy dialect that wastes nothing on vowels. Words compress. Syllables drop. A sentence that would take eight syllables elsewhere takes four here, pushed out in a burst that conserves breath, because breath is a resource you manage carefully at this altitude, in this humidity, where the air is thick with water and thin in oxygen and every lungful costs slightly more effort than it should. They laugh loud and short. They argue constantly and without heat — argument is social glue, a way of maintaining connection in a place where the noise isolates even when you stand shoulder to shoulder.

They do not whisper. Whispering is useless. They have, however, developed an extensive sign language — not formal, not codified, but a shared vocabulary of gesture that operates alongside speech and often replaces it. These signs are large, visible from across a ledge-way, readable through mist. Some are obvious: the flat palm pushed downward means *the water's rising*. The two fingers flicked sideways mean *the press is cycling*. Others are opaque to outsiders: the closed fist pressed to the sternum, the index finger drawn slowly across the collarbone. The gestures have the quality of a vocabulary that evolved for emergencies and then migrated into ordinary use, until even lovers sitting in a dry hall will sign to each other across a table rather than raise their voices again.

They sleep in the deep hours, when the factories cycle down — not off, never off, but into their lower rhythms, the presses slowing, the steam vents spacing further apart. Even then the water roars. They sleep in shifts, in the stone halls with their thick walls, on beds raised from the floor on iron frames to avoid the damp that creeps up through every surface. They sleep lightly. They wake alert. They do not lie in bed. The concept of lingering drowsiness, of the slow sweet surfacing from sleep, is culturally absent — you are asleep or you are awake, and the transition is instantaneous, a snap, like a door closing.

They dream, they say, of silence. Not often. But when they do, the silence is so strange and so total in the dream that they wake unsettled, sometimes weeping, sometimes convinced something has gone wrong — a pump failure, a blocked channel, a catastrophic silence — and they lie there with their hand on the wall feeling for the vibration, and when it comes, the familiar tremor of the water moving through the mesa's veins, they settle. They are more frightened by the absence of sound than by the sound itself. This is the thing visitors cannot understand. The noise is not suffering to them. The noise is proof that the city is alive.

## The Children

Children here learn to walk twice. The first walking is the ordinary one — upright, toddling, the usual human milestone. The second is the ledge-walk, the deliberate shuffle, and it begins almost as early. A child who has not mastered the shuffle does not go on the ledge-ways. They are carried, or they stay in the dry halls. The transition is a rite of passage, unformalized but absolute: the day a child walks alone on a slick iron grate above a roaring cut, gripping the railing with those broad-fingered hands, is the day they become part of the city.

They are loud children. They have to be. They learn early to project, to pitch their voices above the water, to make themselves heard. They play in the dry halls — running games, chasing games, games that involve shouting and stamping, all the noise they can make, because noise is the medium of existence here and a quiet child is an invisible child and an invisible child is in danger. They play less on the ledge-ways, but when they do, their games are spatial and vertical: climbing, traversing, balancing. They know the architecture of the city — the handholds, the safe routes, the places where the stone has softened and the places where it hasn't — with an intimacy that adults have partly lost. Children are the cartographers of the Weeping Terraces. They know every shortcut, every crossing, every ledge-wide enough for small feet and every gap too far even for a running jump.

They are not afraid of the water. This is a source of anxiety for visiting parents from elsewhere, if such visitors ever come. The children of the Terraces lean over the railings and look down into the cuts with interest, not fear. They throw things in — stones, bits of brick, their toys — and watch the water take them. They know the water is dangerous, but the knowledge is abstract, geological, the way they know the cliff at the mesa's edge is a drop to the basin. The danger is so constant and so total that it becomes a condition of existence rather than a threat. You do not fear the air you breathe. They do not fear the water they hear.

What they do fear: the deep places. The shafts. The boreholes with their iron ladders descending into warm dark. Every child here knows the stories — not all of them consistent, not all of them plausible, but consistent in their essential shape. Something is down there. Something moved in when the people moved up. The children don't name it. They don't describe it. They just know that you don't go down, and that if you hear something from below that isn't water, you tell someone, and you leave the area, and you don't look back.

Whether this is real — whether anything actually lives in the deep shafts and the flooded lower levels — is a question the adults treat with careful ambiguity. Some dismiss it. Some maintain it. None of them go down either, except the maintenance workers, who go armed with lights and rope and the practiced fatalism of people who accept that their job involves a risk they cannot quantify.

## The Maintenance Workers

There is a caste — though they would not use that word — whose labor is the city's holding-action against entropy. They are the ones who replace the corroded iron, who repoint the mortar, who clear the channels of debris, who shim the buttresses and bolt new cladding plates over the soft chalk where the water has undercut too far. They are not numerous. A few hundred, perhaps, in a city that once held thousands. But they are the mechanism by which the Weeping Terraces continue to exist.

They are built for the work. The adaptation is most visible in them: the broad hands, the high shoulders, the permanent squint from years of working in mist and spray. They are stronger than they look — not bulky, but dense, the kind of strength that comes from hauling wet stone and iron in a high-altitude, low-oxygen environment for decades. Their lungs are compromised. The warm mineral mist they breathe all day, every day, leaves deposits in the bronchioles that hardened into calcified nodules over years. The older ones have a cough — dry, rattling, productive of nothing, because there is nothing to bring up. The damage is done. It is inside the tissue. They call it *stone-lung*, and they regard it the way they regard the erosion of the canyon walls: as a process that is patient and constant and that you hold back as long as you can and then you don't, and that is all.

They work in crews of three to five, moving through the city on a maintenance cycle that has been refined over generations. They know which sections are critical — which buttress, if it fails, will take a factory floor with it. They know which channels are close to breakthrough, where the water has nearly carved a new path through the soft rock and will emerge, one day soon, in a place no one expected. They know the sounds. A maintenance worker can stand on a ledge-way, close their eyes, and tell you which pump is running rough, which press needs shimming, which section of channel has debris building. They read the city the way a doctor reads a body — by symptoms, by deviation from the baseline, by the small changes that announce larger failures to come.

They go into the deep places. Not often, and not willingly, but the boreholes need inspection, the lower pump stations need servicing, and the flooded sections need assessment. They go in pairs, never alone, with rope and lamps and a signal-code of tugs on the line. They do not linger. They do not explore. They go down, they do what needs doing, and they come back up, and if you ask them what they saw down there they will say *water* and *rock* and *the usual*, and they will not meet your eyes, and they will change the subject.

One of them, once — an older woman, near retirement, stone-lung advanced, hands so calloused she could grip a fish — said something to a visitor. She said: *The water down there isn't waiting. It's looking.* She meant it geologically, she said. She meant the water is always seeking new paths, new fractures, new ways out. That's all she meant. She said this twice, as if repetition could make it true, and then she went back to work.

## The Geologists

There is a remnant. An academic tradition, nearly extinct, maintained by a handful of people who work out of a stone hall on the central ridge that was once a research institute and is now something between a library and a relic. They are the ones who map the aquifer, who track the flow rates, who measure the water's chemistry and the rock's composition and the slow, slow decline of the fossil water that is the city's only resource.

They are a strange breed — quieter than the other residents, more inward, given to long periods of silence that would be impossible elsewhere but that here constitute a kind of luxury, because the stone hall is thick-walled and small-windowed and the roar of the water is muted to a bearable murmur. They speak softly when they speak, and their voices have a quality that other residents find unsettling — too gentle, too considered, as if they are used to being heard without shouting.

They know the water is finite. They have known this for generations. The aquifer is draining. Not fast — on a timescale of centuries, maybe longer — but measurably, verifiably, the flow rates are declining. The warm mineral water that seeps from every seam is the discharge of a reservoir that took millennia to fill and that is emptying, slowly, into the cuts and the channels and the long curtains of mist that fall from the mesa's edge. They have models. They have projections. They have, buried in a drawer in the stone hall, a graph that shows the city's water supply declining to zero in a date that is comfortably distant — four, five hundred years — but that is not infinite.

They do not share this widely. Not out of secrecy, but because it doesn't change anything. The people of the Weeping Terraces know, in a general sense, that the water will not last forever. They know the mesa is finite. They know the city is in slow decline. But the decline is so slow, the timescale so far beyond a human life, that it functions as background — like the erosion of a mountain, like the cooling of the sun. You know it. You don't feel it. You go to work. You clear the channels. You paint the iron. The water flows. The water flows. The water flows.

The geologists also map the deep places. Their maps are incomplete — the lower levels are partly flooded, partly collapsed, partly inaccessible — but they are the closest thing the city has to a record of what lies beneath. The maps show the borehole network, the pump stations, the known cavities where the aquifer has created voids by dissolving the softer rock. The maps also show, in red ink, in the margins, in a handwriting that gets smaller and more cramped as it goes deeper, notations that are not explained. *Sounds — irregular. Not water.* *Temperature anomaly — +4° above gradient.* *Wall surface — scored. Not tool-marks.* These notations are old. The geologists who made them are dead. The current geologists look at them sometimes and do not know what to make of them and put them back in the drawer.

## The Birds

They are not pigeons. They look like pigeons — roughly pigeon-shaped, pigeon-sized, pigeon-colored in the dull grey-brown of urban birds everywhere — but they are not pigeons. Their feathers are different: denser, overlapped more tightly, with a natural oil that gives them the slick, wet sheen that the description notes. This is not pigeon preen-oil. It is heavier, more persistent, and it has a faint mineral smell — the same sulfurous closed-in scent as the water itself. The birds have, over generations, incorporated something of the place into their biology.

They nest in the upper ledges of the canyon walls, in the gaps between brick courses and stone blocks where the mortar has softened enough to hollow out. They build no nests — or rather, the nest is the cavity itself, lined with nothing, because there is nothing to line it with. No grass. No twigs. No soft materials. The young hatch on bare wet stone and survive or don't, and the survival rate is correspondingly low, which is offset by a correspondingly high rate of reproduction. The birds breed year-round. There is no season here. There is only the constant wet and the constant roar, and the birds have no reason to time their breeding to anything.

Their calls are extraordinary. They have abandoned the coo of the pigeon entirely — it cannot compete with the water. Instead, they have a sharp, high, percussive call that sounds almost mechanical, like a stone striking stone, like a bolt being driven. It cuts through the roar cleanly, in a frequency band that the water doesn't fill. They use it for territory, for mating, for warning. They have a second call — lower, more sustained, almost a trill — that appears to be social, used between birds who are already in proximity, a contact-call, a confirmation of presence. You hear them constantly in the upper terraces. You almost never see them clearly. They are shapes in the mist, flickering between ledges, their oil-slick feathers catching what light there is and throwing it back in brief iridescent flashes.

They eat what the water carries. Organic matter in the channels — the detritus of the city, the waste that washes through the cuts. They perch on the channel edges and pick at the surface. They also eat the dark filamentous algae that grows in the fast water, plucking it from the channel floors with a quick dipping motion that requires precise timing against the current. They are, in a sense, the only animals that have fully adapted to the Terraces' ecology. They belong here in a way that even the people don't quite — the people maintain the city, but the birds just live in it, perfectly fitted to its conditions, requiring nothing that it does not provide.

There is something else about them. They avoid the deep shafts. This is not surprising — most animals avoid confined spaces — but the consistency of their avoidance is notable. A bird will perch on a borehole's lip, on the iron grating that covers it, but will not enter. If the grating is removed, the bird will not go down. It will stand at the edge, peer over, and then leave. Quickly. This has been observed enough times to be a pattern rather than an anecdote, and the maintenance workers, who noticed it before the geologists did, use it as an informal gauge: if the birds won't go near a shaft, neither do they.

## The Rats

They live in the lower channels, in the debris that accumulates there — the collapsed stone, the rusted machinery, the organic waste that the water carries down through the cuts. They are large. The warm water and the constant food supply and the absence of predators have allowed them to grow beyond the usual rat dimensions, and the largest of them are the size of small cats, heavy-bodied, slow-moving, their fur matted and dark with the mineral water.

They are not the disease-carriers that rats are elsewhere. The water's mineral content — the same compounds that stain the residents' skin and calcify the workers' lungs — appears to inhibit the pathogens that rats typically harbor. They are, in their way, clean. Their teeth are stained dark. Their eyes are clear. They swim the channels with a practiced ease, riding the current where it's manageable, hauling themselves out on debris-piles where it isn't.

The residents tolerate them. The rats serve a function — they consume organic waste that would otherwise clog the channels, and their presence is a rough indicator of water quality. If the rats leave a section of channel, the residents know something has changed: the water's chemistry has shifted, or the temperature has spiked, or something has entered the system from below that the rats don't like. The rats are canaries, in a sense. The residents watch them.

There are stories about the rats in the deep places. Larger ones. The ones that went down the shafts and stayed. The ones that had generations in the warm dark and grew to a size that the upper rats don't reach. The maintenance workers report sounds in the deep shafts — scraping, movement, the click of claws on wet rock — that they attribute to rats, and this is probably correct, and this is the explanation they prefer, and they do not investigate further.

## What Lives Below

This requires care.

There is no monster. There is no creature. There is nothing in the deep places that a visiting naturalist could identify, classify, name. The city is not haunted in any sense that the word conventionally carries.

But.

The deep shafts are not empty. The geologists' maps acknowledge this in their cramped red notations. The maintenance workers' avoidance acknowledges it in their careful protocols. The birds' refusal to enter acknowledges it in instinct. The children's stories acknowledge it in their urgent, formless certainty. Something is down there. Or some things. Or some *condition* — some presence or process or phenomenon that occupies the flooded lower levels of the Weeping Terraces and that the residents have, over generations, decided to coexist with by not engaging.

The water at depth does things that the upper water doesn't. It is hotter — sometimes significantly hotter than the geothermal gradient predicts. It moves in patterns that don't correlate with the known aquifer dynamics. It carries compounds that the upper water doesn't — dissolved minerals in unfamiliar combinations, organic molecules in trace quantities that the geologists' instruments can detect but not identify, that resemble, in their structure, the breakdown products of biological material, except that the concentrations are wrong and the ratios are wrong and the source cannot be determined.

The sounds in the deep shafts are not water. The maintenance workers who have been down there — the ones who will talk about it, after, after some time, after a drink — describe a sound that is not the roar of the cuts, not the rush of pressurized water, not the mechanical throb of pumps. They describe a sound that is lower than any of these, lower than hearing, almost — a vibration in the chest, in the abdomen, a pulse that feels biological. Like breathing. Like the breathing of something very large, very slow, drawing air — or water — through a body that is mostly fluid and mostly dark and mostly stone.

This is what they say, or what some of them say, or what the ones who drink say, in the dry halls, in the muted roar, when the shift is over and the lamps are off and the deep places are closed for another night. They say it feels alive down there. Not like an animal alive. Like a geology that has become biological. Like the water and the rock and the heat and the pressure have, over millennia, produced something that is not quite mineral and not quite organic and not quite either and not quite neither. Something that the mesa holds the way a body holds an organ. Something that seeps.

They do not go down. The water still flows. The city still stands. The sound still roars, above, where the people walk the slick ledge-ways with their broad-fingered hands and their mineral-pale skin and their high shoulders and their short loud words. The water comes from below. It has always come from below. They use it. They maintain the channels. They paint the iron. They clear the debris. They do not ask where the water comes from, not anymore, not in any way that requires an answer they can hold in their hands and examine under the thin, clear, mineral light of the upper terraces.

The water flows. The water flows. The water flows. And whatever it flows through, on its way up — whatever it passes through in the warm dark of the deep places, whatever it touches, whatever touches it — is left below, is held below, is kept below, in the parts of the city that the city has decided are no longer the city, in the parts that belong to the mesa, to the water, to the thing that is not quite the mesa and not quite the water and that breathes, slowly, in a frequency below sound, in a place below maps, in a time below the time of people.

They live above it. They have always lived above it. This is enough.
