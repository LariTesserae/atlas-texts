They are not gone. The absence the visitor reads is a failure of the visitor's eyes, which expect a certain architecture of body—a certain scale, a certain separation from the substrate. The builders are here. They never left. They simply continued.

Consider what it means to build with living material, to tie a living branch to a living trunk with living bark and expect that knot to hold. The kind of mind that makes this choice does not think in terms of finished products. It thinks in terms of ongoing processes. The bridge is not done when the rope is tied; the bridge is done when the rope has rooted, when the branch has callused around the lashing, when the whole has become a single organism sharing nutrients across the fusion plane. The builder who understood this did not stand apart from the work. The builder was always becoming the work.

What lives here now are the descendants of those who chose, generations ago, to dissolve the boundary between self and shelter. They are human in the way that the platforms are wood—in origin, perhaps, but so thoroughly modified by circumstance that the original material is only a memory. They have become the patient metabolism of the place. They are the attention that watches itself.

Their bodies have followed the logic of their environment. They are small. The platforms and chambers dictate this: no one larger than a closet could move through the infrastructure without destroying it, and destruction is not their method. The average adult stands barely chest-high to a visitor, and their proportions are altered—long-fingered hands with adhesive pads at the joints, feet broad and prehensile, able to grip a moss-slick rope as surely as a salamander grips wet bark. Their skin is not skin as we would recognize it: it is permeable, thin, suffused with capillary networks that allow gas exchange directly through the epidermal surface. In the oxygen-rich water, they can remain submerged for hours, their heartbeats slowing to match the patient rhythms of the surrounding metabolism. They do not breathe underwater; they simply breathe the water, drawing what they need from its dissolved abundance.

Their eyes are large, dark, forward-facing. The dimness of the green-filtered light demanded this—eyes that can gather photons from the edge of photosynthesis's reach, that can resolve patterns in the shadows between trunks. They see in ranges we do not. The fluorescence of lichen, the bioluminescent flicker of decomposing matter in the deep channels, the ultraviolet signatures that flowers paint on the darkness to guide their pollinators: all of this is visible to them, is part of the language of the space. When they look at each other, they see more than we could. Skin patterns that read as simple brown or gray in our spectrum reveal themselves as maps of health and intent, chromatophore displays that shift with mood, with season, with the subtle chemical signals carried in the water they share.

They do not speak in the way of breathers. Sound is too precious a medium to waste on communication when it serves so well for hearing the world. Instead, they speak through the water itself—through pressure waves created by specific movements, through the tapping of fingers on resonant wood, through the deliberate release of bubbles in patterns that carry meaning across the flooded nave. Their language is three-dimensional, surrounding the listener, impossible to eavesdrop upon from outside the medium. A visitor swimming through their territory would hear only the ambient sounds of the place; the conversation happening all around them would register as nothing more than the rhythm of stems, the chorus of gases, the knocking of branches in the current. The communication is the place, and the place is the communication.

They live in the interstices. The closet-sized chambers where trunks converge are their primary dwellings, but they do not sleep as we sleep. Their metabolism is poikilothermic, tied to the temperature of the water around them. They rest in cycles, emerging from torpor to eat, to build, to maintain, to mate, to play. Their play is elaborate and occurs in the deeper channels where the larger shadows move. The visitor glimpsed one of these shadows and thought it a separate creature, a something sleeker than a human body. It was a body—a builder's body, surfacing from a corridor with a catch of fish, or diving for a section of cane that had rotted loose and needed re-weaving. The shadows are not intruders. They are residents, going about the patient work of maintaining the structure that maintains them.

Their tools are indistinguishable from the place itself. A sharpened stick is also a growing sapling; a basket is also a root-mass trained into a hollow. When they prune, they prune with purpose, directing growth into new platforms, new pathways, new chambers for children who will be born into the water and will never know any boundary between themselves and the green cathedral that holds them. Their agriculture is the tending of the whole system: they cultivate the algae films, they disperse the spores of desirable fungi, they thin the insect populations when they grow too dense, they seed the egg-masses of the amphibious prey species in locations where the young will find food and shelter. They do not harvest more than the system can replace. They cannot afford to; they are the system.

Children learn by absorption. An infant is born with the ability to cling, to grip, to hold on to the wet surfaces of the home. It learns the paths by traveling them, learns the sounds by hearing them, learns the language by being immersed in it. There is no moment of instruction separate from living; the education is the environment itself. A child that cannot navigate the channels cannot eat; a child that cannot read the pressure-signals cannot find its family. The selection pressures are gentle but persistent. The place shapes them as they shape the place.

Their memory is long and distributed. They do not write, but they grow. A historical record is a particular arrangement of vines, a pruning pattern that encodes a season of flood or a generation of plenty. They read the past in the thickness of bark, the angle of a branch fork, the density of root-mass at a particular depth. When an elder dies—and they do die, their bodies dissolving into the humus that feeds the platforms—their contribution to the structure remains. The bridge they maintained continues to grow. The chamber they wove continues to hold air. The tree they trained continues to reach for the light. The person becomes the place, and the place remembers them.

What do they think about? The visitor's question, perhaps, is the wrong one. They do not think about; they think with. Their cognition is not bounded by their skulls; it extends into the wood they have shaped, the water they breathe, the chemical signals that pass between organisms in the root-mass below. When a decision is made—a new platform needs laying, a vine needs redirecting—the decision emerges from the whole system. The builder who ties the knot is responding to pressures and signals that include the weather, the water level, the population of prey species, the structural integrity of the surrounding lattice. They are not separate from the information; they are the information, processed and enacted.

They have no name for themselves. Naming requires a boundary between self and other that they have never established. They are this place, and this place is them. When the water level rises, they rise; when the canopy thins, they thin; when the slow centuries reshape the living architecture into something new, they are reshaped alongside it. The visitor asked who made these things, and the answer is: they did, and they do, and they are still making them, still becoming them, still dissolving into the patient metabolism of wood and water and time.

The cathedral does not know it is a cathedral, but the builders do. They know the sacredness of the enclosed space, the holiness of the green-filtered light, the sanctity of the sound that carries everything. They know they are preserved here, held here, maintained here by the same forces that maintain the structure. And they know—perhaps not in words, but in the slow chemical language of root touching root—that they are not the first thing to live this way. The place was making itself before they arrived; the place will continue making itself after they are gone. Their contribution is a particular pattern in the growth, a set of tendencies, a shape that their descendants will carry forward without knowing it was ever a choice.

The room at the top of the world is not empty. It has never been empty. It swarms with the life that made it and was made by it, the patient builders who are also the patient building, the hands that shaped the wood and the wood that shaped the hands, the breathers of water and the eaters of light, the small green people of the high hollow, the ones who stayed and became the place that held them.
