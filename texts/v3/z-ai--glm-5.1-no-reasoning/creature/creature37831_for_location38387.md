# What Lives in the Thermals of Venn's Row

---

The first thing you must understand is that nothing here chose to live here. Everything arrived by accident or by drift, and then—upon contact with this place—simply stopped arriving anywhere else.

---

## The Copper-Winged Moths

They are not really copper. That is the light doing the lying. In the permanent bruised twilight of Venn's Row, the scales on their forewings catch the grimy louver-light and refract it into something that looks almost like burnished metal, almost like the dull sheen on a penny left in a gutter. But hold one in your palm—if you could manage to isolate a single specimen from the writhing, packed blankets of them clustered along the discharge pipes—and you would see that the wing is the grey-brown of old mortar, dusted with a powder that rubs off on your fingers like soot mixed with chalk. The copper is a trick. It is the place speaking through them, not they who possess it.

They are smaller than you would imagine. Flecks of ash, the description said, and that is precisely right—each moth no larger than the torn corner of a playing card, their bodies narrow and flattened as though pressed between the pages of something heavy. They do not flutter. Fluttering implies will, implies destination, implies the possibility of elsewhere. What they do is *shift*. En masse. A million wings lifting and resettling in a single convulsive ripple that crosses the surface of the pipe like a wind moving through a field, except there is no wind in Venn's Row. There is only the shifting itself, causeless, the way a man might turn over in his sleep without waking.

They have no mouths. Not in the functional sense. The proboscis is vestigial, a shriveled thread curled against the face like a dried umbilical cord. They do not eat. They do not need to eat. The tepid discharge pipes provide something—not nourishment, exactly, but a thermal sustenance that has replaced the need for it, the way a body in a coma no longer requires the gestures of living. They absorb heat through the ventral surface of their abdomens, pressed flat against the warm metal, and this heat sustains them in a way that no entomologist outside of Venn's Row would credit. They are, in the most literal sense, solar panels shaped like insects. Or rather, *thermal* panels. The distinction matters because there is no sun here.

Their lifecycle is an obscenity against the word *cycle*. There is no caterpillar. There is no chrysalis. The females—distinguishable only by a slightly darker band along the trailing edge of the hindwing—extrude eggs directly onto the pipe surfaces in clusters the size and color of a wart. The eggs are already warm. They are warm because the pipe is warm, and they have been warm since the moment of their laying, and they will remain warm until the moment they hatch, which is not triggered by season or photoperiod or any of the sensible cues that govern the rest of the natural world but by *accumulation*—a certain total quantity of heat absorbed, a thermal sum reached, and then the egg splits and a moth emerges, fully formed, fully winged, identical to its parent in every particular. No metamorphosis. No transformation. Just a smaller version of the same thing, pressing itself against the same pipe, in the same spot, forever.

They do not die of age. They die of *displacement*—pushed off the pipe surface by the press of new bodies, falling to the wet basalt below, where the pale geckos find them. Or they die of wing-scale depletion, the copper-brown-grey powder rubbed away by the constant friction of their neighbors until the wings become transparent, then torn, then useless, and the moth can no longer maintain its position on the pipe and slides, silently, into the steam below, where it dissolves. Not decomposes. *Dissolves*. The hot, mineral-saturated condensation that puddles in the seams between the paving stones breaks down the chitin in a matter of hours, reducing what was a living moth to a greyish film on the surface of the water, a film that the frogs drink without noticing.

The sound they make is not a sound they intend. It is the sound of their wings—brittle, dry, scaled—knocking against one another and against the metal pipes as they shift. A dry rattling clatter, like someone dragging a fingernail across a washboard, but continuous, layered, multiplied by the millions until it becomes a texture rather than a noise, a granular hiss that sits precisely in the frequency range between unpleasant and ignorable. It is the sound of Venn's Row the way the thrum of the pumps is the sound of Venn's Row. Remove either, and the silence would be unbearable.

## The Pale Geckos

They are not albino. This is important. Albino implies a genetic condition, a mistake, a deviation from a norm. There is no norm here. The geckos of Venn's Row are simply *uncolored*—as though pigment were a resource that this place could not afford to allocate, a luxury dispensed with in favor of some more essential survival mechanism. Their skin is the translucent white-grey of a boiled shrimp, or of a creature that has spent its entire life in a cave, except that they have not. They have spent their entire life in a room. A specific room, with walls of riveted steel and weeping mortar, where the light never changes and the heat never falters and the food—such as it is—arrives in a constant, inexhaustible rain of dead and dying moths falling from the pipes above.

They are small. Four inches from nose to tail-tip, the tail accounting for slightly more than half the measurement. Their bodies are narrow, almost serpentine, and they move with a speed that makes individual observation nearly impossible—a twitching, stuttering locomotion that seems to skip frames, as though they exist at a slightly different frame rate than the rest of the world. You see them *here*, and then you see them *there*, and the between is a blur of pale motion that your eye cannot parse. They climb the weeping walls with an effortless, mechanical precision, their toe-pads adhering to the wet metal and wet brick and wet stone with equal tenacity, and the word *wet* appears three times in that sentence because there is nothing in Venn's Row that is not wet, and the geckos have adapted to this by developing toe-pads that work *better* when lubricated. In the outside world, a gecko's adhesion is compromised by moisture. Here, it is enhanced. The surface of their toe-pads is textured with microscopic channels that wick water away from the contact point, creating a dry seal against a wet surface—a feat of engineering that human industry has never matched, achieved by an animal that has never seen the sun.

Their eyes are the most disturbing thing about them. Not because they are blind—the description says *pale, blind geckos*, and that is accurate, but it does not prepare you for what blind eyes look like in a creature that is otherwise so precisely, so aggressively functional. The eyes are present. They are the right shape, the right size, set into the skull at the correct angles. But they are *filled in*. Overlaid with a film of skin the same translucent white-grey as the rest of the body, so that the gecko appears to be wearing a blindfold of its own flesh. Beneath the film, you can see the vestigial structure of the eye—the dark mass of the retina, the faint ridge of the lens—but they are non-functional, sealed over, as though the animal decided at some point in its evolutionary history that vision was a sense it could not afford to maintain in a place where there is nothing to see that was not already known by touch and heat and vibration.

They eat constantly. This is not gluttony; it is arithmetic. The moths fall from the pipes at a rate of—there is no way to measure this precisely, but an observer sitting still for one hour in Venn's Row would witness hundreds of moths losing their grip and tumbling, spinning, to the stone below. The geckos intercept them. Not all of them. Most. A gecko will position itself on the wall at a slight downward angle, head tilted, mouth slightly open, and *wait*. The waiting is the stillness in their existence—otherwise, they are always moving, always twitching, always repositioning. But in the feeding stance, they are motionless, and the motionlessness is so absolute, so unlike their usual frame-skipping blur, that it appears to be a different creature entirely. A statue of a gecko. A pale carving on a wet wall. And then a moth falls past, and the tongue emerges—pink, startlingly pink against the white-grey of the mouth, the only color in the animal's entire body—and the moth is gone, and the gecko is moving again, repositioning, finding a new angle, settling back into stillness.

They do not interact with each other. Not in any way that could be called social. Two geckos will occupy the same square foot of wall, will hunt the same column of falling prey, will pass within inches of each other in their constant repositioning, and neither will acknowledge the other's existence. They do not compete. There is enough. There is always enough. The moths fall in numbers that exceed the geckos' capacity to consume them, and the excess falls to the stone below, where the frogs wait. The geckos have no predators. They have no parasites. They have no diseases. They have nothing at all except the wall, the prey, and the heat, and they have these in such perfect, unvarying abundance that the concept of *struggle*—the engine of biology everywhere else in the world—simply does not apply.

They reproduce in a manner that the word *reproduce* barely fits. A female gecko will, at intervals that no observer has been able to predict or explain, produce a single egg. She will affix it to the wall—always in a corner, always where two surfaces meet, always in the warmest available micro-location—with a secretion that hardens into a cement-like bond. The egg is the same translucent white-grey as her body. It is warm to the touch because the wall is warm. And after a period of time that is, again, unpredictable, the egg splits and a gecko emerges, fully formed, fully pale, fully blind, and immediately—*immediately*, within seconds of emergence—begins to hunt.

There is no childhood in Venn's Row. There is no adolescence. There is no learning. Everything is born complete and dies complete, and the between is a flat, featureless line of existence unbroken by growth or change.

## The Frogs

They sit in the damp crevices. That is almost all that can be said about them, because *sitting* is almost all that they do, and yet the sitting is so specific, so deliberate, so perfectly suited to the conditions of this place that it demands a respect that the word *sitting* does not usually command.

Their skin is the color of industrial coolant. Not the bright, theatrical green of a tree frog, and not the mottled brown of a pond frog, but a specific, dull, slightly metallic green that looks manufactured—as though it were a pigment mixed for a purpose, formulated to match the patina on the copper pipes, the algae-stained brick, the oxidized steel. It is not camouflage. Nothing in Venn's Row hunts the frogs. The color serves no survival function that can be identified. It is simply the color they are, the color this place has made them, the way the heat has made the moths wingless-eaters and the geckos eyeless-climbers. The environment does not select for fitness here. It *imprints*. The frogs are green because Venn's Row is green—in its pipes, in its corrosion, in the faint, mineral-tinted slime that coats the wet stone—and the frogs have absorbed this green into their flesh the way a cloth absorbs dye.

They are larger than you would expect. Not large in absolute terms—perhaps three inches from snout to vent—but large relative to the geckos, large relative to the moths, large enough that their presence in a crevice is a *presence*, a density, a small dark-green weight of living tissue pressed into a gap in the stonework with a deliberate, settled patience. Their bodies are slick. Not merely damp—slick, as though coated in something more viscous than water, a secretion that may be a mucus or may be something absorbed from the mineral-saturated environment and re-expressed through the skin. When they move—which is rarely, and only when necessary—they leave a brief, glistening trail on the stone that evaporates within seconds in the heat.

Their throats pulse. This is the defining behavior of the frogs of Venn's Row, and it is the behavior that the description captures with the phrase *synchronized, unending rhythm*, but those three words do not convey what it is like to stand in this place and *hear* it. The pulsing is not croaking. It is not vocalization. It is the expansion and contraction of the vocal sac, the rhythmic inflation and deflation of a membrane that in other frogs serves to amplify a mating call, but that here serves no communicative function that any observer has been able to identify. The frogs pulse their throats in a rhythm that is synchronized—not approximately, not loosely, but *precisely*, as though they were components of a single organism, as though the rhythm originated not in the frogs themselves but in the place, in the thrum of the pumps, in the vibration of the pipes, and the frogs were merely *expressing* it, translating a mechanical frequency into a biological gesture.

The rhythm is slow. Slower than a heartbeat. Perhaps one pulse every four seconds, a long, deliberate inflation followed by a slow, controlled deflation, and the sound it makes—a low, soft, wet sound, like someone very gently squeezing a sponge—is just audible beneath the mechanical noise of Venn's Row, a biological undertone beneath the industrial overture. When you first hear it, you think it is part of the machinery. When you realize it is coming from the frogs, you cannot un-hear it, and the space becomes, suddenly, more alive than you had understood it to be.

They eat what the geckos do not. The moths that fall past the geckos, that tumble past the walls and strike the wet stone below, are consumed by the frogs. A frog will sit in its crevice, motionless, its throat pulsing, and when a moth lands within range—a range that is surprisingly large, perhaps six inches—the tongue emerges. Not with the explosive speed of a typical frog. Slowly. Deliberately. The tongue is dark grey, almost black, and it extends with a creeping, deliberate motion that is somehow more disturbing than speed would be—a slow, inevitable retraction that draws the moth into the mouth over the course of a full second, and the moth is alive for most of that second, its wings beating against the frog's face in a frantic, futile flutter that produces a tiny, dry rustling sound, and then the mouth closes, and the throat pulses, and the rhythm does not change.

The frogs do not reproduce visibly. No observer has seen frog eggs, tadpoles, or froglets in Venn's Row. And yet the population is stable—it does not grow, does not shrink, and the frogs that occupy the crevices today are, as far as can be determined, the same frogs that occupied them a decade ago. They do not appear to age. They do not appear to die. Whether they are, in fact, the same individual frogs, or whether they are being replaced at a rate that precisely matches attrition, is unknown. The question is academic. In a place where nothing changes, the distinction between *the same frog* and *a frog in the same role* dissolves. The frog is the crevice. The crevice is the frog. The system is the system.

## The Lichens

They are barely alive. They are the minimum viable expression of life in a place that does not reward life—life's most reductionist form, its most stubborn, most parsimonious gesture. They exist in the mortar joints where the heat is slightly less infernal, and they exist in a state that can only be described as *arrested*. Not dormant. Dormancy implies the possibility of resumption. The lichens of Venn's Row will never resume. They reached the limit of what this environment could sustain, and they stopped, and they have not moved since.

They are pale. Not the vivid, calculated paleness of the geckos, but a *drained* paleness—the grey-white of something from which color has been pulled, rather than something to which color was never given. They are scabrous, as the description notes, and the word is exactly right: they have the texture of a scab, of dried crust, of something that formed to cover a wound and then remained. If you touched one—it is not recommended; the heat alone would deter you, and the stone is sharp—it would feel like a flake of old paint. Like the dried skin of a blister. Like something that had once been soft and alive and was now merely *there*, a residue of vitality rather than vitality itself.

They do not grow. They do not reproduce. They do not respond to stimuli. They do not photosynthesize, because there is no light to speak of—only the permanent bruised twilight filtered through the grime-smeared louvers—and the lichen's algal component has, in any case, long since ceased to function. What remains is the fungal matrix, the structural scaffolding, holding the shape of a living thing without the substance of one. A skeleton of an organism. An architectural memory of life.

And yet they are, technically, alive. They respire—barely, at the limit of measurability. They maintain cellular integrity. They have not crossed the threshold into death, and in a place where nothing decays, they may never cross it. They are the closest thing in Venn's Row to the place's own nature: permanent, unchanging, frozen at the boundary between *is* and *was*, perpetually held.

## What Else

There is nothing else. Or rather—there may be. The haze is thick, the light is dim, the noise is overwhelming, and the stone is freshly cut, its sharp corners and dark surface creating shadows that could hide smaller things. There have been reports—unverified, perhaps unverifiable—of something in the water table beneath the basalt. A vibration, a pulse, something that is not the pumps. Observers who have stood still long enough to isolate the mechanical frequencies from the biological ones claim to feel, through the soles of their boots, through the saturated clay, a slow, deep, rhythmic pressure that seems to originate from below. It is not the pump engines. It is not the steam mains. It is not the frogs.

It may be nothing. It may be the resonance of the bedrock, conducting the combined vibration of the city's industrial gut into the marsh basin. It may be the water table itself, moving in some vast, slow, subterranean rhythm that the surface dwellers—the moths, the geckos, the frogs, the lichens—cannot perceive but that they are, in some unmeasurable way, attuned to.

Or it may be something living. Something that has been here longer than the aqueduct, longer than the basalt, longer than the industrial infrastructure that has been built on top of it. Something that does not move because it does not need to move, that does not eat because it does not need to eat, that does not reproduce because it does not need to reproduce, because it is *sufficient*—complete in itself, sustained by the heat and the water and the mineral-rich clay in a way that no surface organism could understand.

If it is there, it is the oldest thing in Venn's Row. It may be the only thing in Venn's Row that was not an accident.

But this is speculation. In Venn's Row, speculation dissolves in the condensation. What remains is only what *is*: the moths on the pipes, the geckos on the walls, the frogs in the crevices, the lichens in the mortar joints, the heat, the wet, the noise, the dark.

All of it, held. All of it, still. All of it, repeating.

---

*The pumps thrum. The water weeps. The moths shift. The geckos twitch. The frogs pulse. The lichens hold. The thing beneath the stone—if it is there—does whatever it does.*

*And Venn's Row continues to not change.*
