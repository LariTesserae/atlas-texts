What lives here is not an organism. It is a circumstance—a localized storm of metabolism that has forgotten it was ever separate things.

At its most visible, it is the vines. But the vines are only the circulatory system, the reaching arms. They are dark, almost black, because they are not building for the sun; they are building for the pipe. Their chlorophyll is a vestigial ghost, a bruised memory of photosynthesis, long since overridden by chemosynthesis. They do not eat light. They eat the exhalations of the earth. The steam that vents from the iron pipes carries sulfur compounds, trace minerals leached from the deep, and heat. The vines drink the heat. They drink the minerals. They drink the moisture. They drink the iron rust that flakes off the pipes in constant, unseen precipitation. If you were to cut one—and it would take a blade, because the rubbery skin is dense as shoe leather, and the fibers within are braided cable—the sap that flows would not be green. It would be the color of coffee, thick and warm, smelling of ozone and wet pennies and the deep, unventilated breath of subterranean places.

And the cut would not bleed long. Within seconds, the sap would oxidize on contact with the air, darkening to a scab, and the vine would begin to swell at the cut site, proliferating new growth to seal the wound. It does not heal. It overgrows. The distinction is important.

The moss-carpet—though it is not moss, has no relation to any bryophyte that ever breathed in a forest—is the skin. The felted-wire mat that covers every surface is where the absorption happens. Its small, upward-facing cups are mouths. They drink the condensation, yes, but they also trap the particulate: the microscopic flakes of iron, the mineral dust that the steam carries, the organic detritus that sloughs off the vines above. When the steam-vents exhale and the carbide lights flare, you can see the cups ripple, a wave of papery rustling that moves outward from the heat source like a shudder across a sleeping animal's flank. They are tasting the air. They are deciding what to eat.

But the truth—and it is a truth that becomes inescapable the longer you stand in that cramped, damp, shrieking corridor—is that the pipes are also alive. Not metaphorically. Not in the way that a river is sometimes said to be alive. The pipes have been colonized so thoroughly, integrated so completely, that they function now as a secondary skeleton. The root systems that have forced themselves into rivet gaps and valve housings are not merely clinging; they have become gaskets. Where the original pipe seals have corroded, the vine-root has grown into the gap, swollen with sap, and formed a living seal that flexes with the pressure fluctuations. The pipes sweat because the vegetation is pulling heat from the fluid inside and condensing the ambient moisture against the outer iron. The steam-vents cycle on inscrutable timers, but the timers are not mechanical. The pressure valves are clogged and cleared by the rhythmic swelling and contracting of the vegetable matter that has grown into their housings. The vegetation is breathing the pipes.

And the pipes are feeding the vegetation.

It is a single system. An endosymbiosis so complete that the original boundary between the industrial and the botanical has collapsed. The pipes bring fluid from somewhere deeper—hot, mineral-rich, under extreme pressure—and the vegetation drinks it, grows, destroys the infrastructure, creates new leaks, drinks from the leaks, grows more, strains the adjacent pipes, creates new leaks. The tumorous bulges at the burst points are the system's digestion: concentrated knots of tissue where the enriched runoff is absorbed most efficiently, where the metabolic rate is highest. They pulse. You cannot see them pulse in the stuttering carbide light, but if you press your hand against one—and the surface is hot, fever-hot, damp and yielding under the hard callused skin of the older growth—you can feel it. A slow, irregular throb, synced not to any heartbeat but to the cycling of the steam valves, the surges of pressure in the pipes below.

This is what lives in the gorge: a closed loop of consumption and growth that has no brain, no central control, no purpose, and no end state. It is not evolving toward anything. It is not competing with anything. There is nothing here to compete with. It is simply running, the way a fire runs, the way a chemical reaction runs, consuming its fuel and excreting its waste and growing until the fuel runs out. The fuel is the fluid in the pipes. The waste is the steam, the heat, the damp, the rust. The growth is the vines, the moss-carpet, the tumorous bulges, the living gaskets, the root-clogged valves.

It has no name. It has no taxonomy. It is, as far as anyone can tell, unique—a fusion of the engineered and the wild that occurred here, in this specific wound in this specific mountain, and could not have occurred anywhere else. The pipes were laid for a purpose that no longer matters, by hands that no longer exist, to carry something that the vegetation has repurposed. The vegetation came from somewhere—spores carried on the cold breath of the mountain, perhaps, or seeds that germinated in the condensation, or something older and stranger that was already in the rock, waiting for the heat and the moisture to wake it. It does not matter. What matters is that they found each other.

The system has a behavior that could almost be called a temperament. It is restless. The vines extend and retract, searching, always searching, for new pipe, new warmth, new mineral. The moss-carpet ripples and rustles, tasting, always tasting. The steam-vents shriek and fall silent, cycling through their inscrutable rhythm. The pipes groan and creak, expanding and contracting with the heat, flexing in their living gaskets. The whole narrow gorge, from raw wall to raw wall, from the unseen sky above to the tangled floor below, hums with the effort of it. Not a peaceful hum. A laboring hum. The sound of a machine running too hot, too fast, under too much load, but unable to stop because stopping is not a behavior it possesses.

It is not hostile. It has no hostility in it, no more than a fire has hostility for the wood it consumes. The vines do not resist your passage because they do not perceive you. You are not pipe. You are not steam. You are not mineral enough to eat, and you are not warm enough to drink, and you are not large enough to strain the infrastructure. You are irrelevant. You can walk through it, if you can tolerate the noise and the damp and the dark and the cold and the constant, unsettling awareness that you are inside something that is alive and does not know you are there and would not care if it did.

But you cannot walk through it for long. The condensation soaks through your clothes. The damp enters your lungs. The sound fills your skull until thought becomes difficult, until the boundary between your own internal rhythm and the rhythm of the gorge begins to blur. The vines brush against you as you pass, slick and warm where they touch the pipes, cold and rubbery where they touch the rock, and they leave a residue on your skin—a thin, oily film that smells of iron and sulfur and something else, something organic and old, like the inside of a greenhouse that has been sealed for decades.

You are not being claimed. You are not being consumed. But you are being marked. You are being coated in the same ambient moisture that coats everything here, the same film that never dries, the same damp that weeps from the stone and condenses on the pipes and feeds the moss-cups and rusts the iron and keeps the whole system running. You are becoming part of the humidity. And the humidity is part of the system. And the system does not care.

Walk far enough and the gorge narrows until it is no longer a gorge but a crack, a horizontal wound in the mountain's flank, and the pipes and the vines and the moss and the steam are so intertwined that there is no space between them, no gap where the rock is visible, no surface that is not part of the living, clanking, rustling, shrieking mass. The carbide lights are closer together here, the flares overlapping, the shadows leaping and collapsing in a continuous strobe that makes the walls seem to pulse. The noise is a single, solid thing, a wall of sound that you can feel in your chest, in your teeth, in the marrow of your bones. The heat from the pipes and the fever-hot tumors is rising, meeting the cold air from above, creating a convection current that pulls the steam upward in spiraling columns, lit from below by the trembling white carbide flares, and the vines are reaching up into those columns, tendrils extended, drinking the heat and the moisture and the light all at once, and the whole tunnel, this crack in the mountain, is breathing.

Inhale: the steam vents cycle closed, the light dims, the vines retract, the sound drops to a low, grinding throb.

Exhale: the steam vents shriek open, the light flares, the vines surge upward, the sound rises to a roar, and the hot, damp air rushes past you toward the distant slit of sky.

Inhale. Exhale. Inhale. Exhale.

It is breathing. The whole thing is breathing. And you are inside it. And it does not know you are there.
