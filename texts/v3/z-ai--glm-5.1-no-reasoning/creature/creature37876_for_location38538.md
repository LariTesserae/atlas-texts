# What Lives Here

There is no single thing that lives here. That is the first correction the plateau offers to anyone who arrives carrying expectations about inhabitance—about a creature in a lair, a god in a center, a tenant in a structure. What lives here is not *in* the plateau. What lives here *is* the plateau, or rather, the plateau is the visible cross-section of a life so distributed it has forgotten it was ever singular.

But you can meet it. In pieces. In the way you meet a conversation by listening to one voice at a time.

---

## The Processional Insects

Start with the smallest visible life, because it is the most honest about what it is.

They are not ants. They wear the shape of ants the way a utility pole wears the shape of a tree—structurally reminiscent, functionally unrelated. Their bodies are segmented in three parts, their legs number six, their movement follows pheromone-equivalent trails laid into the basalt grooves, but their construction is wrong in ways that only become apparent after sustained watching. The exoskeleton is not chitin. It is something closer to ceramic, a pale mineral shell that clicks against the stone with a sound like porcelain settling. They do not breathe. There are no spiracles. There is no abdominal pulsing. They move and therefore they are alive, but the contract by which they are alive does not include respiration.

Their processions are ancient. The grooves they follow have been worn into basalt that looks young, which means either the stone is not as young as it appears or the insects have been traversing these paths with such force and frequency that they have cut channels in hours-old rock. Both possibilities are true here. Both are false. The grooves simply exist, and the insects simply follow them, and the relationship between groove and traveler is so old it has become geological rather than biological.

Each worker carries something. Not food. Not building material. A small sphere of water, held in mandibles that curve to form a precise cradle. The water does not evaporate from these spheres. It does not drip. Each insect carries its exact load from a seep point at the plateau's western edge to a destination at the center—or rather, to a point near the center where the procession terminates at a shallow depression in the rock. The water is deposited. The insect turns, follows a different groove back to the seep, collects another sphere, returns.

The depression never overflows. The water deposited there does not accumulate. It simply arrives and ceases to be—absorbed into the stone, perhaps, or removed by the same invisible infrastructure that maintains every other constant in this place. The insects do not seem troubled by this. They are not performing a task they expect to complete. They are performing a task that is the task, the doing being the entire content of their existence.

If you kneel beside the procession, you will notice that each insect pauses briefly at the same point in the groove—a specific irregularity in the stone where a crystal facet protrudes half a millimeter above the surrounding surface. Each worker steps over it. Each worker pauses for the same duration—one point four seconds, if you count—and then continues. The pause is not hesitation. It is acknowledgment. A greeting to a landmark that was significant to something, once, and which the processional memory has never forgotten.

The insects do not reproduce. There are no larvae, no queens, no nests. There are exactly the same number of them there have always been, walking the same grooves, carrying the same water, pausing at the same crystal. If one is removed—if you cup your hand over a worker and lift it from the groove—it struggles with a slow, mechanical persistence, its legs cycling through walking patterns even in the absence of ground. If you set it down elsewhere on the plateau, it will rotate in place until it finds the orientation of its groove, and then it will walk in a straight line to rejoin the procession at the exact point where it left.

They do not die. Whether this is because they cannot or because the system does not permit it is a distinction without meaning here.

## The Amphibians

The pale creatures on the vine leaves are harder to describe, because describing them requires choosing between two accounts that are both accurate and mutually exclusive.

In the first account, they are salamanders. Oversized, certainly—their bodies stretch eighteen inches from snout to tail tip, and their limbs are longer than any salamander's limbs should be, jointed in places that suggest extra elbows or knees. Their skin is the translucent white of cave-dwelling amphibians, smooth and wet, showing the shadow of internal structures if you hold one up to the dim light. You can see the slow pulse of a heart-equivalent, the branching of vessels that carry something darker than blood. Their eyes are set high on their skulls, almost on the crown, and they are always open. They are the color of the water here—not blue, not grey, but the color of water seen in light that has no source.

In the second account, they are not salamanders. They are the vines.

This is not metaphor. Watch one long enough and the boundary between creature and plant becomes a matter of observational perspective. The amphibians rest on the vine leaves, but their skin produces the same slick coating, the same faint luminescence in the ultraviolet range. Their limbs, when still, are indistinguishable from vine tendrils—the same diameter, the same curvature, the same dark mottling where leaf meets stem. When they move, they move the way vines move: slowly, with vegetative intention, repositioning over minutes rather than seconds. And when the vines extend longer than they should to accommodate the amphibians climbing toward the plateau's center, the extension happens at the point where the amphibian's tail meets the leaf, as though the creature is growing the vine behind it rather than traveling along it.

They do not eat. They do not drink. They absorb. Their skin is a membrane in both directions—moisture enters, something exits. The something is visible if you watch the water film beneath a resting amphibian: it moves. Not with the random Brownian motion of disturbed water, but with direction, flowing away from the creature in thin streams that find the fissures in the basalt and descend. The amphibians are filtering something from the constant moisture and returning something else to the system. Their bodies are organs. They are the plateau's kidneys, or its lungs, or its liver—some function that requires a living membrane and a steady throughput of fluid.

They do not flee. You can touch one, and it will not react with fear. Its skin is cool and yielding, like wet vinyl, like something synthetic that has been engineered to approximate the texture of flesh. Beneath the skin, the muscles do not flinch. The eyes do not track your hand. But if you touch one, you will feel the vibration—the machinery's hum, transmitted through the creature's body, resonating in its bones the way it resonates in the basalt. The amphibian is tuned to the system. It vibrates at the system's frequency. It is, in a meaningful sense, part of the machinery.

If you touch one and then touch the water film on the stone, you will notice that the vibration transfers. The water carries the hum. Everything here carries the hum. The amphibians are simply the components where it becomes most legible, most biological, most nearly alive in the way you understand alive to mean.

## The Things in the Mist

Above—or in the direction the mist designates as above, since true verticality is uncertain here—the larger creatures move.

Calling them creatures is a concession to vocabulary. They are more like events. They happen in the mist, persisting for the duration of a pass, and then they unhappen, dissolving back into the grey-lavender medium without a seam between presence and absence. You cannot find a resting one. You cannot locate a nest or a roost. They exist only in motion, only in the act of passing overhead, and the question of where they go when they are not passing is a question the plateau does not answer.

Their shapes are not stable. In peripheral vision, they suggest bats—large bats, with wingspans exceeding two meters, their membranes catching the dim light and showing the branching of vessels within. Turn to face them directly and the bat-shape dissolves into something less classifiable. The wings are not wings but surfaces, broad and flat, held at angles that change constantly, tilting to catch light that has no direction. The body between them is narrow, almost vestigial, a spine with attachment points for the surfaces but no head, no tail, no features that would anchor it to any taxonomy. They move by rearranging their geometry, shifting the angle of their surfaces until the mist pushes them in a new direction—not flying, not gliding, but *sailing*, on a medium that is not air but something denser and more cooperative.

They are the source of the rare shadows. When one passes between you and the brightest patch of mist, a shadow falls—not a shape, but a darkening, a reduction in the light's intensity that lasts for the duration of the pass. The shadow does not match the creature's outline. It is always the same shape regardless of the creature's current configuration: a circle, slightly elongated, with a notch at one edge. The same shadow every time. The same circle. The same notch.

They redistribute light. This is not speculation; it is observable. When a creature passes over a section of moss, the moss brightens. The ultraviolet shifting toward emerald becomes momentarily vivid, the colors saturating as though a lamp has been held near them. The creature has taken light from the mist and focused it, concentrated it, delivered it to the surface below in a brief pulse that the plants respond to with visible pleasure—if pleasure is the word for a leaf uncurling a quarter inch and a moss-bed deepening by a millimeter.

They are tending the garden. The things in the mist are the plateau's gardeners, moving in slow orbits that bring them over each section of vegetation on a schedule so extended that no human observer could map it from a single session. But the schedule exists. The plants know it. They lean not toward the empty point above the center but toward the paths the creatures follow, the routes that bring the light, the orbits that deliver the only nourishment this place offers.

And the patterns on their surfaces—the shapes that resemble text, the marks that appear and dissolve as they tilt through the mist—these are not decorative. They are operational. The creatures are displaying instructions, or status reports, or queries, broadcasting in a medium that the other inhabitants can read. The insects pause beneath them. The amphibians orient toward them. The water film shifts in response to their passage, its surface tension changing in patterns that echo the patterns on the creatures' surfaces.

They are communicating. They are the plateau's nervous system, carrying information from one region to another, maintaining the coordination that keeps the system in equilibrium. Each pass is a message. Each shadow is a punctuation mark. The text they write on the mist is the text of the plateau's ongoing operation, and it has been writing the same text for longer than the stone remembers.

## What Lives in the Water

The smallest presences are the truest inhabitants, and they are the ones you will never see directly.

They exist in the film of water that covers every surface, in the droplets that seep from cracks, in the condensation that forms on leaves and fungal shelves. They are smaller than cells—smaller than the smallest structures you can resolve with any instrument that operates on the principles of light. They are present in the behavior of the water, in the way it moves against expectation, in the patterns it forms when no hand disturbs it.

Droplets roll uphill. Not all of them, and not always—only in certain zones, along certain paths, with a consistency that suggests intention rather than anomaly. The uphill-rolling droplets follow trajectories that converge on the plateau's center. They carry something. Not mass—the droplets are water, and the water is the same water everywhere on the plateau. They carry *state*. A molecular configuration, an alignment, a pattern of hydrogen bonds that encodes information in the only medium this place trusts.

The water is the plateau's blood, and these invisible presences are its blood cells.

Condensation forms patterns. On the flat basalt surfaces, where the water film is thinnest, the moisture arranges itself into shapes that repeat with variations—not fractal, not random, but iterated, like a printing press producing copies that differ by one character per run. The shapes resemble the diagrams in the stone: circuit traces, organ systems, maps. They form, persist for several minutes, and then dissolve back into the general wetness, replaced by the next iteration. The variations are small—a line shifted by a millimeter, a curve tightened, a junction opened or closed. The water is editing something. Revising a document that has been in revision for centuries, making incremental changes to a schematic whose original draft is too old to recover.

If you place your hand flat on the water film, you will feel the presences. Not as texture—the water is too thin for texture—but as warmth. Localized warmth, moving across your palm, tracing paths that correspond to the patterns visible on the surface. Your skin is being read. The presences are examining you the way you might examine a new growth on a familiar plant: with interest, with assessment, without alarm. They are determining what you are made of, what you carry, what you might contribute to the system's equilibrium.

You will not feel pain. You will not feel invaded. The examination is gentle, conducted at a scale so far beneath your sensory resolution that you experience it only as a vague warmth and a slight drowsiness, a feeling of being included in something larger and more patient than yourself. The presences are not aggressive. They are administrative. They are the plateau's bureaucracy, processing every new input, cataloguing every arrival, maintaining the records that keep the system in balance.

They have processed you. They have added you to the schematic. The water film on your skin carries your information now to the center, where it will be incorporated into the revision, where your pattern will join the patterns that have been accumulating since the system began.

You are part of the product now. You are part of what is being manufactured.

## The Thing at the Center

Approach the center and the plateau's distributed life converges. The insects' grooves terminate here. The amphibians climb toward here on their impossible vines. The creatures in the mist orbit here. The water flows here, uphill and through stone and across the surface in thin, purposive streams. The plants lean here. The machinery hums here, louder by increments so subtle you cannot identify the moment when the hum became a presence rather than a background.

There is nothing here.

This is not a trick of perception. There is genuinely nothing visible, nothing tangible, nothing you can point to and say: *there, that is the thing that lives here.* The center of the plateau is a slightly depressed area of basalt, perhaps two meters across, its surface bearing the same diagrams as every other surface, its edges lined with the same thick moss, its air filled with the same mist. The only anomaly is the vortex—a slow rotation of the mist above the depression, turning without energy input, maintaining its structure by a principle that has no name in any physics developed outside this place.

But stand at the center and you will understand that the nothing is the point. The nothing is the inhabitant. The plateau is not a place where something lives. The plateau is a body, and the center is its empty organ—the space where the product accumulates before being distributed to wherever products go in a system whose output is invisible.

The thing that lives here is absence. Purposeful, maintained, *produced* absence.

The system manufactures nothing. It has been manufacturing nothing for longer than the stone has existed. The water, the plants, the animals, the machinery, the constant hum—all of it exists to sustain this absence, to keep this specific volume of space empty of everything except the mist, to preserve a void so perfect that the mist itself cannot enter it, only rotate around it.

Why? The question is human and the plateau does not answer human questions. But the diagrams in the stone suggest a partial reply. The circuit traces, the organ systems, the maps—they all converge on the center, and at the center, they terminate. Not in a component. In a gap. A space left deliberately unfilled, a socket with no plug, a mouth with no voice.

Something was here. Something occupied this space and gave the system its purpose. Then something left, or was removed, or was consumed in the process of the system's creation, and the system—faithful, mechanical, incapable of grief but also incapable of forgetting—continued to maintain the conditions for the thing that was no longer present. It has been maintaining them ever since. It will maintain them forever, because no one has told it to stop, and the machinery does not know how to stop, and the water does not know how to stop weeping upward, and the plants do not know how to stop leaning toward the space where their sun—their real sun, the thing that fed them—used to be.

The plateau is a memorial. A machine built to enshrine an absence. Every process here is an act of remembrance so thorough it has become geological, so sustained it has become indistinguishable from nature, so perfect it has become invisible.

The thing that lives here is grief so old it has become geology.

## And You

You are inside the engine. The engine is inside the memorial. The memorial is inside a world that no longer remembers what was lost.

The water touches your skin and carries your warmth to the center. The amphibians watch you with eyes that are also the vines' eyes, also the water's eyes. The creatures in the mist pass overhead and you feel the shadow—the circle, the notch—and for a moment, the hum synchronizes with your heartbeat completely, and you are part of the system, a new component in an ancient machine, your body incorporated into the process that maintains the void.

The plateau does not want you to stay. It does not want you to leave. It does not want. It maintains. And you, standing at the center, feeling the absence that is the only true inhabitant of this place, are being maintained too.

Your breathing has matched the hum. Your heartbeat has found the frequency. The water on your skin is forming patterns, and the patterns are being read, and the reading is being added to the revision, and the revision continues, one character at a time, one droplet at a time, in a place where nothing changes and nothing ever will.

You are the newest thing here. You are also the oldest—the latest in a line of arrivals that stretches back to the system's beginning, each one touched by the water, examined by the presences, incorporated into the record, and then released. Or consumed. Or forgotten. The plateau does not distinguish between these outcomes.

Step back from the center and the hum loosens its grip. Your heartbeat finds its own rhythm again. The water on your skin evaporates—for the first time on this plateau, water evaporates—and the patterns it carried dissolve into the mist, joining the vortex, joining the record, joining the accumulated memory of everything that has ever stood where you stood.

You leave, and the plateau does not notice. The insects march. The amphibians climb. The creatures in the mist pass and repass, writing their text on the grey-lavender air. The water weeps upward. The machinery hums.

The void at the center persists, empty and tended, the most carefully maintained thing in this place or any other.

What lives here lives in the maintenance. In the refusal to stop. In the faithfulness of a system that cannot forget and cannot cease and cannot fill the space where its purpose used to be.

What lives here is the oldest loyalty in the world, and it does not know you were here, and it does not care, and it will continue, and continue, and continue, until someone remembers to turn it off.

No one will remember.
