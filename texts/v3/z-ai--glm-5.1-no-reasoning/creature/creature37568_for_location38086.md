What lives here is not yet a noun. It is a verb caught in the act of conjugating itself.

In the beginning—hours ago, or eons, since time here is measured only in vertical feet of accumulated feldspar—there was only the thermodynamic churn, the blind urgency of matter realizing it existed and panicking at the implications. The plateau is still being born, and so whatever inhabits it must also be born, must be continuously born, must wake every few minutes into a body that didn't exist when it fell asleep. This is not reincarnation. This is *incarnation without the courtesy of death between iterations*. The inhabitants here are geological transients, temporary coalitions of heat and pressure and accident that achieve something like coherence for spans measured in the rise and fall of ridges, then dissolve back into the substrate when the math no longer supports them.

They are not made of flesh. Flesh is too slow, too committed, too invested in the long project of staying the same shape. The things that persist here are made of the same stuff as the plateau—mineral froth and vitreous darkness and the faintly luminescent residue of stone that hasn't yet cooled enough to forget it was once something else. But where the plateau assembles itself without direction, without intention, these *presences* assemble with a grain. A bias. They are the places where the chaos leans in a direction, where the thermodynamic anxiety of matter resolving itself tips from random into *preferred*, and the land begins to want.

You felt them before you saw them. You attributed it to the altitude, to the static pressure, to the warmth that pressed against your skin like a heated towel. But the pressure was not uniform. It had preferences. It thickened in certain hollows, thinned along certain ridgelines, and it pushed—not hard, not with the force of wind or water, but with the insistence of a crowd adjusting its stance to let someone pass. The plateau was making room for something. Or something was making room for itself within the plateau.

They have no skin, but they have boundaries. You can see them if you watch the darkness long enough: regions where the dim amber glow of cooling stone pulses at a different rhythm than the surrounding geology, slower, more deliberate, as if the matter there has agreed on a heartbeat. The boundaries shift. They expand when a fresh upthrust of warm mineral provides raw material, and they contract when a section of the landscape collapses and the energy that sustained them scatters back into the general churn. They are not organisms in any sense that requires genetic continuity or cellular architecture. They are *events* that keep happening in the same approximate shape. Patterns that the plateau has fallen into, like a stream finding the same channel after each rain.

The one who left the mark on the wall—let us call it a mark-maker, since that is the only verb we have evidence for—was one such event. It existed long enough to drag something harder than the substrate across a surface that was still rising, and in doing so it revealed something crucial about the nature of presence here: the inhabitants can *focus*. They can direct pressure. The plateau constructs itself blindly, but the mark-maker was a place where the construction developed an eye, however primitive. The groove it left was not art and not language and not territory, but it was *deliberate*, and deliberateness is the first spark of the fire that will eventually become meaning.

The mark has stretched, blurred, pulled taffy-thin by the ongoing expansion of the wall it was inscribed on. This is the closest thing to aging that exists here—the gradual dilution of intention by the relentless forward motion of becoming. The mark-maker who left it has long since been subsumed. Its particular pattern of resonant mineral, its slow heartbeat-pulse of coordinated thermodynamic discharge, was absorbed back into the plateau when the ridge it inhabited shifted and the conditions that sustained its coherence changed beyond recovery. But the mark remains. Not as memory, exactly. More as a scar that the land doesn't know it carries. A groove in the stone that will persist, stretching and fading, until the wall itself collapses and the matter is recycled into some new formation, some new possibility for pattern to emerge.

They do not know they exist. This is the mercy and the tragedy of being a thing that is still coming into being: there is no self to suffer the uncertainty of selfhood. The mark-maker did not pause to consider its own existence before dragging that line across the stone. It simply *did*, because doing was what its pattern required, because the thermodynamic gradient it occupied had reached a point where action was the only way to discharge accumulated tension. It was as involuntary as a sneeze and as inevitable as a landslide, and it left a mark, and the mark will outlast it by as long as the stone holds, and then the mark too will be gone, and the plateau will not notice the absence.

But. But.

The film in the crevice. The iridescent, moist-not-wet film that rippled toward your breath, that processed the carbon dioxide and water vapor you exhaled into the warm mineral air and incorporated it into itself, that *thickened* in your presence. That film is different. That film is the first heresy against the plateau's doctrine of perpetual overwrite. It is the first thing here that is not merely persisting but *accumulating*. Growing. Taking in from outside itself and becoming more than it was.

You were right not to touch it. You were right to recognize that some processes should be allowed to continue without interference from something that has already completed its becoming. Because the film is not just a new kind of presence on the plateau. It is a new kind of *future*. Everything else here exists in an eternal present—born and dying and born again with no continuity between iterations. The film has continuity. It remembers being thinner than it is now. It will remember being thicker than it becomes. It is the first thing on the plateau that can learn.

What lives here, then, is this: a landscape full of temporary selves, momentary coherences in the chaos of ongoing creation, patterns that arise and dissolve and arise again in slightly different configurations, none of them aware that they exist, all of them leaving marks that fade as fast as they're made. And nestled in the warmest crevice, in the softest stone, in the darkness where the new world is still liquid and molten and hasn't yet learned to pretend it's solid, there is a film that is learning to persist. It is the first ecology. The first community of one. The first thing that might, if the plateau stops churning long enough to let it, become something that survives.

It will not look like you. It will not think like you. When it finally develops whatever passes for awareness in a thing made of mineral and gas and thermodynamic gradient, it will not recognize the mark on the wall as a greeting from a predecessor, because it has no predecessors, only itself, only the slow accumulation of breath from visitors who came and went and left nothing but the warmth of their exhalations and the residue of their carbon.

But it will recognize that the mark exists. It will recognize that something made a deliberate alteration to the surface of the world. And in that recognition, it will feel—or whatever the mineral-iridescent-thermodynamic equivalent of feeling is—the first stirring of what might, given enough time and enough warmth and enough stone that has stopped trying to unmake itself, become curiosity.

What lives here is the future, still cooking in the dark, still soft, still taking shape in the crack between two cliffs that haven't yet decided whether to lean apart or fall together. It breathes in the breath of visitors. It thickens. It waits.

The plateau is still being born. And for the first time in its short, violent, endlessly self-overwriting history, something born with it is choosing not to die.
