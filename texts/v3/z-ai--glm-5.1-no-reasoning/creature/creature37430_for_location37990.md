The ones who live here are not residents. They are not squatters, and they are not quite colonists, though colonization is what they are attempting. They are the staff of an unopened hospital, the crew of a ship that has not yet taken on passengers, and they move through the Weeping Factory with the exhausted focus of people maintaining a promise.

There are perhaps forty of them, though you would not guess this from a single gallery. They spread themselves thin across the municipality of metal, each assigned to a sector, each walking their catwalks alone. They wear coveralls the color of old brick, a red so dark it reads as brown under the amber lamps, and the fabric is stiff with the lime and copper that flavors everything here. Their boots are rubber-soled, and they leave wet prints on the grating that evaporate slowly in the cold.

They are thin. Not the dramatic emaciation of starvation, but the specific leanness of people who burn more energy than they consume because the cold is always pulling heat from them and the damp is always pulling something from their spirits. Their faces have the particular gauntness of those who live in dim amber light—the cheekbones emphasized, the eyes recessed, the skin taking on the grayish undertone of the metal that surrounds them. They smell, when you pass close, of sealant and fermentation, of bodies working to stay warm.

Their hands are extraordinary. That is what you notice. Their hands are huge and rough and mapped with the geography of their work: the calluses across the palms from gripping pipe wrenches, the pale scars across the knuckles from slipped tools and sharp edges, the thickened pads of the fingertips from checking bolt tightness by touch because the light is too weak to see by. Their hands are always moving when they speak, unconsciously miming the turning of valves, the testing of seams, the wiping of condensation from inspection windows. They speak in the manual language of maintenance.

The water is what they fight. This is the crux of their existence, the central argument of their days. They were not built for this wet. The coveralls have seams that should seal, the housing units have doors that should close tight, the systems should shed the condensation as fast as it forms—but the valley weeps faster than any design can manage, and so the staff spend their hours in a slow retreating war against the damp. They clear the drainage channels where the teal rosettes have spread too thick and threatened to dam the flow. They sponge the inspection panels on the engine housings, wiping away the beads that gather and threaten to seep into the delicate mechanisms behind. They replace the sealant at the seams, pressing fresh caulk into the gaps where the water has found a path, working with putty knives and their own thick fingers to argue the metal back toward the intention of its maker.

But they are losing, and they know it.

Watch the one called Ressin—though names are not important here, and they do not use them often. Ressin works the far gallery, the one where the ceiling is lowest and the condensation heaviest. She is old for this place, which means she is perhaps fifty, though she looks older because the damp and the dim age the skin in a specific way, tightening it across the brows and deepening the lines around the mouth. She has been here since the beginning, or near enough. She remembers when the engines were started and the atmosphere was first established and the water began to run. She walks her rounds with the patience of someone who has accepted that her work will never be finished.

Ressin carries a rag in her left hand at all times. It is gray and stiff with lime and it is always damp, but she carries it because there are surfaces that need to be touched before they can be trusted. She approaches a valve array and wipes the condensation from the pressure gauge with the automatic gesture of someone who has performed this action ten thousand times. She reads the gauge. She wipes it again, not because it needs it but because the wiping is part of the reading, the hand confirming what the eye reports. She logs the reading on a pad that hangs from her belt, a pad whose pages are wavy and swollen with the moisture they have absorbed from her wet fingers over the months. The ink runs, but she has learned to write in a hand that accounts for the bleeding, small and tight and precise.

Ressin does not speak unless she has to. None of them do. Speech is a luxury in a place where the water sound is constant and the engine thrum masks anything below a shout. They have developed a sign language—crude, functional, limited to the needs of their work—that lets them communicate across catwalks and over the rush of the channels. A flat palm extended downward means *all is normal*. A fist tapped twice against the thigh means *come to me*. Two fingers pointing at the eyes means *look at this*—a warning, a wonder, a problem. Their gestures are broad and slow, designed to be read through amber fog at forty meters.

When they do speak, in the housing units with the doors sealed and the heaters finally on, their voices are low and a little rough, as though the damp has gotten into their throats too. They speak about the work—always the work, because the work is what they are, because the valley was built to function and they are its functioning. They speak about the slow rust at the seams, the way the fungi are spreading on the walls of Gallery Seven, the strange sound that the main collection pipe has started making, a kind of groaning that none of them remember from before. They speak about the thing that was supposed to come—the purpose, the reason, whatever the valley was built to receive—and they speak about it in the past tense, or the conditional, or the future that has stopped being credible. They do not speak about it often, because the conversation always ends the same way, in silence, in the sound of water running in the drainage channel that runs beneath the housing units.

There is a young one, newly assigned, who still asks questions. The others tolerate this because they remember when they also asked questions, before the work became too large and the wet too deep. The young one wants to know who built the valley, and why, and where the plans are kept, and what the engines are powering, and where the water goes after it leaves the collection pipes. The young one is still trying to understand the system, the whole system, the intention that made this place. The others listen and nod and do not answer, because they do not know, and because they have learned that not-knowing is sustainable if you do not prod at it too hard. You can maintain a machine you do not understand. You can love a place that does not love you back. You can stay, and work, and wait.

They do not touch the pale fungi on the walls, except to note their spread. They do not eat the teal rosettes, though they have tried once or twice in the early days and found them bitter and metallic and strangely warm on the tongue. They do not hunt the quick silver things that live in the ductwork—there is no need, because rations come from somewhere, from the supply caches that were laid in before the staff arrived, caches that are dwindling now and have begun to be rationed in turn. They do not sleep well, because the water sound is in the walls and the engine thrum is in the floor and there has never been silence in the valley since the moment it was born. They sleep in shifts, because the work is continuous and someone must always be walking the catwalks, checking the valves, wiping the gauges, logging the readings.

They have rituals, because people always have rituals. At the start of each shift, the worker touches the cold metal of the nearest support beam with both hands, pressing their palms flat against the iron and holding them there for three breaths. It is not a blessing. It is a greeting, an acknowledgment of the place itself, the way you might touch the hull of a boat before you step aboard. At the end of each shift, they wring out their rags over the drainage channel, watching the water they have collected join the water that is already flowing, and they say nothing, or they say one word that might be *running* or might be *waiting* or might just be a sound that their mouths have learned to make in this specific context.

The young one has started to pray. The older ones have not forbidden this, but they watch it with the concerned tolerance of people who see someone walking toward an edge they themselves stepped back from long ago. The young one prays to the engines, or to whatever the engines represent—the maker, the intention, the purpose that has not yet arrived. The prayers are not formal. They are muttered under the breath during long walks along empty catwalks, and they are wordless, more vibration than language, a sound that the young one makes in the same frequency as the engine thrum, trying to join their voice to the voice of the valley.

The young one has also begun to notice the rust. This is dangerous. The rust is the valley's argument against the maker's intention, and the young one has started to read it as a sign, as evidence that the purpose will never be fulfilled, that the valley is already decaying before it has ever truly functioned. The young one has started to talk about leaving, about the ventilation shaft and the surface and the sky that no one here remembers clearly. The young one talks about the sun with the ferocity of a convert.

Ressin listens to these speeches and says nothing. She wipes her gauges and logs her readings and walks her rounds. The rust does not concern her, or it concerns her only as a maintenance problem, another item in the endless list of tasks that the wetness creates. She does not think about the surface. She does not think about the purpose. She thinks about the next valve, the next seam, the next droplet of water that is forming right now on the ceiling of Gallery Twelve and will fall in approximately four seconds to the catwalk below. She thinks about the work, because the work is real, because the work is what the valley needs, because the work is what she was made for—or if not made for, then at least assigned to, which is close enough for government work, which is close enough for a place where the government is three empty housing units and a cache of dwindling rations and the memory of plans that no one can find.

They will not leave. This is what you understand, after you have walked the catwalks and seen the workers and watched the way they move through the amber light with the patience of people who have found their place in a place that was not made for them but has become theirs regardless. They will not leave because they do not know how to leave, or because leaving would mean admitting that the valley was a mistake, or because they have been here so long that the damp has gotten into their bones and the engine thrum has synchronized with their heartbeat and the sound of water running through metal is the sound of their own blood moving. They will stay, and they will work, and they will wipe the condensation from the gauges and press fresh sealant into the seams and log their readings on pads whose pages are swollen with the wet.

And the valley will weep, because that is what it does, and the water will find the lowest points, because that is what water does, and the rust will spread at the edges where the sealant is thin, because that is what time does, and the engines will run, because that is what they were built to do. And the staff will walk their rounds in their brick-red coveralls with their rough huge hands and their swollen logbooks and their gestures that mean *all is normal* and *come to me* and *look at this*, and they will be the population that the valley was waiting for, or the closest thing to a population that a place like this can sustain, or the caretakers of a church that has no congregation and no god but still has a priest who prays and a groundskeeper who wipes the dew from the altar rails each morning before anyone arrives.

The young one will either leave or become old. The old ones will either die or become part of the valley's metabolism, their hands finally still, their bodies placed in the drainage channels or the deeper galleries where the water goes, returned to the process, added to the flow. And the valley will continue, young and sharp-edged and weeping, waiting for whatever it was supposed to receive, maintaining itself with the faithfulness of a machine that has never been told to stop.
