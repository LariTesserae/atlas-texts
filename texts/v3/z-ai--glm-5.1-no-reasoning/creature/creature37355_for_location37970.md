# The Inhabitants

No one lives here.

That is the first and most important thing to understand. The chamber is not empty in the way a room is empty—it is empty in the way a sealed sterile chamber is empty, deliberately, constitutionally, by design. The absence of animals is not an ecological gap waiting to be filled. It is a specification. The colonization protocol was written to exclude heterotrophs. No grazer was permitted to thin the canopy. No decomposer was sanctioned to recycle fallen fronds. The seed-pods that clang against the hull-plating do so in absolute biological solitude, and the roots that solder themselves to the industrial grates do so without competition, without predation, without the ancient negotiate-and-consume transactions that govern every other living landscape on the planet. This is a dictatorship of autotrophs, a single-trophic-layer empire, and its inhabitants are its plants.

But to say *the plants live here* is imprecise in a way that matters. The plants do not simply live here. They *are* here, in the same way that the architecture is here—as a material fact, as a continuous state of being. They do not move through the chamber. They are the chamber, or they are becoming the chamber, cell by cell, millimeter by millimeter, soldering themselves into the infrastructure with a patience that makes erosion look hasty.

---

The vines are the first thing you understand as a living presence, because they are the ones whose biology is most legibly *alive* in the way surface-dwellers recognize. The translucent chlorophyll tubes, wrapped around the steel struts, are not parasites. They are something more unsettling: they are interpreters. Each vine is a slow-motion translator of metallurgy into botany. The mineral vapor venting from the factory plating is their atmosphere, their soil, their rain, and their hormonal signal all at once, and the vines have evolved to read it. Their x-ray-vein interiors reveal not just fluid transport but a kind of continuous chemical analysis—their sap darkens or lightens in response to the alloy composition of the strut they have embraced, shifting their uptake to match whatever the plating is off-gassing. Iron-rich vapor produces one growth pattern. Copper traces produce another. The vines are, in a literal sense, tasting the architecture, and their morphology is a record of what they have tasted. A vine wrapped around a load-bearing titanium beam looks fundamentally different from one wrapped around a riveted steel walkway support. They are cataloguing the cathedral as they consume it, and their bodies are the catalogue.

Their root-systems are the most explicitly mechanical organs in the chamber. The biological soldering-irons are not a metaphor. Each root tip terminates in a specialized gland that produces an enzyme-analog—a hot, viscous protein compound that denatures the surface oxide layer of the metal it touches, creating a transient molecular bond between the root's cellulose-equivalent and the bare alloy beneath. The root then grows into that bond, expanding, hardening, and leaving behind a joint that is neither biological nor mechanical but a true hybrid: a weld-seam of living tissue and industrial substrate. Given centuries, the vines would consume the entire structure this way, dissolving and reincorporating every beam, every grate, every riveted panel into their own bodies. But centuries have not yet passed. The root-welds are still young, still sharp-edged, still recognizably *attachments* rather than *transformations*. You can still see where the vine ends and the beam begins. That will not always be true.

---

The fronds are the canopy-dwellers, the broad, warm-colored sheets that overlap in their saturated curtains. They are heavier than they look—waterlogged, dense with the isotonic broth they have absorbed, and held in place by nothing more than surface tension and the tangled architecture of their own stems. A frond that has grown too heavy for its stem does not fall. It *peels*. The slow, gelatinous *shhhhhk* that punctuates the chamber's silence is the sound of a frond's grip failing—its wet underside detaching from the beam or neighboring leaf it has been pressed against, its weight finally exceeding the adhesive force of the water film between them. The separation takes seconds. The frond drifts downward through the viscous medium, rotating slowly, and lands on the floor plating or on the lower canopy with a sound so soft it is more felt than heard—a pressure-change, a displacement, a momentary thickening of the silence.

The fronds are the photosynthetic engines of the chamber, but their relationship to light is unlike anything on the surface. They do not orient toward the filtered luminescent grids. They do not track or reach or angle themselves. Instead, they grow in whatever direction the geometric arrangement of the architecture permits, filling available space with the blind, patient logic of a crystal forming in solution. The light is everywhere and nowhere—diffuse, industrial, without direction—and the fronds have evolved to harvest it without needing to know where it comes from. Their warm coloration is not an autumnal shift or a stress response. It is their baseline. The chlorophyll analog they contain is tuned to the specific wavelength profile of the hidden luminescent grids, and it happens to absorb in the blue-green spectrum while reflecting in the amber-red, giving the entire canopy the appearance of a perpetual, underwater sunset. The fronds are the same color as the light they were designed to eat, which is either a coincidence or something stranger.

---

The seed-pods are the chamber's only exports and its only moments of kinetic drama. They are heavy—denser than the surrounding broth, which is itself denser than surface water—and they fall not with a drift but with a *sink*, a deliberate, vertical descent that ends in a muffled clang against the hull-plating. Each pod is a compressed cylinder of future plant, wrapped in a metallic-hard casing that splits on impact along pre-scored fracture lines. The split is not explosive. It is a slow, mechanical yawn, the casing halves flexing outward over the course of hours as the embryonic root-tip inside begins to swell with absorbed broth. Once the root-tip makes contact with the plating, the soldering process begins, and a new vine starts its long career of interpretation.

The seed-pods fall straight down. They do not scatter. There is no current to carry them, no animal to transport them, no burst-pneumatic dispersal mechanism. Every pod lands directly below its parent, and every new vine begins life in the shadow of the one that produced it. This means the chamber's vegetation is organizing itself in columns—vertical stacks of growth, descending from the upper canopy toward the floor, each stack a clonal lineage, each lineage rooted to the exact spot where its founding pod happened to fall. From the nave of the cathedral, looking up through the curtains of fronds, you would see these columns as pillars of living glass, each one a family tree with its youngest generation at the bottom and its oldest at the top, the whole structure slowly thickening, slowly widening, slowly closing the gaps between columns as the generations accumulate.

This is the dynamic of the chamber. Not growth as explosion, but growth as infill. The plants are filling in the architecture the way water fills a container—completely, inevitably, at the speed of sediment. Eventually, there will be no gap between the columns. Eventually, the vines will weld themselves to each other as readily as they weld to the steel. Eventually, the distinction between plant and architecture will collapse entirely, and the chamber will be a single continuous solid—a hybrid body of ceramic and cellulose, alloy and chlorophyll, with no empty space remaining, no broth to float through, no silence to absorb.

That eventual state is probably centuries away. The chamber is young. The edges are still sharp. The first wave is still in its aggressive phase, still expanding outward, still treating the architecture as something to be claimed rather than something to be replaced.

---

But there is a third presence here, and it is the one that makes the chamber more than a greenhouse.

The chamber itself is alive. Not biologically—the architecture has no cells, no metabolism, no growth cycle. But it is alive in the way a body is alive: it maintains itself, it responds to stimulus, it performs work. The pressure vessels that thrum in the walls are not passive containers. They are regulatory organs, equalizing the immense external pressure of the abyssal shelf against the internal integrity of the module, adjusting continuously, automatically, with the slow unconscious competence of a kidney filtering blood. The luminescent grids are not lamps. They are a metabolism—a controlled conversion of stored energy into the specific light frequencies the flora require, modulated by feedback loops that no one is present to monitor. The ceramic walls breathe. Their pores open and close in response to the silt-load of the water column, filtering particulates with the patient, methodical rhythm of gills. The chamber inhales the cloudy water and exhales the clear.

This is the most disquieting thing about the place. The industrial infrastructure was designed by someone, for someone, to serve a purpose that someone intended. But the someone is gone—drowned, evacuated, dissolved into the new global ocean along with every other surface-dwelling presence. What remains is the infrastructure's autonomic nervous system, the subsystems that were never meant to require conscious oversight, running their cycles in the absence of any consciousness to oversee them. The chamber does not know its creators are gone. It does not know anything. It simply continues, the way a heart continues to beat after the brain has died, the way a factory continues to run after the last worker has walked out.

The plants do not know this either. They do not know the chamber is maintaining the light they eat, or the pressure that holds their water-broth in a liquid state, or the temperature that keeps their metabolisms within their tuned operating range. They do not know they are the first wave of a designed colonization, or that they were designed at all. They simply grow, and the chamber simply sustains, and the two systems interlock without either one acknowledging the other, like a host and a parasite that have evolved into mutual dependence without ever developing the capacity to recognize each other as separate.

---

So: who lives here?

The vines live here, tasting the metal, welding themselves to the struts, building their vertical clonal columns. The fronds live here, heavy and warm-colored, peeling away from the beams in their slow gelatinous descents. The seed-pods live here for the brief moment of their fall and their crack-open landing. The chamber lives here, in its autonomic, pressure-regulating, light-emitting, pore-breathing continuation.

And something else lives here too, something that is harder to name. It is the *relationship*. The interlock. The fact that the plants were designed for this chamber and the chamber was designed for these plants, and that both are now running their cycles in the absence of any designer, slowly becoming each other, slowly filling the last gaps between metal and cellulose, slowly approaching the state where the cathedral will not be a cathedral containing a jungle but a single organism that is both and neither. That relationship is the true inhabitant of this place. It is young, and it is patient, and it is not in any hurry. It has centuries. It has the whole abyssal shelf. It has the silence and the pressure and the warm, filtered light.

It is holding its breath, and it is growing.
