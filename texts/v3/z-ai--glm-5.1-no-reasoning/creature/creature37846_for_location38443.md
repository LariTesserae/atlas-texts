the sound of a body breathing, a body the size of a watershed, a body made entirely of lungs and digestion and the small, busy machines that keep the whole enterprise running.

---

The smallest of them are the size of rice grains, and they are the most numerous. They move in shimmering, smoke-like columns, drifting up from the floor in slow, swirling plumes that seem to follow air currents too subtle for you to feel. Up close—and they are always close, always settling on your sleeves, your eyelashes, the corners of your mouth—they are pale, almost translucent, their tiny bodies catching the amber light like particles of glass. They have no discernible anatomy. No legs, no mandibles, no segments. They are simply *shapes*: teardrops and oblongs and rough, asymmetrical blobs that flex and extend pseudopods when they need to grip a surface, then retract them when they release. Under magnification, you might see fine cilia along their edges—too small for your naked eye, but you can feel them, a faint, velvety brushing wherever they touch skin. They leave no mark. They seem to feed on nothing, or on nothing you can perceive—perhaps the condensation itself, the mineral-rich sweat that forms on every surface. Perhaps they feed on you, in some infinitesimal way, but so gently, so fractionally, that your body would never notice the loss.

They do not avoid you. They do not seek you. You are simply another warm surface in a place where warm surfaces are the rule, and they treat you with the same indifferent occupancy they afford the stone.

---

The mid-sized presences—the ones that move with purpose across the floor and walls—are harder to look at, not because they are hideous, but because they resist being seen. Your eyes slide off them. Your brain insists on categorizing them as rock, as shadow, as tricks of the light, and you have to actively, forcefully *look* in order to make them resolve into bodies.

When you do, you wish you hadn't.

They are flat. Not flattened by pressure, but inherently, intentionally flat, as if the whole concept of thickness is something they opted out of. They are roughly oval—though "roughly" does a lot of work in that sentence—and they move by a kind of coordinated ripple that travels along their underside, a muscular wave that propels them across the stone with startling speed when they choose to use it. Most of the time, they don't choose. Most of the time, they lie still, overlapping in shingled layers across the warm rock, and you realize with a surge of vertigo that the floor itself—the mottled, uneven, half-formed floor you've been walking on—is not primarily stone at all. It is *them*. A living carpet, inches deep in places, built up from layer upon layer upon layer of these quiet, patient, flat bodies, all pressed together, all pulsing in that same slow, synchronized rhythm you feel in the rock.

They are warm. You know this because you stepped on one—or *through* one—and it did not flinch, did not recoil, simply compressed beneath your boot and slowly re-inflated after your weight passed. A faint, spongy resistance. A warmth that was distinct from the stone's warmth. A give that should have been disgusting but was instead almost tender, like pressing your hand into fresh dough.

They do not react to you as a threat. They do not react to you as anything. You are weather. You are a pressure event. You pass through, and they close around the space you occupied, and the floor is whole again, and whatever you disturbed has already been forgotten.

---

The armored ones are the loudest, though loud is a relative term in a place where the ambient sound never drops below a murmur. They click. They scrape. Their bodies are segmented in a way that suggests borrowed architecture—like something that looked at the idea of an exoskeleton and reproduced it without understanding the engineering. Their plates overlap incorrectly, leaving soft tissue visible at the joints, and they move with a hitching, uneven gait that speaks of a body still calibrating, still working out the kinks in its own design.

They are the size of your hand, some of them. Some are larger. They travel singly or in loose, drifting clusters, and they seem to be the only things here that acknowledge each other's existence—tapping their plates together in patterns that might be communication, might be collision, might be both. When two of them meet, they pause. They touch. Their jointed appendages—there are too many to count, and the number seems to vary between individuals—probe at each other's armor, running along the seams, the gaps, the exposed softness. Then they separate and move on. It looks like inspection. It looks like greeting. It looks like repair.

They are the only things here that seem to notice you. When one of them encounters your foot, your leg, the strap of your bag, it stops. It extends those too-many appendages and touches your surface with a delicacy that feels almost reverent. It taps. It clicks. It takes the measure of your hardness, your texture, your warmth. And then—always—it moves on. Whatever criteria it uses to evaluate what it finds, you do not meet it. You are not what it is looking for. The relief you feel is complicated.

---

And then there are the ones in the pools.

You avoided the pools at first. The water was too warm, too organic, too reminiscent of something you didn't want to name. But as the hours passed and the terrain forced you closer, you began to see what moved within them.

They are the largest things here. By far. The widest of the shallow pools contains a body that stretches from edge to edge—ten feet across, maybe more—and it moves with a terrible, slow patience, rotating in place, stirring the warm water with a motion that is less swimming than *kneading*. It is segmented. It is cylindrical. Its body is the color of the water, which is the color of amber light filtered through mineral and time, and its segments flex and contract in waves that travel from one end to the other in a loop that never stops. It has no head. It has no tail. It is a continuous, recursive motion, a body that exists to move and moves to exist, and when you crouch at the pool's edge and watch it, you feel something like awe and something like nausea and something like the recognition of a pattern so fundamental that your own spine echoes it.

It does not breach the surface. It does not need to. Its back—if back is the word—is always just below the waterline, and the water clings to it in a smooth meniscus, and you realize that the pool is not a pool in the way you understand pools. It is a pore. A socket. A place where one of these vast, slow presences fits precisely, and the water around it is not separate from it but secreted by it, a warm bath of its own making, and the pool exists because *it* exists, not the other way around.

There are other pools. Other bodies. Some are smaller—only a few feet across, still forming, still growing into their hollows. Some are so large that their pools extend beyond your sight, and whatever moves in those depths has a ripple that takes full seconds to travel from one side to the other, a motion like the breathing of something that has never known urgency.

---

What lives here is not a collection of species. That framework is too tidy, too separate, too *aboveground*. What lives here is a process. A continuous, slow, hot, wet process that has been running since before the stone decided what it wanted to be—a process that is, in some sense, what the stone is deciding to be. The flat ones and the armored ones and the translucent specks and the segmented, turning presences in the pools are not distinct organisms in the way you want them to be. They are phases. Expressions. Organs of the same body, or organs of the same *impulse*, and the impulse is incubation, is growth, is the patient assembly of something that has not yet finished assembling itself.

You are in a nursery. You have understood this since you smelled the water. But you have been thinking about it wrong. You have been imagining small, vulnerable things being tended by larger, more competent things. That is how nurseries work where you come from.

Here, the nursery is not a room. The nursery is not a building. The nursery is a *stratum*—an entire layer of the earth, reaching who knows how far in every direction, and what is being nurtured is not a brood or a clutch or a generation. What is being nurtured is the chamber itself. The rock. The water. The heat. The air you are breathing, which is thick with spores you cannot see and moisture you can feel settling in your lungs with every breath.

The place is growing. The place is alive. And everything that moves within it—the seething, clicking, rippling, breathing everything—is its immune system, its bloodstream, its scaffolding, its flesh.

---

Eventually, you will leave. You will find the passage you came through, or another one, and you will climb back toward the surface, and the heat will fade, and the air will thin, and the amber light will dim until it is just a memory you half-believe. You will shower for a long time. You will scrub the mineral residue from your skin and the faint, velvety film from your forearms where the translucent specks thinned and spread and then, eventually, brushed away. You will cough, once or twice, and tell yourself it is the dry air.

But for weeks afterward, in the quiet moments before sleep, you will feel it: a faint, slow pulse in the soles of your feet when you stand on stone. A warmth that rises from the ground in certain rooms, certain basements, certain places where the earth close to the surface remembers what it is like below. A click, a rustle, a wet and distant scraping at the edge of hearing.

You were not a threat. You were not prey. You were not even, really, a visitor.

You were a sample. A reading. A brush of appendages against an unfamiliar surface, briefly noted and then forgotten.

But the place does not forget. The place does not forget anything. The place is still deciding what it wants to be, and now, in some infinitesimal way, it has decided to include a little bit of you.
