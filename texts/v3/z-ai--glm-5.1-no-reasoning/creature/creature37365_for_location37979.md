Who lives in the High Scour must be a question asked loosely, for nothing here merely *lives*—everything endures, adapts, and is ultimately scoured. The humanoids who built those mortarless stone shelters, who carved the spirals into the hard outcrops, are gone, perhaps subsumed by the moss or crushed by a sudden swell of the breathing crust. But the Scour is not empty. It is populated by those who have solved the riddle of the turbulence: to survive here, you must not resist the movement. You must become a part of the frequency.

The primary inhabitants are the Verge-walkers.

They are descendants of the builders, though the erosion of the plateau has worked on them just as it worked on the mountains. They are tall, shockingly thin, their bodies worn down by the acoustic assault and the low-yield atmosphere to an aerodynamic spareness. Their skin is the texture and color of the dense, glass-like strata of stone—fissured, wind-polished, rust-red and bone-white in patchy camouflage. They do not speak; the Scour made speech obsolete eons ago. Instead, they communicate through a subsonic tactile language, pressing their long, calloused fingers against the stone to read the tremors, or tapping rhythms against the dense vines that bridge the chasms. A tap travels for kilometers through the vegetative connective tissue, a slow, reverberating morse code of the deep plateau.

The Verge-walkers are nomads of the slow surge. They do not fight the turbulence; they read it with a precognitive intuition born of lifetimes spent on the heaving crust. When the ground begins to swell—a ridge forming over days—they dismantle their stone shelters, moving the heavy blocks to the subsiding troughs, reassembling them just before the old ridge collapses and a new depression forms. They are always moving, always building and unbuilding, locked in a Sisyphian architectural dance. When the deep thrum of the plateau changes pitch, signaling a rapid shift, they drop flat, pressing their ears to the moss, listening to the stomach of the world, waiting for the stone to find its new equilibrium.

They are deeply symbiotic with the Scour's impossible vegetation. The Verge-walkers do not eat the moss; they wear it. They cultivate "cloaks" of the living emerald stratum across their shoulders and backs. This mobile moss draws the sideways rain and condensing mists, funneling drinking water directly to their mouths through hollow reed-stems woven into their collars. The sulfur-yellow and rust-orange flowers grow from their garments, rotating on their chest and shoulders to track the moisture-rich winds, acting as organic weather vanes. To stand still in the mist is to watch a Verge-walker slowly vanish into the green, becoming just another mossy hummock on the shifting plain.

Their relationship with the fauna is one of mutual indifference and occasional utility. The striders, those three-meter green phantoms, are occasionally ridden—never tamed. A Walker might wait motionless for days beside a transient pool until a strider comes to drink, then leap upon its jointed back, guiding it not with reins but by leaning into the creature's instinctual path-finding, allowing it to step precisely where the stone is stable. When the strider drops its head to feed, the rider dismounts and fades away, leaving the beast unaware it was ever a vessel.

The swimmers in the transient pools are sacred to them. When the depressions form and the rain boils up, the Verge-walkers gather. They step into the shallow, humming water, letting the swimmers—those transparent, ephemeral ghosts—hatch against their legs. The Walkers believe the swimmers are the thoughts of the plateau given form, briefly instantiated before the water drains back into the stone. They anoint their foreheads with the pool water, coating their skin in the gelatinous eggs that will lie dormant in their pores for decades, ensuring that when the next flood comes, wherever they are, the swimmers will be born again.

But the most telling aspect of the Verge-walkers, the thing that proves they are no longer quite human, is how they sleep.

The roar of the Scour would shatter the mind of any lowlander within hours, driving them to madness or deafness. But the Walkers have turned the acoustic assault into a cradle. At night, they navigate to the deep erosion channels where the subterranean water roars its loudest, and they seek out the active colonies of the borers. They lie down in the soft chalky stone, pressing their spines against the rock. The crunching, grinding rhythm of the borers' teeth, combined with the resonant chord of the plateau, produces a frequency that induces a state akin to suspended animation. Their heartbeats slow to match the glacial breathing of the crust; their brainwaves synchronize with the weird, composed harmonics of the stone. They do not dream in images; they dream in vibration. They dream of the roots of the vines threading endlessly downward, into the dark, wet secret that moves the world.

In the morning, when the temperature shifts and the mist rises, the chord changes. The Verge-walkers wake, shake the condensation from their moss cloaks, and listen to the shift in the wind. The ground beneath their feet rumbles, rising a few centimeters to the east. 

They pick up their stone tools, and they begin to walk.
