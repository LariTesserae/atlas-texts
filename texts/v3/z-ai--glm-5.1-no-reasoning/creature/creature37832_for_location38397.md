They are called the Thrice-Settled, though not by themselves—they have no need for names, for names require a listener, and there is nothing here that listens.

Their bodies tell the story of adaptation to a place that requires no adaptation. Generations in the fever-warmth have left them with skin like old parchment left too near a hearth—parchment that has forgotten it was ever paper, that has become something closer to cured leather, something closer to the soot-stained brick they walk between. It is not an unhealthy pallor. It is simply the color of belonging. Their skin has learned to hold the heat without sweating, to radiate it outward at the exact rate it arrives, so that they exist in perfect thermal equilibrium with their environment. They do not shiver. They do not flush. They are as temperature-stable as the fused silica beneath their feet.

Their eyes are the most obvious change. In a world of bruised twilight and sodium-vapor gloom, vision has become a vestigial sense—like the pale creatures that sometimes scuttle along runoff channels, the Thrice-Settled have begun to lose the organs that no longer serve. Their eyes are not gone, not yet, but they have retreated. The sockets have grown shallow, the lids heavy and hooded, perpetually half-lowered against a brightness that never comes. What sight remains is tuned to narrow bands: the orange fissure of a blast furnace, the sulfur-yellow glow of the lamps. They navigate by heat and vibration more than light, reading the world through the soles of their feet and the pulse of the subsonic engines in their marrow. A stranger approaching would be felt before being seen—a displacement in the warm air, a subtle alteration in the rhythm of footfalls.

They are not born in the way their distant ancestors were born. The Settlements contain deep chambers, lower even than the engine rooms, where the heat is dense and wet and close. Here, in pools of mineral-laden water warmed by the mechanical hearts below, new inhabitants are grown. It is not reproduction in any biological sense—no union, no genetic recombination. It is replication. A body is assembled from the same templates that have been used for uncounted generations, formed from the dissolved minerals and the tepid water and the endless warmth. They emerge adult, already knowing the layout of the streets, already feeling the pull of the engines' rhythm. They do not have childhoods. They do not have parents. They simply arrive, and take their place in the cycle.

Their hands are their most expressive feature—broad, callused, with thick nails that have hardened into something like iron. A Thrice-Settled can run their palm along a pipe and tell you, without looking, the temperature and pressure of the steam within. They can press their ear to a gear housing and diagnose a misalignment three stories down. These hands are their connection to the machines, and the machines are their connection to existence. There is no separation between maintainer and maintained. When a Thrice-Settled oils a piston, it is as much an act of self-care as when they slowly, methodically chew the compressed nutrient cakes produced in the Settlement's lower levels. The cakes taste of mineral and nothing. They do not mind. Mind, in the way we understand it, is something they have largely set aside.

They do not think in propositions. They do not plan for tomorrow or remember yesterday. Their consciousness, if it can be called that, operates in the same closed loop as the engines: sensation, response, sensation, response. The warmth on their skin tells them to walk toward the furnace that needs stoking. The vibration in the floor tells them which corridor to patrol. The sound of a piston missing its stroke pulls them like a current toward the necessary repair. It is not obedience, and it is not instinct. It is something in between—a state of being so perfectly attuned to a single environment that choice becomes irrelevant. There are no choices to make in Uval-Kth. There is only the next step in the endless cycle.

Occasionally, very rarely, one of them will stop walking. They will stand in the warm dark, between the black iron walls, and something will pass across their half-lidded eyes—a flicker, a confusion, a moment in which the cycle stutters and they become aware, just barely, of the weight of time. In these moments, they might look toward the horizon, where the plateau dissolves into the black air. They might feel, in some inaccessible chamber of their being, a pull toward the edge, toward the three-kilometer drop to the basalt foundations, toward anything that is not this. 

But the moment passes. The engines thrum. The warmth presses in. And they turn back to their work.

They are not unhappy. That is the important thing to understand. Unhappiness requires the concept of an alternative, and they have none. They are not prisoners, for there is no warden and no cell. They are not slaves, for there is no master and no labor extracted. They are components—organic, warm, and exquisitely adapted—of a system that has achieved perfect equilibrium. The Settlements breathe because they breathe. The engines run because they maintain them. The heat persists because they, too, are sources of heat, walking slowly through the dark canyons, adding their own faint thermal signature to the endless, breathless warmth.

If you could ask them who they are, they would not understand the question. Not because they lack language—they could speak if they chose to, their throats still capable of producing sound—but because the question implies a self that exists apart from function. They are what they do. And what they do is continue.

At night, if night can be said to exist in a place of perpetual twilight, they gather in the deep interiors of the Settlements, in chambers where the engine-thrum is strongest. They do not sleep as we sleep. They enter a state that might be called rest, sitting in rows along the iron walls, their breathing synchronized with the piston strokes, their hearts beating in time with the subterranean rhythm. It is not communion. It is not comfort. It is simply the most efficient configuration—the point at which their own internal cycles align most perfectly with the cycles of the machines, and for a time, they are indistinguishable from what they maintain.

In the morning, which is identical to the night, they rise and return to their routes. The soot settles on their shoulders. The warmth embraces them. And the plateau of Uval-Kth turns another degree in the dark, patient and eternal, its inhabitants as much a part of its geology as the iron-hard pan beneath their slow, deliberate feet.
