What lives here are the Ember-Mantles—though they have no name for themselves, possessing no language, no need for one, no organ that could shape sound into anything but the dry percussion of their own bodies. They are a species that has solved existence so completely that evolution simply stopped trying. There is nothing to improve. They are perfect machines for a single purpose: *to be where the heat is.*

Consider the individual. It is the size of a human palm, yes, but that description fails to capture the particular density of the thing. An Ember-Mantle weighs twice what you expect when you hold one—though you will never hold one willingly, and they will never offer themselves to be held. Their heft comes from a chitin thick with incorporated minerals, metals drawn from the volcanic breath and bound into their shells like armor plating forged from the earth's own slag. Iron gives their carapace that deep burgundy hue. Sulfur yellows the joints. Copper traces green-blue lines along the spiracles, delicate as circuitry, marking where the hottest air enters their bodies. They are walking ore. They are ingots that learned to move.

The underside is where the living happens. Beneath the armored shell, the legs—six of them, short and jointed and tipped with grip-hairs that can cling to stone still viscous with heat—are merely infrastructure. The real organ is the ventral plate: a broad, flat expanse of tissue that presses against warm rock like a tongue against a roof of a mouth. This is the feeder. This is what metabolizes temperature. The tissue is dark, almost black, glistening with a secretion that protects it from burning while maximizing thermal conductivity. When an Ember-Mantle feeds, it flattens itself completely, shell parallel to stone, and the ventral plate drinks. You can watch it happen in the dull red glow of the heating wall: a creature settling onto fresh basalt, its body shuddering once as the heat enters, and then a stillness so profound it seems to have died. It has not died. It is eating.

They do not think. Attribution of consciousness to the Ember-Mantle is the gravest mistake an observer can make, because it leads to the second mistake: the belief that they recognize you. They do not. Your arrival in the chamber produces a physical response—the rearrangement of bodies to accommodate a new obstacle—but it is no more aware than the way water flows around a stone. You displace them. They flow. The hollow that opens for your body is not hospitality; it is physics. You are an object between them and their food. They reshape themselves to minimize the interruption. If you stood still for ten thousand years, they would incorporate you completely, colonizing your cooled remains with the same patience they bring to every surface that stops moving.

And yet—*and yet*—there is something in their collective behavior that resists simple dismissal. The way they mass. The way they breathe together. The spiracles along their flanks pulse in visible rhythm, and when hundreds of them cluster shoulder to shoulder, the rhythm synchronizes, becomes a single respiration that moves through the colony like a slow wave. This is not communication. It is not cooperation in any meaningful sense. It is simply what happens when identical organisms share identical needs in an enclosed space: their cycles align. But standing in the chamber, feeling that shared pulse through the soles of your boots, it is difficult not to feel that you are in the presence of a single vast creature rather than a million small ones.

The old ones tell you everything you need to know about their history. The floor of the chamber is layered with discarded carapaces—molts, shed as the creatures grow, accumulating in drifts that compact under their own weight into a brittle insulation. The deepest layers are different. If you could dig down through the centuries of shed shells—a thing the living Mantles would not permit, as the insulation preserves heat and disturbing it would cool their precious floor—you would find carapaces of a different shape. Longer. Narrower. Equipped with mouthparts designed for chewing, not just pressing. The ancestral Mantles ate stone directly, grinding basalt between their jaws, extracting what meager thermal energy they could from rock that had already cooled. They were generalists. Scavengers. They survived on the margins of heat, in cracks and fissures where the earth's warmth was a rumor rather than a certainty.

Then the chamber was built.

The engineering of the pocket—its perfect angles, its thermal funneling, its concentration of geothermal flow into a single collectors' space—transformed the Mantles from survivors into inhabitants. The niche was so rich, so perfectly tuned to concentrate what they needed, that they no longer required the machinery of survival. Mouthparts atrophied. Jaws fused. The ventral plate expanded, refined, became a single dedicated organ for absorbing concentrated heat. They lost the ability to eat anything else. They became dependent on the architecture that housed them, in the way that a creature born in a terrarium becomes shaped by the dimensions of its glass.

This is why they cannot leave. Not because the exit is too small or the climb too arduous—though both are true—but because the world beyond the chamber is *cold*. Not cold as you understand it, but cold as they do: an absence so total it means starvation. A Mantle removed from the heat source will survive for perhaps an hour, its body consuming its own reserves in a desperate attempt to maintain the temperature its metabolism requires. Then it will slow. Its movements will become erratic, then feeble, then still. Its shell will fade from burgundy to grey. The spiracles will cease their pulse. It will become what it has always been at its core: a piece of the earth, no more alive than the stone it once fed upon.

They do not know they are imprisoned. The concept requires a model of the world that extends beyond the chamber's walls, and the chamber is their world entire. The glow of the heating stone is their sun. The breath of the vent above is their weather. The fresh extrusions of rock are their seasons, arriving unpredictably, bringing feasts of warmth that send the colony into spasms of colonization so intense the sound becomes a sustained shriek of shell against shell. They lack the cognitive architecture for boredom or despair. They exist in a perpetual present of feeding, growing, molting, and feeding again, packed so tightly that individual identity is a fiction—each Mantle is a cell in a larger organism, interchangeable, replaceable, defined only by its position in the thermal gradient.

The soft ones are the only deviation. When a Mantle molts, the new shell beneath is pale, almost white, and the creature that emerges is vulnerable in a way its armored kin are not. The soft ones burrow into the thickest part of the colony, seeking not just heat but the physical protection of surrounding bodies. They are hedged in by their elders, shouldered into gaps where their exposed flesh cannot be scraped by stone or singed by direct contact with the hottest surfaces. If there is tenderness in this behavior, it is entirely accidental—a mature Mantle jostling a soft one toward the center of the mass is no more nurturing than a blood cell carrying oxygen. But the effect is the same. The young survive. The colony persists. The slow pulse continues.

There is no parent. No child. The Mantles reproduce by a process so simple it barely qualifies: when enough heat has been absorbed, when the body reaches a threshold of stored energy, a bud forms at the posterior of the ventral plate—a tiny nub of tissue that swells over days until it detaches, a new Mantle in miniature, already armored in a soft translucent shell that will harden within hours. The parent does not notice the departure. The offspring does not seek the parent. They are machines that build smaller machines when fuel is sufficient. Love is a surface-dweller's invention, meaningless in a world where warmth is the only currency and every body is a bank.

The sound they make—*that* sound, the rattling, hissing, chitin-on-chitin percussion that fills the chamber like a physical substance—is their only byproduct. They produce no waste. Their metabolisms are nearly closed systems, converting thermal energy into growth with an efficiency that would shame any surface-bound organism. But they cannot silence themselves. The movement of bodies against bodies, the tap of grip-hairs on stone, the crack of a molting shell splitting open—these are the exhalations of their existence, the proof that they are alive and not merely geological formations that happen to move. The sound is their signature, their mark on the world, and it never ceases. Even in the deepest stillness, when the colony has settled into its feeding trance and the only motion is the slow pulse of spiracles, there is a low susurrus, a whisper of shell on shell, a reminder that the chamber is not empty, has never been empty, will never be empty as long as the heat flows and the earth continues its slow birth.

What lives here is patience. What lives here is hunger so perfectly met that it has become peace. What lives here is the answer to a question the builders of the chamber never thought to ask: *What happens when you create a paradise and then abandon it?* The Mantles happened. They found it, they filled it, they became it. They are the tenants of an empty house, the congregation of a dead church, the citizens of a city that was never meant for them but fits them better than it ever fit its makers.

They are the mountain's only children. And they will outlast us all.
