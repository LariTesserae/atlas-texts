The people of Old Vosth do not think of themselves as a distinct ethnicity, because they have no memory of any other way to be. They are of the mountain as wholly as the moss or the carriers or the grey schist itself. If you asked them who they are, they would give you their trade, their terrace, their mother's name. They do not have a word for outsider, because the concept rarely occurs to them; they have a word for *the ones who leave*, which is also the word for *the ones who die*, though leaving is possible and dying is certain, and the conflation tells you something about how they imagine the boundaries of the world.

They are not tall. The altitude and the diet see to that. Generations of thin air and limited calories have compressed them, made them dense and efficient, their chests deep, their limbs compact, their bones thick with the calcium they mine from the mountain's own skeleton. They move through their steep city with a gait that visitors find unsettling — too slow for walking, too rhythmic for climbing, a kind of continuous controlled fall that relies on the angles of the stairs and the streets as a swimmer relies on the current. They do not walk *on* Old Vosth. They walk *with* it. Their feet are broad and calloused; many do not wear shoes below the Fourth Terrace, where the moss is thick enough to serve as a cushion and the stone is polished smooth by ten thousand years of bare and sandaled feet.

Their skin is the color of the stone. This is not metaphor. It is the color of the Paleozoic schist — a greenish-grey, a charcoal touched faintly with the mineral tones of iron and copper and the green of the moss that they are always touching, always breathing, always eating in small quantities as it flavors their water and their food and coats the surfaces of their homes. They are born paler and darken with age, as if the mountain were slowly claiming them, and the eldest among them, the ones who have spent eighty or ninety years breathing the mist and handling the stone and sleeping in the fungi-light, have a faintly luminous quality to their skin, a shimmer that is visible only in the dimmest interiors, where the bioluminescence of the walls catches the bioluminescence that has colonized their own skin. They are not glowing. They are *colonized*, in the gentlest possible sense — inhabited by the same organisms that inhabit everything, and this is not considered remarkable or disturbing. It is simply what happens to a body that lives long enough in a place like this.

Their hair is dark, straight, fine, and they wear it long, bound back with cord or, among the factory workers, tucked beneath caps made from carrier-wool. The wool is dark too, almost black, and when they wear it layered in the traditional fashion — a loose inner robe, a heavier outer coat, a shawl for the evenings — they have the appearance of the ironwood trees: dark, dense, rooted to their spot, barely moving in the wind that does not come. Their eyes are the one feature that departs from the grey-green palette. Their eyes are pale — grey, blue-grey, occasionally a washed-out green — and they do not reflect light so much as absorb it, which gives them an expression of permanent inwardness, as if they were watching something behind their own thoughts.

---

They speak Vosthi, which is not related to any language group on the surrounding lowlands. The linguists who have attempted classification (again, few) have left frustrated, their comparative methods useless against a language that has no cognates, no borrowed roots, no evidence of contact with any other tongue. It is a language of murmurs. The phonemes are soft, indistinct, heavy on nasals and fricatives, light on plosives — the language of people who do not want to be overheard, who have evolved their speech to be absorbed by the moss and the damp stone before it can travel far. Vosthi carries perhaps ten meters in the open air. In the corridors between buildings, where the moss is thick and the walls are close, it carries five. The residents have compensated by developing a system of gestures that accompanies and occasionally replaces speech — small movements of the hands, the shoulders, the eyes, subtle enough that a visitor would not notice them, specific enough that a resident can conduct an entire transaction in the market without speaking a word.

The market occupies the Ninth Terrace, which is broad and flat and has the best exposure to what passes for sunlight. The goods are practical: carrier-wool cloth, stone-cut tools, mushrooms cultivated in the drainage grooves, lye soap from the factories, ironwork of extraordinary precision, fermented preparations of the moss that serve as medicine and seasoning and, in the evenings, as a mild intoxicant. There is no meat sold in the market. The carriers are too valuable to eat, the insects too small to bother with, and whatever else might be available is not discussed. The residents are not vegetarian by conviction but by circumstance, and their bodies show it — they are lean, dense, long-lived, slow to heal and slow to sicken, with the patient metabolisms of people who have been calibrated over generations to a limited diet and thin air.

Children are rare. This is the other thing you notice. The population is not growing; it is maintaining, holding steady at a number that seems to have been determined long ago by forces the residents do not control. Families are small — one child, sometimes two, rarely three — and the birth rate matches the death rate with a precision that, like the precision of the architecture and the machinery, suggests an engineering that no living resident can claim. They do not remark on this. They love their children with a fierceness that is all the more intense for its compression, and they raise them in the way that people raise children in a place that does not change: they teach them what they were taught, show them what they were shown, and trust that the mountain will do the rest.

The children learn to read the stone. This is the first skill — not language, not trade, but *reading*: the ability to look at a wall and determine whether it is load-bearing, to look at a drainage groove and estimate the flow, to look at a crack and know whether it has been there for a year or a millennium. The stone is their text, their scripture, their daily news. Everything that matters in Old Vosth is written in rock, and the residents are literate in a way that has nothing to do with the alphabets they also know. They can read the moisture content of a wall by touching it. They can read the stress on a retaining structure by listening to it. They can read the age of a building by the particular species of moss growing on its eastern face. This is not specialized knowledge, not the province of engineers or scholars. This is what everyone knows, the way everyone knows how to breathe.

---

The elders are the true keepers of Old Vosth, though they would reject the title and the responsibility it implies. To be old in Vosth is to have become a feature of the landscape. The elders do not retire, because there is no retirement from a place that is not working toward anything — the factories run, the maintenance continues, but there is no progress to retire from, no destination to rest after reaching. The elders simply slow. They move less, speak less, sit more in the small courtyards and on the steps and in the dim interiors, and they listen. They listen to the factories and the water and the mountain, and sometimes, in the evenings, they answer. Not with words. With a kind of attunement — a shifting of the body, a settling, a way of being present that the mountain seems to recognize. The offerings left at the boundaries are, increasingly, left by the elders, and the offerings have changed over the generations: less food now, more worked stone, more complex arrangements, as if the conversation between the listeners and the listened-to has been evolving toward something that no participant can name.

The elders do not die differently than anyone else in Vosth, but they are mourned differently. A death in the lower terraces is a practical event — the body is taken to the Seventh Terrace, where the crematory ovens share a chimney with a factory, and the smoke rises white and mineral-scented and joins the mist. But an elder's death is accompanied by a ritual that the residents call *the returning*, which involves carrying the body to the boundary between the highest inhabited terrace and the raw mountainside and leaving it there, wrapped in carrier-wool, beside a new cairn. By morning, the body is gone. Not taken by animals — there are no scavengers at this altitude, and the carriers are docile herbivores besides. Gone. Absorbed. Returned. The residents do not discuss what this means. They do it because it has always been done, and because it feels correct, and because the mountain, they believe, knows what to do with its own.

---

The factory workers are the closest thing to a separate caste, though caste implies hierarchy and the workers do not consider themselves below or above anyone. They are simply the people who work the machines, and the machines require a kind of person that the terraces do not. They are stronger, for one thing — the work is physical, the hours are long, and the thrumming of the machinery, which the rest of the city feels as a distant pulse, the workers feel in their bones, their teeth, their spines, with an intimacy that changes a body over time. Factory workers develop a slight tremor in their hands, not from damage but from resonance — their bodies have adapted to vibrate with the machines, and even outside the factory, even in the silence of their homes, the tremor persists, a reminder that they are, in some sense, still connected to the machinery they serve.

They are proud of this. The tremor is a mark of belonging, a visible sign of their pact with the devices that keep the city alive. They wear their hair shorter than the rest of the population, and they favor tight-fitting caps and high-collared jackets that protect against the lye-steam and the heat. Their skin is darker than the terrace-dwellers — not from sun, which is weak, but from the heated stone and the chemical vapors and the proximity to the furnaces. They are the most outgoing of Vosth's residents, the most likely to make eye contact, the most likely to speak above a murmur, as if the noise of the factory had colonized their voices as well as their bodies. They are also the most likely to leave. Not permanently — the mountain calls them back, always — but in their youth, some of the factory workers descend the switchback trail with the carrier-trains, and they see the lowlands, the green world, the places where the air is thick and the sky is blue and birds exist, and they come back changed, quieter, more certain of their allegiance to the grey.

What the factories produce is not a secret, exactly. It is a *propriety* — a matter of knowing that certain things are not to be discussed outside the Fourth through Seventh Terraces, and that the wrapped packages on the carrier-trains are not to be opened, and that the lye and the heated stone and the precision-machined components serve a purpose that the mountain itself has ordained. The residents of the higher terraces suspect — they do not know, but they feel — that the factories are making replacement parts. Not for the machines themselves, which are maintained in the usual way, but for something else. Something larger. Something that requires, and has required for millennia, a slow, steady supply of precisely manufactured components that are carried down the mountain and dispersed to destinations that no one in Vosth can name. The factory workers know more than the others, or they believe they do, and this belief — that they are participating in a project whose scope exceeds their understanding — gives them their particular gravity, their sense of weight and purpose, their willingness to vibrate with the machines until their bones hum.

---

There is a quality to the people of Old Vosth that visitors find difficult to name. It is not sadness — they are not sad, or not sadder than anyone living in a place this beautiful and this strange. It is not resignation — they are active, engaged, fully present in their maintenance of the city and the machines and each other. It is *completion*. They are a people who have arrived at the thing they were meant to be. Not a destination, not an achievement, but a *form* — the way a river arrives at its final shape, the way a canyon arrives at its depth, the way a species arrives at its adaptation. They are the human beings who fit this particular notch in this particular mountain, and they know it, and the knowing gives them a stillness that is not passivity but *rest* — the rest of a body that has found its position, a voice that has found its pitch, a soul that has found its bedrock.

They do not dream of leaving. The ones who leave come back. The ones who stay do not wonder what else the world might hold. They have their work and their moss and their dim light and their offerings and their quiet voices and their slow children and their vibrating elders and their unanswered questions about the listeners and the terraces and the machines, and they live within these things the way a person lives within a house that was built before they were born and will stand after they are gone: with care, with attention, with a respect for the walls that is indistinguishable from love.

At night, when the mist comes down and the fungi glow and the factories shift their rhythm and the mountain breathes, the people of Old Vosth sit in their dim rooms and listen. They do not listen *for* anything. They listen *to* — to the city, to the stone, to the slow pulse of the machines and the slower pulse of the mountain and the barely-perceptible pulse of something else, something deeper, something that might be the listeners or might be the terraces or might be the geological heartbeat of the Spine itself. They listen the way you listen to a person who is sleeping beside you: not for information, not for meaning, but for the simple reassurance of presence. The mountain is there. The city is there. The listeners are there, at the edge, in the dark, taking their offerings, keeping their silence. And the people are there, in the grey, in the damp, in the moss-scented air, in the faint blue light, doing what they have always done: maintaining, repairing, living in a shape they did not choose and do not question, waiting for nothing, expecting nothing, simply *being*, with a completeness that the rest of us, in our bright and noisy and temporary cities, will never achieve and perhaps cannot even imagine.
