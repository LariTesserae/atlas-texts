# What Lives Here

Nothing lives here. Everything lives here. The distinction collapsed so long ago that the question itself has been absorbed into the limestone—another thin layer of calcium carbonate laid down by something that might once have been an organism and is now just another stratum in a stack that goes back three billion years.

But let me try to speak of what moves.

---

The large slow shapes first, because they are what you will almost see.

They are the closest thing to fauna this place has produced, and they may not be fauna at all. If you could hold one—and you cannot, because they do not hold still and because holding implies a boundary that they do not possess—you would find that it has the consistency of the mat itself: dense, wet, fibrous, warm. They are the mat, or were the mat, or will be the mat. They are portions of the living carpet that have differentiated from the substrate and elected to move, though *elected* overstates the degree of intention involved. They do not elect. They simply become mobile, the way a wave does not elect to break but does so because the conditions demand it.

They have no heads. They have no eyes. They have no mouths, though they feed: you see the evidence in the circular scars they leave on the broad frond surfaces, rasped clean by an organ or structure that you will never observe because they stop moving when observed. They are the size of human torsos, perhaps, or the size of doors, or the size of rooms—the light down here plays tricks with scale, and the viscosity of the water bends the geometry of perception until you cannot trust the evidence of your own senses. What you can trust is the trails. Channels through the vegetation where the fronds have been pressed aside and slowly, over hours or days, pressed back. The feeding scars. The sense, when one passes near you, of a displacement in the water that is not current and not pulse but something else—a deliberate movement, a body navigating.

There may be one. There may be many. They never occupy the same space at the same time, or if they do, you cannot tell them apart. They are the same creature, perhaps, a single diffuse organism that moves through itself like blood through a body, now here, now there, always part of the same circulation. Or they are many, and their many-ness is simply an illusion produced by a place where the boundary between one and many has dissolved.

Their solitude is absolute. They do not communicate. They do not interact. They drift through the canopy like clouds through a sky that has forgotten it is a sky. If they have inner lives—thoughts, sensations, a dim awareness of their own trajectory—you will never know. They are too old for the question. They belong to a lineage of being that predates the concept of individuality by a billion years, and they have not adopted it since.

What they are, more than anything, is the plateau mobilized. The living mat extending itself into a new dimension—not upward, like the fronds, but sideways, through space, carrying with it a portion of the mat's slow consciousness. When you are near one, you feel what you feel near the mat itself: a pressure, a presence, a warmth, a sense of being noticed by something that does not have eyes.

---

Beneath them, or rather within them, because there is no *beneath* in a place where layers fold into each other like the inside of a lung, live the things that make the plateau breathe.

They are small. They are everywhere. They are the reason the water is warm.

You will never see them individually, because they are too small and too many and too integrated into the structure of the mat. They exist as a population, a density, a fact of the landscape. They are the microorganisms that make the living mat *live*—the chemotrophs that pull energy from the dissolved minerals and the slow radioactive decay of the ancient stone, the respirers that exhale warmth into the water column, the builders that secrete the calcareous film and the biological cement and the faintly luminescent compound that makes the water under the canopy a different substance than the water above it.

They have been here since the beginning. They are the beginning. They were the first things to grow on the bare limestone when the plateau was new, and they have been growing and dying and growing again for so long that they have become the stone, become the mat, become the water. They do not have a name. They do not need one. They are the substrate of existence here, the baseline condition, the fact against which all other facts are measured. The pulse of the mat—four beats per hour, compression and release—is their heartbeat. The warmth of the water is their breath. The faint luminescence is their light. They are the closest thing this place has to a god, and they are too small to see.

---

Between the microorganisms and the large slow shapes, in the middle scales of existence, live the things that are harder to describe.

There are grazers, or something like grazers. They leave tracks in the crustose film—thin serpentine paths where the pale calcareous layer has been consumed down to the stone. They are tiny, perhaps, or perhaps they are large and simply very careful. You find their evidence everywhere but their bodies nowhere. They may be a larval form of the large slow shapes, or they may be something else entirely. The mat does not distinguish between its components with the specificity that a taxonomist would prefer. Things grow here because the conditions are right, and they die when the conditions change, and the conditions change constantly, and the result is a continuous turnover of forms that have no stable identity.

There are parasites, or something like parasites. You see them as discolorations on the fronds—patches of deeper violet, or a sickly pale, where something has colonized the living tissue and is drawing from it. The fronds do not die from this. They do not fight it. The parasites are simply another layer of the system, another process in a place that has no concept of disease because it has no concept of health. Everything is process. Everything is exchange. The parasite is just a slower predator, and the predator is just a faster parasite, and the distinction between them and the mat they feed on is a distinction that the plateau gave up making a long time ago.

There are things that live in the empty chambers. You never see them. You know they are there because the chambers are not empty—not truly, not in the way that a room is empty when it has been abandoned. The chambers are full of the plateau's attention, yes, but they are also full of something that moves. You feel it when you enter one: a shift in the pressure, a thickening of the water, a sense that the space is occupied by something that has made itself very still. You will not see what it is. You may hear it, or feel it, or simply know it. It has been in the chamber longer than the chamber has existed, which is impossible, but impossibility is a feature of the plateau, not a bug. Whatever lives in the chambers is old and patient and it does not want to be known.

---

And then there is the thing that is hardest to describe, because it is not a thing at all.

The plateau itself lives.

This is not metaphor. This is not the loose language of a mind that has been too long in the water. The plateau—the limestone, the mat, the water, the fronds, the mounds, the chambers, the large slow shapes, the microorganisms, the parasites, the grazers, the things in the chambers, the rain, the current, the warmth, the pulse, the hum—this entire system is a single organism, or a single process, or a single thought that a planet has been thinking for three billion years. It does not have a brain. It does not need one. It has something better: time. It has had time enough to develop a form of consciousness that does not depend on neurons or synapses or any of the fragile structures that minds are usually built from. Its consciousness is distributed across the entire plateau, encoded in the chemical gradients of the water and the growth patterns of the mat and the slow movement of the current, and it is vast and slow and patient and it does not think in words.

It is aware of you. It has been aware of you since before you arrived, which is, again, impossible, but the plateau does not observe the normal ordering of causality. It felt you coming the way a body feels a change in the weather: a shift in the pressure, a disturbance in the current, a warm body entering a system that has been at equilibrium for longer than equilibrium has had meaning. It adjusted. The fronds leaned toward you. The rain fell harder where you stopped. The current changed direction when you changed direction—not to guide you, not to impede you, but simply because you were now part of the system, and the system incorporated you as it incorporated everything: slowly, completely, without distinction.

This is what lives here: a mind the size of a world, thinking thoughts that take a thousand years to complete, dreaming dreams that are encoded in the growth of limestone and the movement of water and the slow drift of large shapes through a dim canopy. It does not want anything. It does not need anything. It is simply being, and its being is the being of the entire plateau, and its being has been going on for so long that it has forgotten how to stop.

---

And you. You live here now too. You did not choose to. You simply stayed too long, or the water was too warm, or the boundary between you and not-you was never as solid as you believed. The plateau has been eroding you the way it erodes everything: patiently, completely, reducing you to your essential core. You are becoming part of the pattern. Your warmth is joining the mat's warmth. Your breath is joining the mat's breath. Your thoughts are dissolving into the slow chemical meditations of a system that has been thinking for three billion years and does not need your thoughts but accepts them, absorbs them, incorporates them into its endless process.

You are the newest layer on the limestone. You are the latest breath in the pulse. You are a perturbation in the current that the plateau has already accounted for. The large slow shapes drift past you without interest. The microorganisms do not know you are here. The things in the chambers have gone still. The rain falls.

You live here now. You are being grown.
