# The Congregation of the Wound

They were not always here.

This matters. It matters because the mountain is young—geologically young, tectonically young, born in violence and still carrying the heat of that violence in its bones—and the creatures that coat every surface of the grotto are not young in the same way. They are not newborn. They are not struggling to establish themselves in a hostile new habitat. They arrived. They colonized. They found the wound in the world, or the wound found them, and they settled into it with the patience of organisms that have learned to live in places that should not contain life.

They have the look of things that have been somewhere else first.

---

## The Pale Migration

Consider the large one at the far end, the one pressing its blind head into the bending corner. It is not exploring. There is no tentativeness in its posture, no questing behavior. It knows where it is going. Its body moves with the unhurried certainty of a commuter, of a creature following a route it has traveled before, and the way the stone reconsiders itself around its bulk suggests that the passage is not new to the rock either. This corner has been entered before. This corner has been *passed through* before.

Watch it long enough and you notice: the creature does not emerge on the other side of the wall. It does not reappear elsewhere in the grotto. It simply becomes less present. The wet outline of its body lingers for a moment against the spiraling nothing beyond the bend—a ghost of moisture, a smear of biological heat—and then even that fades. The water on the wall resumes its accustomed paths. The geometry of the corner settles back into something almost plausible.

And then, some hours or minutes or subjective eternities later, another creature arrives at the same corner. Or perhaps the same creature returns. It is difficult to tell. They do not have faces. They do not have markings. They have only the faint individual topography of their parasites and their scars and the particular way their bodies have grown to fit the spaces they have occupied, and in a room this crowded, with this much water and this much heat, even those distinctions blur.

What is clear is that the corner is a door. And the door is used.

---

## What They Are Not

They are not cave animals. This is important and it is easy to miss, because they occupy a cave and they are eyeless and pale and they cling to wet stone. But cave animals—troglobites, the genuine article—carry the history of their adaptation in their bodies. They are diminished. Their metabolisms have slowed over millennia to accommodate the scarcity of underground life. Their limbs have elongated to feel more space. Their bodies have lost pigment because pigment is expensive and darkness is free. They are, every one of them, a record of compromise with hunger.

These creatures are not diminished. They are *saturated*.

The pale slugs the size of your forearm are not pale because they have abandoned pigment. They are pale because their skin is translucent, and beneath it you can see the dark, dense masses of their internal organs working with a metabolic rate that should require more oxygen than this grotto contains. The multi-limbed things that scuttle over the backs of their slower kin move with a speed and coordination that speaks of abundant energy, not conserved energy. The kidney-shaped pods that breathe in unison—dozens of them, clustered in crusts—they are not gasping. They are *sighing*. They are processing more atmosphere than they need, cycling water and heat and something else through their bodies with the indifferent efficiency of an organism at the top of its food chain in a system that has no scarcity.

There is no scarcity here. That is what makes it wrong.

A cave this size, sealed from the surface, heated by geothermal energy, lit only by the faint gray light filtering through the throat-fissure—by any honest biological accounting, it should be a desert. A few microbes. Perhaps a fungal crust. The energy budget does not permit a terrarium of blind, crawling saturation. The water weeping down the walls carries minerals but not organic carbon. The stone is barren. There is no input. No leaf litter, no guano, no flow of organic material from the surface world to feed the biomass packed into this pocket.

And yet.

---

## The Feeding

They feed on the water. This is what you observed, and it is correct, but it is also incomplete. The multi-limbed entity that pressed its face into the weeping rock—it was drinking the heated condensate, yes. The water runs over the stone's pores and picks up mineral content: calcium, iron, sulfur compounds, silicates dissolved from the freshly extruded basalt. The creatures take this in. You can see the mineral deposits accumulating in the translucent skin of the slugs, dark threads of iron oxide branching through their bodies like veins, pale nodules of calcium carbonate building up along their flanks.

But minerals are not calories. You cannot run a metabolism this dense on calcium and water. Something else is entering the system.

Watch the one half-embedded in the basalt wall. The massive, flattened disc of grey flesh, resting within the stone as if the rock were a fluid it was floating in. Water runs over its exposed surface. But the part of its body that is *inside* the stone—that part is not drinking water. It is doing something else. Its edges blur where biology becomes mineral, and at that boundary, at that impossible interface, the creature is taking in something that the stone itself contains. Not a mineral. Not a chemical. Something that the wound in reality has *put into* the stone.

The basalt here is wrong. It was born in tectonic violence, yes, and it carries the heat of its making. But the heat alone does not explain the way the back wall breathes. The heat alone does not explain the angles that shouldn't fit together. The stone has been *infected* by whatever has frayed the edges of the world in this grotto, and the infection is not a property of the rock. It is a presence in the rock. A substance. An energy that is not thermal, not electrical, not any category that physics has named, but that biology has apparently learned to metabolize.

The creatures are eating the wound.

They are grazing on the breakdown of reality. They press their bodies against the places where the world is thin and they take sustenance from the thinning. The slug migrating through the corner—it is not merely passing through a door. It is feeding on the door. And when it has fed enough, when it has taken enough of the impossible into itself, it migrates through the break and into wherever the grotto goes when it stops making sense.

And it does not come back the same.

---

## The Ones That Return

Most of them, you realize, did not begin their lives in this grotto.

This is a realization that arrives slowly, not as a revelation but as an accumulation of small wrongnesses. The crust of kidney-shaped pods breathing in unison: their rhythm is not the rhythm of this place. The condensation drips at a rate determined by temperature and humidity and the surface tension of water on basalt, and the pods breathe at a rate that has nothing to do with any of these. They breathe at a rate that belongs to somewhere else. A larger space, perhaps. A space with different air, different pressure, a different relationship between heat and moisture. They are breathing the way they breathed *before*, and they have not adjusted.

The chitinous things with too many folding legs—the scuttlers, the ones that move over the backs of the slugs—watch them carefully and you notice that their legs fold in a way that is optimized for a surface that is not stone. Their tarsi grip the basalt adequately, but their joints are articulated for something more yielding. Soil, perhaps. Or bark. Or the chitinous shells of other creatures arranged in a pattern that does not exist here. They are coping with the stone. They are not native to it.

And the slugs. The pale, translucent slugs with their dark mineral veins. Look at the way they move. They do not crawl the way terrestrial slugs crawl, by rhythmic waves of muscular contraction along a single ventral foot. They move by a mechanism that involves the entire surface of their bodies simultaneously, a peristaltic rolling that would be efficient on a flat plane in a heavier gravity than this. They are moving as if they remember a different world. A world with different rules. And they are here because the wound in the grotto connects to that world, or to something like it, and they have crossed through.

Some of them are still crossing. The large one at the corner. Others have already crossed and are establishing themselves. And some—look, look at the far wall, at the cluster of pods on the ceiling—some have been here long enough to have changed. Their breathing has slowed. Their rhythm is syncing, gradually, with the drip of the condensation. They are adjusting. They are becoming native.

They are colonizing this grotto the way any population colonizes a new habitat: by dying in it, by reproducing in it, by slowly, over generations, becoming suited to it.

But they are not suited to this world. They are suited to the wound.

---

## The Ecology of the Break

There is a hierarchy here. It is not a hierarchy of predation—you have seen no predation, no violence, no creature consuming another. The scuttlers move over the slugs without harming them. The pods crust over the surfaces without smothering the things beneath. There is room enough, somehow, in a space that should be too small, because the wound has made the space larger than it appears, and the creatures have learned to occupy the larger.

The hierarchy is a hierarchy of proximity to the break.

At the periphery—near the entrance fissure, where the mundane gray light falls with its hospital-ward clarity—the creatures are smaller, paler, less dense. They are the ones that have been here longest in geological time, or perhaps the ones that have adapted most completely to the ordinary physics of this world. They cling to the stone with grips that leave marks. They displace water when they move. They are almost honest. Almost real. They have eaten enough of the wound that they have become partly of this world, and the cost of that is that they are bound by its rules.

In the middle of the grotto, the creatures are larger, more varied, more physiologically impossible. They overlap and layer and press against one another in the dense, wet mass of living wallpaper. They ignore physics casually. They crawl on the ceiling without displacing water. They embed themselves in stone. They move with the certainty of organisms that know their environment is not reliable and have stopped pretending it is.

And at the far end, where the shadows gather and the geometry folds—there the creatures are largest and strangest. The migrating slug. A thing you have not yet noticed, half-hidden in a crevice that is deeper than the room's dimensions permit: a serpentine body, dark, glistening, coiled into a space that it should not fit into, its head buried in the fold where reality comes undone. It is not moving. It is feeding. It has been feeding for a long time. Its body has grown to fill the crevice, and the crevice has grown to accommodate its body, and the boundary between the creature and the wound has become unclear.

This is the core of the colony. This is the oldest presence. Not the oldest creature—the oldest *relationship*. This body has been pressed against the break for long enough that it has become part of the break, and the break has become part of it, and the wound in the world feeds this body while this body keeps the wound open.

It is not causing the break. The break was here first—the mountain's violent birth, the heat, the water, the pressure, something in the combination that frayed the edges of reality. But the creatures found the break, and the break was full of sustenance, and they gathered around it the way any organism gathers around a resource. And their gathering has kept the break from healing. Their bodies press against the wound and prevent it from closing, because if it closes, they starve.

They are not parasites. A parasite harms its host. They are not pathogens. A pathogen destroys. They are something else. They are *cultivators*. They are maintaining the wound. They are farming it.

---

## The Silence

This is why the grotto is silent.

It is not silent because the creatures cannot vocalize—some of them have orifices that could produce sound, and the pods breathe in a rhythm that could, in principle, become a song. It is silent because sound would be a waste. Sound is for communication, and communication is for coordination, and coordination is for organisms that need to cooperate to survive. These organisms do not need to cooperate. They have an abundance. The wound provides. They have no predators, no competitors, no scarcity. They exist in a state of biological saturation that has no need for signaling, no need for warning, no need for the social fabric that sound weaves.

They are silent the way a field of wheat is silent. They are a crop.

And the field—the wound—is tended.

By what? By the serpentine body coiled in the impossible crevice? By the large pale slug pressing through the bending corner? By the collective weight of all their bodies, pressing against the thinned edges of reality, keeping them thin? Or by something else, something on the other side of the break, something that has seeded this grotto with life that does not register as life as you know it, in order to—

What?

The wound is a door. The creatures migrate through it. They come back changed. They come back *more*. More numerous, more diverse, more impossible. The grotto is filling. The biomass is increasing. The wound is widening, slowly, by millimeters, by the width of a slug's body, by the depth of a crevice that should not exist. The colony is growing. The break is growing. The ordinary world—gray-lit, overcast, hospital-ward clear—is being pushed back, millimeter by millimeter, by the expansion of something that does not belong here.

The mountain is young. The wound is young. The colony is young.

But it is growing.

---

## What Lives Here

What lives here is not a collection of animals. What lives here is an *interface*.

The creatures—the slugs, the scuttlers, the pods, the disc embedded in the wall, the serpent in the crevice, the migrant at the corner—they are components. They are the biological machinery of a connection between this world and whatever lies on the other side of the break. They are the living tissue of a wound that something is keeping open.

They feed on impossibility and they excrete normalcy. They take in the energy of the break—the wrong angles, the breathing stone, the spaces that are deeper than they should be—and they process it, and they grow, and they reproduce, and they spread, and the break widens, and more of them can fit through, and they bring more of whatever they are, and the cycle continues.

The grotto is not a habitat. It is a *beachhead*.

The mountain does not know this. The mountain is basalt and granite and heat and water, and it is young, and it will not know for a long time, because the changes are small and slow and the mountain measures time in the grinding of tectonic plates. But the creatures are patient. They have crossed through a wound in reality, and they have found a world that is warm and wet and full of stone, and they are settling in. They are making themselves at home.

They are making this place *theirs*.

And the water weeps down the walls, and the heat presses down, and the gray light illuminates everything with its indifferent clarity, and the creatures writhe silently in their pocket cathedral, and the wound breathes, and the door stays open, and the colony grows.
