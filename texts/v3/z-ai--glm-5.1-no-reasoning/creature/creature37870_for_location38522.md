To live here is to be a splinter in the skin of a god, and to know it.

The inhabitants of this green geometry are not a people who have mastered their environment; they are a people who have been digested by it. They are the incidental fauna of a continental organism, clinging to the margins of a vegetative war that does not even register their existence as a combatant action. They call themselves something that translates, roughly, to *the Exhaled*, for they believe they were not born, but coughed up from the rhizome’s respiration, accidental condensation given frail, shivering form.

They are humanoid, but only in the loosest, most desperate sense. Generations of subsisting on the freezing, near-vacuum margins of the ecosystem have stretched and hollowed them. They stand tall and impossibly thin—spindly, tensile things, built like the young, aggressive shoots that erupt from the root-floor, for they share the same survival strategy: grow upward quickly, reach the diffuse light, or be swallowed by the cannibalistic dark below. Their skin is not skin, but a pale, semi-translucent membrane, mottled with the sickly green of chlorophyll-laced symbionts that colonize their pores. It is a chitin-thin armor against the cold, stretched taut over spindly, elongated limbs. Through their chests and bellies, if you looked closely in the blinding glare, you could see the dark, slow pulse of a heart adapted to a thinning atmosphere—a heart that beats perhaps once every ten seconds, conserving warmth, throttling life down to a bare, tenacious simmer.

They have no eyes. The sourceless, shadowless white of the cathedral burned out the need for sight in their ancestors long ago. In a light that erases depth, eyes are only sources of pain. Instead, the center of their faces holds a smooth, concave depression—a dish of taut, hairless skin studded with microscopic, sub-dermal vibration receptors. They do not see the collapse of a fungal city; they feel it. They feel the subsonic pressure wave roll through the rhizome, they feel the wet thump in the hollows of their own bones. They navigate their world by reading the tectonic shudders of the root-floor, the hum of sap rising in the colossal trunks, the near-ultrasonic scream of a chitin-cellulose bark splitting under its own aggressive growth. To them, the world is not a blinding void; it is a symphony of pressure, a topography of trembling tension. 

They are perpetually, achingly cold. The damp, refrigerated air clings to them, and their bodies have adapted to neither generate nor retain excessive heat. They are ectothermic in spirit, if not strictly in biology, drawing what little warmth they can from the slow compost of the floor, huddling against the massive trunks that stand like the pillars of a冷冻 cathedral, siphoning the faint exothermic reactions of the deep root-mass. When the pea-soup fog rolls in, they do not hide from it; they open their mouths and drink it directly, their lungless chests expanding in slow, shallow bellows that filter the moisture from the chill. They are creatures of condensation, born of the micro-climate, living off its breath.

Their society—if it can be called that—is as sparse and dispersed as the great trunks. They are solitary wanderers, migrating endlessly across the root-lattices. They do not form villages. A collection of *the Exhaled* is a gathering of the doomed, a drain on the meager atmospheric water and the scarce, edible spores of a collapsing shelf-fungus. To stay is to starve; to cluster is to attract the creeping rust-mold, which consumes the dead and the slow with the same vegetative hatred it reserves for its rivals.

They meet only in passing, on the long, treacherous walks between the trunks, and their interactions are minimalist to the point of telepathy. They have no spoken language; in the pressurized, acoustically dead silence of the cathedral, shouting is useless, and whispers are swallowed. Instead, they communicate through *tapping*. A knuckle rapped against the living floor, a pattern of strikes against their own chitin-thin chests, the rhythm of a walking staff knocked against the pale, cartilaginous roots. They feel the vibrations through the soles of their feet, reading the distance, the intent, the warning. A slow, deep thrum means *flee, the mold is rising*. A rapid, staccato clatter means *condensation falling, take shelter*. A single, long, low drone, struck and let fade, means *I am here, I am alive, pass by in peace*.

Their tools are heartbreakingly primitive, carved from the pith of stems and the shards of fallen chitin-bark. They are pre-metal not for lack of ingenuity, but because metal would be too heavy, too cold, too dead. Their survival depends on remaining part of the living substrate. A wedge of stem-pith, driven into a crack, will draw a trickle of sugary sap—a stolen trickle of the god's blood. But they must harvest and flee before the wound seals, before the aggressive growth of the rhizome swallows the tool and crushes it to pulp. Their shelters are fleeting, desperate things: blind walls of woven fungal hyphae, erected against the fog-chill, abandoned and consumed by lichen within a cycle. They do not build for tomorrow. Tomorrow, the floor will have rearranged itself. Tomorrow, a new trunk may erupt from the ground, splitting the shelter in two with a tectonic shudder. They live in the continuous present, a state of perpetual, precarious migration, picking at the frost-bitten remains of the forest's civil wars.

They do not worship the green geometry. To worship something, you must believe it has a will, and the botanic monadnock has no will—it has only *appetite*. It is a mindless, Solaris-like engine of creation and destruction, and they are the mites living in its pores. They do not pray to it; they appease it with their transience. They move softly, they tap quietly, they leave no permanent marks. To be noticed by the forest is to be swallowed by it—to have the floor grow over you in the night, to be entombed in the expanding lattice of the root, to become substrate.

And yet, they possess a quiet, profound resilience. They are the ghosts of the cathedral, the scavengers of the void. They endure the blinding glare that aches their eyeless faces, they endure the freezing fog that settles in their hollow bones, they endure the subsonic booms of falling cities that rattle their ribs. They endure because they are small, and because they are fleeting. They are the brief, shivering condensation on the surface of an unfathomable, botanic god—a god that builds and destroys itself in the silent, bright, freezing emptiness, forever unaware of the pale, tapping things that scurry through the roots, eking out a life in the margins of its own endless, green riot.
