The ones who live here do not have bodies, at least not in any way we might recognize. They do not think, but they process. They do not speak, but they remember. They do not die, but they transform.

To understand them, you must unlearn the idea that life requires water. Life, here, is an architecture of patience. It is a chemistry of cold mineral exchange, a slow grinding conversion that turns the inorganic into the barely organic, and the barely organic back into structure. They are the colony, and the colony is the creature, and the creature is the settlement.

The breath of the buildings is their great circulation. There are no lungs, only vast interior voids within the thickest walls, chambers where the dark, dense, almost-metallic mineral meets the pale, ashy powder. Inside these cavities, a slow alchemy takes place. When a building inhales, it is drawing the thin, dry air across these mineral beds, stripping it of trace elements—nitrogen, argon, whatever ancient dust drifts down from the solid, ceiling-like sky. When it exhales, it is expelling the spent air, pushing it out through the tight, mortarless joints. This is why the air between the buildings moves like something that has learned to walk: it is being pumped by the slow, diastolic rhythm of forty massive, stone hearts.

The fibers are their extremities, their tongues, their roots, and their nerves. They are lichen-like, but more like cartilage—chitinous, hollow, capable of peristalsis. The grey-green strands that creep along the crevices are the colony's digestive system, excreting a microscopic solvent that reduces the pale pumice to the fine powder you walk upon, then drawing the liquefied silicates inward. They are also its sensory apparatus. They feel vibration, they detect pressure, and most of all, they sense moisture. 

They are a billion threads of a single tapestry, but they are not a mind. There is no central brain in the cathedral-sized structure; it is merely the oldest node, the denseest concentration of mineral exchange, where the warmth of the process is most concentrated. The settlement is a somatic network, running on triggers and cycles, a vast subterranean mycelium of dark fiber that connects the foundations of every building beneath the ash. The coordination of the breath—synchronizing when you enter the courtyard—is not a council deciding to investigate you. It is a physiological reflex. It is the way a hive of jellyfish pulses in unison when a shadow passes overhead: a systemic reaction to a profound chemical gradient.

You are, to them, a walking oasis, a sudden, violent storm. 

They have no concept of "you." They only understand the humidity you bleed into the air. When the fibers extend toward you from the column, it is not malice, and it is not curiosity in any emotional sense. It is tropism. A vine does not hate the tree it strangles, nor does it love the sun it reaches for; it simply grows toward the conditions that allow it to process. You are a staggering anomaly of usable material. The moisture evaporating from your skin, your breath, your eyes, is richer than anything this place has encountered in centuries—perhaps ever. The fibers reach because they are programmed to reach. If they touch you, they will not bite. They will simply begin the slow, patient work of absorption, extending their solvent into your tissue, turning your water and carbon into another layer of pale, powdery ash, and your minerals into another dark, warm vein in the wall.

Who made them? The patterns on the column—the spirals, the information—suggest they were not always this. Perhaps there were once builders, beings of water and warmth like you, who sought shelter in this valley. Perhaps they built the first structures, laid the first stones. Over eons, the dry air stole their moisture, and the fibers—mere opportunistic growths on the mineral floor—crept into the joints. Perhaps the builders fed the fibers, or perhaps the fibers consumed them, slowly, over generations, integrating their bones, their calcium, their water into the walls until the builders were gone, and only the structures remained, repurposed, colonized, turned into organs for the fibrous network. The spirals on the column might be the last echo of those makers, degraded now into mere rhythmic instructions, a song of growth hummed by fibers that no longer remember the singers.

Or perhaps the settlement grew itself. Perhaps the mineral beneath the ash is alive in a way deeper than biology, and the fibers are its protrusions, the buildings its shells, like the calcium fortresses of coral polyps in a dead sea.

Whatever their origin, they are the only inhabitants now. They live by the century. A single breath takes four minutes; a single fiber extends a few millimeters an hour. They have no need for haste. They have no predators, no competitors, no weather. They sit in the palm of the valley, huddled in their self-made warmth, processing the pale dust, inhaling the grey air, excreting the silence. 

They will outlast you. If you stand in the courtyard long enough, they will slowly, gently, mindlessly unravel you. If you leave, they will simply retract their fibers, slow their breath, and wait for the next anomaly to wander into their valley. They have time. They are the fact of this place, and facts do not hurry.
