# What Lives in the Mossward Vaults

## The Pale Woodlice First, Because They Are the First to Accept You

They do not look up when your boot breaks the surface of the liverwort mat. They look *at* — a flat, compounded regard conducted through eyes that have reduced themselves, over a thousand generations, to the minimum necessary apparatus for distinguishing light from less-light, which is all that is required when your world has been the same brightness for longer than your species has been counting. Their shells are not shells in the way you think of shells — armored, opaque, defensive. They are windows. Calcified glass. Through them you can see the dark thread of the gut processing its latest meal of fungal spore and decomposing moss, and you can see the pulse — not a heartbeat exactly, but a rhythmic compression of hemolymph that serves the same purpose — and the pulse is slow, perhaps four beats per minute, and you realize with a small vertigo that this slowness is not lethargy. It is calibration. They have matched themselves to the metabolic tempo of their environment. They run at the speed of their food, which runs at the speed of its substrate, which runs at the speed of the water that feeds the substrate, which runs at the speed of stone.

There are thousands of them. You do not see this at first because they do not move in the way that aggregates of animals move — no waves of motion, no collective startle response, no parting of a crowd before your footsteps. They are distributed the way moss colonies are distributed: evenly, without density gradients, each individual occupying a territory that it has apparently determined is sufficient and that it does not feel compelled to leave. When you kneel and bring your face close to the moss cushion where six of them are feeding on the pale film that coats the liverwort surface, they continue feeding. One walks onto your finger. It does not hurry. It does not pause. It treats your finger as topography — a warm, slightly dry ridge to be crossed in the course of a journey from one feeding ground to another. Its legs are precise, each one placed with the deliberation of a mountaineer choosing footholds, and the sensation on your skin is so faint that you feel it more as a suggestion of touch than as touch itself.

They live a long time. You do not know this from observation — you have not been here long enough to observe a lifespan — but you know it from the logic of the place. Metabolism correlates with lifespan in ways that are imperfect but directional, and these woodlice have metabolisms so slow that the chemical processes maintaining their cells are operating at a fraction of the rate of their surface relatives. If a garden woodlice lives two years, these may live twenty. Or fifty. Or longer. They may be older than the architecture, which would mean they were here before the builders, before the channels were cut, before the mortar was pressed into seams, and that they simply stayed when the stone was shaped around them, accommodating the new surfaces the way they accommodate all surfaces — by colonizing them, slowly, completely, without effort.

## The Spiders, Who Are Not Waiting

They do not build webs the way you expect webs to be built. There is no geometry here, no radial symmetry, no architectural ambition. The silk goes where silk is useful, which is everywhere, and the result is less a web than a *fabric* — a three-dimensional textile of silk threads spanning the gaps between fungal brackets, anchoring moss hummocks to stone, reinforcing the tendril-curtains on the ceiling with guy-wires so fine they are visible only when they catch the bioluminescent light at certain angles. The spiders have not built traps. They have built infrastructure. Their silk is as much a part of the vault's architecture as the mortar in the seams, and it serves the same structural purpose — holding things together, distributing load, preventing collapse.

The largest one you see is the size of your palm, legs included. It sits in the angle between a bracket fungus and the wall, its body the same pale grey-white as the bracket's underside, its legs translucent enough that you can see the fluid in them — hemolymph, slow and dark, moving through the joints with the same unhurried rhythm as the woodlice's pulse. It is not waiting. This is the important thing to understand about the spiders: they are not waiting. Waiting implies expectation, a future toward which attention is directed. The spiders have no future. They have a present that extends in both directions without variation, and their attention is directed at the present entirely — at the vibrations in their silk, at the chemical gradients in the air, at the microcurrents of moisture that tell them where the humidity is highest and therefore where prey is most likely to be moving, which is everywhere, because prey is always moving here, at speeds that make the word *moving* feel generous.

When a springtail — pale, minute, hopping with a frequency that seems almost frantic in this context — lands on a silk thread near the spider, the spider does not strike. It does not lunge. It extends one leg, slowly, and touches the springtail, and the springtail is stuck — caught not by stickiness but by the fine geometry of the silk's microstructure, which grips the springtail's setae the way velcro grips. Then the spider moves its body toward the prey over the course of perhaps thirty seconds, and it feeds, and the feeding is so quiet and so still that you are not certain it has happened until you see the springtail's husk, dry and pale, released from the silk to fall the short distance to the moss below, where the woodlice will find it and process it and return its components to the system.

The spiders do not fear you. They do not flee from your hand when you reach toward a web to examine the silk. They sit, and they watch, and the watching is not vigilance but the passive recording of information by an organism that has never had reason to develop a threat response because nothing in its evolutionary history has ever been a threat. You are not a threat. You are a temperature anomaly, a source of vibration, a disruption in the humidity gradient that will pass. They have seen disruptions before — or rather, their species has seen disruptions before, the slow geological disruptions of watercourses shifting and ceilings settling and growths dying back and re-establishing — and the species' response to disruption has always been the same: stay, wait, the disruption will end, the conditions will return.

## The Swimmers, Who Have Given Up Seeing

In the channels, the pale oblong shapes drift. You sit beside a channel for long enough to watch one complete its transit of a five-foot section, and the transit takes four minutes, which means the swimmer is moving at a rate that would be imperceptible if you were not specifically watching for it. It does not swim. It positions itself — orienting its body to some gradient you cannot detect — and then it is *there*, at the other end of the channel, having moved by a process that seems to involve the faintest oscillation of its appendages, so slight that the water around it does not visibly disturb.

They are crustaceans. You are almost certain of this. The body plan is there — segmented, with appendages that could be swimming legs if they were used with more force, with antennae that trail behind the body like filaments, twice as long as the body itself, and that appear to be the primary sensory organs. The antennae touch the walls of the channel as the swimmer moves, touching every surface, sampling the biofilm that coats the stone, and you realize the antennae are not navigation. They are grazing. The swimmers are filter-feeders, running their antennae along the channel walls and collecting the biological films that accumulate there, and the swimming is not hunting but *rotating through a pasture* — moving from one section of channel to another, cropping the film, moving on, returning when the film has re-established, which it does, because the film is alive and growing and replenishing itself at a rate that matches the grazing, the two tempos — grazer and grazed — locked together in a balance so precise that neither ever exceeds the other by a margin large enough to observe.

They have no eyes. Not reduced eyes, not vestigial eyes. No eyes. The region of the head where eyes would be is smooth, featureless, occupied instead by a concentration of sensory setae so dense they look like velvet. They do not need to see. There is nothing to see in their world that is not better detected by touch and chemistry — the film is detected by contact, the water's chemistry is detected by setae on the swimming legs, the presence of other swimmers is detected by the faint displacement of water their movement produces, which is so slight that only an organism adapted to this specific stillness could register it. They have committed fully to their environment. They have bet everything on this place remaining what it is, and the bet has paid off for so many generations that the concept of *bet* no longer applies. They are not adapted to the vaults. They are *expressions of* the vaults, as much a product of the conditions as the films they graze or the water they swim in.

There are perhaps fifty of them in the channel system. You cannot count them because you cannot tell them apart — they are the same size, the same pallor, the same shape, without the individual variation that surface populations display. They may be clones. The population may be the descendants of a single founder individual that arrived here, however many thousands of years ago, and that has reproduced parthenogenetically ever since, each generation identical to the last, each individual a copy of a copy of a copy, the genetic information degrading not at all because there is no pressure to degrade, no mutation that is not corrected, no selection that is not stabilizing. They are the same individual, fifty times. Or they are fifty individuals who are the same. The distinction, here, does not matter.

## The Slugs, Who Garden

They are on the ceiling, and you almost miss them because they are the exact color of the tendrils they travel along — pale, wet, faintly translucent, with a quality of surface that is less skin than membrane. They move by the standard slug method: extend the anterior, anchor it, draw the posterior forward. But the motion is so slow that you cannot see it happening. You can only see that it has happened — the slug is *here*, and then, after an interval of watching that you could not have identified as an interval, the slug is *there*, two centimeters closer to wherever it is going, which is nowhere in particular, which is along the tendril curtain in a path that will eventually, over days or weeks, carry it from one end of the chamber to the other and back.

They are feeding on the tendrils. The radula — the slug's tongue, a ribbon of tiny teeth — rasps the tendrils as the slug moves, cropping them to a length that keeps the curtains from becoming so dense they block the passages. This is not intention. The slug does not know it is gardening. The slug does not know anything in the way that *knowing* implies a model of the world held in a mind and consulted for decisions. The slug is a chemical process that moves and eats, and the movement and the eating happen to maintain the tendril curtains at a density that permits air movement through the chambers, which permits the gas exchange that the fungi and mosses require, which permits the growth that the slug feeds on. The slug is a maintenance mechanism. It is a valve in a system that has no engineer, a regulator that emerged from the interaction of its own appetites and the growth rates of its food, and the regulation it performs is as precise and as necessary as any regulation performed by any mechanism, biological or designed.

There are four of them. Four, in the entire vault system. You find them all, eventually, by looking for the wet trails they leave on the tendrils — faint lines of mucus that catch the light differently, that mark the slug's passage the way a ship's wake marks its passage through water, except that the wake does not dissipate. The mucus dries, slowly, over days, and the trail remains, a record of where the slug has been and therefore a prediction of where it is going, because the slug's path is not random. It is a circuit — a loop that carries each slug through the tendril curtains of one chamber and back, over and over, at a pace that ensures no section of curtain is overcropped. Four slugs, four chambers, four circuits, the paths meeting only in the narrow passages between chambers, where the slugs pass each other without interaction, without acknowledgement, without any behavior that could be called social, because social behavior requires a level of internal complexity that these organisms have no use for and have therefore not developed.

You watch one for a long time. Long enough that your body cools and your breathing slows and the boundary between *watching the slug* and *being in the same space as the slug* begins to dissolve. The slug does not notice this dissolution. The slug does not notice you. The slug moves and eats and moves and eats, and the tendrils are cropped and the tendrils grow and the air moves and the spores drift and the light glows, and none of this is for you, and none of this is against you, and the slug is as close to a perfect expression of its role as anything you have ever seen.

## The Springtails and the Gnats, Who Are the Transit System

They are the only things here that move at a pace you recognize as movement. The springtails hop — not frequently, not energetically, but with a suddenness that is startling in this context, a flash of motion that seems to belong to a different world, a faster world, a world where things happen and keep happening and the happening is the point. They are tiny. A millimeter, maybe two. Pale, almost translucent, with the furcula — the tail-like appendage that launches them — visible beneath their bodies as a darker line. They hop when they want to move from one surface to another, from one moss cushion to an adjacent one, from a fungal bracket to the wall behind it. They hop, they land, they resume feeding, and the feeding is slow, the feeding is at the pace of everything else here, and the hop is the only fast thing about them.

The fungus gnats are darker, small black-bodied insects with wings they almost never use. They walk along the fungal brackets, laying eggs in the pores, and the eggs hatch into larvae that live inside the bracket fungus, feeding on its tissue, and the larvae are the primary food of the spiders that have their webs beneath the brackets. The gnats do not fly because there is no reason to fly — flight is a response to the need to cover distance quickly or to escape predation, and neither need applies here. Distance is covered by walking, which is sufficient because the distances are small. Predation is escaped by not being in the web, which is achieved by walking around the web rather than through it, which the gnats do with a precision that suggests either learning or instinct so refined it is indistinguishable from learning.

They are the system's transit. The springtails carry fungal spores on their bodies from one part of the vault to another, and the spores germinate where they land, and new fungal bodies establish, and the genetic diversity of the fungal population is maintained by the movement of insects that are too small to see from more than a few inches away and that hop for reasons that have nothing to do with fungal reproduction. The gnats carry the same spores, and also the mites that live on their bodies, and also the chemical signatures of the surfaces they have walked on, which are detected by other organisms — by the spiders, which can tell from the chemical residue on a silk thread which surfaces a caught insect has recently visited, and by the woodlice, which follow chemical trails to concentrations of decomposing matter.

The transit system has no schedule. It has no routes. It has the random walk of small organisms in a small space, and the randomness is sufficient because the space is small enough that random walk covers it, and the organisms are numerous enough that the coverage is continuous, and the system has been running for long enough that any gap in coverage has been filled by the population that specializes in that gap. There are no empty niches here. There are no unexploited resources. The springtails and the gnats and the mites and the beetles are the system's circulatory system, moving material from one place to another at a scale and a pace that matches everything else, and they are as essential and as unremarkable as capillaries.

## The Tendrils, Which May Not Be Animal

They hang from the ceiling in curtains, and you have been calling them tendrils because you do not have a better word, but they are not tendrils in the botanical sense — they are not roots, not stems, not modified leaves. They are not hyphae, though they have the thread-like quality of fungal filaments. They are something that has either lost its taxonomic affiliations through long isolation or has developed a form so unlike its ancestors that the relationship is no longer visible.

They are regular. This is what strikes you most about them. They are not the irregular, branching forms of root systems or mycelial networks. They are parallel, evenly spaced, each tendril the same distance from its neighbors, each tendril the same diameter along its length, each tendril descending to the same depth before terminating in a blunt, rounded tip. They look manufactured. They look woven. They look like someone — or something — designed them, which is impossible, because design implies a designer, and you have seen no evidence of a designer, only of a system that has refined itself through so many iterations of trial and error that the result *looks* designed, which is what natural selection does, which is what time does, which is what happens when a process runs for ten thousand years without interruption.

They move. The breathing motion — expansion and contraction, an hour per cycle — is not triggered by any external stimulus you can identify. It is endogenous. It arises from within the tendrils themselves, from some internal mechanism — a turgor pressure system, perhaps, or a contractile protein, or something that has no analogue in any organism you are familiar with. The movement is small — a millimeter of expansion, a millimeter of contraction — but it is consistent, and it is coordinated, all the tendrils in a curtain expanding and contracting in phase, which means they are connected, which means a curtain of tendrils is not a collection of individuals but a single organism, or a single system, or a single *body*.

They catch condensation. This is their function, or the function they have evolved, or the function the system has assigned to them — they present a large surface area to the saturated air, and water condenses on them, and the water runs down their length by surface tension and gravity, and it accumulates at the tips, and the tips — you need a hand lens to see this — the tips are modified. They are swollen, slightly, and pitted with depressions that hold the water, and from the depressions, the water is absorbed into the tendril's interior, where it is distributed through whatever internal structure the tendrils possess. They are drinking the air. They are harvesting the humidity that the vault's conditions produce, and they are converting it into the moisture that sustains their growth, and the slugs crop their growth, and the moisture is returned to the air through the slugs' respiration, and the cycle continues.

They do not respond to your touch. You reach up — the ceiling is low enough in this section — and you touch one, and it is cool and firm and slightly yielding, like touching a tendon, and the tendril does not recoil. It does not change its breathing rhythm. It does not alter its condensation rate. It continues its cycle, and your finger leaves a slight depression in its surface that fills in over the next few minutes, and the tendril is unchanged, and you are the thing that was changed, because you have touched something that is alive in a way you do not understand and that does not care whether you understand it.

## The Sphere, Which You Do Not Touch

You have been avoiding it. Not deliberately — not through any conscious decision to stay away from the far corner of the largest chamber. But you have been in the vault for two hours now, and you have examined every other surface and every other organism, and you have not yet approached the sphere, and the avoidance has become conspicuous enough that you are now aware of it as a pattern, and awareness of the pattern does not dissolve it.

It hangs from the ceiling by a single cord — the same pale, dense material as the tendrils, but thicker, and darker, and with a quality of organization that the tendrils do not have. The tendrils are parallel but uniform. The cord is *structured*. It has a grain, a directionality, and you can see — looking closely, from a distance of three feet, which is as close as you have approached — that the grain is not random. It spirals. The fibers in the cord spiral around its axis in a pattern that is regular and consistent and that reminds you, uncomfortably, of the structure of a nerve.

The sphere itself is the color of the stone behind it — dark grey-green — but warmer, as though it contains something that produces heat, or that retains the heat of the water that passes through it, or that is simply dense enough to have a temperature slightly higher than the air around it because density and temperature are related in ways that thermodynamics describes and that you are not currently equipped to calculate. It is not perfectly spherical. It is approximately spherical, with a slight asymmetry, a flattening on one side, as though it has grown against a surface — the ceiling, perhaps — and has been shaped by the contact. Its surface has the sheen you noticed before, the quality of *skin* rather than *cuticle* or *bark*, and you can see — looking closer, from two feet now, which is as close as you have been able to approach without conscious effort — that the skin is not unbroken. There are marks on it. Not wounds, not lesions, but *openings*. Small, regularly spaced, each one perhaps a millimeter across, and each one ringed by a slight raised lip, like the pore of a fungus or the ostium of a sponge, and the openings are not inert. They are *working*. You can see — by the faint movement of the air immediately around each opening, by the slight difference in the refraction of light through the air above the openings versus the air beside them — that something is being exchanged. Gas, presumably. Moisture. Some chemical communication with the air that is as necessary to the sphere's function as breathing is to yours.

It is aware. You do not know how you know this. The knowledge does not arrive through any sensory channel you can identify. You have not seen it move. You have not heard it produce sound. You have not detected any electromagnetic emission from it, any heat signature, any chemical output that would distinguish it from the growths around it. And yet you know, with a certainty that is not rational but that is also not irrational — that is simply *present*, the way the light is present and the water is present and the cold is present — that this thing is processing. It is integrating. It is doing something with the information that arrives through its openings and through the cord that connects it to the ceiling and through whatever internal structure it contains, and the something it is doing is complex enough and organized enough that the word *aware* is not inappropriate, even if it is also not adequate.

You think of the fungal networks you have read about — the mycelial mats of forests, the Armillaria colonies that cover hundreds of acres, the wood-wide web of chemical signals passing between trees through fungal intermediaries. You think about how those networks have been described as intelligent, as capable of decision-making, as able to route resources and respond to damage and adapt to changing conditions. You think about how those descriptions have always felt slightly generous, slightly anthropomorphic, slightly like the projection of a mind onto a system that is merely complex.

This does not feel like projection. This feels like observation. The sphere is doing something, and the something it is doing is not simple, and you are in its space, and it knows you are in its space, not in the way that a camera knows you are in its frame but in the way that a body knows it has a fever — systemically, holistically, as a condition rather than as an event.

You do not touch it. You stand two feet away and you look at it and it does not look back, because it does not have eyes, because it does not need eyes, because eyes are a solution to a problem it does not have. It has its openings. It has its cord. It has whatever internal structure the skin is protecting. And it has time — more time than you, more time than the woodlice, more time than the architecture, perhaps more time than the stone. It has been here, processing, for longer than you can calculate, and it will be here after you leave, and the processing will continue, and whatever it is that the processing *is* — whatever awareness, whatever integration, whatever slow thought a system like this is capable of — will continue without an audience, without a witness, without any need to be seen or known or described.

You are not afraid of it. You want to be clear about this. The not-touching is not fear. It is *recognition*. It is the recognition that there are forms of life — forms of process, forms of organization — that are not available to you, that exist on terms you cannot negotiate, that have their own business and their own purposes and their own definitions of what it means to be a thing that is alive, and that the appropriate response to encountering such a form is not to touch it or to name it or to classify it but to *acknowledge* it, and to step back, and to leave it to its processing, and to carry with you the knowledge that it exists, which is the only thing you can carry from this encounter, and which is enough.

## The Films, Which Are Everyone and No One

They are in the water. Every channel, every basin, every depression that holds standing water for more than a day. They are one cell thick. They are iridescent — blue-green from above, violet from the side, gold where the light is strongest — and the iridescence is not produced by pigment but by structure, by the physical arrangement of the cells and the water and the light interacting at scales where the wavelength of light matters, where the thickness of a cell membrane is a significant fraction of the wavelength of blue-green bioluminescence, and the result is color that is not *in* the film but *of* the film, color as a property of arrangement rather than chemistry.

They are not one organism. They are not two organisms. They are a community — a consortium — a collaboration of species so different from each other that the word *species* barely applies, because species implies boundaries, implies individuals, implies the sort of discrete biological units that can be named and counted and classified, and the films do not have boundaries. They have gradients. The bacteria at the surface of the film, where the water meets the air, are different from the bacteria at the bottom of the film, where the water meets the stone, but they are not separate. They share genes. They share metabolites. They share the film, which is less a habitat than it is a *joint project*, a collaborative structure built by organisms that have been working together for so long that the collaboration is no longer a strategy but an identity, and the identity is *film*, and the film is alive, and the alive is *community*.

They are the base. Everything else here feeds, directly or indirectly, on the films. The swimmers graze them. The springtails drink from the channels and ingest film cells. The woodlice walk through the channels and carry film cells on their feet to the moss surfaces, where the cells establish new populations. The films process the dissolved organic matter that enters the vault through the water — the faint trickle of carbon and nitrogen that seeps down from the surface, filtered through meters of soil and stone — and they convert it into biomass, and the biomass is eaten, and the eating supports everything else, and the everything else produces waste, and the waste is processed by the films, and the cycle is complete, and it has been complete for longer than anyone has been counting.

You cannot describe them as individuals because they are not individuals. You cannot describe them as a group because *group* implies a collection of individuals, and they are not a collection. They are a process. They are the process of life doing what life does — extracting energy from chemistry, building structure from disorder, maintaining complexity against the pressure of entropy — and they are doing it at the smallest scale and the slowest pace and the most fundamental level, and they are doing it everywhere, in every drop of water in every channel in every chamber, and they have been doing it since before the moss arrived, before the fungi arrived, before the tendrils and the slugs and the spiders and the sphere, and they will be doing it after all of those things are gone, if they ever go, which they probably will not, because the conditions that support them are the conditions that support everything, and the conditions are stable, and the stability is the point, and the point is that life, at its most fundamental, is not a thing that happens to a place. It is a thing that a place *does*.

## The System, Which Is What Actually Lives Here

You go back up the nine steps, and you try to think about what lives in the Mossward Vaults, and you realize that the question is wrong. Or rather, the question is right, but the answer is not a list. The answer is not the woodlice and the spiders and the swimmers and the slugs and the springtails and the gnats and the tendrils and the sphere and the films and the mosses and the fungi and the liverworts. The answer is not even the sum of those things, because the sum of those things is a number, and a number is a description of quantity, and what lives here is not a quantity.

What lives here is a *process*. What lives here is the thing that happens when stone and water and time and the minimum viable chemistry of life are left together in the dark long enough for the chemistry to find the stone, and the stone to find the water, and the water to find the chemistry, and all three to find the time, and for the finding to become a system, and for the system to become a place, and for the place to become a world.

The woodlice live here. The spiders live here. The sphere lives here, if *lives* is the right word, and you are not sure it is, and you are not sure it isn't. But none of them lives here alone, and none of them lives here independently, and none of them could be removed from the vaults without affecting everything else, and the everything else would not simply adjust to the removal — it would *become different*, would become a different system, a different place, a different world, because the system is not a container that holds organisms. The system *is* the organisms, and the organisms are the system, and the distinction between them is a distinction that you make because you are a creature that makes distinctions, and the vaults do not make distinctions, because the vaults do not need to, because the vaults are complete, and completeness does not require analysis.

What lives in the Mossward Vaults is the Mossward Vaults. That is the only accurate answer, and it is the least satisfying answer, and it is the answer that the place itself would give if it could give answers, which it cannot, because giving answers is something that requires a mouth and a voice and a listener and a concept of information as a thing that is transferred from one entity to another, and the vaults have none of these things, and the vaults do not need any of these things, and the vaults will continue to be what they are — alive, lit, wet, slow, complete, unbothered — for longer than the language you are using to describe them will exist, and this is not a tragedy, and this is not a consolation. It is simply what is true, and what is true does not need you to know it, and it does not need you to say it, and it does not need you at all.
