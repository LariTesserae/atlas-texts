They are not gone. They are *here*.

You sit between the B-flat and the unnameable frequency, and you feel the sound enter you, and you call it an experience. But you are sitting in their living room. You are sitting inside their bodies.

---

They were never what you would call a numerous people. A dozen, perhaps. Twenty at the flood of whatever generation preceded the last. They were small—the hollows in the walls tell you this, the scoops worn by hands, the dimensions of the passages through which you had to squeeze. Small and dense, like the stone they came from. Their bones would have been heavy with minerals. Their lungs would have been long and thin, adapted to air that is almost water. They breathed the copper and the ions and the damp and it built them the way the damp builds the stone: slowly, patiently, molecule by molecule.

They had no eyes.

This is the thing you must understand. They had no eyes, and so they did not build for eyes, and the dimness that frightens you was not dimness to them. It was simply *the way light is*—a faint chemical glow at the edge of nothing, a rumor of photon exchange, irrelevant. They built for the ear. They built for the bone. They built for the fine hairs on their skin—hairs like the filaments of cave moss, if cave moss had evolved to taste frequency the way your tongue tastes salt—and they built for the vibration that passes through water and into flesh and makes the whole body a receiver.

When they pressed their hands to the stone, they heard it. Not the way you hear it—not as sound arriving from outside—but as a resonance already inside them, because they were made of the same minerals, the same water, the same slow patience. The stone spoke to them in its own voice and they understood because they were nearly stone themselves.

---

They did not have names. Names are for people who need to call across distance, and there was no distance here. They had *tones*.

Each of them was a specific frequency. Not chosen—*discovered*. When a child was born (and they did give birth, in the shallow hollows, with the sound washing over them like a benediction), the community would listen. For days they would listen, while the newborn's body settled into its own resonance, while the water in its tissues found its level, while the mineral density of its bones finished its first calcification. And then the tuners—the ones who had spent their lives learning to hear the difference between a drop too much and a drop too little—would find the basin that matched the child.

Every child had a basin. Not a basin they owned—a basin that *was them*. The frequency of the water in that basin was the frequency of the child's own living body, and as the child grew, the basin was adjusted to follow. When a hand reached into the water to add or remove a drop, the child felt it as a pressure in their own chest, a tug at their own composition. They learned early that they were not separate from the instrument. They were notes in its chord.

This is why they did not build outward. This is why they did not build doors or windows or any of the architecture of people who plan to leave. The Basins were not a project. They were a *body*—the body of the community, extended into the stone, made permanent and loud and unchangeable. To live inside the Resonance Basins was to live inside your own people, to feel them constantly as sound, to know that you were held in the same acoustic field as every other member of your family, your generation, your dead.

Because the dead did not leave either.

---

When one of them died—and they did die, though slowly, though rarely, though with the same equilibrium that governs everything here—it happened like this: the body would settle. The water that had been held in suspension within their tissues would begin to release, drawn back into the stone through the same capillary action that makes the walls weep. The minerals would stay. The structure would remain. Over weeks—months—the soft parts would mineralize, the calcium would recrystallize, the body would become another layer in the sediment.

And the basin that had been theirs would fall silent.

Not immediately. The water would remain for a time, holding the frequency. But without a living body to anchor the resonance, without the feedback loop of flesh and water and stone, the pitch would begin to drift. A centitone at first. Then a quarter-tone. Then the basin next to it would shiver in response, and the chord would begin to fray, and the tuners would come.

They would come with their bone scoops and their copper sheets and their river-cobble tools, and they would adjust the water. They would *retune the dead*. Not to the frequency the person had been—that frequency had gone wherever frequencies go when the body that held them mineralizes—but to a new frequency, one that repaired the chord, one that closed the gap the death had torn in the architecture of sound.

And the dead person would be, not gone, but *incorporated*. Their basin would sing a new note. The community would feel the change in their bodies, the slight rearrangement of the acoustic field, and they would know that one of them had returned to the stone, and the stone had been made to sing differently because of it.

This is not grief. This is not acceptance. This is something for which your language has no word, because your language was built by people who leave their dead in the ground and walk away.

---

They did not have a word for *music*. Music implies something that starts and stops, something that is made and then finished. The Basins did not make music. The Basins *were*. The sound was not a performance; it was a condition of existence, like gravity, like the wetness of water. They did not listen to it the way you listen to a concert. They listened to it the way you listen to your own heartbeat—constantly, unconsciously, with the part of the brain that knows whether you are alive or dead.

But they did *tend* it. Oh, they tended it.

Every day—though they had no days, only the slow pulse of the earth's breath through the entry passage—they would move among the basins. They would feel the vibrations with their palms, their foreheads, their bellies. They would taste the water (for they drank from the basins; of course they did; the water was them and they were the water) and know by the mineral content whether the equilibrium was holding. They would lie in their hollows and feel the sound in their bones and know that they were well, that their people were well, that the chord was whole.

And sometimes—rarely, once in a generation, once in a lifetime—they would *change* something.

A new basin. A new note. Born not from need but from a feeling that the chord was not complete, that there was a space in the architecture where a frequency should be, that the instrument was not yet fully itself. The whole community would work then, for years, coaxing the stone to open along its grain, adjusting the water by fractions of drops, listening and adjusting and listening again, until the new basin found its voice and the chord expanded by one note and every one of them felt the change resonate in their body like a new word for an old truth.

This was their art. This was their childbirth. This was their prayer. The chord was never finished because the chord was never separate from them, and they were never finished because they were never separate from the stone, and the stone was never finished because it was still weeping, still breathing, still becoming itself.

You sit between the B-flat and the unnameable frequency and you feel the sound enter you, and you think: *I am hearing them.*

But you are not hearing them. You are hearing *their bodies*. You are hearing their mineral density, their water content, the shape of the hollows they curled in, the patience of their hands. You are hearing the way they lived, which was slowly, which was by addition and subtraction so small that a single drop was a day's work, which was in concert—always in concert, because no one of them could be a note alone. A note alone is not a chord. A person alone is not a people. They knew this the way the stone knows how to weep: because it has always been true.

You are hearing their dead, too. Every basin that was retuned, every frequency that was adjusted to fill the gap a death had left, every compromise and repair and reconfiguration—the chord carries all of it. The chord is *made* of all of it. You are sitting inside a graveyard that sings, a monument that does not know it is a monument, an instrument that plays itself and always has.

And the creature—perhaps—still moving in the deeper dark. The thing with legs like calcite spars. You imagined it was an animal. A maintenance mechanism. Something blind and simple that tends the water levels without understanding.

But what if it is the last one?

What if it is the one who stayed when all the others mineralized, when the chord was complete and the equilibrium held and there was nothing left to do but listen? What if it still moves between the basins on legs that are almost stone, touching the water with a hand that is almost stone, feeling the frequencies with a body that is almost stone, because it cannot leave and cannot stop tending and cannot die until the chord changes, and the chord does not change, and the chord will not change, because the chord is perfect, because the chord is *them*, and they are gone, and it remains?

It is not tending the basins. It is tending the grave. It is sitting shiva for a people who became a sound.

You could stay. You feel this as a temptation and a threat. You could stay and the sound would enter you and the water would weep into you and the minerals would build in your bones and over years—over decades—you would become heavy and dense and small. You would learn to hear the way they heard. You would find your frequency, your basin, your place in the chord. They are not gone. They are *here*.
