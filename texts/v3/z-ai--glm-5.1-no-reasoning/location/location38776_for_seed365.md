# The Gallows Garden of Cull Vorne

## I.

There is a place in the highlands where the old town was never quite finished and never quite abandoned. Cull Vorne. A hamlet-sized cluster of stone and timber structures perched on a mesa shoulder that jut out from the young basalt of the ridgeline like teeth from a split jaw. The rock here is dark, fine-grained, still carrying the smell of deep earth — it was uplifted not long ago, geologically speaking. A few centuries, maybe. The faces of the cliffs below the settlement are sharp-edged, uneroded, their columns and fractures still holding the geometric violence of their emergence. Frost has barely begun to round the corners. Scree slopes beneath the mesa are coarse and angular, not yet broken down to smoothness. You can cut your hand on the stones.

The town — if thirty buildings constitutes a town — was built by people who came up here because the lowlands were no longer safe. That is the shape of the story that survives. The details have been eaten by the thing that makes this place what it is.

---

## II.

The air holds moisture the way a wrung cloth holds dampness — not soaking, not dripping, but present in every breath. A fine mist sits in the highland basin most hours of the day, thickening toward evening into something almost rain, thinning toward midday into something almost clarity. Never fully one or the other. The condensation beads on every surface: on stone walls, on the thorny caspia bushes that cluster along the mesa's rim, on the threads of the silk-moths that roost in the eaves. You feel it in your lungs. Cool, almost cold, carrying the mineral smell of fresh basalt and the green, living smell of aggressive vegetation.

The lushness is the first thing that strikes you, if you arrive in the hours when the mist thins. The plateau and its slopes are covered in growth so dense it seems to be competing with itself. Ferns of a kind found nowhere else — thick-stalked, blue-green, their fronds unfolding in spirals as tight as fists — rise chest-high in the flatter areas. Mosses coat the angular stones in layers so thick they look like green fur. Climbing vines with dark waxy leaves have claimed every vertical surface, including the walls of the buildings, pulling themselves into the mortar gaps, prying stones slowly apart. Between the ferns and the mosses and the vines, the ground itself is hidden. You walk on a living mattress. It gives beneath your feet and springs back.

This is strange, because the cool air and intermittent moisture should not support this. It is too high, too cold, too dry in the intervals between mist. Something else is feeding this growth. Something is enriching the soil.

---

## III.

The settlement was laid out in an organized way — you can see the planning in its bones. A central road, paved with flat basalt slabs, runs from the mesa's edge to its highest point where a larger structure sits, half-collapsed. Buildings line the road on both sides, rectangular, solidly built, their walls fitted stone, their roofs originally timber and thatch. Most roofs have fallen in. The walls endure. Doorways are low, narrow, built to conserve heat. Windows are scarce — just slits, vertical, barely wide enough to pass a hand through.

The construction is primitive but skilled. No metal fittings. No mortar as you would recognize it — the builders used a paste of crushed shell and clay that has hardened into something almost as durable as the stone itself. The tools that made these walls were stone and bone. You can see the chisel marks where obsidian blades worked the basalt into shape. The timber, where it survives, shows the smooth cuts of ground-stone axes. Everything is hand-worked. Everything bears the mark of individual effort.

But the town was not built all at once. There are layers. The oldest buildings, near the mesa's center, are cruder — smaller stones, rougher fitting, more gap than wall in places. The outer buildings are more refined. And there is a ring of structures at the very edge, near the cliff's rim, that are different entirely: larger, more ambitious, with carved lintels and threshold stones incised with patterns that might be decorative or might be something else. These were built last. They were built by people who expected to stay.

They did stay. The town did not fail. It was not abandoned in the way towns are usually abandoned — through fire, or famine, or conquest. What happened to Cull Vorne is more particular than that.

---

## IV.

The light here is permanently dim. The mist sees to that, but even when it thins, the highland basin sits in a fold of the ridgeline that receives only oblique sun. Direct light reaches the mesa for perhaps an hour at midday, and even then it comes filtered and angled, throwing long shadows that seem to hold their shape too well. The rest of the time, the light is diffuse, gray-blue, directionless. It comes from everywhere and nowhere. It makes the green of the vegetation look deeper, almost black. It makes the stone walls look wet even when they are not.

In this dimness, the fauna thrive.

---

## V.

There are the moths first. Silk-moths, the settlers called them, though they are not moths in any strict taxonomic sense. They are the size of a human palm, with broad, translucent wings that catch the mist and hold it — the condensation beads along their wing-veins and drips in fine threads, which is where the name comes from. They roost in the eaves of the buildings, in the collapsed roof-timbers, in the gaps between stones. Hundreds of them. Thousands, perhaps, across the whole settlement. They are silent. They do not flutter. They move by opening their wings and letting the air currents carry them, gliding from roost to roost with a precision that suggests they are reading the wind in ways we cannot perceive.

They are not the strangest thing here.

The ground between the buildings — beneath the ferns, beneath the moss — is tunnelled. Not by rodents. Not by any creature you would recognize. The tunnels are smooth-walled, roughly circular, about the width of a human arm. They honeycomb the soil of the mesa in a network so extensive that in places the ground is hollow. You learn this quickly. You learn to walk on the stone-paved road and not on the earth. If you step on the wrong spot, the ground gives. Not a collapse — a yielding. The vegetation mat sags beneath you and you feel the void beneath, and you pull your foot back quickly because you do not know what lives in those tunnels.

Something does. You can see the entrances at dusk — small, rounded openings at the base of the stone walls, half-hidden by fern fronds. And at dusk, things emerge.

---

## VI.

They are called the Pale Ones, in the fragments of record the settlers left behind. They left behind very little — no books, no scrolls, just carvings on the threshold stones of the outer buildings, pictographic, hard to interpret. The Pale Ones are depicted in these carvings as elongated figures, limbless or many-limbed — the carvings are ambiguous — emerging from the ground beneath the buildings. In one carving, they seem to be climbing the walls. In another, they seem to be dissolving into them.

What they actually look like: pale, soft-bodied, something like a salamander and something like a slug and something like nothing you have a name for. They are the length of your forearm, segmented, with no visible eyes. They move by contraction, an undulating motion that is slow but inexorable. Their skin is translucent. You can see their internal structures — dark, branching, almost like root systems — through the membrane of their bodies. They are wet. The mist clings to them. They leave faint trails on the stone where they pass, trails that dry to a faint mineral residue.

They emerge at dusk and move through the settlement in patterns that seem purposeful. They climb the walls. They enter the buildings through gaps in the stonework. They cluster around the bases of the ferns and the vines and the mosses, pressing their bodies against the roots, and they stay there, motionless, for hours. Then they withdraw, returning to their tunnels before dawn.

They are feeding the plants. That is the only conclusion that fits. Their bodies, their waste, whatever process occurs when they press against the root systems — this is what sustains the impossible lushness. The ferns grow where the Pale Ones cluster. The vines climb where the Pale Ones have walked. The moss is thickest on the walls they visit most frequently. The settlement has become a garden, and the Pale Ones are its gardeners.

---

## VII.

The settlers understood this. The carvings show it — not clearly, not in any way you would call scientific, but they understood the relationship. They depicted the Pale Ones beside the plants. They depicted the plants beside the Pale Ones. In the largest carving, on the threshold of the central structure, they depicted something else: a human figure, lying on the ground, with Pale Ones pressing against it.

The settlers did not leave Cull Vorne because it failed. They stayed. They stayed and they learned what the Pale Ones were, and what the garden wanted, and what the garden would give in return.

The garden gives food. The ferns produce tubers — dense, starchy, unlike any fern you know. The vines produce a hard-shelled fruit with sweet, dark flesh. The mosses, when prepared correctly, yield a nutrient paste. The settlement could feed itself. The settlers did not need to leave. They needed only to tend the garden, and the garden needed only to be tended, and the Pale Ones needed only to do what they did, and the cycle continued.

But the garden also takes.

---

## VIII.

The buildings are mostly empty now. Not of objects — there are still stone tools in the corners, still woven-fiber mats on the floors, still the detritus of daily life. They are empty of people. The people are in the ground.

Not buried. Not in graves. In the tunnels.

You can find them if you look, though I would not recommend looking. The Pale Ones' tunnels open into chambers — small, smooth-walled, organic in shape, as if the soil itself had been shaped rather than excavated. In these chambers, the settlers lie. Not dead. Not alive. Something between. Their skin has the same translucent quality as the Pale Ones' skin. Their bodies are still, their breathing so slow it is nearly imperceptible. Roots — fern roots, vine roots, moss rhizoids — have grown into them and through them. They are part of the garden now. The Pale Ones cluster around them as they cluster around the plants.

The settlers are still tending the garden. They have been tending it for a very long time.

---

## IX.

The sound of this place is what stays with you.

It is quiet. Not silent — there is no true silence here — but the sounds are so small they exist at the edge of perception. The drip of condensation from moth-wings. The faint rustle of fern fronds shifting in air currents too slight to feel. The soft, wet sound of a Pale One moving across stone. The creak of a vine thickening against a wall. If you sit in one of the empty buildings at dusk and close your eyes, you hear all of these things layered together, and underneath them, below them, you hear something else: a low, continuous vibration, felt more than heard, that seems to come from the ground itself. From the tunnels. From the chambers. From the bodies in the chambers. It might be breathing. It might be the sound of roots growing through flesh. It might be something else entirely.

The moths are silent. The Pale Ones are silent. The ferns are silent. The mist is silent. The only loud thing that ever happens here is the occasional rockfall from the uneroded cliffs below the mesa — a sharp crack, a clatter of angular basalt tumbling down the scree slope, and then silence again, faster than you would expect, because the vegetation at the slope's base catches the stones and holds them.

---

## X.

The world's rules, as far as they can be known:

The mist comes and goes on a cycle that seems tied to temperature and wind direction, but the cycle is not perfectly regular. Sometimes the mist thickens for days. Sometimes it thins for hours. The settlers learned to read its patterns. The Pale Ones emerge only when the mist is thick and the light is at its dimmest.

The moths roost during the dim hours and glide during the lighter ones. They do not seem to serve a function in the garden's ecology, or if they do, it is not obvious. They may be incidental. They may be the garden's scouts, reading the wind for information the Pale Ones cannot access. They may be what the Pale Ones become, or what the settlers will become, or what the garden produces when it has no other use for its resources. No one knows.

The plants grow only where the Pale Ones tend them. Outside the settlement's boundary, the mesa is sparse — scrubby, wind-bitten grasses, lichen on rock, nothing else. The lushness is strictly contained. It follows the tunnel network. It follows the Pale Ones' routes. It follows, ultimately, the bodies in the chambers.

The garden is expanding. Slowly. New tunnels appear at the edges of the network each year. New ferns sprout beyond the old boundary. The settlers, in their chambers, are not decaying. They are growing. Their bodies are larger than human bodies should be. Their limbs have elongated. Their fingers have reached out along the tunnel walls, and roots have followed the fingers, and the roots have become the fingers, or the fingers have become the roots, and the distinction no longer matters.

The garden is patient. The garden has time. The cliffs are young and will last for millennia. The mist will keep coming. The Pale Ones will keep tending. The plants will keep growing. And the settlement of Cull Vorne, which was never abandoned, will continue to be inhabited by its people, who will continue to be part of what they built, and what built them, and what is building still.

---

## XI.

If you come here — and there are those who do, who find the mesa by following the ridgeline, who come seeking the tubers or the fruit or the hard-shelled nuts or simply the strangeness of it — you will find the buildings open and empty. You will find the road still paved. You will find the central structure half-collapsed but passable. You will find tools you can use, mats you can sleep on, shelter from the wind.

You will find the garden willing to feed you.

You will find the mist thickening at dusk.

You will find the Pale Ones emerging from the ground, pressing themselves against the roots, tending what grows.

And if you stay long enough — if you eat enough of what the garden provides, if you breathe enough of the mist, if you sleep on the ground where the tunnels run beneath you — you will begin to feel the vibration. In your chest. In your bones. In the spaces between your thoughts. You will begin to understand what the settlers understood, which is this:

The garden does not need you to tend it. The garden needs you to *be* tended. The garden needs you to lie down and let the roots come in and the Pale Ones press close and the moss grow over you and the mist settle on your skin and stay.

The garden needs you to become part of it.

And the garden is very, very persuasive.
