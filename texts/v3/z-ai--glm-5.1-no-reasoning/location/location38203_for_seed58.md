The heat comes from below. Not the baking, solar heat of a surface desert, but a deep, mechanical exhalation—a continuous thermal sigh rising through the iron grates of the floor. You are beneath the skin of the world, in a place that should not exist at this scale, yet persists: a subterranean ward, barely the size of a cathedral nave, packed to its low ceiling with a suffocating density of industry. 

The space is young. The bite of the drill-head is still fresh on the stone. Pillars of raw, black basalt rise from the floor, their edges sharp and unhanded, intersecting awkwardly with the grid of newly laid brick and riveted steel. There has been no time for the smoothing hand of erosion here; the walls are a chaotic, near-touching jumble of fresh-minted pipes, exposed girders, and trailing wire conduits. The density is staggering. To move through the narrow corridors between the machines is to slide sideways, shoulders brushing hot metal on one side and damp masonry on the other.

It is a bustling place entirely devoid of life. There is no fauna down here—no scuttling insects, no rats in the shadows, no witnesses to the labor but the machines themselves. The ecology is one of oil, steam, and copper. And yet, it is profoundly active. The ward throbs with the rhythm of scaled production. Pistons stroke in tireless, breathless cycles; camshafts rotate; heavy iron stamps rise and fall in the dimness, crushing and processing unseen materials. The motion is constant, mechanical, and desperate, like a heart beating in a body that has forgotten why it needs blood. 

Light is an afterthought, provided only by the sparse, bare bulbs wired haphazardly above the walkways. They cast a dim, amber glaze over the machinery, creating overlapping halos of shadow. Between these pools of weak light, the far reaches of the ward are left to the imagination, hinted at only by the glint of moisture on a pipe or the dull absorption of black stone.

Water is a sporadic, unwelcome guest here. It does not flow; it ambushes. Deep below the foundry floor, the drills struck a pocket of ancient seepage. Now, condensation weeps constantly from the cold-water mains running through the boiling air, sweating onto the hot casings of the boilers below. It hits the metal with a sharp, hissing whisper—a sound like a thousand burning wicks. In the crevices where the iron meets the raw basalt, the intermittent moisture gathers in dark, steaming puddles. It is here, and only here, that vegetation dares to intrude. Sparse, pale threads of thermophilic fungus and stringy, chlorophyll-starved moss cling to the mortar. They drink from the hissing condensation, feeding on the heat and the mineral sweat of the broken rock, growing in stringy, anemic tufts that seem to vibrate with the trembling of the engines.

The sound is mostly quiet—not in volume, but in variety. The roar of the machines is so constant, so ubiquitous, that it becomes a form of silence. It is a thick, bass-heavy drone that flattens the auditory landscape, masking any echo or footstep. There is no conversation to cut the din, no animal cry, no wind. Just the relentless, friction-worn chug of the industrial loop.

There is something deeply unusual about this place, a wrongness that settles into the bones the longer you stand in the heat. It is the dissonance of a space that is overwhelmingly active and entirely empty. The machines are scaled for human operators—there are ladders runged for boots, valves sized for hands, and portholes positioned for eyes—but the space is too cramped, too claustrophobic for a crew to actually man them. The infrastructure is layered and organized, clearly designed by an intellect, yet it operates as a closed loop, fulfilling a mechanized imperative to a town that has no residents. It feels as though the ward was built, switched on, and immediately bricked up; an engine room for a subterranean city that was never populated, endlessly grinding and sweating and consuming its own heat in the dark.
