The plateau lies so high that the clouds press against it like a low ceiling, and the cold is a physical weight—a slow, deep blue cold that has been pressing down on this place for geological ages. The air is thin. Breathing feels like an act of negotiation.

Once, this was a city. You can still see the logic of it in the worn-down stone. Streets, or what were streets, run in shallow grooves between the remains of walls that now rise no higher than a man's knee. The stone is so ancient, so eroded by time and moisture, that it has lost all sharpness. Everything has been worn to the smoothness of river rocks. The buildings—though calling them that feels generous now—are mere suggestions, ankle-high outlines in the rock, like the foundations of a dollhouse exposed. The erosion here is primordial. This place reached its final form long ago and has simply been resting in it. Nothing grows tall here except the cold.

But the lushness—there is lushness here, and it is strange. Cushions of moss cover everything, thick and deep, in shades of green so dark they are nearly black. The moss drinks from the intermittent rain and from the condensation that forms on the cold stone every evening when the fog rolls in like a slow tide. Between the moss, small ferns grow in the sheltered hollows where the city's drains once ran, finding water in the grooves the old engineers carved. There are no trees. Nothing stands upright. The vegetation lies flat against the ancient stone, as if pressing itself downward against the cold, hugging the earth. Flowers bloom at ground level—small, pale things that open like secrets. The roots have had eons to work; they hold the worn stone together now, a symbiosis so old the city and the moss have become one material.

The scale is intimate. You could walk the perimeter of this place in ten minutes. It is a room, essentially—a room open to the sky, walled by the shallowest ridges of ancient masonry, floored in moss and slow time. There is a single central square, maybe fifteen paces across, where the stone is bare and slightly concave, holding a thin sheet of cold water after the rains. Around it, the low walls create the faintest sense of enclosure, of interiority, as if the city remembers being a city and maintains the shape of one out of habit.

The emptiness is profound. This is not a place of absence but of evacuation—a hollow that once held many lives and now holds almost none. The space between any two presences feels vast. A single crow lands on a low wall and the whole place feels occupied. The emptiness has a texture; it presses against you. You feel like an intruder in a place that has been slowly, patiently emptying itself for longer than your species has had language.

And yet it is loud. The sound is the first thing you notice, the thing that makes this place uncanny. The wind is constant—a high, keening wind that finds every shallow groove in the ancient stone and sings through them. The old drainage channels, the worn doorways, the gutters the builders carved: they have all become instruments. The city plays itself. The sound changes with the fog and the rain, rising to a moan when the clouds drop, softening to a hum when the air clears. Sometimes it sounds like voices, very distant, speaking a language that was already dead before the ice came. The moss dampens nothing; the sound moves through this place as if the stone were still fresh and new, as if the walls still stood.

Life is rare here, but present. There are insects in the moss—small black beetles that move very slowly, conserving heat. A type of salamander lives in the perpetual dampness of the central pool, pale and nearly transparent, its heart visible and slow. Sometimes a hawk circles far above, riding the wind, but it never lands. There are no mammals. The cold forbids them. The ecosystem is minimal—a few species locked in ancient relationship, moving at the pace of the cold.

The strangeness of the place accumulates slowly. It is not any single thing but the totality: a city so old it has become geology, a lushness that grows only horizontally, a loudness that seems to come from the stone itself, an emptiness that feels deliberate. The fog makes it stranger. When it thickens, the low walls seem to rise, the outlines of buildings almost reassemble, and you can almost see the people who lived here—almost hear them beneath the wind's keening, going about their lives in a city that has been dying for a hundred thousand years and has finally, peacefully, finished.

The place has no name now. It may have had one once, but the syllables wore away with the stone. What remains is a pattern: walls, channels, moss, cold, sound. A persistence. The world has moved on from this plateau—there are no roads leading here, no rivers nearby, no reason to come—but the city remains, worn to its essence, lying flat against the earth, singing in the wind.
