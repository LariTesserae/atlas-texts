# Firstgrove, in the Long Room

You come down two kilometres of rock in a cage, and step out into a darkness so large it has distance. That is the first surprise of the Long Room: not that it is underground, but that it is *big* — a cavern with the floor-plan of a valley, a ceiling three hundred metres up, and no walls you can see from the middle. The second surprise is that the ceiling is lit. A lattice of cables and vines is strung up there like rigging on an inverted ship, and it glows — a soft, diffuse amber-white that reads as an overcast morning, bright enough to work by, honest enough that greens look green and wax looks white. The town's day hangs overhead like a manufactured sky, because it is one.

Below it, scattered across the dark floor, the town itself: not one settlement but a loose constellation of lit groves, connected by lines of light — vine-ropes strung on poles from grove to grove, thin bright threads across the black. Between the groves, nothing. Fallow ground, raw gravel, darkness. Firstgrove is a town the way a handful of embers is a fire: sparse, spaced, each point burning on its own.

The geology is new, and it looks new. The south face of the cavern, where the boring machines still grind, is glassy-fresh rock, unweathered, edges sharp as struck flint. There is no rain down here, no frost, no wind — nothing to wear anything down — so nothing in the Long Room erodes. It only gets *overgrown*. Dust falls where it falls and stays there, fine as flour, never stirred. The scree fans at the base of the cut walls keep the fracture-bright faces they were born with. Age, in this place, is not a matter of weathering. Age is girth. The oldest district is not the most worn; it is the most grown.

The air is cool — ten degrees, dry as paper, smelling of turpentine and hot wax and cold stone, with a mineral sweetness underneath like struck flint. There is no water here at all. The whole biosphere runs dry. The trees — and everything is trees, eventually — have waxy bark and resin sap, a light amber solvent that is the blood of the entire economy. Nothing rots the wet way. Nothing molds, nothing rusts, nothing mildews. Things that die here mummify into their own gum. And damp, when it ever appears, is treated as pollution: great banks of deliquescent salt called the lungs stand along the cavern walls, crystal shelves that breathe the air and eat its moisture, tended by guilds of salters. If it ever rained in the Long Room, it would be a catastrophe.

Because everything is fuel, the oldest law is fire. No open flame, anywhere, ever. All heat lives inside something: the forges are ceramic hearts buried in sand, the engines burn in sealed cells, the metalworkers spark behind blankets of spun rock. The only legal warmth is biological. Each home's hearth is a thermogenic trunk — a heat-tree, slow and fat, that burns its own sap internally and holds its heart-wood at blood-warm. Benches are grafted in a ring around it. Social life in Firstgrove happens in warm crowns, families gathered under a heat-tree like people around a stove.

The architecture is horticulture. Halls are trained, not built: stillwood saplings grown up around frames, hollowing as the trunks widen, until the frame is removed and the room remains — walls of dense living wood, furniture grafted in place, doors that must be pruned twice a year or they close. A building here is a child. It is planted, trained, fed, and eventually it outgrows its family and becomes a public hall. The streets are simply the spaces the trees agreed to leave, and the paving is a root mat, grown inch by inch over the raw gravel until the path is soft, springy, and slightly warm.

Under the main street run the sap mains, and this is the town's heartbeat: a slow two-beat pulse, once every thirty seconds, felt in your boots before you hear it. Sap pressure rises as the air cools, so the tapping happens in the hours before night — tappers walking the rows with their buckets in the dimming, the way dairymen once walked in the dark. The refinery sits in a stone shed at the old grove: copper stills, grown columns, the raw sweet sap split into fractions — light solvent, lamp-feed, heavy pitch, feedstock. It is an industrial town in the way a hive is industrial: mechanized, piped, scheduled, and alive underneath.

The day itself is scheduled. Dawn in Firstgrove is an event with a crew. The dosers walk the feed-lines at the fixed hour and knock the valves, and the lamp-vines overhead begin to brighten in rings around each feed point, the light spreading stain-wise across the lattice until, an hour later, the whole ceiling is a white morning. Dusk runs the same film backwards. The calendar is a feed schedule. The seasons are decisions made by the dosers' guild — they run a long summer over the Nursery in growth season, a dim cold month over the old grove when the stillwoods harden off. If you ask someone here when something happened, they will tell you in feeds.

And the light runs the ecology, because everything down here has organized itself around it. At dusk the lampmoths rise — millions of them, wings of dry wax-paper that rasp when they fly, like pages turning in another room. They pollinate the day-vines; they are the reason the manufactured sky works. Moth-wardens tend the swarms. Red mites graze the wax blooms on the bark and are herded in slow migrating runs along the lamp roads, driven by children with wax-tipped wands. Borer-oxen — six-legged, hide like bark, patient as furniture — chew the fallen timber and haul the sap carts, and at rest they stand under the heat-trees, warming themselves, in an arrangement so old nobody knows which species domesticated which.

At the edge of the light live the stillhunters: flat grey animals that stand at the margin pretending to be slabs, and are, until a moth crosses too low. Beyond them, out in the unlit country between groves — the fallows, the dark miles — there is something larger. Nobody has lit it. Its footfalls come up through the ground as a slow pressure in the dust, and its tracks are where the lamp lines end. The rule is simple and universally kept: travel the lit lines. The dark between the groves belongs to other things.

For all its machinery, the town is mostly quiet. The sounds are few and each one matters: the two-beat of the mains; the tick of wood cooling after dusk, the whole town faintly clicking as it contracts like a ship at night; the paper-rasp of the moths; the patient far-off grind of the cutters opening the cavern southward; the faint crackle of the salt lungs at their work; the knock of gas in a pipe. Your own boots are the loudest thing on a side street. People speak low here, mostly because everything else does.

The town remembers by growing. Its archive is a grove of message-trees, where records are carved into sapwood and swell outward into bark over the years, the letters stretching but legible, the oldest records high overhead and unreadable without a ladder and a squint. Its dead are not buried but *set* — fixed in pitch and wax, slowly going amber, the founders standing at the main crossroads like dark lanterns. No one finds this grim. It is what the material wants to do.

And the material wants to do a great deal, because the whole place is barely born. The Nursery quarter is scaffolds and bandages — grafts wax-wrapped, trellises, half-formed rooms you can stand inside of and watch being built by inches. The cutters chew the south face a little further every season, and behind the machines come the roots, plastering and stabilizing the raw rooms the way finishers follow the rough crew. Machines open; plants finish. Give Firstgrove thirty years and the Nursery will be a district, the fallows a neighborhood, the lamp lines a grid, the cavern a country. Everyone here is living inside a beginning, and they know it, and there is a frontier cheerfulness in the low voices and the warm crowns.

At dusk the dosers throttle down, the ceiling dims to ember-traces along the feed-lines, and the moths rise in columns to the last bright thread. The town ticks as it cools. Out in the fallows, once, a heavy step, and then nothing. Firstgrove sleeps the way children sleep — quietly, at a slight angle, and growing the whole time.
