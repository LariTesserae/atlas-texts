The settlement fits in the palm of a shallow valley. It is cathedral-sized, maybe less—a cluster of forty structures huddled together as if for warmth, though warmth does not come. The land around it is flat, newly exposed, as if a skin of soil was peeled back only centuries ago. Maybe less. The edges of things are still sharp here. The stone walls of the buildings have not yet been softened by wind. The ground beneath your feet is powdery, mineral, compacted—somewhere between pumice and ash, but colder. Pale grey. Almost white where the thin light hits it right.

There is no water. Not in any form. No streams, no dew, no moisture in the air that your skin can register. The air is its own substance—thin, dry, moving slowly through the corridors between buildings like something that has learned to walk carefully. If you breathe through your mouth, your tongue goes dry in seconds. If you stand still, you can feel the air pulling moisture out of you, a slow, patient theft.

The buildings are not ruins. They are occupied, though not by anyone you can see right now. They are low, windowless, built from the same pale mineral as the ground, fitted together without mortar. The joints are tight. Someone knew how to make these. Someone needed to. The doors are narrow, and they face different directions—no obvious pattern, no cardinal alignment, no grid. The settlement grew, or was grown. The walls are thick. Inside them, you can assume, the air is different. Slower. Possibly damper, though that might be wishful thinking.

The light comes from everywhere and nowhere. The sky is low, pale, luminous with cloud cover so uniform it seems solid—a ceiling of grey light with no visible source. No sun, no shadows, no direction. Objects cast a faint, diffuse darkness beneath them, more like stains than shadows. Everything is visible and nothing is distinct. The furthest buildings blur into the grey air at a distance of perhaps two hundred meters. Beyond them, the land continues—you assume—but you cannot see it.

Silence. True silence. Not the silence of wilderness, which is full of insect drone and leaf movement and distant water. This is a silence like the inside of a sealed container. Your footsteps make no sound on the mineral ground—too soft, too fine. If you clap your hands, the sound vanishes. It doesn't echo. It doesn't travel. The air absorbs it, or the ground does, or the walls do. You are inside a fact, and facts do not reverberate.

And yet something is happening. The place is not dead.

The buildings breathe.

You notice it first as a pressure change—a subtle rhythm in the air around the largest structure, the one at the center of the cluster, which is slightly taller than the rest and has a ridge along its roof that might be decorative or might be functional. The walls of this building expand, almost imperceptibly, then contract. A cycle. In, out. Slow—perhaps one full breath every four minutes. The other buildings do it too, each at their own pace, desynchronized, like a village of sleepers.

Air moves because of this. Thin currents slide between the structures, shifting direction as one building exhales and another inhales. This is the wind here—not weather, but respiration. It carries nothing. No scent, no moisture, no pollen. Just pressure, displacement. The movement of emptiness through emptiness.

Something lives in the walls.

You see it finally—a dark line moving along the mortarless joint between two stones. It is not an insect. It is not a worm. It is more like a fiber, or a root, dark grey-green, the color of a bruise. It moves slowly, by extension—pushing forward from the tip, contracting behind. It is searching. For what, you cannot tell. It does not respond to your presence. It does not respond to light. It moves along the crevice and disappears into another.

There are more. In the lower joints, where the walls meet the ground, small networks of these fibers have established themselves—fibrous mats, grey-green and dry, spreading across the mineral surface like veins. They are rooted in the crevices and they extend outward, probing, branching. They do not drink. There is nothing to drink. They are conducting some other process—conversion, perhaps. Transforming the mineral itself into something they can use. The pale powder near the fibers is slightly discolored, slightly more fine. They are eating the stone. Very slowly.

This is the vegetation of this place. Root-like, vein-like, dry and dark against the pale ground.

When the buildings exhale, the fibers lengthen. When the buildings inhale, they retract slightly, pulling tight against the stone. They are synchronized with the breath. They are fed by it, or they feed it—part of the same system. The buildings and the fibers are one organism, or one ecosystem. The settlement is not built on the ground. It is grown from it, and it is still growing.

You walk between two structures and the corridor is narrow—barely wide enough for your shoulders. The walls here are covered in the grey-green fiber, thicker than in the open area, interwoven, forming a kind of matte surface that is soft to the touch and slightly warmer than the surrounding air. The fibers pulse. You feel it more than see it—a rhythm of expansion and contraction, slightly different from the building's breath, slightly faster. Something is being processed here. Something is being moved.

The corridor opens into a small courtyard—perhaps ten meters across, surrounded on all sides by the blank walls of buildings. The ground here is different. The pale mineral has been worn away in patches, exposing a darker layer beneath—heavier, denser, almost metallic in its dullness. The fibers are thick here, converging on the exposed patches, covering them completely in places. And in the center of the courtyard, rising from the floor, there is a column of the dark mineral, roughly waist-high, clearly shaped—wider at the base, narrowing to a flat top. It is a seat, or an altar, or a workstation.

It is not abandoned.

The fibers on the column's surface are arranged in patterns—not random growth, but deliberate. Spirals, tightly wound, moving upward. Information, possibly. Or decoration. Or a record. The flat top of the column is warm to the touch. Noticeably warm—the first warmth you have felt here. The fibers here are not grey-green but darker, almost black, and they are moving more quickly, expanding and contracting in a rhythm that suggests urgency, or attention.

Something knows you are here.

Not in the courtyard. Not visible. But the rhythm has changed—the building-breaths around you have synchronized, slightly, just enough to notice. The currents of air in the courtyard have shifted, all moving toward you, very gently. Not threatening. Investigating. The place is tasting you, through the air you displace, through the moisture you lose just by standing here.

You are the wettest thing in this landscape. You are an anomaly. A sudden abundance of water, walking through a world that has never known it.

The fibers on the column extend toward you. Slowly. A few millimeters per minute. They will not reach you unless you stand here for hours. But they are reaching.

The settlement continues to breathe around you, its forty structures expanding and contracting in their slow, patient cycle. The sky remains featureless, sourceless, dim. The air remains dry, remains thin, continues its slow theft of whatever you carry. And somewhere in the walls—in the deep crevices where the dark mineral meets the pale, in the spaces behind the fibers, in the warmth at the center of the largest structure—the process continues. Whatever this place is doing. Whatever it is making.

It does not need you. But it is curious about you, in the only way it can be—slowly, patiently, breath by breath, fiber by fiber, turning you into the same pale dust it has made of everything else that came here carrying something it could use.
