# The Vastifactory

The first thing you notice is the heat. It arrives not as weather but as property — as though temperature is simply what this place *is*, baked into its architecture the way mass is baked into stone. There is no sun to blame. The light comes from everywhere and nowhere, a diffuse sourceless illumination at the level of an overcast afternoon, and yet the heat is enormous, oppressive, a slow relentless pressure against the skin. It radiates upward from surfaces. It pools. It breathes.

The ground beneath your feet is not ground. There is no soil, no bedrock, no geological substrate. What you stand on is a floor — though *floor* feels too domestic a word for something that stretches to every horizon without break or seam. It is a material like dark ceramic, faintly warm, faintly textured with a fine grain that catches the diffuse light in irregular patterns. Not tile, not metal, not glass. Something else. Something manufactured at a scale that makes the word *manufactured* feel insufficient. If you kneel and press your palm flat against it, you can feel a barely perceptible vibration, deep and slow, like the heartbeat of something immense and patient.

This is a made world. Not a built world in the sense of structures added to a landscape — there is no landscape underneath. The landscape *itself* is the construction. Every surface, every contour, every gentle rise and broad shallow basin is the product of intent. Or at least of process. Whether anything remains that holds that intent is a different question.

---

The terrain is not flat, but it is open. Across distances that take hours to cross on foot, the floor varies in elevation by no more than a few meters, rising and falling in long swells that suggest the topography of a calm sea frozen mid-motion. These undulations create the illusion of horizons — the floor curves up ahead of you, rises to a low ridge, and falls away beyond, hiding whatever lies on the other side. But when you reach the ridge and look down, there is only more of the same: the dark floor extending, the sourceless light unchanging, the heat undiminished.

In the basins between these swells, moisture collects. Not water — not exactly. A film, a dampness, something between condensation and dew, beading on the ceramic surface in patterns that follow the micro-texture of the material. In the deeper basins, where the floor dips low enough and the trapped humid air grows thick enough, this condensation accumulates into shallow pools — never more than ankle-deep, usually less, warm and perfectly still, their surfaces mirroring the diffused light in pale gray sheets. These pools are scattered across the landscape with no obvious pattern. You might walk for an hour through dry floor and then pass three pools within a hundred meters, each one sitting in its shallow depression like a dropped mirror.

The water — if it is water — is warm. Body-temperature or slightly above. It has no taste, or rather, it has the taste of warmth itself, mineral and flat. It does not support life. Nothing lives in it. Nothing lives anywhere here.

---

There is no wind. This is important. In a place this vast — and it is vast, genuinely continental in scale, large enough that its own moisture cycles form a crude weather system — you would expect air movement. But the atmosphere is strangely static. The heat builds. Moisture evaporates from the pool surfaces in invisible threads, rising into the thick air. Humidity accumulates until the air can hold no more. And then, without warning, it condenses again. Not as rain, exactly — as a sudden heavy mist that descends from above like a curtain dropping, coating every surface in a fresh layer of dampness that persists for ten or twenty minutes before the heat reasserts itself and the cycle begins again.

These mist-events are the closest thing this place has to weather. They arrive without sound. There is no wind to announce them, no drop in temperature, no shift in pressure that you can feel. The air simply becomes wet, visually wet, visibility reducing from kilometers to perhaps a hundred meters as the moisture thickens into something almost opaque. The ceramic floor grows slick. The pools shiver, their surfaces finally disturbed, rippling in patterns that reveal the floor's deep vibration more clearly than anything else. And then the mist lifts — or rather, dissipates, dissolving back into the heavy air — and the landscape returns to its dry, hot, silent self.

This cycle — evaporation, saturation, condensation, evaporation — is the primary dynamic of this place. It is always running. The heat drives it. The scale sustains it. The vast enclosed volume of air — enclosed by what? By nothing visible. The sky, if there is a sky, is lost in the same sourceless diffusion that provides the light. There is no ceiling, or if there is, it is so far above that it plays no role in the felt experience of the place. The weather simply exists, self-contained, self-driving, a closed thermodynamic loop running on heat that comes from the floor itself.

---

Vegetation is nearly absent, and what exists is difficult to categorize. In the shallower basins, where moisture lingers longest before evaporating, there are growths — not plants, not exactly, but something organic that has found a foothold in the micro-texture of the ceramic surface. They are flat, almost film-like, spreading across the damp floor in pale patches that range from the size of a hand to the size of a large table. Their color is ambiguous — gray-green in the dry phases, deepening to a translucent emerald when the mist falls and moisture saturates them. They have no structure. No leaves, no stems, no roots. They are simply *there*, a biological scum that exists at the absolute minimum threshold of what could be called life, persisting in the narrow ecological niche where heat and moisture overlap on a surface that was never meant to support anything.

These growths do not move. They do not visibly grow or shrink on any timescale you can observe. But they are not static — over the course of hours, their edges advance, almost imperceptibly, across the damp floor, following the moisture, retreating from the dry zones. They are the only sign that this place supports anything organic at all. There are no animals. No insects. No birds. The air is clear of everything except humidity and heat.

The silence is profound. Not the silence of a sealed room — this is an open silence, a silence of scale, the kind of silence that exists when sound has nothing to bounce off and nothing to originate from. The pools do not lap. The mist makes no sound as it falls. The floor's deep vibration is below the threshold of hearing, felt only through the soles of your feet if you stand still long enough. If you speak, your voice falls into the air and vanishes — no echo, no reverberation, the sound absorbed by the vastness as though the space itself is hungry for it.

---

What makes this place unsettling is not any single quality but the accumulation of them. The heat. The silence. The featureless floor extending to every horizon. The knowledge — and it settles on you slowly, inevitably — that *everything here is made*. The floor. The air, or the system that produces the air. The light. The moisture cycle. The temperature. Not a single element of this landscape is natural in the sense of having arisen without intent. Someone, or something, designed this. Built it. Turned it on.

But the design is incomplete. Or perhaps it is post-complete — the thing it was designed to be has come and gone, and what remains is the infrastructure, still running, still cycling, still producing heat and moisture and light according to instructions that no longer have a purpose. The pale organic growths in the basins are not part of the design. They are opportunists, colonists that found the cracks in a system still warm enough to sustain them. They are the only sign that this place has been forgotten rather than merely abandoned. Forgotten things accumulate. Abandoned things are preserved.

You can walk for days in any direction and the landscape does not change in its essentials. The swells rise and fall. The pools appear and evaporate. The mist descends and lifts. The heat is constant. Occasionally, the floor's texture changes — the fine grain becoming coarser, or smoother, or shifting to a slightly different shade of dark — and these transitions feel meaningful, like crossing from one district to another in an endless city. But there are no walls, no markers, no structures of any kind. Just the floor, the air, the light, the water, and the heat, repeating at a scale that defeats comprehension.

This is a world in its embryonic stage — or in its afterlife. A made environment still running its processes long after the maker has departed, vast and hot and nearly silent, its only inhabitants a film of green something growing in the damp spots, its only weather a mist that falls and lifts and falls again, its only sound the vibration of a floor that has been warm for longer than anyone can remember.

It is not hostile. It is not welcoming. It simply *is* — enormous, purposeless, and perfectly maintained, waiting for an instruction that may never come.
