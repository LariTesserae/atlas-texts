The lowlands stretch in every direction, a vast and shallow bowl cut into the crust of the earth by forces that ceased their work eons ago. Here, at the bottom of the world, the horizon feels like a drawn-out compromise—a long, slow curve of dun-colored earth meeting a perpetually overcast, pale gray sky. The warmth is ubiquitous and thick, a fever-heat radiating upward from the ground and downward from the atmosphere, trapped in this low elevation like a breath held too long. It is a heat that carries weight, pressing against the skin, humid and close. 

And it is damp, but only just. Rain never falls. Instead, the air sweats. A constant, heavy condensation clings to every surface, weeping from the sky in the form of a fine, intermittent mist that drifts across the flats like breath. Beneath this mist, the ground is a mosaic of saturated clays and exposed, weathered stone. Shallow, meandering rivulets cut through the terrain, rarely more than an inch deep—just enough to catch the diffused, ordinary light of the sky and reflect it back as dull silver ribbons. Pools of water stand in the deeper scars of the land, warm and still, their surfaces undisturbed by wind or life.

Nothing grows here. Not a single blade of grass, not a scrap of moss, not a root or a tendril. It is a mineral world, a post-life landscape scoured clean of green. The earth is patched with deep ochres, bruised purples, and the bleached white of ancient, leached limestone. Yet, the ground is not empty. It is crawling with a fungal presence that defies the ordinary shapes of nature—vast, flat sheets of rust-colored lichen that cling to the stone like dried skin. They do not fruit, they do not bud. They simply exist, absorbing the warmth and the damp, an interface between the rock and the wet air. They crack underfoot like delicate parchment, but they never truly die; they are in a state of frozen equilibrium, growing so slowly that a century passes without a visible change in their reach.

If this were all, the place might feel like a tomb. But the lowlands are not dead. They are heavily, relentlessly industrialized—yet entirely devoid of builders.

Across the flats, the structures rise. They are not built; they are extruded. Massive, brutalist cylinders of black iron and pitted steel push up from the saturated earth at irregular intervals, spaced far apart across the regional scale of the basin. Some are the size of houses, others the size of grain silos, all stained with long-running streaks of orange oxidation. They are engines without operators, factories without workers. Thick bundles of corroded pipes snake between them, half-buried in the damp clay, connecting the cylinders into an automated network that spans miles. There is no glass in the few openings that dot the metal, no doors, no tools left behind. The structures simply grew from the earth, or were placed here by a logic that left no artifacts behind—only function.

And the sound. The sound is inescapable. It is the only thing in the lowlands that moves. The thrumming is constant, a deep, chest-rattling mechanical rhythm that vibrates through the wet clay and the standing water. It is the sound of unseen engines turning somewhere deep beneath the rusted cylinders, an endless mechanical heartbeat. Every few seconds, a sharp, pneumatic hiss cuts through the bass thrum—one of the distant valves exhaling a plume of white steam that immediately dissolves into the ever-present mist. The noise fills the emptiness, giving the sparse landscape an oppressive, localized gravity.

The light is flat and ordinary, the kind of diffused glare that casts no hard shadows and makes the distances hard to judge. It reveals a landscape in absolute equilibrium. Nothing decays any faster than it already has. Nothing drifts except the mist. Nothing grows but the slow, patient rust. The water stands at the same level it has stood for millennia, fed by condensation and drained by the same slow seep. The engines breathe in and out, their cycles unvarying, their purpose forgotten. 

Fauna is a rumor here. Occasionally, something moves in the warm, shallow water—a pale, eyeless form, perhaps a remnant of a simpler biology, drawn to the heat of the outflow pipes. It breaks the surface with a faint ripple, then sinks back into the murk, leaving only a ring of tiny waves that quickly still. The sound drowns out the splash. The mist swallows the ripple. The flat, wet expanse returns to its timeless, unchanging state. The machines pump. The water condenses. The world remains exactly as it is.
