# The Cistern Canton

There is a district beneath the old textile quarter that the drainage maps do not name. The maps mark a grey rectangle and label it *Sump 11—Decommissioned*, and this is a lie told to people who do not need to know better. The sump was never decommissioned. It was forgotten in a way that left it running.

---

You reach it through a maintenance stairwell whose cast-iron door has been ajar for longer than anyone currently alive has been working for the municipal water authority. The stairs descend three flights. The air changes on the second flight. It becomes heavier, cooler, and it tastes of iron and moss and something faintly chemical—a residue, like a laboratory that has been closed for a season but not cleaned.

The stairwell opens into a chamber that someone, at some point, treated as a room. A *place*. Not merely infrastructure. There is a difference between a space people pass through and a space people inhabit, and this is the latter, though the people who inhabited it are not here now and have not been for some time.

---

The chamber is roughly the size of a cathedral nave, though lower—perhaps five meters to the ceiling, which is vaulted brick laid in the old style, each course slightly irregular, each joint thick with mineral deposit. The brick has been weeping for decades. Water migrates through it in a process so slow it is almost geological, emerging not as drops but as a continuous film that coats the ceiling and upper walls in a glistening layer that catches what little light there is and multiplies it into faint, directionless halos. The water collects into beads at the lowest points of the vaulting and falls. Not regularly. Not as rain. As isolated, patient ticks. A drop strikes the floor every eight or twelve or twenty seconds. The intervals are irregular enough that you cannot anticipate them. You only notice them after they happen, which gives the place a quality of being always slightly surprised by itself.

The floor is flagstone, laid with craftsmen's care—tight joints, level surface—but it has been underwater enough times that a fine silt has settled into every seam and low spot, and moss has followed the silt. Not dense moss. A thin, dark varnish of it, more stain than growth, tracing the joints between stones like mortar's ghost. The floor is wet in patterns that make sense only if you have watched the ceiling weep for a long time: channels form where the water finds the same path down the walls, pools where the floor dips by half a centimeter, drains where the original drainage slots still function, carrying water away into channels cut into the stone at regular intervals. The drainage works. It has always worked. The water comes and goes in a cycle that takes hours or days—nobody is sure, because nobody has watched long enough—and the floor is never fully dry, but it is never fully flooded either. It exists in a state of patient negotiation with the water table, which sits close beneath the flagstones, close enough that if you stand still you can feel it through your boots as a kind of pressure, a denser coolness, as though the ground beneath you is thinking about becoming a lake and has not yet committed.

---

There are structures here. Not freestanding buildings—nothing so assertive—but partitions, platforms, workspaces. Someone built a settlement inside this cistern. They did it with the materials at hand and the sensibility of people who expected to stay.

The largest structure is a wooden platform raised thirty centimeters off the flagstone floor on short brick pillars, covering perhaps forty square meters. The wood is old and dark and wet, but not rotten—somebody chose their timber well, or the chemistry of the water has preserved it in a state more like bog-oak than lumber. The platform has a low railing, more suggestive than functional, and on it sit the remnants of a workspace: a long table, two stools, a rack of glass vessels some of which are intact and some of which have shattered in a way that suggests not violence but simple thermal fatigue—freeze, thaw, freeze, thaw, until the glass gave up. The vessels contain residues. Pale crystals. Dark sediment. A greenish film that might be copper salt or might be something alive. You cannot tell without touching, and the place communicates, in its quiet way, that touching would be a decision.

Against one wall, someone has installed a row of iron tanks—four of them, each the height of a man and roughly cylindrical, riveted construction, the kind of thing that was made in a foundry a century ago for storing industrial fluids. They are connected by pipes that run along the ceiling and down the walls, and the pipes are connected to a pump—a single, compact, steam-era pump mounted on a brick plinth—that still turns. The pump. It turns. Not continuously. It cycles on for perhaps ninety seconds every forty minutes, driven by water pressure that builds in a chamber somewhere behind the south wall until a mechanical valve trips and the pump engages and the pistons—two of them, iron, ground smooth by a century of operation—move in their cylinders with a sound like a large animal breathing slowly in its sleep. The pump moves water from the tanks through the pipes and back to the tanks in a closed loop that accomplishes nothing anyone can identify. It is not heating anything. It is not filtering anything. It is not powering anything. It is simply running, as it has run since before anyone currently alive was born, and the water it circulates has been the same water, or nearly the same water, for all of that time, cycling through iron pipes and iron tanks until it has taken on a character that is no longer quite water—a dark, heavy, mineral-saturated fluid that smells of deep wells and old stone.

The pump is the loudest thing in the place, and it is not loud. When it runs, it fills the chamber with a low mechanical vibration that you feel in your sternum more than hear with your ears. When it stops, the silence that returns is not the same silence that preceded it. It is a silence that has been shaped by the pump's absence, a silence with a vibration-shaped hole in it, and this is the silence that predominates.

---

There is electric light. Bare bulbs, four of them, hung from the vaulted ceiling on cloth-wrapped wire that has been threaded through ceramic insulators nailed into the brick. The bulbs are old—filament bulbs, warm even at their coolest, and dim, and slightly different in color from one another, so that the light in the chamber has a patchwork quality: one bulb casts a faint amber, another a cooler white, another a greenish tinge that might be the bulb or might be the moisture on it. They are not all on at the same time. They cycle. Not on any schedule that makes electrical sense—they seem to respond to something in the wiring, a fluctuation in current that nobody has traced, so that at any given moment two or three of the four are lit and the others are dark or in a state of uncertain glow, and the lit ones change over the course of an hour or two, so that the pattern of light in the chamber shifts slowly, a slow migration of illuminated and shadowed zones that gives the place the quality of breathing. The light never rises above what you would need to read by, and only just. It is the light of a place that was designed for people who knew where everything was and did not need to see it clearly.

---

The fauna is the surprise. The place is full of life.

Not obvious life—not herds or flocks or anything that announces itself. But when you stand still and let your eyes adjust, you begin to see movement everywhere, and what you see is so various and so particular to this place that you understand it has been here as long as the pump has, as long as the lights have, evolving alongside the infrastructure into something that fits the niches the infrastructure created.

The ceiling has spiders. Not web-building spiders—hunting spiders, flat, dark, with legs that span a large coin, and they move across the wet brick in a deliberate, patrols-like way, stopping at the spots where water beads collect and drinking. They are territorial. Each spider has a section of ceiling it patrols, and the boundaries between territories are marked by nothing visible but respected absolutely. When two spiders encounter each other at a boundary, they pause, touch forelegs briefly, and retreat. They have been doing this for longer than the bricks have been weeping, or so it seems.

The pools on the floor contain things that are not quite insects and not quite crustaceans. Pale, many-legged, the size of a fingernail, they move through the shallows with a motion that is somewhere between swimming and walking. They feed on the moss. They are fed on, in turn, by something else—something that lives in the drainage channels and surfaces only at the intervals when the water level drops, a flat, dark, languid thing that moves like a small ray and has no eyes that you can see. The eyeless things are the largest creatures in the chamber. Perhaps fifteen centimeters across. They are slow and seem to be in no distress. They surface, feed, and return to the channels with the unhurried certainty of animals that have never known a predator.

There are moths. Pale moths, small, the color of old paper, and they orbit the electric bulbs in erratic spirals, landing on the glass and staying there, wings trembling, for long minutes at a time. The moths do not seem to reproduce here. They seem to come from somewhere else—from the stairwell, from the upper world—and they come in small numbers, one or two a day, and they die here, and their small bodies accumulate on the flagstones beneath the bulbs in neat, circular deposits that nobody sweeps away.

And there is something in the walls. You hear it, very faintly, when the pump is off and the water has stopped falling and the silence is at its deepest. A sound within the brick. A scratching, or a clicking, or a tapping—too faint and too irregular to identify. It might be animals. It might be the water moving through channels in the masonry. It might be the bricks themselves, settling by microns, the mineral deposits growing and fracturing and growing again. The sound is frequent enough to be notable and rare enough to be startling. You hear it once, and you listen for it for ten minutes, and you do not hear it again, and you conclude that you imagined it, and then you hear it again.

---

The settlement—the human residue of this place—is sparse but organized. There are signs of long habitation arranged with the care of people who treated the chamber as home. A shelf of books, swollen with damp, their pages fused into solid blocks that will never be opened again but that have been kept, arranged spine-out, as objects of significance. A row of hooks on one wall, holding three coats that have moldered into shapes that still suggest the shoulders that wore them. A hearth—a small, brick-lined hearth, absurd in a place this wet, with a chimney that goes up three meters and then turns and enters the wall and goes nowhere. Someone tried to build a fire here. The hearth is blackened inside but the surrounding brick is only lightly smoke-stained, which suggests the fire was brief, or did not draw, or both. The hearth contains ash that has turned to a damp grey paste and in which nothing grows.

There is a bed. A cot, iron-framed, with a mattress that has been reduced to a layer of compressed fiber and rust. The cot is positioned beneath the one spot in the ceiling where the vaulting is highest and the weeping is least, and this positioning is not accidental. Whoever slept here knew the chamber's moisture patterns well enough to find the one dry place in it, or the least wet place, and that is where they slept.

There are not many personal objects. A cup, enamelware, on the table. A knife, corroded shut. A notebook—no, not a notebook. A ledger. A municipal ledger, with columns and dates, and the entries are numbers that correspond to nothing obvious. Water levels, perhaps. Pump cycles. Temperature readings. The entries stop on a date that is not recent, and they do not resume, and there is no explanation for their cessation, just as there is no explanation for the cessation of habitation. The people who lived here did not leave in a hurry. They left the way people leave a place they have been tending: slowly, with the sense that the tending would continue in their absence, because it always had.

And it has. The pump runs. The lights cycle. The water rises and falls. The spiders patrol. The eyeless things surface and submerge. The moths come and die. The weeping ceiling replenishes what the drainage channels carry away. The ledger's last entry faces the door, and if you stand where the keeper stood, you can see the whole chamber—the tanks, the platform, the cot, the hearth, the bulbs in their slow rotation—and the chamber looks back at you with the patience of something that has been looked at before and has not yet finished being looked at.

---

The place has a rule, which it does not enforce but which you sense. The rule is: *do not change the rate*. Everything here moves at the speed at which it has been moving. The pump cycles every forty minutes. The water rises over hours. The spiders patrol at their pace. The bulbs shift over the course of evenings. The moss grows in millimeters per year. The place does not resist change. It simply does not perform it. If you came here and stayed, you would slow to its tempo. You would begin to move with the deliberate, unhurried certainty of the eyeless things, and you would begin to understand that the chamber is not abandoned. It is *tended*. It tends itself. And the person who tended it last did not leave because the work was done. They left because they had become part of the work, and the work did not require them specifically. It required only that someone, or something, remain in the cycle.

The pump breathes. A drop falls. A bulb dims. A spider pauses at a boundary, touches the empty air where another spider was, and moves on.

The chamber continues.
