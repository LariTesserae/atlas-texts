# The High Gardens of the Lade

## what it is

There is a mountain — no. There was a mountain, once. What remains is a shell, a carapace of manufacture so total that no stone surfaces unshaped, no soil lies where it was not placed, no rain falls without first passing through a lattice of pipe and gutter. The mountain was hollowed and rebuilt as itself, taller by a third, its shoulders broadened with buttress-terraces that cascade down to valleys that are themselves lined — the riverbeds paved, the banks set with retaining walls so old they have acquired the look of geology.

They call it the Lade. The word means a watercourse, but the Lade is not a river. It is a machine for being a mountain, and it has been running for so long that no one remembers who built it, and the machine has forgotten too.

## the structure

The Lade rises something over four thousand meters at its crown — not a peak but a platform, a broad flat expanse of cast iron and poured stone, crowned with a forest of stacks and vents that breathe slow white plumes into air that is thin and bright and warm. Warm at this altitude, which is wrong, which is the first thing a visitor notices: the air should bite, should thin the blood, should carry ice even in summer. Instead it is bathwater-warm, swollen with humidity, thick enough to taste. The warmth comes from below. The Lade's foundations — its roots, sunk hundreds of meters into the gutted mountain — are a working industrial complex: engines, furnaces, boilers, all of ancient vintage, all still turning, still pumping heat upward through a vascular system of ducts and flues. No one tends them. They are too deep, too vast, too entangled with the structure they heat to be reached or serviced. They simply run, as they have for centuries, on whatever fuel was last fed to them — or on something else, something the Lade has learned to consume on its own, because the systems have been self-modifying for longer than anyone has been watching.

The heat rises through thermal stacks set into the walls of every terrace, warming the stone, warming the soil that covers the stone, warming the roots of the enormous gardens that have been planted — or that planted themselves — across every horizontal surface the Lade offers. And there are many. The mountain flares outward as it rises, stepping back in twenty-three great terraces, each one a shelf a hundred meters deep and circling the whole circumference, each one packed with growth so dense the underlying iron is invisible.

## the vegetation

Lush does not capture it. The Lade is drowning in green. The terraces support a riotous, layered canopy — not a forest in any natural sense, because nothing here grows from natural ground, but something that has evolved its own logic over centuries of untended proliferation. The lowest terraces, warmest and wettest, are hung with draperies of moss and fern so thick they resemble curtains, dripping, swaying, catching the bright light that pours down from above in a way that turns each frond translucent. Vines climb the thermal stacks, their roots threaded into the joints between iron plates, drawing warmth and moisture both. Higher up, the vegetation shifts — broadleaf trees with trunks that have grown around the railing-pipes, incorporating metal into their wood, so that a cut branch shows rings of iron among the rings of cellulose. Higher still, near the crown, the air thins enough that the plants change: hardy alpine species intermixed with cultivated flowers that have gone feral, poppies the size of plates, lupines in colors that don't exist in any wild stock.

And everything is wet. Not soaked — not rainforest-wet, not submerged. Damp. The Lade sweats. Condensation forms on every cool surface — the underside of leaves, the inside of pipes, the shaded stone of stairwells — and collects, and runs, and finds its way through the layered structure down to the next terrace, where it is taken up by roots or evaporated by the warm stone and sent up again. The cycle is slow, patient, nearly closed. Occasional rain falls, though the Lade's upper works interrupt even the weather: clouds form against the warm plume from the stacks and burst against the platform's edge, dumping water that sheets across minutes or hours drains through the gutter-lattice and is distributed. Pockets of standing water collect in low spots on each terrace — small pools, dark with tannin from fallen leaves, warm to the touch, smelling of iron and vegetal rot and something faintly mineral that might be the deep engines expressing themselves upward.

## the fauna

The Lade is not short of inhabitants, and never has been, not since the first gardens grew thick enough to shelter them. Birds are the most visible — the terraces are loud with them, a constant calling and fluttering and territorial dispute that layers into a wash of sound as persistent as the machinery's hum. The species are mixed: some are descendants of birds brought here intentionally, canaries and finches that have gone wild and grown larger and stranger over generations; others are migrants who found the warm mountain and stayed, nesting in the duct-work, raising young that never leave. There are hawks near the crown, riding the thermals the Lade itself generates, hunting the smaller birds in long slow spirals.

Insects fill every gap — the air is thick with them on the lower terraces, where the damp and warmth and rotting vegetation create conditions they adore. Beetles the length of a thumb, glossy and clattering, navigate the moss slopes. Moths with wingspans wider than a hand rest on the warm iron of the thermal stacks, their wings trembling. Spiders have built web-networks between the rail-pipes that catch condensation droplets and glitter in the bright light like installations.

And mammals: rodents in the walls, in the ducts, in the spaces between terraces where the structure is hollow. They have colonized the Lade's interior as thoroughly as the birds have colonized its surface. Cats — descendant of someone's pets, surely, generations ago — patrol the lower terraces with the proprietary calm of apex predators who have never known scarcity. On the middle terraces, something larger moves through the undergrowth: goats, perhaps, or what were once goats, now adapted to the narrow shelves and vertical life, their hooves sure on the iron railings, their mouths relentless against the climbing vines.

Nothing here is in a hurry. The Lade's timescale is long — the plants grow slowly in the thin soil, the animals live unhurried lives in the constant warmth, the machinery cycles on its deep rhythms. Days pass marked mainly by the shifting of light as the sun tracks across the sky, the bright unobstructed light of high altitude that washes everything in clarity and shadow.

## the sound

The Lade is never quiet, but the sound is not urgent. It layers. From far below, so deep it is felt more than heard, comes the rhythmic thud of the engines — slow, patient, a heartbeat measured in seconds rather than fractions. Above that, the constant hiss and trickle of water moving through the lattice: condensation running down gutters, drips landing in pools, the whisper of moisture being drawn up through heated stone. The thermal stacks exhale with a sound like breath held and released, long cycles that take minutes to complete. Bird calls pierce through it, and the mechanical chittering of insects, and the creak of old iron expanding in the warmth and contracting, microscopically, in the cool of the evening.

It is loud, in the way that a room full of conversation is loud — not painful, not overwhelming, but filling, a saturation of the auditory sense so complete that silence would feel like a pressure change, a wrongness. Visitors to the Lade sleep poorly at first, their bodies waiting for a quiet that never comes, and then adjust, and then find they cannot sleep without it, and this is one of the reasons people who come to the Lade tend to stay.

## the people

There are people here. Not many — the Lade is not a city, not even a town in the usual sense. But there are settlements, small clusters of habitation on several of the middle terraces, where the vegetation has been cleared back enough to expose the old iron decking and where makeshift structures have been erected: corrugated metal roofs, canvas walls, wooden frames scavenged from the Lade's own railings and catwalks. The residents are a mix: some are descendants of the Lade's original tenders, families who stayed when the maintenance was abandoned and the machines kept running without them; others are migrants, drawn to the Lade's warmth and abundance the way the birds are, settling into the niches the structure offers.

They garden, in a desultory way, harvesting from the growth that surrounds them — fruit from trees that have gone feral, greens from the moss-patches, fungi that fruit in the damp shadows of the thermal stacks. They tend small water-catchments, directing the condensation flow to cisterns they've carved from the hollow spaces in the Lade's walls. They keep animals — the goats, some poultry — and they eat insects, which are plentiful and protein-rich and which no one finds remarkable because it has been done here for as long as anyone can remember.

Their relationship with the Lade itself is domestic, almost familial. They do not worship it. They do not maintain it, exactly — the Lade does not require maintenance, or has passed beyond the point where maintenance is possible, so thoroughly has it self-modified. But they live within it as one lives within a house: knowing its moods, its sounds, its rhythms, the way the heat increases in the afternoon as the engines reach some peak in their cycle, the way the condensation patterns shift when the wind changes, the way the thermal stacks on the north face run cooler than those on the south and so support different ecologies.

They know, in the way that practical people know things without saying them, that the Lade is slowly changing. Not failing — there is no sign of failure, no breakdown, no decay that would suggest an ending. But growing. The root-systems of the larger trees have threaded so deeply into the structure that they are beginning to deform the iron, opening gaps that widen year by year. The condensation channels are silting up with organic debris, redirecting water into new paths, creating damp spots where there were none, drying out areas that were once wet. The engines in the deep foundations have shifted their rhythm — slightly, incrementally, over decades — and the heat distribution has altered, so that the upper terraces are warmer than they were a generation ago, and the lower ones cooler.

The residents watch this the way one watches weather: with attention, with adaptation, without urgency. The Lade is what it is, and will become what it will become, and they will live in it as long as it lets them, and when it no longer does, they will leave, or they will not, and either way the Lade will continue, breathing its slow breath, cycling its slow water, green and iron and warm and bright under the high thin sky.

## rules of the place

1. The Lade does not need you. This is the first rule and the last. Everything here — the warmth, the water, the growth, the animals — exists without human intervention and has for longer than human memory. You are not a custodian. You are a tenant, and your tenancy is at the pleasure of the structure.

2. Water moves down. Heat moves up. These are the gradients that govern everything. To understand the Lade, follow the water or follow the warmth, and you will find the logic of the place.

3. The iron is older than the soil on top of it, and the soil is older than the growth in it, and the growth is older than you. Respect the layering.

4. Do not go below the seventh terrace's foundations. This is not superstition — it is practical. The deep structure is unstable in ways the upper levels are not, and the engines' exhaust collects in the lower chambers, and things live down there that have been living in the dark and the warmth for a very long time and do not appreciate interruption. The residents will tell you this if you ask, and they will not elaborate, and you should not press them.

5. The Lade is bright. The altitude and the unobstructed sky and the pale iron that reflects light upward through the canopy create a luminous environment that takes getting used to. Bring your squint. Bring your shade. The light is beautiful, and it is relentless, and it hides nothing — every pipe, every vine, every rust-streak, every small face watching you from the undergrowth is rendered in perfect clarity, and you will see it all whether you want to or not.

6. Everything here is slow. The engines, the water, the growth, the people, the animals. If you move quickly, you will miss things, and you will disturb things, and the Lade will notice you, and it is better — much better — to be unnoticed by the Lade. Walk as though you are wading. Speak as though you are listening. Wait.

The Lade waits with you. It has been waiting a long time, and it is very good at it, and it will go on waiting, warm and green and iron and damp and bright, for a long time yet.
