This is a city that has outlived its builders and is slowly dissolving into steam.

It sits high—almost impossibly high—on a mesa of rust-colored ferric sandstone, planted where the crust fractures and the deep heat breaches the surface. The elevation is vast and exposed, yet the eye struggles to find the horizon. The atmosphere is perpetually condensed, choked with a warm, wet fog that refuses to lift. It is not a fog of the coast, but of the boil—water dragged up from the planet's mantle, superheated, then exhaled into the cooler high air. Visibility rarely exceeds a hundred meters. Beyond that, the world softens into a dim, amber-tinged nothingness.

There is no sun, only a diffuse, bruised glow somewhere above the cloud deck. The light level is perpetually stuck in the state of a heavy twilight, casting no shadows, only a flat, humid illumination that makes everything look like a specimen preserved in amber fluid. 

Down at foot-level, the city is real enough. The built environment is dense and layered—a sprawl of industrial brick, corroded iron, and vast concrete silos that climb haphazardly over one another. It has the bones of a Victorian manufacturing district scaled up to metropolis size: retorts, furnaces, and boiler houses linked by a cat's-cradle of iron walkways and rusted pipe bridges. Everything is constructed. There is no plant life. Not a single blade of grass forces its way through the cracks; no moss softens the angles of the stone. The only colors are the deep rouge of oxidized iron, the sulfurous yellow of mineral deposits, and the black, pitch-stained slick of old soot.

Yet this is a wet place. Water: 1.9. The rain falls almost constantly, but it is hot rain, falling in thick, heavy droplets that hiss when they strike the ironwork. The true water, however, comes from below. The city was built to harvest it. Everywhere, the ground weeps. Mineral-heavy water forces itself up through the pores of the sandstone, streaming down the facades of the buildings in rivulets that never dry. The air is so saturated that moisture condenses on every surface, beading into heavy, teardrop shapes that glide down the soot-stained walls, leaving clean, slick trails behind them. The stone underfoot is treacherous, polished smooth by millennia of this slow, chemical bleed.

The most striking feature is the network of steam vents. They are the city's breath and its engine. Great grates and cracked pipes expel plumes of white vapor into the dim air, roaring softly before settling into a constant, pressurized sigh. This is an industrial landscape, but it is an industry of maintenance, not production. The few remaining automated systems—driven by geothermal pressure and simple, brutal mechanics—exist only to clear the drains, to pump the condensing water out of the lower levels before they flood, to keep the vast subterranean boilers from cooling entirely. Engines turn, but they turn idly, grinding through cycles of compression and release with no product to show for it.

The scale is regional. The city extends outward through the fog in a loose, sprawling web, climbing over the uneven terrain of the plateau. But for all its constructed mass, the place feels profoundly empty. The density is near-void. The avenues are wide and utterly vacant. No crowds ever gathered here; the architecture was made to house machines and steam, not people. The population, whatever it was, departed long ago, or was never present in great numbers to begin with. Now, the only movement is the mechanical cycling of pistons in distant pump-houses, the steady flow of water, and the slow drift of fog.

Because of the acoustic dampening effect of the wet air, it is mostly quiet. The sounds that do survive arrive muffled and thick. The clank of an automated stamp-mill resonates dully through the mist, felt in the chest more than heard by the ear. The dripping of water echoes in the vast, hollow interiors of abandoned foundries, a rhythmic plinking that sounds like a clock counting down to an eternity no one will be around to see. 

Fauna is almost nonexistent, but not entirely absent. In the deepest, warmest pools of runoff—where the water gathers in the hollowed-out cabs of rusted lorries and the basins of collapsed smokestacks—something moves. It is not biology as it is commonly understood. Lacking carbon's familiar structures, it is a pre-cellular ecology, a chemo-synthetic sludge that thrives in the boiling, mineral-saturated water. Occasionally, a macroscopic mass of this slime—heavy, gelatinous, and striated with metallic veins—will detach from a vent wall and flop into a lower channel, propelled by internal gas expansion. It is rare, glimpsed only in the peripheral blur of the fog, a slow, oozing presence that feeds on the heat and the iron. It is the closest thing this world has to an ecosystem.

The weirdness of the place lies in its inversion of expectation. It is a hot swamp made of metal and stone. It is a busy infrastructure with no workforce. The erosion is established, in balance—the water and the heat have already done their violent work, rounding the sharp edges off the brickwork, fusing the iron into brittle, unmovable lumps. The city is no longer decaying; it has reached a state of mineral stillness, a slow-motion equilibrium where the rust is as solid as the steel it replaced. 

Stand in the center of the intersection where the main thoroughfare splits into three sloping avenues. The rain taps your shoulders, warm as bathwater. The fog presses in, smelling of ozone, sulfur, and the metallic tang of ancient blood. Somewhere below your feet, a pump the size of a house cycles on, shuddering through the bedrock, pushing a geyser of condensation out of a nearby grate. The steam shoots up, catches the dim light, and for a moment, a pillar of glowing white holds up the low sky. Then it dissipates, falling back as rain, and the avenue is empty again, stretching away into the wet, dim silence.
