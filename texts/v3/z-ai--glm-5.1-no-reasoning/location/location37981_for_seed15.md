A pocket of warm, bright, deafening submersion, entirely hand-built and newly flooded.

You are inside a room—though "room" fails to capture the strangeness of an enclosed space that has been claimed by the ocean. The scale is intimate, barely larger than a chapel nave, carved out or built into the flank of something massive and submerged. There is no natural substrate here; every surface you touch—the walls, the ceiling close enough to brush with your knuckles, the fixtures—is made. Someone fabricated this place. Mortared stone, fitted timbers, hand-planed wood, crude iron brackets driven by hammers. The technology is primitive, the workmanship rough but deliberate, the artifact of hands working with hand-tools. And yet it is entirely underwater.

The flood is recent. That is the key to everything. This place was not designed to be submerged; it was designed to breathe air, and only moments ago—in geological time, perhaps in living memory—the water found it. You can feel the youth of the disaster in the sharpness of the edges: wooden corners still crisp, not yet softened by months of soaking; iron fixtures only beginning to bloom with their first thin fuzz of rust; mortared joints still holding, still rigid, the lime not yet leached away by the warm sea. The water has not had time to make this place its own. It has merely forced entry.

And the water is warm. Not the cold of the deep ocean, not the chill of a winter flood—this is tropical warmth, bathwater warmth, the kind of heat that makes the liquid feel like a second skin. It presses against you from every direction but carries no shock. You could fall asleep in it, if the sound would let you.

The sound will not let you.

This pocket is loud—shockingly, oppressively loud. Water conducts sound with merciless efficiency, and the flood has turned this handmade room into a resonating chamber. Somewhere outside, perhaps far above, the sea is busy: currents drum against the outer walls with a low, rhythmic thudding; pressure shifts groan through the timbers; and there is a constant, rushing white noise—the voice of the water itself, moving, pushing, testing every joint and seal. The primitive construction creaks in response. Wood fibers under strain shriek softly. A stone shifts a millimeter in its bed and announces the movement with a deep, resonant *boom* that reverberates through the water and through your chest cavity. The place is alive with the sounds of a structure fighting—and slowly losing—a battle against the sea that has swallowed it.

And yet it is bright. Unbearably, impossibly bright. The water above must be shallow, or a gap in whatever buries this pocket must let the sun pour through. The light comes from everywhere and nowhere—refracted through the warm water, scattered into a golden haze that eliminates shadow. Every surface glows: the wood, the stone, the iron, the water itself. You can see clearly to every corner of this small space. There is no dimness, no hiding. The brightness has the quality of a shallow tropical sea at noon, but here it illuminates a room that was never meant to be an aquarium.

Sparse vegetation clings to what it can. A few strands of green—some opportunistic sea grass or algae, carried in on the flood—have already begun to anchor themselves in the gaps between stones, rootling into the mortar as if it were soil. They are sparse, scattered, barely there, but they are the only living things. No fish have found this pocket yet, or perhaps the entrances are too small, or the space too new. No crabs, no urchins, no worms. No animal life at all. Only you, the warm water, the bright light, the deafening sound, and the handmade room slowly realizing it has drowned.

It is a place in the middle of becoming something else. The processes are active: the wood is swelling, the iron is rusting, the mortar is weakening, the water is rising or falling with tides you can feel as subtle pressure changes against your eardrums. The sea grass is growing. The light shifts as clouds move far above, brightening and dimming in slow, warm pulses. The structure is settling, adjusting, complaining. Every few minutes, something shifts—a beam flexes, a stone seats itself more firmly, a bracket gives up a flake of rust that drifts downward like a brown snowflake in the golden water. The room is not still. It is working through its own destruction.

There is something almost normal about it, which is the strangest part. This is not an alien landscape, not a realm of impossible physics or warped space. It is simply a room—a well-made, primitively crafted, human-scaled room—that has been filled with warm seawater. The weirdness is zero; the tragedy is plain. Someone built this place with their hands. Someone fitted the stones and drove the iron and planed the wood. They stood in this space when it was dry and warm and filled with air, and they may still be alive somewhere, on the surface, wondering what the sea has done to their work.

And here you are, floating in the middle of it, listening to it come apart.
