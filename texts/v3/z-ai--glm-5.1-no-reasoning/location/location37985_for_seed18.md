There is no ground anywhere in the world, if by ground you mean something that was not put there. The Works cover everything — a machine laid down over whatever the planet was before, range upon range of iron and masonry and glass, so old that its plates have settled into themselves the way mountains do, its rust gone stable and dark, its joints worn to a kind of patience. Elevation here is not climbed so much as ascended through: days of switchback stairs cut into the flanks of the structure, landings the size of village squares, rivets big as rooms, until the air outside goes thin and cold and the sky comes down close and hard.

Near the top of the western range, under a crown of glass, is a room. The stenciling on the hatch reads EXPANSION 9, though the survey lists three thousand throats like it scattered across the Works, each on its own summit, each roaring at a pitch of its own. The stencil is the last quiet thing about it. When you dog the hatch open, the sound comes out at you like weather.

It is a roar with a floor to it. A fundamental you feel in the sternum and the back teeth, overtones ringing off the glazing, a shimmer in the upper frequencies that thickens and thins over hours. Yet nothing in the room moves. That is the first wrongness and the deepest one: a sound with the violence of a waterfall or a foundry floor, and beneath it a stillness so complete that dust hangs in the light. The roar is not effort. The roar is what patience sounds like at this scale.

The room is a lung. That is its whole function and its whole shape. Cathedral-height, cathedral-nave hollow, but bounded — you can stand at one rail and see all four walls, see the seams where the panels were lifted in, see the doors. It is one room, no bigger than that, and nearly all of it is empty air, because the emptiness is the working part. The column of air in the middle is the machinery. Floor is open grating over a throat that drops away into the dark of the structure; far below, if you press your face to the rail and let your eyes adjust, there is the faint sheen of a diaphragm the size of a lake, moss-crowned, descending. If you watch it, it does not move. Leave, come back, and it has moved.

The Works breathes. It draws thin summit air down through this chamber for most of a day, and expels it for most of a day after, and has done so for longer than the records run. The gauges on the north wall are brass-faced and calm: one needle makes a single full sweep per breath; another, larger, is the calendar, sweeping once a year. Nobody set them to agree with the sun, and they don't quite — the breath and the day drift against each other, sliding out of alignment and back, so that once a year the peak of the exhale lands exactly at noon, and the room fills at the same moment with hard white altitude-light and the bloom of everything that flowers, and then slides out of phase again for another year. The birds keep this festival. No one else attends.

Warmth comes up from below — the waste heat of whatever the Works does at its roots, rising through the throat like body heat — so that at this elevation, where the outside ledge would kill you in an hour of wind, the chamber is blood-warm, greenhouse-warm. And here is where the water is born. The outer skin of the room, glass and cold iron at extreme altitude, chills against all that rising warmth, and the air against it gives up its moisture. The chamber sweats. Every rivet wears a bead. The wired panes of the glazing run in threads that braid and gather, and the old drainage channels — cut for machine condensate, long since repurposed by everything alive — carry actual rivulets along the catwalk edges to a cistern under the floor. Twice a day, at the turns of the breath, when the pressure swings and the temperature layers flip, it rains indoors: a soft, businesslike rain, ten minutes of it, beading out of the ceiling iron. Then it stops, and the air dries again, and waits. Damp, then dry, then damp — the whole hydrology of the place is a schedule rather than a source, and every living thing in the room keeps that schedule without needing to be told.

Because the room is alive. That is the second surprise, after the stillness-under-roar: the hollow is hollow, the column of air is empty of everything, and every surface around it — rails, gussets, cable trays, the tops of dead valve bodies, the ledges of the glazing frames — carries a skin of green. Moss first, an unbroken pelt of it, dark as oxide and continuous as paint. Then ferns in ranks, and here the room's strangest legibility: the ferns do not grow where they like. They grow in lines. Bands of green, bare iron, green again, striping the walls and columns in ranks as regular as set type. The roar has standing waves, nodes and antinodes fixed by the chamber's geometry, and at the antinodes the leaves shake all day and the dew is shaken off them and nothing holds; at the nodes the air is still. So the moss is thickest along the quiet lines, the ferns stand in them like soldiers, and in the exact dead centers, where the stillness pools deepest, white orchids grow — cool to the touch, precise, found nowhere else. The old keepers called them the hush. The animals nest only in the quiet lines too. The room is a sound map you can read with your feet.

Higher up, where the antinodal walls thrum, the vines grow instead — long streaming things, leaf-shredded by decades of vibration into ribbons that all lean one way during the exhale, like flags. They have set their flowering to the breath: at peak exhale, together, in a wave that starts at the throat and runs up the walls, they open, and the outgoing wind takes their pollen and carries it out through the vents in the glazing, down-range, to other summits, other expansion chambers, other gardens. The Works pollinates itself. Its three thousand lofts are one organism, breeding across the sky by machine breath, and no gardener planned any of it.

The soil is manufactured too — a century and more of leaf-fall composting in the grating and the cable trays, made humus on made iron. The garden, in turn, has become machinery. The moss insulates the pipes so they neither sweat themselves dry nor crack with cold; where moss dies, the keepers' old notes say, gaskets fail within a season. Insect larvae comb the intake filters. The green skin damps the high frequencies — the room is measurably quieter than it was built to be. The garden has tuned it. Somewhere in the deep past the machine made the conditions for the garden, and now the garden is load-bearing, and neither can be called the other's host anymore.

And the fauna, in abundance, all of them fluent in this. Dew-midges hatch in the first minutes of indoor rain and rise in columns at the antinodes, where the roar shakes them into visible spirals and ladders — sound made legible in insects. The white birds (knockbirds, the keepers called them) cannot sing and have never needed to; they carry a pebble, most of them in the left foot, and strike it on resonant plates in signature rhythms — courtship as a drum duet you feel through the perch, begging chicks answered by taps rather than calls. Sail-mice glide the updrafts on stretched skin from wrist to ankle, riding the inhale from grating level up to the high gantries, drinking dew straight off the cable runs; a young sail-mouse may go its first year without once touching still floor. Pipe-stoats patrol the gutters, long and mist-oiled and glossy as poured water, hunting by vibration through their feet. Spiders build funnel webs in the lee of gusset plates, anchored like rigging, never an orb — an orb would tear in an hour. In the cistern live pale eyeless fish, descendants of feeder fish someone kept, spawning on the breath-turn, tasting the condensate. In one gutter-stream there is a toad. It has never sung. There has never been any point.

On the north catwalk stands a riveted booth, a keeper's bothy: bunk, kettle, a chalk wall of tallies in fives that runs to thousands and stops mid-row. There is a chart of the hand-alphabet — you speak in this room with your hands, or by writing on the sweating glass, where the words run as soon as they are answered. The chair has gone to moss. A pair of knockbirds nests in the kettle's spout. The last tally is old. No one has added to it in a long time, and nothing here has decayed because of that; erosion here is not decline but settlement, the Works having arrived, ages back, at a balance it now simply inhabits — a geology that happens to run.

If you stay through the turn: your ears pop, the shimmer rises, the glazing bows outward a half-inch on its frames as the room begins to let go of its day of air, and then comes the Horn — a deep flute-note under the roar, from somewhere in the throat, felt in the sinuses — and the vines flag and flower in their running wave, and the midges lift, and far below the lake-sized diaphragm begins its patient descent. The loudest room in the mountains, doing almost nothing, very slowly, forever.

And when you dog the hatch behind you and step onto the outer ledge, the silence is enormous — the summit is silent; all the sound was inside — and the cold thin wind says nothing, and away to the horizons of the Works the other summits stand spaced wide apart in all that made emptiness, each crowned with trembling glass, each roaring its own single endless note across the gaps to the others, inaudible, patient, green.
