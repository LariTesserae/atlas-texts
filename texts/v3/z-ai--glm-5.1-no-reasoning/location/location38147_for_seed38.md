Once it was mountain country, jagged as broken glass; then it was hill country; now, after more time than most worlds are given, it has settled into what it was always going to become: the Level. A table of land hundreds of leagues across, lifted high, and worn flat not by water alone but by its own green skin — because here, erosion is something the plants do.

Dig anywhere on the Level and you will not find stone. You will find the past. A hand's depth of living sward, and beneath it another, and another: black-gold peat threaded with roots that are still drinking, layered on rhizomes layered on compressed generations layered on the mulched memory of forests that stood when the mountains were still pretending to last. The ground is plant all the way down, warm all the way down, and it is warm from itself — the slow oxidizing burn of a billion seasons of ancestors composting under their own descendants. Kneel and you feel it through your knees, blood-warm, the heat of a body at rest. The sun here is a minor character. Warmth comes from beneath, and the whole green world stands on its own dead and eats them.

Which is why, on the Level, water does not fall so much as it rises. At dusk the ground exhales: mist stands up out of the sward, first as beads, then as a standing haze at knee height, then as a slow flood of vapor that pours all night across the flat and spills over the escarpment at the rim like smoke going over a railing. The leaves have evolved for this inverted rain. Their undersides are furred to catch the rising dew. The long strap-leaves lie in gutters and channels, tilting their load inward and downward toward the roots. Rosettes are built as funnels, low whorls guiding each drop to the center. And out in the sward the great pad-leaves hold their saucers of water until the cup runs over and the whole leaf tips with a soft drumbeat — one of the loudest sounds the Level makes, and it makes it rarely, and everything pauses for it.

There are eyes, too: places where the warm water inside the mat rises on its own and wells out into ring-shaped pools, tea-dark, blood-warm, fringed with the softest moss in the world. The streams they feed are so slow they look like flaws in a mirror; watch one for an hour and you will see it has moved a body-length, carrying its tint of tannin and old leaves toward the rim. At the rim, the few remaining stones of the vanished mountains surface through the sward like the last bones of a skeleton, and they weep — the warm breath of the mat condensing on their cool flanks in threads that never dry. The plateau's edge is a dripping wall of moss and hanging root, a waterfall that arrives mostly as mist. From the rim, the cloud the Level makes pours over the side and down, and below it there is only white. The plateau seems to sail above its own weather, a raft of living ground adrift above a hidden world.

The sky is a ceiling. The Level is high and warm and wet, and its own breath has made a permanent low deck of cloud, so the light arrives as a gray-gold diffusion with no source and no shadow. Nothing here casts a shadow; shapes are defined by sheen and wetness instead. Night is not dark so much as a deeper version of the same gray, and in it the small lights come out — threads of luminous fungus running under the sward like wiring in a dim room, cold green, and the mirror-petaled flowers that face those threads and gather their light, faintly, to themselves. The sun is a rumor, a paler patch that crosses the ceiling once a day. No one on the Level keeps time by it. They keep time by the ground.

Because the ground breathes. Once a day the vast sponge of the mat inhales at dawn — you can feel the fog sink back into the sward around your ankles, and the whole surface of the land, over three hundred leagues of it, rises and falls a centimeter like the chest of something sleeping. At dusk it exhales, and the exhale has a sound: not wind but a hush, the faint hiss of ten million stomata sighing at once. That is the weather here. That is the day, and the mist-hour at either end of it is when things happen.

And there is a great deal of thing, packed into the flatness. The Level is dense the way sediment is dense — nothing rises, everything overlaps. The canopy lies down: no trees reaching, but a horizontal architecture of ground-figs whose limbs run flat along the earth and root at every node, cane-groves that knock softly together when the fog pushes through, pad-ponds where leaves have grown until they touch and hold standing water in green saucers a stride across. Every surface is a habitat; the underside of a single strap-leaf is as crowded as a city street. Long ago, the plants of the Level settled their competition, and the treaty they signed said: spread, don't rise. Light is a seasoning here, not a meal — the sap runs warm on the decay beneath — so there is no race for the sky, only an endless patient negotiation of ground, every gap filled, every edge occupied. Flowers open only in the fog, white and brown and the color of shadow, and speak in scent the way other worlds speak in color: crushed cucumber, rain, warm leaf-mold, the mineral coldness of the weeping stones.

The animals live inside that treaty. The mowers — huge, soft, gray-green things like terrestrial manatees — drift across the sward in loose herds, grazing the moss like barges under tow, and their footfalls are not so much heard as felt, soft thuds rising through the mat into your own knees. Moth-things with eyes like wet glass hang in the standing haze. The pipers give the Level its one regular voice: a single soft wooden note at the mist-hour, once, and then silence, and everyone resumes. Nothing hunts at speed; the only violence is mostly the quiet snap of the pad-traps among the canes, and even that is half-symbiotic, the trapped things feeding the grove that feeds the herds. Speech here is scent and sheen and touch. Sound is almost never worth its cost.

The builders are small, quick-eyed, careful creatures — call them the gardeners — and they are the closest thing this world has to a people, and they have no tools. What they have is patience, which works better. A gardener bower is grown, not built: living canes woven where they bend, a season's wait for the grafts to knit, moss thatch trained over the arch until the hall is a single breathing structure, cool inside, dim, its walls alive. Across the whole vast flat these halls stand scattered, a few in a day's walk of each other and never more, some new, some abandoned — though "abandoned" is the wrong word, because on the Level nothing is ever ruined. The ground eats everything. A dead bower is reabsorbed in a few quiet years; bone and cane and dropped leaf all go back in through the sward. It is a world incapable of keeping a memento. The only old things are the things still alive.

So the gardeners tend instead of keeping. They prune the lanes the mowers have braided over generations — sunken green paths the sward has learned to grow low along, which drift by a stride a decade across the flat. They place a single wet stone beside each spring-eye, its reason known to them alone, so that at every warm well there is one bone-colored pebble sitting bright against the moss, like a word left at a door. And they tend the bone-groves: the hollow places where the old mowers go at the end to lie down warm. There the ribs become trellises, the vines climb them, and the gardeners weed the moss around them and take, in their season, the fruit that grows there. Whatever this is — gratitude, farming, burial — the Level does not distinguish between those words, and does not need to.

Underfoot, under all of it, the old rules of the place can be read plainly if you stand still long enough. Warmth comes from below, so nothing waits for summer. Water goes up, so nothing waits for rain. Everything that dies is eaten by what it begat, so nothing is hoarded and nothing is lost. And nothing hurries, because the race was run eons ago and won, in the end, by the slowest — by everything that learned to feed its neighbors rather than outpace them. Succession is over. What the Level has instead is maintenance, world without end, the last stable state of a green idea.

Stand still and you will hear what that sounds like: a drip into a leaf-cup, somewhere off to the left. The tick of a strap-leaf letting go of its load. The dry knock of canes. A fine, continuous, near-inaudible hiss of mist being swallowed home into the ground. Then a long nothing with texture to it, like felt. Then, far out in the flat, a piper's single soft note, and the hush closing over it again.

And then the mist-hour comes, and the Level takes one slow breath in through ten million stomata, the fog sinking around your ankles and the whole warm floor rising a centimeter under you like the chest of something that is not asleep but merely finished — and somewhere out in all that hush a pad-leaf fills and tips and drums, once, the way you hear a heart through a wall.
