The plateau sits high above the cloud line, an elevated vastness where the air is too thin to carry a voice and the heat sits like a physical weight. It is a world of stark, blinding brightness. The sun reflects off vast expanses of bleached salt-crystal and rusted iron, forcing the eye to seek refuge in the dark, vertical strokes of the city that rises from the plateau's center. This is a landscape that has found its shape and settled into it—weathered into balance over millennia, its crags and flats worn smooth by ages of wind that no longer blow with such fury. The forms here are established, resolved, permanent.

And yet, it is a drowned world. Not from above, but from below. The water table is so close to the surface that the stone weeps constantly. Every overhang drips; every hollow brims. The heat transforms this steady exudation into a perpetual, oppressive humidity that beads on every surface. The ground underfoot is never dry—a slick patina of mineral moisture that makes the traversing of the open spaces a slow, deliberate act. There is no rain, no open water, only the endless condensation of a hot atmosphere pressing against stone colder beneath its skin.

There is no plant life. Not a scrap of green, not a trace of chlorophyll. The world is entirely mineral, and yet it is not dead. The abundance here belongs to the thermophilic and the chemoautotrophic—to mats of rust-colored slime algae and towering, pale fungal blooms that cling to the wet, hot walls of the city. These are simple organisms, primitive in their structure but vast in their spread. They form a slippery, living carpet over everything, feeding on the minerals leeched from the stone, thriving in the steamy heat. They are the base of a strange, lightless food chain that has no use for the sun's blazing gift.

And above these mats, the fauna moves. Thousands of pale, sightless insects click and rustle in the damp—armored isopods and blind, scrambling beetles that feed on the fungal growths. They are hunted by larger things: long, multi-limbed predators with translucent carapaces that reveal the slow churning of their internal fluids. These creatures move with a terrible, delicate silence, their many legs distributing their weight across the wet stone so that they produce no sound at all. The ecosystem pulses with life, with the slow rhythm of predation and decay, but it does so in a hush so profound that you can hear the drip of water from a ledge a hundred meters away.

The city itself is a marvel of misplaced industry. It stretches across the middle distance, a settlement of tall, narrow towers and interlocking walkways, all constructed from the same pale, weeping stone. It is urban in its density and its intent—there are clear streets, clear districts, clear infrastructure—but it is abandoned by whatever hands built it. The factories still turn, driven by the geothermal heat that boils the water beneath the plateau. Engines thump and wheeze in the lower levels, their pistons moving in slow, inevitable cycles, powered by the very heat that makes the air so unbearable. But there are no operators. The machines have been left to their own devices, and they have adapted. They have grown strange. The long, empty years have allowed the mineral-rich water to deposit layers of travertine and calcite on the moving parts, so that the machines now operate within sheaths of living stone. They have become geological processes, part of the landscape's slow breathing.

Electricity still arcs and hums through cables that hang between the towers, but it follows no grid that a human engineer would recognize. The power pulses erratically, responding to the pressure of the water below, the heat of the stone, the slow growth of the crystal deposits on the contacts. At night—if night ever comes here—the city flickers with a pale, uncertain light, a luminescence that seems to seep from the stone itself rather than from any bulb or filament. It is bright, oppressively bright in the day, but it is the brightness of a hospital, of an operating theater—bright and empty.

The silence is the most oppressive feature of this place. For all its industry, for all its motion, it is nearly soundless. The water drips. The machines thump in the deep places, muffled by stone. The insects click faintly. But there is no wind to carry sound, no vegetation to rustle, no voices to echo off the walls. The city sits in its own silence, attended only by the pale things that scuttle through its streets and the steam that rises from its foundations.

And the strangest thing of all: the city is not still. It is growing. The mineral deposits from the weeping water are not just coating the machines—they are building new structures. New walls rise from the condensation, new arches and buttresses form over the course of months and years. The city is building itself, slowly, silently, without any guiding intelligence that can be recognized. The infrastructure is denser in the center, where the water flows fastest and the heat is greatest, and it thins out toward the edges, where the sparse, open plateau stretches away into the bright haze. The city is becoming a geological formation, a thing of layers and strata, a record of the water and the heat and the time that has passed through it.

This is the place: a high, hot, wet silence, where the machines build their own tombs and the blind things breed in the steam.
