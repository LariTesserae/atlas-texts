The plateau rose from the deeper earth only a few centuries ago, and it has not yet learned to be still. Its bones are sharp, its edges unrounded by time—vast shelves of basalt and raw feldspar that crack and shift with the heat, thrusting upward into air so thick with moisture that it feels like cloth. This is a young landscape, an adolescent geography, and everything here is in the violent, fertile process of becoming.

It is hot. A deep, humid, muscular heat that presses down on the high plateau and makes the air shimmer even under the normal, mid-range light of the sun. There are no clouds today, only the white haze of absolute evaporation. The elevation should bring coolness, but the heat rises from the newly exposed mantle below while the atmosphere condenses above, trapping the warmth in a suffocating thermal inversion. You breathe in water; you breathe in heat. The two are inseparable here.

And yet, despite the oppressive climate, the space feels remarkably open. The plateau stretches wide—a valley-sized expanse of stepped terraces and sudden ravines that extend past the immediate horizon. For all its turbulence, the land is sparse. There is room here. Room to see the shape of the place, to track the sharp ridgelines against the sky, to watch the steam rise from half a kilometer away. The emptiness is not desolation; it is restraint. The ecology knows how to occupy space without cluttering it.

Because this world is entirely built. Entirely grown. There is no natural substrate left—no bare rock, no dead soil, no mineral surface untouched by the architecture of living things. Every square centimeter of this high plateau is constructed from plant matter. But this is no forest; this is no green tangle or leafy profusion. This is *architecture*. The plants here have evolved to be walls, to be cantilevers, to be load-bearing columns and watertight membranes. They grow in geometric precision—hexagonal stems that weave into lattice screens, broad caps that seal against rain, root systems that knit into foundations stronger than concrete. The substrate is alive because the substrate *is* the structure. There is no ground beneath the ground; there is only deeper life.

And it is all plant. Total vegetation. The ecosystem is a pure-plant system—no fauna, no animals, no insects, no chordates of any kind. Nothing moves through these halls that is not rooted. The space is entirely autonomous, entirely botanical, and yet it feels more designed than any city. Walkways of dense, interwoven fiber span the ravines. Chambers of pressed cellulose open into courtyards where filtered light falls through translucent membrane-roofs. Stairways descend in smooth, spiraling ramps of living wood, their surfaces worn smooth by the passage of... no one. Nothing walks here. The architecture has no occupant. It builds itself for reasons that have nothing to do with habitation.

The water is everywhere. Not flowing, not pooled—*expressing*. The plants weep. Every surface exudes moisture; every column runs with slow tears; every wall is damp to the touch. The water table sits just below the living substrate, and the root-systems draw it up constantly, releasing it through their surfaces in a slow, continuous act of transpiration. The air condenses on the cooler undersides of the lattice-structures, dripping down in a rhythm that approximates rain. You can hear it: the steady, resonant *drip-drip-drip* of water falling from arches into catch-basins of cupped leaf. The sound is loud—amplified by the acoustic chambers of the architecture, bouncing off the taut membranes, echoing through the hollow stems until the entire plateau hums with the percussion of water. It is a concert of dripping, a deafening rhythm of slow flood.

But the rhythm is not steady. Nothing here is steady. The dynamic is *turbulent*—Solaris-like in its constant creation and destruction. The plant-architecture grows as you watch, new chambers extruding from the tips of columns, fresh walkways extending across gaps that didn't exist yesterday. And simultaneously, other structures collapse—rotting from within, undermined by the very water that feeds them, folding in on themselves with a wet, fibrous crunch that reverberates across the open space. The landscape is a bazaar of growth and decay, a storm-state of biological hyperactivity. Chambers appear and fill with condensation; walls thicken and then hollow out; bridges arch across ravines only to sag and dissolve before they can be crossed. The noise of construction—creaking, splitting, the deep groan of cellulose under tension—mixes with the drip and the occasional thunderous collapse. The plateau is never the same place twice.

And here is the strangeness: the architecture responds. Not to you—there is nothing aware here, nothing that thinks—but to *itself*. The growth patterns shift in response to the collapses. New structures grow toward the gaps left by old ones. Water is channeled differently after each shift, and the channels themselves are grown, not carved. The entire plateau behaves like a single organism, or like an anthill without ants—a system that builds and rebuilds with purpose but without intent. It is deeply, unsettlingly strange. You walk through a chamber that was not there an hour ago, and already its walls are darkening with the first signs of rot. By tomorrow, it will be gone, and something else will have grown in its place.

The light is normal—daylight, unremarkable, without supernatural glow or eldritch shadow—but it falls strangely here. It filters through the membrane-roofs in amber columns. It catches the dripping water and turns each droplet into a brief lens. It illuminates the geometry of the architecture: the impossible angles of self-building structures, the fractal repetition of branch and buttress, the way every surface curves slightly, because straight lines do not grow. In this ordinary light, the place looks almost like a ruin—except that ruins are dead, and this place is aggressively, violently alive.

There are no animals. No birds to carry seeds, no insects to pollinate, no herbivores to prune. The plants do not need them. They reproduce through fragmentation—broken pieces of architecture taking root where they fall, growing into new chambers, new walls. They feed on water and mineral solutions drawn from the young, crumbling rock below. They are a closed system, a perpetual-motion machine of growth and decay, and they have filled this high, hot, wet plateau with a city that builds itself and empties itself in the same breath.

The sound never stops. The drip, the groan, the crack of collapse, the hiss of new growth pushing through fiber. It echoes across the open space, loud and layered, a symphony of construction without a constructor. Stand still and you can feel it in your chest—the low vibration of roots tightening, the higher pitch of membranes stretching, the deep bass of a chamber finally giving way.

This is the place. A hot, high plateau of young rock and older water, where the plant-architecture grows and dies and grows again in an endless turbulent cycle, loud with its own becoming, strange in its purposeless purpose, empty of everything except the relentless, constructed life that fills it.
