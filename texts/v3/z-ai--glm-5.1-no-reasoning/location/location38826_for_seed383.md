# The Sunken Bazaars of Orham

Below the crust, where the earth's skin has been ground down by a billion years of patient water, there is a city. Or the memory of one. Or the thing a city becomes when it outlives every purpose that built it.

---

The elevation is deceptive. You descend through a wound in the plateau—a gap where the overlaying strata collapsed inward millennia ago—and find yourself not in a canyon but in a vast shallow bowl, a depression the size of a river valley scooped into the bedrock. The walls rise around you, but they are not cliff faces. They are slumps. Everything here has settled. The rim is rounded, softened, ground down to the gentle inclines of extreme age. This is a landscape at the end of its erosion. The wind and water have done all they will do. What remains is the final form: the low undulation, the almost-flat, the shape that persists when all the temporary edges are gone.

But it is not empty.

---

The constructions spread across the basin floor like a crust, like something grown rather than built. They are stone—local stone, the same grey-green schist and dull quartz that forms the bowl itself—fitted together without mortar, without binding, held by mass and interlock and the patient accumulation of weight. The walls are thick. The passages are narrow. The roofs, where they survive, are slab-laid, monolithic, each stone a single flat piece quarried from some vein of laminate rock that has since collapsed into its own excavation.

There is no metal. No worked wood—there is no wood. No cordage, no ceramic, no brick. The tech is stone and stone only: the hammerstone, the wedge, the lever, the patience of hands working material that resists. Doorways are tall and narrow, cut with chisels of harder stone, their lintels single spanning blocks. Thresholds are worn an inch deep by the passage of feet over centuries. Or longer.

The settlement has no center. It sprawls, but sparsely—clusters of chambers and passages separated by open stone floor, by bare rock swept clean by intermittent water. The gaps are as important as the structures. Between one block and the next, twenty paces of nothing. Thirty. The city breathes through these absences. It is built to a rhythm of presence and void, and the void is not failure. It is architecture.

---

There is no plant life. No root, no leaf, no stem, no green thing of any kind. The stone is bare. The air is clear of pollen, free of the faint haze that vegetation casts. This is a mineral world, a place of rock and water and the thin pale things that occupy the space where plants might have been.

The dampness is pervasive but not pooling. You feel it on your skin—a cool, constant humidity, the kind that accumulates without ever quite precipitating. The stone is never dry to the touch. There is no rain, not here below the rim, but water seeps. It seeps from the basin walls in the cool hours, running in thread-thin streams down the schist faces, pooling in the worn thresholds, collecting in the shallow channels carved into the floors of the larger chambers. These channels connect—someone cut them, long ago, linking the seeps and drips into a slow circulatory system that moves water through the settlement without ever holding much of it. A sip here, a film there. The city is damp the way a cave is damp: constantly, mildly, without urgency.

Condensation beads on the undersides of the roof slabs in the coldest hours before dawn and falls in irregular rhythm—a drip from this ceiling, a drip from that, never synchronized, a sparse percussion that is the closest thing to weather the basin possesses.

---

The light is the first true strangeness.

The basin faces open sky—this is not a cavern, not a closed space—and the sun enters at a low angle, sliding across the floor for much of the day as it traces its path above the rim. But the light that fills the bowl is more than the sun's. The stone itself holds luminosity.

Not glow. Not radiance. Something subtler. The schist—particularly the fine-grained bands of it, the layers that run through the construction stones like veins—retains light. ---

The fauna is the second strangeness, and the one that redefines everything.

They are not large. They are not dramatic. But they are everywhere, and they are busy, and their activity is the engine that turns the settlement from ruin into something else.

The most visible are the runners. Four-legged, low-slung, the size of a man's forearm, covered in a pale chitinous hide that matches the stone. They move in lines—always in lines, single-file, nose to tail, following chemical traces laid down by the first in the queue. They pour through the passages like fluid, like a thin river of pale bodies, and they carry things. Stone chips. Fragments of worked schist too small for any hand to use. Debris from collapsed walls. They carry these to the edges of the settlement, to designated waste zones that have accumulated over generations into low mounds of sorted material—shale here, quartz there, the dust-fine tailings in a third place. They are cleaning. They are maintaining. The settlement is not abandoned. It is tended.

The runners have no evident nest, no central colony. They live in the gaps between the construction blocks, in the spaces too narrow for anything larger, in the walls themselves. Every stone wall is also a city for them. You hear them sometimes—a faint scritching, a dry whisper of limb on stone—but they are nearly silent, and the basin's acoustics swallow what little sound they make.

Below the runners, smaller still, are the grazers. They are slow things, flat things, adhered to the damp stone surfaces like pale irregular blisters. They feed on something in the condensation—mineral, perhaps, or the thin biological film that the constant moisture encourages on the stone's surface. They leave trails, faintly etched pathways that cross and recross the walls in patterns too regular to be random. They are cultivating. Or being cultivated. The runners avoid the grazers' trails, routing around them with a precision that suggests either communication or rigid instinct.

And in the water channels—the thin slow seep-carvings that network the floors—there are swimmers. Translucent, finger-length, eyeless, they keep to the moving water and die if a channel dries. They eat whatever accumulates in the flow, and their waste is the film the grazers consume. The runners maintain the channels, clearing blockages, widening constrictions, ensuring the water reaches the chambers where the swimmers congregate in their thinnest densities.

It is a system. It is an economy. It is a metabolism that has occupied the stonework of the dead builders and made it into a body.

---

The quiet is profound. This is the most disorienting thing.

The basin should echo. The stone passages should ring. A place of hard surfaces and open volumes should reverberate with every footstep, every click of runner-claw, every drip of condensation. Instead, sound dies. It is absorbed, swallowed, attenuated to nothing at short range. You can stand at the mouth of a passage and hear nothing of what occurs thirty paces within. The runners' scritching is a whisper even at arm's length. Your own footsteps become soft, intimate, as if the stone is padding them, refusing to let you announce yourself.

The acoustics suggest something about the stone's structure—perhaps the same fine layering that allows it to hold light also permits it to dampen vibration. Or perhaps the passages are shaped to cancel resonance, their proportions tuned to destroy standing waves. The builders knew their material. They may have known its acoustic properties as well.

In the deep quiet, you become aware of a sound that is not quite a sound: a low periodic pulse, felt more than heard, that rises and falls on a cycle of perhaps thirty seconds. It comes from the floor, from the basin's bedrock, from somewhere below. It may be water moving through deep channels, or the thermal breathing of stone warming and cooling, or the activity of some subterranean organism on a scale larger than the runners but slower, patient, geological in its rhythm. The basin pulses. It has a heartbeat, or something close enough to feel like one.

---

The turbulence is the third strangeness, and the most difficult to convey because it is not obvious, not at first.

The settlement appears stable. The walls stand. The roofs hold. The water flows. The runners run. But watch for a day—a full cycle of light and dim and light again—and you will see: the city is rebuilding itself.

A wall that stood in the morning is down by evening, its blocks scattered, the runners flowing toward them in long lines. By the next dim-period, the blocks have been carried elsewhere and fitted into a new construction—another wall, another chamber, in a configuration that did not exist before. The city is in constant slow flux, its structures assembling and disassembling on a timescale just slow enough to seem static if you glance, just fast enough to be visible if you attend.

The runners are the agents of this change, but they are not its architects. They follow chemical traces, and the traces are laid by ... something. The grazers, perhaps, in their regular trails. Or some pattern encoded in the stone's luminosity, some information stored in the light-holding schist that the runners read as instruction. Or the water, moving through its channels, dissolving and depositing mineral traces in configurations that encode structural information. The system is writing itself. The city is reading its own blueprint and editing it in real time, testing configurations, keeping some, discarding others, the stone walls rising and falling like slow flames.

There is no waste. The same blocks cycle through structure after structure, century after century. The settlement's total mass is fixed—nothing is added, nothing removed except by the runners' careful sorting. The city is a finite set of pieces in infinite permutation, a game played without a player, an emergent computation in stone and water and animal labor.

And it is accelerating. The rate of change, if you measure it, has been increasing. A wall that stood for generations falls in a season. A new chamber rises in a week. The runners are more numerous than they were—they must be, to sustain the pace. The channels carry more water. The grazers' trails have grown denser, more complex. The pulse in the bedrock is louder, or at least more distinct, more insistent.

Something is being built toward. The city is converging on a configuration it has been seeking for a very long time, and it is getting close.

---

The cool air moves slowly through the passages, carrying the mineral smell of wet schist, the faint organic must of the grazers' film, the clean absence of anything botanical. There is no rot here, no decay of leaf or fiber. There is only the patient exchange of stone and water and animal, the slow metabolism of a constructed landscape that has found a way to continue without its builders.

Stand in the basin at the brightest hour, when the sun is low and the light is horizontal and every surface is either incandescent or in shadow, and you will see it: the city is alive. Not metaphorically. Not in the way a forest is alive, which is really only the sum of its living parts. Alive in the way a cell is alive, a body, an organism. The runners are its blood. The water is its lymph. The grazers are its epithelium. The stone is its bone, and the light is the signal that coordinates its growth.

It does not know you are here. It does not know it is a city. It only builds.
