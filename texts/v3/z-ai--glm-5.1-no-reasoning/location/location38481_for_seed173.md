# The Cistern

You descend through a hatch that hasn't been opened in someone's lifetime, and the sound finds you before the water does.

A low, mechanical throb — not quite rhythm, not quite hum — rising from below like the resonance in your chest before a cough that never comes. The ladder is iron, flaked with oxidation the color of dried blood, and it goes down much further than the hatch's exterior suggested. Twenty rungs. Thirty. Your hands find each one by the thin tremor running through the metal. The sound gets louder. You understand, eventually, that it is not one sound but many — a congregation of engines, each running at slightly different speeds, creating a interference pattern that presses against your eardrums like a physical thing.

Your boot touches water.

---

It is not ocean. Not lake or flood or any body of water you've named before. It is *process*. It sits at the temperature of a living body's interior — just below comfortable, cool enough to notice, warm enough that the skin stops reading it as wet after a few minutes and reads it as simply *there*. The color of it, what you can see in the low amber glow of industrial fixtures mounted high on the walls, is a pale iron-rust brown. Not muddy. Not silty. *Tanned*. As though the water itself has been treated, processed, brought to some specification that required this exact hue.

You are standing in a manufactured space. There is no natural substrate here — no rock floor, no sediment layer, no geology at all. What you had thought was stone beneath the water's surface is poured concrete, aged to the appearance of sedimentary rock but too regular, too planned. The walls curve overhead in a barrel vault, ribbed every eight feet with iron struts that serve no visible structural purpose but are absolutely covered in pipe runs, cable trays, junction boxes, and valves. Everything is numbered. Little brass tags, green with verdigris but still legible: CV-117, CV-118, CV-119. Someone catalogued this place with the obsessive care of a person who expected others to follow.

The ceiling is lost behind a layer of condensed moisture that drifts and reforms in slow response to air currents you cannot feel. Water beads on every surface. It runs down the walls in sheet-thin rivulets, collects in channels cut into the concrete floor, feeds into grated drains that gurgle with constant ingestion. The sound of this alone — the countless tiny streams, the subterranean drains, the dripping — would be substantial. But it is only a whisper against the engine-throb.

The space is not large. You could walk its length in four minutes if the water weren't thigh-deep. It has the proportions of a nave — high-ceilinged, narrow, processional — and this comparison is not incidental. The layout suggests worship. Not of god, but of function. Every element serves the movement and management of water. This is a cistern, a pressure regulator, a settling tank. It is a single organ in a body of infrastructure so vast that this room, this flooded chamber with its numbered valves and its constant mechanical song, is merely a cell.

---

The creatures arrived before you noticed them.

Not because they were stealthy. Because your eyes, adjusted to the half-light, had categorized them as part of the architecture. They cling to the pipe runs — pale, elongated forms the size of a man's hand, semi-translucent, their internal organs visible as dark masses within milky flesh. They are shaped roughly like lampreys, if lampreys had been designed by a committee that had heard them described but never seen one. Their mouths are circular, equipped with rows of fine filament rather than teeth, and they press these against the iron pipes and *drink*.

Not the water inside. The condensation on the outside. The iron. The verdigris. They are minerovores, adapted to extract minerals from the infrastructure itself. Where they have fed, the pipes are scoured clean, the iron bright and new-looking beneath. In patches. In trails.

There are hundreds of them. Maybe thousands. Most are clinging to the upper structures, safely above the waterline, but you see them on the walls as well, spaced at intervals that seem almost deliberate. A few drift in the water itself — free-swimming juveniles, you guess, not yet heavy enough to cling against the current. They move with lazy confidence, unbothered by your presence. You are not their concern.

A larger shape moves in the deeper water near the center of the chamber. You catch it only as displacement — the way the surface tension changes, the way the pale light bends. Something bottom-dwelling. Something that, from the brief shadow it cast, is perhaps the size of a dog and shaped like a flat, serrated leaf. It has no eyes that you can see. It navigates by vibration, perhaps, or by the chemical signatures of the minerovores above. You understand suddenly that the ecosystem here is not accidental. It was *introduced*. Someone seeded this cistern with organisms that would maintain it, clean it, keep the pipes from furring with mineral buildup. The creatures are not pests. They are staff.

---

The engines — you find them at the far end of the chamber, where the vault ceiling drops to meet a bulkhead pierced by a single massive pipe. The pipe is six feet in diameter, and it thrums. The thrumming is the source of the loudest component of the sound. Inside that pipe, water is moving — being pumped, you realize, by the engines somewhere beyond the bulkhead. You cannot see them, but you can feel them through the floor, through the water, through your own skeleton. They are old. They were installed when this place was built, and they have not been turned off since. The regularity of their operation has achieved a kind of geological constancy. They will outlast the people who built them. They may outlast the purpose they were built for.

A valve on the bulkhead is labeled in the same brass-tag system: PRIMARY DISCHARGE. Below it, smaller: DO NOT CLOSE. Below that, smaller still, hand-painted in a tremulous script that speaks of very old warning: *The water must move.*

---

You stand in the cistern, and you let the sound become your environment. It is too loud for thought, at first, but the mind adapts. After ten minutes, the engine-throb becomes a kind of silence — a baseline against which smaller sounds become legible. The clicking of the minerovores as they shift position on the pipes. The gurgle of the drains. The slow, wet breathing of the deep-water creature as it surfaces briefly, opens a blowhole you hadn't noticed, and submerges again. The faint electrical crackle of the light fixtures, each one fitted with a heavy glass housing and a coil of wire that glows the color of a candle flame seen through amber.

The water is moving. You can feel it now — a slow, deliberate current running from the inlet grates near the hatch to the discharge pipe at the far end. It is moving at the pace of blood through a vein. This is the cistern's function: to hold the water just long enough to regulate its pressure before sending it on. It will never be empty. It will never be full. It exists in a permanent state of equilibrium, a single moment in a process that began before the oldest part of you was born and will continue after the newest part of you has gone.

The minerovores click. The water moves. The engines thrum.

It is cool and dim and terribly, beautifully alive in the way that only a place without people can be alive — teeming with organisms that have inherited a world they did not build, cannot understand, and do not need to understand. The architecture persists. The function endures. The water must move, and so it moves, and the small creatures drink the iron from the pipes, and the deep thing breathes at the surface, and somewhere beyond the bulkhead, engines that were old when the language on the warning signs was current continue their endless revolutions.

You could leave. You could climb the ladder, close the hatch, walk back into a world that knows what sky means. But you are aware, with a certainty that has no rational basis, that if you did, you would spend the rest of your life trying to find this place again. The sound has gotten inside you. The cool, processed water has numbed your legs below the knee, and you understand that this is not discomfort but adaptation. 

The cistern does not need you. But you have been here now, and the place remembers bodies the way it remembers pressure — as a variable that once passed through, changing nothing, but recorded nonetheless in the slow mineral accumulation on the walls, the slight additional load on the drains, the microscopic disturbance in the current that will reach the discharge pipe in eleven minutes and be gone.

The water moves. It must.
