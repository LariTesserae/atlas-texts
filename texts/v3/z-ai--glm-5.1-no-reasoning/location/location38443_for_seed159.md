# The Nursery Below

The heat comes first. Not the dry bake of desert or the close swelter of jungle, but something wetter, more pressurized — like opening a door into a greenhouse that has been sealed for centuries. The air has weight. It presses against your skin with a damp insistence, and within minutes, the condensation begins: first on the cool surfaces of your gear, then on your forearms, the back of your neck, gathering and running in thin rivulets you didn't consent to. You are below the surface, and the surface is sweating on you.

---

The space opens around you in a way that underground spaces shouldn't. This is no cave — or if it is a cave, it has forgotten the compactness that caves usually enforce. The ceiling lifts away into a darkness that normal flashlight beams cannot find the top of, and the walls pull back until they are suggestions rather than boundaries. The chamber — if chamber is even the word for something this voluminous — extends in every direction past the reliable reach of your vision. It is a regional darkness, a subterranean province. You could walk for hours and not find the far wall. You could walk for days.

What light exists arrives from sources you cannot immediately identify. Not from above — the ceiling is pure absence. Not from any single fixture or opening. Instead, a diffuse amber luminescence seems to well up from the floor itself, or perhaps from the air, as though the heat and the moisture are cooperating to generate a dull, indoor twilight. It is enough to see by, barely. Enough to make out shapes. Enough to wish, sometimes, that it weren't.

---

The terrain is raw in a way that feels almost embarrassing to witness — like walking in on geology while it's still dressing. The stone has not yet decided what it wants to be. Great masses of it bulge and sag in forms that suggest intention without achieving it: ridges that might become columns if given another million years, hollows that might mature into proper chambers, shelves and overhangs that protrude at angles that seem provisional, experimental. The rock is warm to the touch, and under your palm, you can feel a faint vibration — a deep, slow pulse, as though the earth here is breathing, or digesting, or thinking very hard about something.

Nothing here has been worn smooth. There is no erosion to speak of, no patient wind or water to round off the sharp edges. The stone breaks in angular, almost crystalline fractures. Surfaces catch the amber light and throw it back in facets. Walking is treacherous — every footing is either too sharp or too unstable, the rubble of a world still under construction, still figuring out its own topography.

And through all of this, the water. Not flowing in any coordinated way — there is no river here, no stream with a destination. Instead, water seeps and collects and drip-drip-drips from places unseen. Pools form in the low points between the raw, half-formed ridges, some no larger than a teacup, some spreading wide and shallow across the floor like mercury. The water is warm, almost body-temperature, and it smells of mineral and something else — something organic, though not quite vegetable, not quite animal. Something that makes you think of nurseries. Of incubation.

---

There are no plants. Not a single stem, not a leaf, not a thread of moss. The word *flora* has no purchase here. And yet the space is far from empty — it is, in fact, almost unbearably full, packed tight with presences that occupy a category your vocabulary hasn't prepared you for.

They are everywhere.

The floor seethes. The walls crawl. The ceiling — that distant, invisible ceiling — rustles and clicks and shifts with weight and movement. What you took for shadow is, in many cases, life. What you took for stone texture is, on second glance, a slow carpet of bodies.

They are not insects, though some move with an insect's mechanical precision. They are not crustaceans, though several varieties bear armored plates that click and overlap. They are not worms, though the largest things you can see — the things that move like sluggish rivers across the wider pools — have the segmented, cylindrical bodies of something that might, in a less careful mood, be called wormlike. Nothing here has a face you can recognize. Nothing here has eyes, at least not eyes that work in any way you understand. They navigate by touch, by vibration, by chemical gradients, by some sense you don't have a name for.
