# The Combers

The district sits low in the city's throat, where the grid gives up and the streets narrow to slots between processing houses. It has always been here. The brickwork knows this—each mortar joint settled into its final position so long ago that the crystallized salts holding the walls together might as well be geology.

Rain comes and goes. A thin persistence of damp lives in the air, not enough to call wet, just enough that every surface carries a film the light catches on. Water beads along the iron gutters, pools in the slight depressions of the cobbled lanes, collects in the scalp-tight spaces between the cobbles themselves. You can taste it when you breathe—mineral and old and faintly chemical, the way all urban damp tastes after a century of the same smoke settling into the same stone.

No green thing grows here. Not moss, not lichen, not the hardy grasses that crack other sidewalks. The air doesn't permit it—something in the persistent fume, the thin chemical wash that rises from the processing houses, keeps the vegetable world at bay. Instead, the damp surfaces are claimed by something simpler. A film, faintly iridescent, that spreads across the brick where water lingers. Not alive in any way that would satisfy a botanist. A surface phenomenon. A reaction between the wet and the old and whatever the factories have been exhaling since before anyone thought to record what they made.

The processing houses dominate the district. Three of them, windowless, their walls seam-welded boilerplate gone the orange-brown of old rust that has decided to become permanent. They run. They have always run. The engines inside turn at a pace so steady that the vibration has become part of the architecture—part of the ground itself, a low rhythmic tremor that you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears. The smokestacks breathe out a pale vapor that doesn't rise so much as mingle, joining the general dampness, becoming the sky's texture. Normal light reaches the streets, but it arrives through a medium, slightly diffused, giving every shadow the soft edge of a world slightly underwater.

The sound is enormous. Not a crash or a roar—a tide. The engines produce a cycling throb, bass-deep, and the venting pipes add a high metallic wheeze on some interval that the mind almost learns to predict but never quite does. The drainage gutters contribute their own voice: water moving in steady rhythmic pulses, synchronized to something in the factory's process. The overall sound has weight. It leans on you. After an hour, you stop hearing it in any conscious way, but your body never quite adjusts. Your jaw stays tight. Your footsteps fall heavier than they need to, as if you've learned to walk against a current you can't see.

And everywhere—the life.

Not human life. The district has no residents in any meaningful sense. The workers who attend the processing houses come from elsewhere, arrive on foot through the slot-streets, perform their maintenance, depart. What lives here, what *overwhelmingly* lives here, is something else entirely.

They come in from the low country surrounding the district—the marsh-laced ground where the city's foundations give way to standing water and chemical seep. They come because the processing houses generate warmth in their long low bodies, because the damp here suits them, because something in the runoff feeds them. They are, if you must name them, arthropodal, though that word stretches. The largest are the length of your forearm, though most are smaller, and their carapaces catch the diffused light with an oil-slick iridescence that makes them seem less like organisms and more like fragments of the factory itself that learned to walk.

They swarm. That is the only adequate word. In the gutters, a moving carpet of them—shimmering, flowing around obstructions, pooling in the low places where water pools. On the walls, they move in processional lines, following chemical traces laid down so long ago that the routes are etched into the brick as faint discolorations. They do not fight. They do not compete. They move with the same mechanical steadiness as the engines, as the draining water, as the pale breath of the smokestacks. They are the district's true inhabitants, and they have been here, in their way, as long as the factories have.

At dusk, the sound doesn't change—has never changed—but the light does, and with it, the character of the life. The iridescence on the carapaces becomes more pronounced. The gutters run with moving color. The processing houses, lit now only by the amber of their own indicator lamps, become dark monoliths above a glittering, crawling floor. The factories do not slow. The engines do not shift their rhythm. The pipes vent on their ancient schedule. The water drains. The creatures move.

This is the equilibrium. This is the state that has held, unshifting, for longer than any record confirms. The district doesn't decay—decay implies a direction, a progression. It simply persists, each element locked in relation to the others: the damp, the engines, the chemical film, the multitudes in the gutters. Nothing is building toward anything. Nothing is winding down. The Combers have arrived at a configuration that requires no adjustment, and so no adjustment occurs.

Standing in the slot-streets at the district's center, you feel it—not peace, not stasis exactly, but the absence of any question. The sound pounds through you. The creatures flow around your boots like dark water. The damp settles on your skin. And nothing, nothing at all, is waiting to happen.
