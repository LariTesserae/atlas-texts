# The Weeping Works

The land has no natural skin. Everything you walk on is made—bolted, welded, poured, riveted—but so old that the notion of "natural" has become a meaningless distinction. This is the final geography. The earth beneath the metal is a rumor, a memory held in the lowest registers of the world. Nothing erodes anymore. The corrosion stopped eons ago, and what remains is the settled-into, the cannot-be-worn-further, the absolute terminal state of matter. Shapes have surrendered their original angles. Everything is rounded, smoothed, flattened to the slowest possible contours. It is not decay. It is the end of decay—the quietus of entropy, where even transformation tires and sits down forever.

---

The scale is difficult. The Works extend past sight in every direction, but you cannot see far because the light will not permit it. A darkness hangs here like a fabric, woven through the superstructure, denser than weather. You look out and the world recedes within thirty meters—shapes becoming assumptions, then rumors, then nothing. The darkness is not an absence. It has texture. It clings to surfaces the way moss would cling to surfaces in a world that still had moss.

What you can see: a plain of metal, rolling in the lowest possible undulations, stretching toward a horizon you must take on faith. The elevation barely exists. The land lies close to the water table—close to the *idea* of a water table, since there is no soil, no aquifer, no geology in any honest sense. But the water is there. It is always there. It presses up from below and condenses from above and the world is caught between these two persistences, this pincer of wetness, and everything is permanently, incurably damp.

The metal underfoot sweats. Every surface—every wall, every pipe, every strut, every plane of the endless manufactured ground—exudes a thin film of moisture. You can feel it on your palms when you steady yourself. You can see it glistening, where the light permits, in beads that form and slide and re-form in the same places they have formed and slid and re-formed for periods longer than the word *period* can meaningfully describe. The water does not pool. It weeps. The entire Works weeps, from every joint, every seam, every worn-fast rivet-head. The water traces paths that were established before the word *path* existed—grooves worn into the metal by the patient friction of a trillion drops following a trillion drops following a trillion drops, each century's water finding the channels its predecessors carved.

There is no rain. There doesn't need to be. The atmosphere is a sponge that has been saturated so thoroughly and for so long that wringing it would require force beyond the available physics. Water collects on the underside of every horizontal surface. It gathers on verticals. It clings to the undersides of overhangs in trembling beads that grow and release and grow and release in rhythms older than breathing.

---

The sound is enormous. Not loud in the way of impact or explosion, but loud in the way of *accumulation*—the collected, layered, compounding roar of a million mechanisms running in parallel, none of them large, none of them silent, all of them persistent beyond any scale of time you were built to comprehend. The sound comes from below, from within, from everywhere at once, because the engines are everywhere. The guts of the Works are engines.

This is what the place is, at its root: a factory that has outlasted its purpose so completely that the purpose has become an archaeological question. The machines still run. They must run—the world would likely collapse if they stopped, though no one remains who could verify this, and no one remains who could stop them. They are engines of unknown function. Pumps, perhaps, judging by the throbbing rhythm that pulses through the pipes and conduits lacing the landscape. Compressors. Refineries. Presses that still press some unknown material through some unknown process toward some unknown end. The industrial heart of the Works beats on, an automaton the size of a region, and the sound it makes is a deafening, continuous, low-frequency roar that you do not hear so much as inhabit. The sound lives in your chest. It vibrates your skeleton. After sufficient time—which is not long—you stop hearing it the way you stop feeling your own pulse, and the silence that replaces it is not silence at all but a kind of perceptual surrender, a tinnitus of the soul.

Interspersed: higher frequencies. The shriek of metal on metal. The whine of turbines in deep housings, spinning at speeds that produce harmonics at the edge of human toleration. The rhythmic clanking of piton-arms, huge and slow, rising and falling somewhere out in the murk. The hiss of steam venting from relief valves that have been opening and closing on the same schedule for epochs. The gurgle and rush of water moving through pipes wider than corridors, water that has been circulating through the Works for so long that it is no longer water in any familiar sense—it is *process fluid*, it is the blood of the machine, carrying minerals and heat and the memory of pressure through arteries that have no veins, only the endless looping circulation of a system that has forgotten its purpose but not its rhythm.

---

The vegetation is sparse but present—unavoidable, really, in a place this wet, this old, this saturated with the residue of living chemistry. What grows here grows in the margins, in the cracks where the endless water pools, in the sheltered grooves where the sound is merely deafening rather than annihilating. It is not vegetation as a forest would understand it. It is more like a film, a scum, a patience—colonies of something that is almost algae and almost lichen and almost neither, spreading across the metal in slow blue-green patches that seem to breathe. They do breathe. You can see them: a barely perceptible pulse, a swelling and subsiding, happening at a rate that makes geological time seem hasty. These are the slowest living things. They may be the oldest. They may remember the builders.

Taller growths emerge in the wettest depressions—stalks of something that resembles a reed but is gray-black and jointed like a finger. They grow in clusters of three to five, never more, spaced across the plain at intervals of fifty or a hundred meters, rising from the puddles that never dry. They do not sway. There is no wind. They stand rigid and faintly gleaming, and if you listen—at the extremity of hearing, beneath the roar—you can hear them clicking softly at their joints, ticking like clocks, counting something.

No animals. Not one. No insects, no birds, no vermin, no pests. The Works are not hostile to animal life; they are simply irrelevant to it, and it to them. There is nothing here for anything that moves with intent. The food chain collapsed or was never built. The closest thing to fauna is the water itself, which moves with what seems like purpose, which finds its way through every barrier, which leaks and seeps and drips with a constancy that borders on intention.

---

The landscape is flat, but it is not featureless. The low elevation means the structures rise—the pipes, the tanks, the cooling towers and smokestacks (though they smoke nothing, venting only steam and sound), the generator housings and relay stations and junction boxes that speckle the plain like the ruins of a city built by a civilization that communicated in pressure differentials. These structures are old beyond reckoning. Their surfaces are smooth where they were once textured. The bolt-heads are domes. The graffiti—if there was ever graffiti—has been worn to nothing by the patient rubbing of moist air. The shapes have the quality of being *settled*: they have found the forms they will hold for the remainder of time, and they are at peace with those forms, and they will not change again.

You walk between them. The distance between each presence is significant—fifty meters, a hundred, two hundred—and in between there is only the weeping metal plain and the darkness and the sound and the moisture collecting on every surface including you. The density is low. The world is mostly space, and what fills the space is the *process*: the ongoing, purposeless, irrevocable process of the Works doing whatever they do, which is what they have always done, which is perhaps nothing more than persisting.

There is a horizon. You know this because you can see where the darkness deepens, where the faintly gleaming shapes stop and the nothing begins. But the horizon could be a kilometer away or ten or a hundred; the light will not tell you, and the sound is the same everywhere, and the wetness is absolute and indifferent to distance. The world extends. It extends past your walking, past your seeing, past your capacity to model it. Somewhere out there, beyond the range of even assumption, the Works continue—the same engines, the same pipes, the same weeping metal, the same blue-green patches breathing their impossibly slow respiration, the same reed-things clicking in the permanent puddles, the same darkness and sound and water. The Works go on. They have always gone on.

---

The rules here are simple, and they are not rules written for anything that resembles you:

*Everything runs.* The engines do not stop. The water does not dry. The sound does not cease. If you sleep, the sound continues. If you die, the sound continues. The Works do not notice you.

*Everything weeps.* The moisture is constant and inescapable. Metal rusted once, long ago, and then the rust itself wore away, and what is left is a surface that water loves and will never leave. You will be damp. Your clothes will be damp. Your lungs will be damp. Accept this.

*Everything is made.* There is no ground beneath the ground. There is no substrate. If you could peel back the metal— you cannot, it has been there too long, it is fused, it is geological now—you would find only more made things, deeper structures, older constructions, engines all the way down.

*Everything is old.* Not the old of centuries. The old of *final states*. This is what remains when everything that can change has changed. The landscape will not surprise you. It has already become whatever it was going to become.

*Nothing is watching.* But the place feels watched. The reed-things click. The patches breathe. The water moves with what seems like purpose. The engines run their unknown processes. You are alone here, in the deafening darkness, in the wet, in the endless Works, and you are not observed, and you are not unobserved. Something in the system registers your presence—a pressure change, a temperature differential, a displacement of water. The Works do not care. But they *incorporate*. They have been incorporating everything for longer than anything has been alive to be incorporated.

You become part of the process. You cannot help it. Your warmth and your breathing and the water that runs off your skin into the channels—these are data now, inputs, perturbations in a system so vast and so old that your entire existence is a rounding error in its calculations. But it calculates. It processes. It runs.

It has always run.
