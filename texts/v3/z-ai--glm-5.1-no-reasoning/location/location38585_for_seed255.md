# The Incession Floors

It is always nearly raining here. Not the open sky kind — there is no sky — but the indoor kind, the kind that happens inside. Moisture gathers along the undersides of surfaces and lets go in fat, erratic drops. You hear them strike the lower terraces before you feel them. The air carries the smell of warm stone and cold metal, and underneath both, something acrid, something almost like vinegar but thinner.

## What the place is

Everything is made. Not *built over* something — made through and through. There is no ground beneath the floors because the floors are the ground. The world is a fabrication, a structure that extends in every direction you can reach and plenty you can't, and if there is anything underneath it, the makers did not leave an access point.

What the makers *did* leave is harder to say. The structure doesn't read as architecture in the way a building reads. There are no clear rooms, no doors in the usual sense. It reads more like geology that happens to be synthetic — strata of material laid down in dense, complicated layers, as if someone were trying to grow a cave system from scratch. Corrugated sheets of a dark, cast substance something like ceramic but heavier, something that rings dull when you knock it. Between them, seams of a pale mineral paste, still slightly tacky, still not fully hardened. In places the paste has extruded through cracks in the ceramic and formed stalagmite-like drips on the surfaces below. In other places it has shrunk back, leaving narrow gaps where the air moves through with a sound like someone breathing through a slit mouth.

The whole place is *young*. That's the strangest thing about it. The materials have the look of things that were set in place recently — not centuries, maybe not even decades. Surfaces still bear the textures of whatever shaped them. Brush marks. Tool marks. The ridged impressions of forms pressed into soft material and pulled away. There is a rawness to everything, an unfinished quality, as though the makers set the whole thing in motion and then stepped back to see what it would do.

## The layout

The terrain is low — no soaring vaults, no dramatic drops. The structure extends mostly horizontal, a vast and only gently undulating expanse of stacked levels, interconnected by shallow ramps, low steps, and gaps you have to step across. The ceiling, where there is one, sits a meter to a meter and a half above the walking surface. You move through this place slightly stooped. The scale is regional — the structure extends well past the range of walking in a day, splitting and reconnecting, terracing down in some places and rising in others, but never more than two or three stories in any direction. A valley-sized interior. A town built for things shorter than people, or for people who expected to spend their lives close to the floor.

And it is dense. Every surface is occupied. Not by objects — by *features*. The makers did not leave empty space. Walls are ribbed with channels, some functional (the water moves through them), some apparently decorative, some whose purpose is unclear. Floors are textured with raised patterns — geometric, repetitive, not quite symmetrical. Columns of stacked ceramic rings stand in rows, spaced closely enough that you have to weave between them. Overhead, bundles of stiff fibrous tubes run in clustered bundles, pressed against the ceiling and each other, branching and rejoining. Everything is near-touching. Everything is layered. You cannot stand anywhere in this place and be more than arm's length from six different made surfaces.

## The water

Water enters the structure from somewhere above — through hairline fractures in the ceiling material, through the seams where bundles meet, through the porous ceramic itself in places where the firing was uneven. It doesn't flow so much as *transpire*. The air is damp, not wet — a persistent humidity that settles on surfaces and combines into droplets at the low points. The channels in the walls collect this moisture and direct it along winding paths to collection basins — shallow ceramic bowls set into the floor at irregular intervals, each one ringed with a lip of pale paste, as if to mark it.

Some of the basins are full. Some are empty and ring when drops hit them. Some have overflowed, and the water has found its own path across the floor, following the raised geometric patterns, pooling in the low points between ridges. The water tastes mineral, slightly metallic. It leaves a faint residue when it dries.

The sound of water is everywhere, but it is not a single sound. It is dozens of sounds — the tick of drops on ceramic, the hollow plunk of drops into standing water, the hiss of moisture running along narrow channels, the deep gurgle of water moving through the tubes overhead. These sounds layer. They combine. In the denser parts of the structure, the combined water sound becomes a continuous low roar, a white noise that fills the space so completely that you feel it in your chest more than hear it in your ears.

## The sound

And the water is only part of it. The structure itself is loud.

The tubes overhead — the bundled fibrous channels — carry things. Not just water. Air, for one: the structure breathes. Air moves through the tube bundles in slow, irregular pulses, and where it exits (through small outlet ports at the ends of branches, always positioned near the ceiling), it produces a low, resonant hum that varies in pitch depending on the length and diameter of the tube. The sound is not mechanical. It is organic, almost vocal — like a pipe organ played by something that doesn't know music, only pressure. The hum changes over the course of hours. Sometimes it is nearly subsonic, felt more than heard. Sometimes it sharpens into a tone that makes the ceramic surfaces vibrate, and small amounts of water shimmy across floors as the resonance shifts.

Additionally, the structure is *settling*. The pale paste between layers is still curing, still hardening, and as it does, it shifts. This produces sounds — creaks, clicks, small percussive reports like knuckles cracking. Sometimes a whole section will adjust at once, and the sound is a deep, structural groan that travels through the ceramic like a wave. You feel it in your feet before you hear it. Dust and small flakes of paste shower down. Water sloshes in the basins.

The combined sound of the Incession Floors — water, air-hum, structural settling — is loud enough that you would need to raise your voice to speak to someone three meters away. And it is never quiet. The structure does not sleep.

## The light

There is almost none. The makers provided no light sources that still function, if they ever did. What light exists comes from two sources, both weak.

First: the paste. The pale mineral paste between layers has a faint luminescence, a cold blue-white glow, like the last light in a sky after sunset. It is barely enough to see by — it turns surfaces into dim shapes, suggests forms rather than revealing them. The glow is uneven, brighter where the paste is thicker, dimmer where it has dried and shrunk. In the gaps where paste has pulled away from ceramic, the seams go dark, and the structure appears to be held together by lines of cold light.

Second: bioluminescence. There are things living here.

## Life

Not plants. Nothing chlorophyll-based, nothing with leaves or roots or stems. The structure's inhabitants are simpler — fungal, possibly, or something adjacent to fungal that doesn't map cleanly onto any taxonomy you'd bring to it.

They exist in the damp places. In the collection basins, where the water stands. Along the channels where moisture flows. In the gaps where the paste has shrunk back and the air is humid. They are not everywhere — you can walk for an hour through the structure and see nothing alive — but when you find them, they are unmistakable.

The most common form is a mat: a thin, spreading film of material that coats damp surfaces, pale gray when dry, translucent when wet. It is slimy to the touch. It grows slowly, following the water, and where two mats meet, they merge rather than competing. In the collection basins that have been full for a long time, the mats grow thick — a centimeter, sometimes two — and develop a layered structure, like a stack of damp paper.

Where the mats are established and the water is reliable, they produce fruiting structures. These are small — finger-length, thin, slightly curved, like incomplete question marks. They are dark, almost black, and they are the source of the second light. The tips of these structures glow a dim, warm amber, like the last coal in a fire. In a dark collection basin, a cluster of these fruiting bodies looks like a handful of fallen stars, or like lights in distant windows.

The light they produce is enough to move by, if you move slowly. It reveals the immediate surroundings in warm detail — the texture of the ceramic, the sheen of water, the geometric patterns on the floor — and leaves everything beyond in darkness. Walking through the populated parts of the structure is like walking through a village at night, where each basin is a house with its lights on, and between the houses is nothing but dark.

There is one other form of life here, rarer still. In the deepest, dampest parts of the structure, where the water has been flowing longest and the mats are thickest, there are things that move. Small things — thumb-length, maybe, hard to see in the dim light. They are dark, like the fruiting bodies, and they move slowly, deliberately, across the surfaces of the mats. They appear to feed on the fungal growth. Their bodies are soft, segmented, and they leave trails in the slime behind them. When disturbed, they contract into tight spirals and do not move again for a long time.

No one named them. There is no one here to name them. They exist in small numbers, in small places, and the structure continues without them.

## The makers' tools

The technology here is simple. Whatever advanced methods were used to produce the ceramic and the paste, the *finishing* was done by hand. Tool marks are visible everywhere — the scrape of something edged across wet ceramic, the jab of something pointed into paste, the sweep of something bristled. The geometric patterns on the floors were pressed into the material while it was soft, using stamps or forms — the same patterns repeat, but with slight variations, as though each was applied by hand and no two impressions are identical.

In a few places, tools have been left behind. A flat stone, smooth on one side, used for pressing. A stick, sharpened, the tip stained with paste. A bundle of stiff fibers, bound at one end, splayed at the other — a brush. These objects sit where they were set down, as if the makers intended to come back. They did not come back. The paste has cured around the bases of the tools. The water has stained them. The fungal mats have begun to grow over them.

## The atmosphere

The Incession Floors are cool — not cold, but the kind of cool that settles into your clothes after an hour. The dampness makes it feel cooler than it is. The air is still in most places, but in the corridors where the tube outlets are positioned, there are slow breezes, warm and humid, that smell of mineral and vinegar and something else, something organic, something like the inside of a mouth.

The structure is active. It is not static. The water cycles — collecting, flowing, evaporating, condensing. The air pulses through the tubes, changing the hum. The paste continues to cure, shifting, settling, cracking. The fungal mats grow and recede with the moisture. The fruiting bodies glow brighter and dimmer over cycles that might correspond to something — temperature, humidity, air pressure — but don't correspond to any day or night you'd recognize. The small dark things move across the mats, feeding, contracting, moving again.

Everything here is in the middle of becoming. The structure was made, but it is not finished. It may never be finished. The makers set processes in motion — water, air, material, life — and left them to run. The structure is still running. The floors are still curing. The water is still finding new paths. The fungal mats are still spreading. The hum is still changing.

You walk through this place stooped, in the dark, in the noise, and you feel that you are inside something that is still being born.
