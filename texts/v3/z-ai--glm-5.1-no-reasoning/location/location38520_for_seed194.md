You descend into it—not downward, exactly, but inward, as if the world folds around you and tightens. The passage is a throat lined with root-hair and pressed loam, and then it opens, and you are inside.

The Cavity.

It is a room. It is a city. It is both, inseparably. The space is roughly ovoid, perhaps forty meters at its longest span, though the walls breathe with such slow regularity that measurement is a negotiation. You stand on a surface that yields slightly, that grips slightly—the interlocked mat of ten thousand generations of root-stock, flattened and fused into a living floor. The roots are the size of your wrist, your thigh, your torso, layered in skeins and whorls, and they are ancient, so ancient their surfaces have worn smooth as river stone. There is no soil between them anymore. There hasn't been soil for epochs. What soil once existed was consumed, digested, reincorporated. The roots are their own substrate now, gripping only each other.

Above you: darkness so complete it has texture. You cannot see the ceiling and will never see it, but you know it is there because the sound tells you so. The Chamber is never silent. It groans. It creaks. A low, arrhythmic moan rises from the walls—the sound of fiber settling under immense patience, of wood compressing across millennia, of the root-mass adjusting to its own weight. Sometimes a sharp crack, distant, muffled: some deep interior beam finally surrendering to the long compression. The sound reverberates. It has nowhere to go. The Chamber holds its noise the way a held breath holds pressure. Every creak accumulates, layers upon itself, until the air hums with a bass drone that you feel in your molars, your sternum, the spaces between your vertebrae.

There is no water. The word *dry* is insufficient. This place has passed through dryness into something more absolute—a state of desiccation so profound it has become a chemical condition. The air tastes of chalk and old resin. Touch any surface and your fingertips come away powder-coated, desiccated. The roots, for all their density, have not known moisture in ages beyond counting. They survive through a process you can sense but not name: a slow drawing, a pulling of something from nothing, a metabolism that runs on time itself rather than water. When you press your palm flat against the floor, you feel a faint warmth—life, yes, but life throttled down to an ember, life as a mathematical proof rather than an event.

And yet—vegetation is all there is.

The Chamber is vegetation. The city is vegetation. There is nothing here that is not plant, was not plant, will not become plant again. The streets—there are streets, you realize, after your eyes adjust to the absolute black—are channels carved by the long-ago passage of roots seeking routes around each other. They form a grid, or near enough: corridors wide enough for two people to walk abreast, walled by root-flanks the color of dried blood and old bone. The corridors intersect. They branch. They dead-end. They form a logic that was never planned but emerged, over eons, from the simple imperative of growth and the constraint of an enclosed space.

And the structures—there are structures. Rooms within rooms. Chambers off the corridors, their walls grown rather than built, their entrances arched by the curving of massive root-buttresses that sought each other across open space and fused. Some chambers are small, closet-sized, and you understand they were storage—granaries, perhaps, though what was stored has long since been consumed. Some are large, communal, their ceilings supported by columns that are simply roots that grew downward rather than outward, finding the floor and anchoring, becoming architecture by accident of orientation.

There are stairs. Not carved—grown. The roots thickened at certain points, creating risers, and over time, as the root-mass below compressed and the root-mass above descended, the risers became steps. They lead to upper levels, or lower levels—the Chamber is three-dimensional, a warren of tiers and half-floors and suspended platforms where root-mats grew dense enough to bear weight. You cannot see any of this. You feel it with your hands and feet. The darkness is absolute. You navigate by touch, by the texture of the walls, by the sound of your own footsteps muffled on the living floor.

The city was not built by hands, but hands once shaped it.

You find the marks. In the smoother corridors, where the root-walls are dense and hard as ironwood, you find the ghost-impressions of tools—stone edges, bone scrapers, the patient work of generations who carved living wood into livable space. They did not cut. They encouraged. A scrape here, a notch there, guiding the growth, persuading the roots to thicken here, thin there, arch overhead, close around. The primitive tech is not gone—it is absorbed. The stone tools are still embedded in some walls, grown around and through, incorporated into the structure like fossils in sediment. You run your fingers over one: a hand-axe of knapped flint, three-quarters swallowed by the root that consumed it, its edge still sharp.

Who were they? There is no one to ask. No animal life stirs in the Chamber—no insects, no vermin, no creatures of any kind. The silence between the creaks is the silence of a world that has never known breath except its own. The people—if people they were—left no bones, no clothing, no artifacts beyond the tools they could not carry. They were absorbed, perhaps. Or they left through the throat-passage you entered by, and never returned. Or they became something else. The roots remember, but roots do not speak in any language you know.

The weirdness of the Chamber is not dramatic. It accumulates. It is the fact that the streets, though organic, are *organized*—too organized for emergence alone. It is the fact that certain chambers have no entrance you can find, as if they were grown sealed, as if something is still inside. It is the fact that the air, though utterly dry, carries a faint sweetness—not rot, not flower, but something between, a scent that belongs to no plant you have known. It is the fact that when you press your ear to the wall, the creaking resolves into something almost like language: a rhythm, a pattern, a syntax of stress and release that seems almost, almost, to mean.

The Chamber is ancient. Not centuries—epochs. The erosion is visible in the smoothness of every surface, in the way root-buttresses have flattened under their own weight until they resemble poured mud rather than living fiber. The city has settled into its final form. It will not change again, not in any timescale that matters to you. The roots still grow, but the growth is glacial—a millimeter per century, perhaps, the living mass creeping inward, filling the last hollow spaces, slowly erasing the corridors and chambers it spent millions of years creating. In another million years, the Chamber will be solid. In ten million, it will be stone. In a hundred million, it will be a curiosity—a spherical inclusion in some future cliff face, dense and dark and faintly warm, that no one will recognize as a city.

But that is later. For now, the Chamber holds. You stand in the dark, in the hum and the creak, in the dry sweet air, in the absolute density of living matter that has become a metropolis, and you listen. The sound is all there is. The sound is enough. It fills the void, presses against your eardrums, becomes a kind of light—a topology of noise that maps the space around you in echoes and reverberations. You close your eyes, though it makes no difference, and you begin to move through the root-city by sound alone, one hand on the wall, feeling the slow pulse of something that is not quite a heartbeat, something that has been beating since before the word *heart* existed, and will continue long after the word is forgotten.

The Chamber does not know you are here. It does not know anything. It simply is—endlessly, patiently, loudly, darkly is—and that is, you realize, the strangest thing of all. Not that a city could grow from roots. Not that people could shape it with stone tools and stubbornness. Not that it could persist in absolute dryness, absolute darkness, absolute solitude. But that it could persist without meaning. That something so vast and so intricate and so *present* could exist without purpose, without witness, without any reason at all except the blind imperative to continue.

And yet you are here. And you are witnessing. And for a moment—a long, slow, patient moment—the creaking seems to shift, to acknowledge, to include the sound of your breathing in its ancient, endless song.
