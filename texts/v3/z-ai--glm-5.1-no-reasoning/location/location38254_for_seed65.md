# The Roar

It begins as pressure before it begins as sound. A fullness in the chest, a vibration in the teeth, and then the ears catch up and the world is *loud* — not loud like a machine or a storm but loud like a place has always been this way, will always be this way, the sound so total and so constant that it stops being something you hear and becomes something you live inside.

This is the high country. The real high country.

---

The plateau sits above four thousand meters and stretches in every direction farther than the eye can reconcile with the thinness of the air. The sky here is not blue the way sky is blue lower down — it is a deep, saturating blue, almost indigo at the zenith, and the light that pours from it is so immediate, so unfiltered, that every surface it touches becomes hyperreal. Stone glows. Shadow cuts like blade-edges. The horizon shivers with clarity even as the distance dissolves into a pale silver haze where earth meets sky and you cannot tell if you are looking at a mountain range fifty kilometers away or a cloud bank resting on the world's edge.

The ground underfoot is ancient. Granitic. The bedrock here has been weathering for longer than anything on two legs has had a name for weather — long enough that the stone has settled into its own logic, its own rounded forms, its own shallow basins and gentle swales. There are no jagged peaks here. That phase passed eons ago. What remains is the bones of something that was once violent and is now, utterly, at rest. The rock surfaces are smooth, almost polished, stained dark with desert varnish in places, pale and salt-frosted in others. They hold their shapes the way a sleeping body holds its shape — without effort, without thought.

Between the stone exposures, the ground breaks into gravel and coarse sand, pale granite grit that crunches softly underfoot — though you cannot hear the crunching. Nothing that quiet can be heard here. The wind takes everything below a shout and swallows it whole.

---

The wind. The wind is the sound. The wind is the world.

It comes from the west, always from the west, funneling up the long gradual slope of the plateau face and accelerating across the bare stone until it hits the eastern escarpment — a kilometer-high drop that the air cannot follow but must instead pour over, around, against, through. The escarpment edge is a wall of compressed turbulence. The wind hits it and roars. Not a howl — a roar, sustained, unbroken, a single continuous note that has been sounding since before the first river cut the first valley in the lowlands far below. It is the sound of the atmosphere encountering a cliff and losing its composure. It is the sound of pressure differentials resolving themselves across stone.

Standing near the edge, the roar is physical. It presses against the skin. It fills the sinuses. You feel it in your gut before your brain finishes processing it as sound. Conversation is impossible. Even shouting is pointless — the wind strips words from your mouth and scatters them meaningless over the escarpment. You are alone with the noise, and the noise is alone with the rock, and both have been here longer than you.

Step back from the edge a hundred meters and the roar softens — not to silence, never to silence, but to a deep, broad, rushing howl that fills the background of every moment. It becomes the sound of the place, the way silence is the sound of a library. You stop noticing it the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat. But it is always there.

---

The moisture comes in fits. Clouds move through here — not above, *through*, at this elevation the clouds are level with you, moving past like slow pale animals grazing on the stone. When they pass, they leave condensation on every surface. The rock darkens. Shallow basins in the granite — some no larger than a cupped hand, some broad enough to bathe in — collect the mist and hold it. Tiny pools, scattered across the plateau like mirrors laid on stone. They reflect the sky. They evaporate slowly in the wind and the light, and by afternoon most are gone, leaving dark stains on the rock and, in the deeper basins, a fine crust of pale mineral salt.

Occasionally it rains — brief, sharp showers that sweep across the plateau in visible curtains and are gone before they fully arrive. The water runs across the smooth stone in thin sheets, finding the ancient channels cut by ten thousand years of the same rain, pooling briefly in the basins, and then vanishing over the escarpment edge as mist — the wind catches the falling water and tears it apart before it can reach the lowlands as anything coherent. From the edge, looking down, you can sometimes see rain falling upward, the droplets shredded and lifted by the updraft, rejoining the clouds that birthed them.

---

Life here is sparse in species but staggering in number.

The vegetation clings to sheltered pockets — south-facing crevices where the wind eddies rather than blasts, where a little grit has accumulated and a little moisture lingers. Tough, silver-leaved cushions, compact and ground-hugging, their roots probing the hairline cracks in the granite for purchase. Occasional wiry grasses, bent permanently eastward by the wind, growing horizontal rather than vertical, their seed heads trailing along the rock like streamers. A few lichen species — crustose, map-like, spreading across the stone in patches of orange and grey-green and almost-black, so slow-growing that the largest of them may be older than any tree in the lowland forests. They are the true inhabitants of the plateau. Everything else is visiting.

The visitors come in flocks.

The birds arrive in the morning thermals, riding the updraft from the escarpment edge in spiraling columns that look, from a distance, like smoke. They are not large — compact, dark-winged, aerodynamic, built for this specific air the way a fish is built for water. They nest in the escarpment face, in the countless pockets and overhangs and shallow caves that the erosion of ages has carved into the cliff. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. The cliff face, seen from the edge, is patterned with their white droppings, streaked like abstract calligraphy down the stone.

They ride the wind all day. They do not fight it — they work with it, the way a sailor works with a current, tacking and wheeling in patterns that look chaotic but are, to the birds, as legible as roads. They feed on the insects — and there are insects here, improbably, a thin fauna of wind-blown gnats and hardy beetles that somehow eke a living from the stone and the lichen and the brief moisture. The birds also scavenge. Anything that dies on the plateau — a bird, a rabbit-sized mammal that has wandered up too high — is found and picked clean within hours, the bones scattered by the wind, bleached by the light, ground to grit by the stone.

There are mammals too, though you would not know it at first. They live in the deeper crevices, in the pockets of gravel between boulders, in the shadowed gaps where the wind cannot reach. Marmot-like creatures, thick-furred, compact, living in burrows that they dig in the loose material that has accumulated in the plateau's hollows. They emerge in the calm hours — dawn and dusk, when the wind drops briefly, almost reluctantly, to perhaps merely loud — and they sit on their haunches and watch the sky with an alertness that suggests they know exactly what lives up there. They eat the cushion plants. They eat the roots of the wire-grass. They eat insects when they can catch them. They are the plateau's only resident warm-bloods, and their presence is furtive, peripheral, easily missed.

A predator works the escarpment edge — a large raptor, broad-winged, dark-backed, riding the same updrafts as the smaller birds but with a different intent. It takes the careless, the slow, the young. It nests somewhere on the cliff face, though no one has ever found the nest. It is the largest thing that lives here, and it moves with a laziness that belies its precision. In the wind, it barely moves its wings at all. It simply leans, and the air does the rest.

---

There are no structures here. No walls, no paths, no markers, no evidence that anything has ever been built or shaped by hands. The plateau is bare of construction. And yet —

There are stones that have been moved. In a few sheltered basins, near the larger water pools, you find arrangements that are not entirely natural. A ring of fist-sized rocks, placed around a darkened patch of granite where something has been burned — recently, or years ago, the wind makes it hard to tell. A long flat stone propped against another, forming a low windbreak, oriented precisely against the prevailing wind. A pile of smaller stones, stacked with more deliberation than gravity or erosion would manage, near the escarpment edge where the view of the lowlands opens up like a vast pale map.

Someone comes here. Someone has been coming here for a long time. Not many. Not often. But the plateau is known, in the way that hard, high, empty places are known — to the people who live in the valleys far below, the plateau is a place you go to when you need to be where the wind is. For ceremony, perhaps. For solitude. For the experience of a silence so loud it erases the self.

There is evidence of stone tools — not here, exactly, but in the gravels where the occasional stream washes down from the snowfields that linger in the highest pockets. Flakes. Cores. The debitage of someone, long ago, sitting in a sheltered hollow and working a piece of chert or obsidian into something useful. The material isn't local. It was carried up. Someone walked up here — days of walking, through the wind, through the thin air — carrying stone, to sit in this roaring emptiness and make something.

---

The thing about the plateau is that nothing changes.

The wind blows. The birds fly. The water pools form and evaporate. The light comes and goes with the rotation of the planet, and the stars wheel overhead in the clearest sky you have ever seen — the Milky Way so bright it casts faint shadows on the pale stone — and then the light returns and the birds resume and the wind is still blowing, has always been blowing, will always be blowing. The rock has not changed shape in any human lifetime. The lichen grows at a rate that makes glaciers look impulsive. The marmot-things sit on the same rocks in the same calm hours and watch the same sky.

There is no season here, not really. The temperature stays cool — cold in the pre-dawn, mild under the midday sun, cold again at dusk. There is no winter, no summer. The clouds come and go on schedules that have nothing to do with calendars. The rain falls when it falls. The only clock is the light, and even that feels less like time passing and more like a lamp being turned up and dimmed by a hand so steady that the change is imperceptible until you notice it has already happened.

You could stand at the escarpment edge for a year and the view would not change. The lowlands would be there, hazy and distant and irrelevant. The birds would wheel. The wind would roar. The stone would hold its shape. You would not age, you feel, so much as erode — slowly, patiently, in balance with everything else, until you too were smooth and weathered and part of the place.

---

The weirdness here is subtle. It is not in anything you can point to but in the way the place feels — or rather, the way the place *doesn't* feel. The wind is so loud that it fills the head. It fills every channel of perception. After a day, you stop thinking in words because words are useless — they cannot be spoken, they cannot be heard, and the part of the brain that makes them seems to tire and go quiet, leaving something else in charge. Something older. Something that understands wind and stone and light without needing to name them.

You dream differently here. The dreams are loud, even in sleep — a roaring, rushing sound that is not unpleasant but is inescapable, and the dreams take on the quality of the landscape: vast, bare, bright, populated by birds and by the sense of something enormous and patient holding you in place. You wake not refreshed but *scoured*, as if the wind has been through your mind and cleaned out the accumulated debris, leaving only the structure.

The birds, if you watch them long enough, behave in ways that are not quite right. They fly in patterns that seem, at moments, to be responding to something other than wind and hunger — formations that hold for seconds too long, synchronicities that exceed coincidence, collective turns that happen a beat before any external cause. It might be the wind. It might be the light. It might be something the plateau itself is doing to the air, to the magnetic field, to the stone that the birds can feel in their bones and you cannot.

Or it might be nothing. The plateau is patient. It does not explain itself.

---

The world below is far away and irrelevant. The world here is rock and wind and water and life and light, and it has been this way since before anyone climbed up to see it, and it will be this way after everyone has climbed back down. It asks nothing. It offers nothing. It simply is — roaring, bright, empty, full, and perfectly, utterly itself.

The wind does not stop.
