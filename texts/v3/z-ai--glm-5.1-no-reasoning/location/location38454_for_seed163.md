The plateau sings. It does not hum, nor does it whisper—it bellows, a ceaseless, deafening roar that vibrates in the molars and turns the air itself into a resonant cavity. Here, at the roof of the world, the sky is an unbearable, blinding blue, reflecting fiercely off the ubiquitous wetness that clings to every surface. The altitude is immense; the air is thin and cold, biting at the lungs, yet it is choked with a humidity so profound that condensation perpetually beads on the skin. It is a cool, high-altitude rainforest built upon a foundation of raw, unfinished earth.

The ground beneath your boots is not stone, not soil, but a sprawling, deep-pile carpet of sphagnum and clubmoss, so saturated with water that each step depresses the earth by inches, releasing a steady, muffled squelch beneath the overwhelming din. This is a landscape in utero. The erosion score is nearly zero; the terrain is embryonic, pre-formation. The underlying geology is soft, unconsolidated slate and mudstone, just heaved up from the planet’s crust, uncarved by any river, unsmoothed by any glacier. It is raw topography waiting for a chisel, a world under construction.

And yet, the chisel has arrived. It is the flora. The vegetation is total—an all-consuming mass that acts as substrate, architecture, and ecosystem all at once. Colossal, ancient horsetails and giant clubmosses stand as the load-bearing pillars of this world, their ribbed trunks rising a hundred feet into the blinding light, choking the horizontal expanse. There are no bare cliffs, no empty ridges. Every square inch of the raw geology is smothered beneath peat-forming lichens, luminescent ferns, and thick mats of liverworts. The plants *are* the landscape. Where the raw earth attempts to form a cliff, roots woven like steel cables hold the embryonic geology in a stranglehold, arresting the erosion before it can begin.

Despite the suffocating density of the flora, the space feels vast and strangely empty. The plateau stretches across a mid-range, regional expanse—a high-altitude basin ringed by jagged, mist-draped peaks. And within this immense living cathedral, there is immense spacing. The colossal trunks of the primeval plants stand far apart, their canopies a distant, sun-dappled ceiling. The undergrowth is low and waterlogged, leaving long, unobstructed sightlines through the misty, bright air. It is sparse in its population, balanced by a profound emptiness. Fauna is virtually nonexistent; an occasional, translucent amphibian might pulse in a bog-pool, or a single, drab moth might flutter against the wet bark, but the ecosystem is 99% plant and water. No birds call. No insects buzz. The animal kingdom has simply not arrived here yet.

The deafening sound—the defining characteristic of this place—emerges from the slow, agonizing friction of this living world against the raw earth, amplified by industrial might. Scattered across the basin, miles apart, are the artifacts of humanity: single, massive, iron-wrought pumping stations, half-strangled by vines but still turning. They are remnants of an industrial incursion, scaled extraction engines that rhythmically heave and churn. The sound is the amalgamation of a thousand such engines across the valley, their low-frequency mechanical thudding traveling for miles through the waterlogged peat and thin air, merging with the ceaseless sound of water—of condensation dripping, of sap weeping from slashed bark, of underground aquifers gurgling just beneath the moss. It is a deafening, subsonic heartbeat of a mechanized ecology that echoes endlessly in the damp, cold air.

Everything here moves with the patience of geology. The machines turn at agonizingly slow RPMs; the water pools and spreads rather than flows; the raw cliffs crumble one pebble a century. The landscape is locked in a slow, grinding feedback loop: the raw earth pushes up, the total vegetation smothers and binds it, the scattered engines pump the excess water to feed the flora, and the deafening sound of their labor rolls endlessly across the wet, blindingly bright plateau. It is a world just coming into being, wet, loud, and overwhelmingly green, still deciding what it wants to be when it finally grows up.
