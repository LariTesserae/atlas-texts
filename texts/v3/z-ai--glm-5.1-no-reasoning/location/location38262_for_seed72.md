# The Cistern Yards

You descend twenty-three steps cut into clay-hardened earth, and the air changes before the light does. It thickens. Becomes soup-warm and carries the smell of iron and wet stone and something underneath both — the yeasty, living odor of a place that has never fully dried. By the seventeenth step you are below grade. By the twenty-third, the sky is a long, narrow strip above you, pale and remote, and you are standing on a walkway of poured concrete gone rough and green at its edges where moisture has worked its way into the capillaries of the structure.

The Cistern Yards occupy a hollow that was never meant to be occupied. A drainage basin, originally. An engineering afterthought — a place where runoff from the eastern foundry district collected before being pumped to the settling tanks. But the pumps failed slowly, over years, and the water stayed longer each season, and the ground softened, and then someone built a retaining wall, and then someone else built a roof over a section of the walkway, and now there is a place here. A long, low, semi-subterranean yard stretched between two black embankments of packed earth and slag. Cathedral-sized in its length — you can walk for three minutes without reaching the far end — but cramped in its height, barely eight feet from the wet floor to the corrugated metal roofing that covers the central third. The uncovered sections at either end let in what light reaches this place: a dim, filtered gray that bounces off standing water and loses itself against the dark walls.

Everything here is wet. Not flowing-wet, not rain-wet, but *seeping*-wet. The water table sits inches below the surface, and the surface itself is a patchwork of poured concrete, cracked paving stone, and exposed earth that has turned to a dark, adhesive mud. Water beads on the metal pipes that run along the ceiling. It gathers in the shallow grooves where the concrete sections meet. It drips — irregularly, persistently — from a dozen points along the roofline, each drip finding its own small pool below, each pool connected to the next by a thin film of water that never quite evaporates because the air is already saturated. The temperature doesn't help. It sits at ninety-six degrees and holds, thick and still, the kind of heat that makes you feel like you're breathing through a wet cloth. Condensation forms on every surface cooler than the air — which is most of them — and runs down in slow, branching rivulets that look like the walls are weeping. Perhaps they are.

The built elements are industrial and half-formed, as if someone began constructing a pump station and then lost interest partway through. A row of iron columns — real ones, structural, eight inches in diameter — supports the roof along the central corridor. They are bolted to concrete footings that have themselves begun to tilt as the soft ground shifts beneath them. Between the columns, on either side, run the infrastructure lines: fat ceramic pipes carrying something warm (you can feel the heat radiating from them), thinner copper pipes green with oxidation, bundled electrical cable in rubber sheathing that has cracked and split to expose the wire beneath. Some of the cables still carry current. A bare bulb burns at the far end of the corridor, its light a sickly yellow that illuminates almost nothing but itself. A generator — a small diesel unit, bolted to a platform above the waterline — chugs somewhere out of sight, providing power to whatever still functions here. Its sound is the bedrock noise of the place: a low, arrhythmic thudding that you feel in your chest more than hear with your ears.

Over that, and over everything, there is the sound of the fauna.

---

They are everywhere.

The insects came first, or perhaps they were always here, evolved from whatever lived in the drainage basin before it became the Yards. They are small — none larger than a thumbnail — and they number in the millions. The standing water that pools in every depression breeds them in continuous cycles. Midges, mostly. Clouds of them, each cloud a loose aggregate of ten thousand individuals, hovering in the still air at about head-height. They don't bite. They don't even seem to have mouthparts. But they *hum*. Each cloud produces a single sustained note, and the clouds are distributed throughout the Yards at intervals of about fifteen feet, so that as you walk the corridor you pass through a gradient of tones — a low buzz here, a higher one there, a chord where two clouds overlap. The combined sound is a wall of vibration, not deafening but relentless, occupying the entire frequency range of the human ear and leaving no silence anywhere. It is the loudest thing in the Yards. It makes the diesel generator seem quiet by comparison.

On the walls, the beetles. They are flat, dark, the color of the slag they live on, and they move in slow, purposeful lines along the pipes and columns, feeding on the film of algae and moisture that coats every surface. You can see their tracks — faint, wet trails that dry and fade over hours. They are not disturbed by your presence. You can put your hand on a column and they will walk over it, or around it, with no change in pace.

In the water itself: larvae. Thousands of them, writhing in the shallows, their bodies translucent, their movements visible as a constant, wriggling turbulence just beneath the surface. Where the water is deeper — a trench runs along the eastern wall, maybe eighteen inches deep — they are denser, packed together so tightly that the water appears to move on its own, a slow, viscous circulation that has nothing to do with current.

And above, in the dim air near the ceiling: spiders. Not many, but enough. Their webs span the gaps between pipes, between columns, between the roof and the walls. The webs are thick with captured midges, and the spiders move slowly across them, feeding, tending, rebuilding. Their presence is the only thing that keeps the midge population from becoming truly suffocating.

There are no birds here. No mammals. No reptiles. The ecosystem is complete in its smallness — insect, arachnid, microbe, algae — and it fills every available niche in the Yards with a thoroughness that makes the place feel less empty than it is. There are perhaps a dozen human-scale objects in the entire space — the columns, the generator platform, a rusted pump housing, a row of empty metal shelving units, a stack of concrete blocks — and between them stretches thirty or forty feet of nothing but wet floor, dim air, and insects. But the emptiness is not vacant. It is *occupied* by the hum, by the humidity, by the slow motion of the beetles and the constant invisible motion of the larvae. The emptiness is full.

---

The Yards are still forming. This is the strangest thing about them, and it is not very strange — it is simply true in a way that takes time to notice. The concrete is still curing in places, especially at the far end where the pour is newest. You can see the damp patches where the chemical process continues, the surface slowly hardening, slowly lightening, over weeks and months. The ground beneath the walkway is still settling. The columns tilt a degree further each year. The mud at the edges of the path advances and retreats with the water table, which itself rises incrementally — a centimeter a season, maybe less — as the drainage above becomes less efficient and more water finds its way down. The electrical cables are still being installed, or perhaps still being removed; it is hard to tell which. Some end in bare wire coiled around a nail. Some end in junction boxes that are not yet connected to anything. The infrastructure is neither complete nor abandoned. It is in medias res, paused at some indefinite point between construction and decay.

The slowness of the place extends to everything. The midges hover; they do not dart. The beetles walk; they do not scurry. The water seeps; it does not flow. Even the condensation on the walls runs down with a patience that seems deliberate, each droplet taking minutes to travel a foot. The only fast things here are the larvae, and even they seem to move in slow motion when viewed through the water that distorts them.

You can stand in the middle of the corridor — the dim yellow bulb behind you, the generator thudding beneath your ribs, the hum of the midges filling your skull — and feel the Yards existing around you with a kind of biological patience that has nothing to do with you. You are the largest thing here. You are also the most temporary. The beetles will outlast you. The midges will outlast the beetles. The water will outlast everything. It sits in the shallow pools and waits for the next centimeter, the next season, the next slow advance of the water table that will eventually — in fifty years, in a hundred — fill this place entirely, drown the columns, submerge the pipes, silence the generator, and leave only the hum of the insects, loud and steady, in the warm dark below.
