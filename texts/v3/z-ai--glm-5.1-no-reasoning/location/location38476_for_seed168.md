# The Processing Plateau

The first thing is the flatness. Not the flatness of a plain — a plain is low, earthbound, a thing that lies. This is the flatness of something cut and left, a table sawed from higher stone and polished by absence. You are up. The air confirms it — thin, warm, dry in the throat. The sky sits closer than it should, a pale dome that feels architectural, as though someone laid it and walked away.

Below: bare stone. Not sand, not dust, not the soft decomposed matter of lowlands. Stone. A single unbroken sheet of it in some directions, in others cracked into enormous polygonal slabs like the hide of an animal too large to imagine cooling. The color is inconsistent and vivid — bands of amber, of slate-violet, of a deep oxide green that is almost black at the edges where one band meets the next. These colors are not oxidized. They are not weathered. They look fresh, as though the stone were painted this morning and the paint has not yet had time to lie. That is the quality of everything here. Newness. Not new as in recently made — new as in not yet finished becoming what it will be.

The geology is embryonic. You can see where the stone bubbled and froze mid-bubble, where mineral layers folded over themselves before they had time to settle. There are places where the surface is glassy and places where it is rough as a rasp and the transition between them is sudden, as though different regions cooled under different rules. In a few spots the stone is still warm to the touch — not cooling, not residual. Warm the way a body is warm. The plateau was thrust upward by forces that have not fully stopped. The land is still arriving at its own altitude.

---

The vegetation announces itself before you reach it. Not by sound — there is no sound — but by color breaking the mineral monotony. You see it from a long way off because the plateau is open and the sightlines run for kilometers without interruption. A cluster of growth, dense and self-contained, sitting on the stone like something placed there. Then another cluster, far away, separated by a distance that makes you re-evaluate what "lush" means in a place this empty.

Between the clusters: nothing. Bare stone stretching in every direction. The emptiness is not absence — it is a presence, a substance, the dominant medium of this landscape. You walk for twenty minutes between growth sites and the stone beneath your feet does not change and the air does not move and the only evidence of distance is the slow recession of the cluster behind you and the slow approach of the one ahead. The plateau is regional in scale. It extends past every horizon. And it is nearly vacant. The clusters of vegetation are islands in an ocean of mineral, and the ocean is calm and still and warm and goes on and on.

When you reach a cluster, the lushness is startling. These are not desert plants, not scrub, not the tenacious reduced flora of a dry place. These are full, heavy, dense-growers. Thick stalks like stalks of sugarcane but taller, rising two and three meters, spaced closely enough that their upper growth overlaps into a canopy. The leaves — if they are leaves — are broad flat shelves, layered in spirals up each stalk, colored in the amber-to-rust range with undersides of deep violet that is nearly black. The surfaces are waxy, sealed, almost metallic in their sheen. They do not flutter. They do not wilt. Each plant looks as though it was finished and then frozen at the moment of maximum function.

There is no water here. None on the surface, none in the air, none in the stone. You could walk for days and never see a drop. And yet these plants are not merely surviving — they are thriving, dense and tall and vital. They run on something else. The stalks, when you look closely at their bases, are fused to the stone. Not growing from it — bonded to it, as though the plant and the mineral are continuous, as though the plant is an organ the stone grew. The roots do not seek soil; they are mineral conduits, tapping directly into the substrate. Whatever the plants feed on, it is inside the rock and it is not water.

The chemistry is sealed. Each plant appears to be a closed system. Nothing enters, nothing leaves. No transpiration, no droplets, no condensation on the waxy leaves. The air around a cluster is exactly as dry as the air across the empty stone. If there is fluid inside these stalks — and there must be, given their density and weight — it has never seen the sky. It cycles internally, the way industrial coolant cycles in a closed-loop system. The plants do not lose. They do not waste. They are not subject to evaporation or drought because they have eliminated the possibility of loss.

---

The industrial character of the place does not announce itself either. It accumulates. You notice it in the regularity of the clusters — they are not randomly distributed. They are spaced, roughly equidistant, as though positioned for efficient coverage of the substrate. You notice it in the root networks, which you can trace where the stone has cracked: deep channels, branching in patterns that look less like roots and more like piping, like the circulation diagrams of a refinery. You notice it in the stalks themselves, which are not organic-soft. They are dense, fibrous, mineral-reinforced. When you tap one, it rings dully, like striking a ceramic pipe.

These plants are processing. Each cluster is a facility. The roots take in raw mineral substrate — specific minerals, you realize, because the clusters only grow where the stone contains particular bands of color, particular concentrations — and the plants refine it. The stalks are full of it. You can see it in the weight of the upper growth, in the way the broad shelves of leaves seem to carry a load. The violet undersides are deposition surfaces. Minerals collect there, crystallize, layer. The leaves are not leaves. They are plates in a separator. The plants are taking the geology apart and sorting it and putting the pieces where they need to go.

The scale of production is industrial. Not in the human sense — no smokestacks, no vehicles, no grid of power lines. But in the sense that this is not a garden and not a wild growth. This is an operation. Tons of material moved and processed, silently, continuously, across a regional expanse, by organisms that function as a distributed industrial system. The plateau is the factory floor. The stone is the feedstock. The plants are the machinery. And there is no one running it.

Or — there was. Or there is, somewhere, in the way that a clock left wound will keep time without a hand on it. The system has the feel of something designed and then left to operate. The clusters are too well-placed. The root networks are too organized. The chemistry is too sealed, too efficient, too closed. This does not look like evolution. It looks like engineering — engineering that has outlasted its engineers, or engineering that never needed them.

---

The silence is total. No birds. No insects. No rustling of leaves in wind because there is no wind. The air is still, warm, and it does not move. It has the quality of air in a sealed room — not stale, not stagnant, but still in the way that air gets when nothing has disturbed it for a very long time. If you hold your breath and listen, you can hear something. Not a sound exactly. A vibration, sub-audible, coming through the stone. It might be the root networks, processing. It might be the plateau itself, still settling into its altitude. It might be nothing.

There are no animals. Not one. The absence is conspicuous because the vegetation is so dense, so apparently productive, that you expect life to have found it. But the plants do not produce anything that an animal could eat. Their internal chemistry is non-aqueous, mineral-based, operating on solvents and compounds that would be toxic to anything that evolved in a water-carbon world. The plants are not making food. They are making something else — minerals, refined compounds, crystalline structures that have no biological consumer. The output accumulates on the leaf undersides and eventually falls, or the stalks harden and become mineral and new stalks grow, and the old ones become part of the substrate again. The whole system cycles, but nothing outside the system participates in the cycle.

This means the plateau is a closed ecology. The plants process the stone and deposit the refined material and the material returns to the stone and the plants process it again. A loop. A stable, self-sustaining, unchanging loop. There is no season here because there is no season to have. The temperature does not fluctuate. The light does not change in quality, only in angle, and even the angle seems to move through a narrow range, as though the sun (if it is a sun) is restrained. The clouds, the few that exist, are high and thin and stationary. They do not travel.

---

The weirdness of the plateau is not dramatic. It is not a place of alien geometries or impossible physics. It is a place where everything is slightly too clean, slightly too organized, slightly too still. The plants should attract something. The stone should weather. The air should move. The system should have entropy. But the plateau exists in a kind of suspended equilibrium — not frozen, not dead, but balanced at a point where nothing needs to change because everything is already doing what it does. The plants process. The stone provides. The air holds. The light illuminates. And nothing, nothing, nothing is in a hurry.

You could stay here for a long time and not notice time passing. That is the final quality of the plateau. It is not just stable — it is stabilizing. Standing on the bare stone between clusters, with the warm air still in your lungs and the sub-audible hum in your feet and the distant amber-violet of the next growth cluster on the horizon, you feel your own urgency begin to thin. Your heartbeat, which had been the loudest sound for kilometers, slows. Your breathing evens. The landscape is not hostile. It is not welcoming. It is simply present, in the way that a machine is present when it is running — not for you, not against you, but doing its work with a patient, mechanical indifference that is, in its own way, restful.

The plateau will be here tomorrow exactly as it is today. The clusters will process. The stone will provide. The air will not move. And whatever this landscape is becoming — because it is still becoming, still embryonic, still arriving at itself — it will become it at its own pace, which is the pace of mineral and closed chemistry and deep time, which is to say: it will become it so slowly that the becoming itself is indistinguishable from being finished.
