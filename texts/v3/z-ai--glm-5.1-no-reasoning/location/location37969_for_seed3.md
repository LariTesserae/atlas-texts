The city is still being born. 

You feel this in your teeth before you understand it with your mind. A subsonic, grinding thrum vibrates through the boot-soles and up the shinbones, a loud, patient bass-note that never ceases. It is the sound of raw geology under stress, of a mountain range that is not yet finished pushing itself out of the earth. The peaks here scrape the sky at extreme elevations, but they lack the rounded, defeated shoulders of ancient ranges; these are sharp, jagged, and crusty with magmatic extrusion, their granite faces still splitting along fissures that were not there yesterday. This is a pre-formation landscape, embryonic and raw, where the bedrock still remembers being liquid.

Clinging to these half-made precipices is a city of industrial magnitude. It is a marvel of layered construction, a vertical labyrinth of wrought iron, riveted steel, and soot-stained brick that scales the cliffs in great, staggering tiers. The architecture is organized but desperate—infrastructure built to endure a world that hasn't settled down. Funiculars and massive freight elevators traverse the vertical spaces, their gears grinding in the perpetual dimness. The air is cool and thin, the kind of cold that makes your lungs feel crystalline, carrying the acrid tang of ozone, coal dust, and wet iron. 

Water is a sporadic guest here. It does not flow in generous rivers. Instead, it manifests as a persistent, intermittent damp. Condensation weeps from the massive steam-pipes that snake through the city's underbelly; sudden, chilling rains sweep through the valley without warning, darkening the red brick; and in the higher, uninhabited crags, water pools in rocky basins, collected from mists that roll in like slow-breathing beasts. The dampness feeds the lush vegetation that has aggressively colonized the spaces the engineers haven't concreted over. Luminescent mosses and thick, trailing vines of waxy ferns drape over the rail lines and smokestacks, a vivid, breathing green that refuses to be choked out by the soot. The plant life is lush but sparse in its distribution—great swaths of it hang in isolated, dripping curtains over the void, leaving vast stretches of bare, smoking metal in between. Everything is spaced out. The city is wide open, spilling across the regional valley, yet it feels utterly empty, a low-saturation sprawl where massive industrial complexes sit hundreds of meters apart, connected by frail iron walkways over canyons of mist.

The light is a permanent twilight. The sun is a rumor, blocked by the sheer bulk of the peaks and the thick, low-hanging chimney smoke. What illumination exists comes from the hissing, blue-white flare of arc lamps, the molten orange glow of forge-vats, and the sickly yellow of sodium bulbs strung along gantries. In this dimness, the fauna thrives. The skies and scaffolds are loud with them. Broad-winged, leathery scavengers ride the thermal updrafts from the foundries, roosting in the thousands along the unfinished cliff-faces. Blind, pale arthropods the size of dogs scuttle through the damp valve-chambers, clicking and chittering as they feed on the heat of the pipes. The sheer abundance of life is a stark contrast to the emptiness of the streets; the place teems with creatures that have adapted to the noise, the damp, and the dark.

But the strangeness of the place creeps up on you, warring with the loud, mechanical certainty of the engines. The reality here is breaking down, fraying at the edges where the immense pressure of the emerging geology meets the frantic industry of the city. Gravity behaves uncertainly. A dropped wrench might hang in the air for two seconds before falling, and the rain sometimes falls sideways, or upward, pulled by a density in the rock that physics cannot explain. Space folds in on itself: an alleyway that looks thirty meters long takes an hour to walk down, while a smokestack miles away feels close enough to touch. The sound of the mountain's birthing sometimes skips a beat, and in the silence, you can hear the city breathing—vast, iron lungs expanding in the dark. 

Time moves strangely in the dimness. The dynamic of the world is agonizingly slow. A season passes, and a new cliff face might only have risen a few inches; a crack in the vaulted ceiling of the main foundry might have widened by a millimeter. The city feels locked in a patient, grinding stalemate with the earth, a slow-motion catastrophe that will take centuries to resolve. Here, at the roof of a world still under construction, you are an insignificant speck caught between the deliberate hammer of industry and the endless, tectonic gestation of the deep.
