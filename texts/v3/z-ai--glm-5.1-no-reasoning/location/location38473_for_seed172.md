You enter through a pressure hatch that does not hiss—there is no atmosphere here to rush in or escape. You step from the antechamber into the Throat.

It is a room. That is the most disorienting part. You are at extreme elevation—you can feel it in the way your suit's pressure settings automatically compensate, in the faint altitude tremor that the gyro-stabilizers in your boots must constantly correct—but you are indoors. Completely, totally indoors. There is no sky. There is no exterior. The place you stand is a pocket, a sealed cavity embedded somewhere within the superstructure of a machine so large it has no known exterior. You are three kilometers above the datum line, and you are inside a room the size of a grand cathedral.

The cold is profound. Not the cold of wind or weather—there is none—but the deep, structural cold of metal that has never been warmed, that was assembled at these temperatures and has known nothing else. The walls around you are iron and riveted steel, hoarfrost crystallizing in fern-like fractals across every surface, though there is no moisture to form such patterns. The frost is not water ice. It is something else—a precipitation of the air itself, or whatever passes for air, the non-aqueous chemistry of this place depositing its solids in temperatures that hover, eternally, just above absolute zero. The frost forms and does not sublimate. It has been forming for the duration.

The light is adequate, which feels wrong. Industrial sodium-vapor fixtures, encased in protective cages, bolted to the ceiling at regular intervals. They hum at a frequency you feel more than hear. Their light is orange-yellow, institutional, the color of factories and freight platforms. It casts everything in a flat, unromantic clarity. You can see every rivet, every weld seam, every structural gusset plate. The architecture is pure function—no aesthetic consideration, no ornament, only the brutal logic of load-bearing and containment. Girders cross the space in a grid that recedes into distance. Ducts and conduits run in bundles along the ceiling. The walls are not walls but the interior faces of something thicker—machinery or structure or material whose outer dimensions you cannot guess.

And the sound.

The sound is *enormous*. It is not a single sound but a composite, a wall of mechanical noise so dense and so unrelenting that it ceases to be heard and becomes instead a physical condition. The bass thrum of turbines—massive, industrial-scale turbines, somewhere below or behind or within the walls—shakes your chest cavity in slow pulses. Over that, the shriek of high-pressure systems venting through pipes that run through the room like arteries. A rhythmic clanking, mechanical and arrhythmic simultaneously, the sound of linkages and camshafts and reciprocating arms doing work whose purpose you cannot identify. The whine of electrical generators under load. The groan of metal under stress. The clatter of chains in channels. It does not stop. It has never stopped. The sound is the place's respiration, its circulation, its metabolism. The machines that make this noise were started long ago and have been running since, and no one is here to tend them and no one is here to hear them and they do not care.

You walk. The floor is grated metal—mesh over a void. Through the grate, you can see down into further structure, further depth, more conduits and pipes and the dim glow of indicator lights on systems you cannot name. The grating is slick with the same non-water frost. Your footsteps make no sound against the roar.

The space is sparse. There is much more emptiness than thing. The room's volume is mostly air—or whatever fills it—and the structures within it are widely spaced: a support column here, a junction box there, a gantry crossing overhead, a ladder ascending to a hatch in the ceiling that leads somewhere else. Distance between objects. Emphasis on absence. The industrial infrastructure outlines the emptiness rather than filling it. You are aware, moving through it, of how much nothing there is between the somethings.

And the sparse vegetation—though calling it that feels like a category error. There is a growth here. It is not rooted, not in soil, for there is no soil. It clings to the walls at intervals, pale fibrous tendrils that extend from the seams between plates, reaching outward in fronds that look almost like bleached kelp. They are not green. They are the color of bone, of milk, of things that have never seen sunlight. They do not move. There is no wind. They do not visibly grow. They simply are—present in scattered clusters, a handful of patches across the vast room, each one identical in size and configuration, as if stamped from a template. They are organic, or were once, or are mimicking something organic. They do not respond to your presence. They may be dead. They may be dormant. They may be something for which those categories do not apply.

No fauna. No scuttling in the corners, no hum of insect life, no rats in the ducts. Nothing moves except the machines. The machines move constantly—valves opening and closing in sequences you can hear but not see, pumps cycling, the whole structure alive with mechanical purpose—but nothing organic moves. You are the only living thing in this space, and you are not of it, and you will leave, and nothing will note your passing.

The strangeness accumulates slowly. It is not in any single feature but in their conjunction. The frost that forms without moisture. The growths that grow without soil or light or warmth. The machines that run without operators. The room that exists at altitude but has no exterior. The hatches that lead to other chambers—you can see several, at various heights on the walls, accessible by ladders and catwalks—and you understand, with a certainty that settles like the cold, that those chambers are identical. Not similar. Identical. The same dimensions, the same layout, the same frost patterns, the same pale growths in the same positions. And beyond those chambers, more chambers. And more. A honeycomb of repeated space, extending in all directions, each pocket the same volume with the same contents and the same machines running the same invisible processes, and none of it has changed since it was made, and none of it will change, and the sound will not stop.

The machines are making something. Or moving something. Or maintaining something. You cannot determine what. The purpose is not for you. It was never for anyone. It is purpose without audience, function without user, an engine that runs because it was built to run and nothing has occurred to stop it.

You stand in the Throat. The sound presses against you. The cold seeps through your suit. The pale tendrils ignore you. The frost does not melt where you breathe. The light does not flicker. The machines do not pause.

This is the world: a room inside a machine inside a structure without boundary, at the top of everything, sealed and cold and loud and empty and running, running, running.
