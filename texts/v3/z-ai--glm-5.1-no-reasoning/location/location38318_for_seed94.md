The plateau goes on forever. Or rather, it feels as though it must—that the horizon is not a boundary but a receding seam, pulling further away the longer you walk. This is a high country, two miles above the sea, but there are no dramatic peaks here, no jagged summits. Just an immensity of elevated land, a continental slab that has settled into its own mass over geological ages. The rock beneath is old, its volcanic temper long cooled, its faults closed and sealed by time and the slow, patient weight of compressed sediment. Erosion has done its work and moved on—what remains are rolling ridges and shallow bowls, forms that have found their final shape and hold it with the quiet stubbornness of things that have nowhere left to go.

And yet the place is not dry. Could never be dry. Water sits just beneath everything here, rising capillary-fast through the porous stone, seeping from weeping cliffs where the aquifer finds its cut, pooling in the endless shallow depressions that dimple the plateau's surface. The ground gives underfoot with a sodden sigh. Not mud—something more structured than mud. The root-mat of ten thousand generations holds the soil in a dense weave, and the water percolates through it like breath through lung tissue. You learn to read the color of the ground ahead: the darker the green-brown, the deeper the water waits beneath. Step wrong and the water rises over your boot; step right and it merely bulges up around your print, sits for a moment like a bright eye, then sinks back into its own darkness.

The air carries that water. It is cool here—the kind of cool that settles into the bone if you stand still too long, the kind that makes you aware of your own warmth as something separate, something the plateau would absorb if it could. Mist does not form so much as gather, materializing in the low places first, then rising as the temperature shifts through its narrow daily cycle. Mornings, the world is a white lung. By midday, the mist pulls back to the hollows and streams, clutching at the ground like something reluctant to leave. Evenings, it returns, creeping from the edges inward. There is no true dry hour. Condensation beads on every surface—on leaf and stone and the carapaces of insects, on the feathers of birds, on the fur of the small mammals that rustle through the undergrowth. You could drink from the air if you had the patience. Many things here do.

And the growth—nothing prepares you for the density of it. This is not a forest of tall boles and shaded understory, not a woodland where you can walk between the trunks and feel the architecture of the place. The vegetation here is packed, compressed, a living wall that must be negotiated rather than traversed. Sphagnum and feather moss form the ground layer, thick as bedding, springy, perpetually damp. Above that: heaths and sedges in dense tussocks, their roots intertwined so thoroughly that a hand pressed into the mass meets no gap, feels only the continuous body of the vegetation itself. Then the dwarf shrubs—crowberry, cloudberry, bearberry—growing in such profusion that their branches overlap and interlock, each bush holding its neighbors upright, the whole forming a single mass that parts only reluctantly before a traveler. And above even these, where the ground rises and the water table dips just slightly, stands of stunted birch and alder, their trunks twisted by the constant wind into shapes that look less like trees and more like the stooped and stubborn remains of something that refused to lie down. They are perhaps a hundred years old and stand no taller than a child. Their branches grow so densely that the canopy within is a separate room, a dark and dripping space where nothing else can grow and only the fungi thrive.

Fungi are everywhere. Bracket and shelf and crust, fruiting from every fallen branch, every exposed root, every dead stump that has not yet been pulled into the moss. Pale shelves of oyster mushrooms climb the birch trunks in spiral patterns. Gelatinous blobs of witches' butter seep from wounds in the bark, orange and trembling. And beneath, in the waterlogged soil, the mycelium spreads—a second root-system, older and vaster than any plant's, connecting everything in a network so fine and so extensive that to disturb one square meter is to send a signal across the entire plateau. The mushrooms glow. Not brightly—this is not some bioluminescent fantasy—but enough. In the deep hollows, in the perpetual mist, the pale fruiting bodies carry a faint phosphorescence, a cold light like the memory of a moon that set hours ago. It gives the place an odd optical quality: the darkness here is never complete, but the light it holds is not warm, not welcoming, not light as humans understand it.

The animals have learned the rules of this place, and the first rule is silence. Sound carries too well here, and there are ears everywhere—the long ears of hares, the cupped ears of foxes, the asymmetrical ears of owls whose calls you will never hear because they have learned to hunt without them. The hares are enormous, white-coated even in summer, moving through the dense vegetation on paths they have worn over generations—narrow tunnels through the shrub-layer that open suddenly into small clearings where they graze on the new growth. They watch with dark, still eyes and do not startle easily. They have few predators here, but those that exist are efficient. The foxes are small and red and move with a deliberate care that seems almost surgical. You might walk for days without seeing one, but they see you. Their scat marks every prominent stone.

Birds are the exception to the silence, but even they are quiet—thrushes with low, fluting calls that carry surprisingly far through the mist; pipits with their thin, descending songs; snipe that drum overhead with their tail-feathers, producing a sound like distant engines. Crows gather in the stunted trees and watch everything with an intelligence that feels architectural. They have learned that the plateau's visitors leave things—wrappers, tools, sometimes food—and they wait with a patience that borders on the philosophical.

But the dominant sound of the plateau is water. Not rushing, not crashing—just the continuous quiet movement of water through and over and beneath everything. The drip from leaf to leaf. The seep from stone. The slow, almost imperceptible flow through the moss itself, a sound so quiet it registers as a feeling rather than a noise, a sense of the ground breathing. And then, in the larger streams that cut the shallow valleys, a low and constant murmur, the voice of the plateau saying the same thing over and over in a language that predates meaning.

There is something else. Something that resists easy description. The plateau has a weather of its own—not merely the climate that its elevation produces, but a self-generated cycle that operates on a scale smaller than the continental weather systems but larger than mere local effect. The mist forms, thickens, moves, disperses according to patterns that do not quite match the atmospheric data. The temperature drops and rises in waves that have no apparent cause. Winds spring up from still air, carrying a smell of peat and cold stone, and die just as suddenly. It is as though the plateau's vegetation—its mosses, its mycorrhizal networks, its infinite surface of transpiring leaves—has reached such a density, such an integration, that it produces its own microclimate, breathes its own weather into existence. The water that the plants pull from the ground enters the air, condenses, falls, is pulled again. The cycle is so tight, so complete, that the plateau has become a closed system, a world that could exist independently of the larger planet that holds it.

You feel this most clearly at night, when the mist closes in and the mushrooms glow and the only sounds are the dripping and the distant murmur of the streams. The scale of the place becomes apparent then—not the scale of miles, though that is real enough, but the scale of integration. Every organism here is connected to every other. The foxes eat the hares that eat the vegetation that grows on the moss that grows on the water that seeps from the rock that holds the mycelium that connects the roots of the trees that shelter the birds that drop the seeds that start the cycle again. There is no gap, no space between presences. The plateau is a single entity, a living room with no empty corners, and you are inside it, and it does not care whether you understand or not.

Stand still long enough and the water will bead on your skin, the mist will clot in your hair, the cold will settle into your joints. The moss will begin to accept you—not as part of itself, never that, but as another surface, another substrate on which the water can gather and the spores can settle. And you will understand that the plateau has been here for longer than memory and will be here for longer than forgetting, and that your presence is a brief and irrelevant warmth in its long, damp, quiet history.

The wind shifts. The mist parts for a moment, and you see the ridge ahead, and beyond it another ridge, and beyond that another, each one darker and more distant, fading into a blue-gray that might be sky or might be more plateau. There is no way to tell. The place is too large for perspective. You walk on, and the ground gives beneath your feet, and the water rises, and the vegetation closes behind you, and the silence takes the sound of your passage and absorbs it into itself, and you are gone from the world you came from, and the plateau continues.
