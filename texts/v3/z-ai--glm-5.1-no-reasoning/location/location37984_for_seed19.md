# The Calm of the Churn

The plateau goes on forever.

Not forever in the way a desert goes on—that would imply distance, and distance requires a horizon, and the horizon here is a problem. It keeps *arriving*. You walk toward it and it is there, same as before, a pale seam where the iron-white ground meets the colorless sky. The plateau is so wide and so flat that perspective breaks down. You cannot tell if you are walking or standing still. The ground does not change under your feet because the ground has not changed in—no one knows. A geological eon. Longer. The erosion parameter is at maximum, which means this landscape has already lost everything it was going to lose. It is the settled remnant of whatever came before, stripped to its final arithmetic. Mountains that once lived here are now the flatness itself, compressed to a mineral memory centimeters thick. You walk on the ghost of a mountain range.

It is cool. Not cold—nothing here is extreme in temperature, just the steady, featureless cool of a world that has exhausted its gradients. Heat has nowhere to go and no reason to move. The air sits.

---

There is no water. This is the first thing you must understand, because it explains everything else. No water in the air, no water in the stone, no aquifers or ice lenses or hydrated minerals. The chemistry of this place finished its drying long ago, and what remains is a desiccation so complete it passes through "arid" and becomes something else—a mineral sterility, a salt-field without the salt. The ground underfoot is not sand, not dust, not anything that implies water was ever involved. It is compacted particulate, compressed by time into a surface hard and smooth as fired clay but brittle, micro-fractured in hexagonal stress patterns you can see if you squat and look closely. The fractures go down. You dropped a stone into one once and never heard it land.

Because there is no water, there is no erosion in the active sense. No rain, no freeze-thaw, no wind carrying moisture. The wind here is a dry exhalation, almost still, moving just enough to remind you that atmosphere exists. It carries no particles. It sculpts nothing. The plateau was already flat when the wind got here, and the wind accepted that. Nothing changes.

Nothing changes. The dynamic parameter is nearly zero. You could leave a footprint and return in a thousand years—some part of you rebels at this, but the mathematics of the place are insistent—and the print would be there, same depth, same edge definition. The particulate does not shift because there is no force to shift it and no lubricant to allow movement. Things stay where they are placed. This is the closest thing to a rule the world has: *stability*. Equilibrium. An old, old equilibrium, the kind that has forgotten what disequilibrium even felt like.

---

The elevation is extreme. This takes a moment to reconcile with the flatness, but it is true: you are standing more than four kilometers above sea level, if there were a sea. You are standing on the ceiling of the world. The plateau is not lowland flattened by time; it is a highland flattened by time, a continental surface that was once a chaos of peaks and valleys and has since settled, grain by grain, epoch by epoch, into this enormous flat plain suspended in the thin air. You feel the altitude in your breath. Each inhalation is slightly insufficient. You take another. It is insufficient too. The air is thin and cool and dry and your body is not designed for it.

The scale is open. You can see for—there is no good unit. Kilometers, maybe, but kilometers feel wrong here because the flatness makes distance illegible. You can see to the pale seam. You can see the features between, and there are features, and this is where the density parameter begins to show itself, because the plateau is not empty. It is *full*, but full of things that do not move.

---

Here is what fills it:

Stones. Countless stones, from pebble-size to human-size, lying on the surface as if placed there by a meticulous hand. They are not local—the particulate ground is too fine-grained to have birthed them. They are foreign, imported, *arranged*. Not in any pattern you can parse, but with a density that exceeds randomness. In any hundred-meter square you might find forty, sixty, a hundred stones, some touching, some stacked in low unstable columns, some half-buried in the micro-fractures, some sitting atop others like a population frozen mid-conversation. They vary in color: rust, manganese purple, a chalky yellow, a deep mineral black. They vary in shape but share a quality of *smoothness*, as if they had been handled. Time and the thin air have not roughened them. Nothing roughens anything here. They are as they were placed.

Here is what else fills it:

Structures. Sparse, scattered, occasional—the built parameter is low, but it is not zero. You find them at wide intervals, perhaps one per square kilometer if you are walking a straight line, and they are always the same: low walls, no higher than your knee, made from the same stones that litter the ground, fitted together without mortar, without gap, without tool marks. The walls form partial enclosures—three sides of a rectangle, perhaps, or a ring with a breach, or a corridor that leads nowhere and divides nothing. They do not shelter. They are too small and too open to shelter anything. They appear to be *gestures* of architecture, sketches of the idea of a building, placed on the plateau by hands that understood walls but not roofs.

Primitive technology. Whoever made the walls had no metal, no fired ceramics, no woven fabric—there is nothing here that suggests a technology beyond the assembly of found stone. The walls are the most complex artifacts, and they are not complex. The stones themselves, the non-local stones, may have been the tools as well as the material. You find some with facets that could be cutting edges, some with worn grooves that could be grips. But the wear is ambiguous. Time has not marked them because nothing marks anything here, so the wear must be original, must be the residue of use, must be the signature of hands that worked and arranged and then—stopped.

They stopped long ago. The structures are not ruins; they are too stable to be ruins, and there is nothing to ruin them. They are simply *finished*. Abandoned at the exact moment they were completed, preserved in that moment by the changeless air and the patient ground, still standing at knee-height, still waiting for a purpose that never arrived and now never will because the world has settled into its final state and final states do not generate purposes.

---

But the fauna.

The fauna is the problem. The fauna is what keeps you from being able to tell anyone a clean story about this place.

The fauna is *overwhelming*.

---

They come at dusk. Not dusk—the light here does not change much, the sun moves behind a perpetual film of high altitude haze—but there is a period when the light goes from "normal" to "slightly less normal," a dimming that is more a change in quality than in quantity, as if the world's resolution decreases by a small increment. And in that transition, the plateau reveals its other population.

They are insects. That is the closest word, but it is not correct.

They emerge from the fractures. You noticed the hexagonal cracks in the ground, the stress patterns, the gaps that go down. The gaps are how they enter the world. They come up from the mineral substrate in columns, in chains, in sequences—first one, then ten, then hundreds, then a flow that is almost liquid, a population so dense that the ground surface seems to shift and ripple where they emerge. They are the consistency of the place made animate: the same iron-white color as the particulate, the same brittle smoothness, the same ancient patience. They are small—most are the size of your thumbnail, some larger, some smaller—but their numbers are *extraordinary*. Within an hour of the dimming, the visible population per square meter exceeds ten thousand. Within two hours, you cannot see the ground.

They do not fly. They do not swim, crawl, or burrow—they have already emerged from the only burrows that exist. They *walk*. They walk on the particulate surface with a motion so slow and so synchronized that it resembles a single organism expanding, a tide of mineral-shelled bodies moving across the plateau in what appears to be a purposeful direction but is not—they are moving, but toward nothing, away from nothing, at a pace so glacial that you must watch for minutes to confirm any displacement at all.

They are everywhere. They are on the stones, on the walls, on each other, layered several deep in the high-traffic corridors between stones, forming ridges and channels of living bodies that reorganize themselves according to rules you cannot discern. They are silent—there is no sound of carapace on carapace, no chittering, no clicking. They move without friction. They move without the *physics* of friction. You have placed your hand among them and felt nothing, not pressure, not warmth, not texture. They move around your fingers like water around a stone, and your skin registers no contact, but you can *see* them, see their tiny limbs articulating, see their segmented bodies progressing, see their aggregate patterns shifting like weather systems across the pale plain.

You do not know what they eat. There is nothing to eat. No water, no vegetation, no organic substrate—no visible ecology that could sustain them. They are not alive in any way you understand. They are present—overwhelmingly, impossibly present—but they do not consume, do not excrete, do not reproduce visibly, do not die in any way you have observed. They emerge, they walk, they are. They are the fauna of a world that has reached its end-state, a fauna that has shed every requirement of metabolism and persists as pure *presence*, as density-as-lifestyle, as a final-form ecosystem where the only remaining operation is *existing at capacity*.

The plateau holds them. That is what it does now. It holds the stones and the walls and the insects and the light and the silence and the cool thin air and the flatness and the distance and the ancient, ancient stillness, and none of it moves toward anything, none of it resolves, none of it has anywhere else to go.

---

The weirdness is in the details.

At first you catalog them as anomalies, then as patterns, then as the texture of the place itself, inseparable from its other qualities. The horizon that arrives instead of receding. The stones that seem to have increased in number since you arrived, though you have been walking a straight line and should not be passing through zones of different density. The walls that appear in configurations you have already seen—an identical three-sided enclosure, you are certain, with the same stone in the same position at the corner, and you are certain because you drew it in the particulate with your finger and the drawing is still there because nothing changes, and now you are passing the same wall again, or a copy of it, or the same wall in a different location, or you are in the same location again—distance has become unreliable. The insects that move around your hand without touching it, that you cannot feel, that may not be fully *here*, that may be bleeding in from a substrate of reality you cannot access, the mineral underworld from which they emerge, where the physics is different, where contact is possible, where density includes touch.

The plateau is thin. This is the best way you have found to describe it: the world here is thin. Not thin in the sense of altitude—though it is high, and the air is thin in the conventional way—but thin in the ontological sense. The ground is a membrane. The fractures go down, and what is down there is not more ground, not rock and magma and the familiar deep earth, but something else, a substrate where the rules are different, where the insects are native, where the stones are native, where the strange permanence of this place originates. The plateau is a surface laid over something older and stranger, and it has worn to the point of transparency. The insects are what shows through.

You listen for sound and hear almost nothing. Your own footsteps, occasionally, when you remember to walk. A faint, unplaceable harmonic when the wind shifts, which might be the resonance of the stones or might be your perception filling the silence with pattern. The insects make no sound. The walls make no sound. The ground, when you press it, makes no sound. The world has been quiet so long it has forgotten how to be loud. Sound requires events and events require gradients and gradients require change and change ended here—

When? When did change end? You cannot find a trace of it. The erosion parameter is at its maximum: the landscape has reached its oldest stable state, its final form. Everything that could wear away has worn away. Everything that could shift has shifted. The plateau is the residue of process, the calm after an infinity of churning, and it has been calm for longer than calm should be possible. Long enough for the real to thin. Long enough for the insects to find the cracks.

---

You sit on a stone the color of manganese and you watch.

The light is "normal," which here means steady, diffuse, without source or shadow—a high-altitude overcast glow that seems to come from the air itself rather than from any sun. It does not change. It will not change. The insects move around the stone in rivers of slow purposeless articulation. The walls stand at knee height in every direction, their gaps facing angles you cannot predict. The pale seam of the horizon sits where it has always sat. You are not sure you have moved. You are not sure movement is a category this place still supports.

This is the end of the world—not with drama, not with fire or flood or the convulsion of continents, but with settling. With the long patience of entropy running to ground. With everything that ever happened here worn smooth and flat and thin and quiet and full—*full*—of the tiny mineral presences that are what remains when everything else has finished.

It is cool. It is still. It is full of things that do not need it to be otherwise.

You could stay. The plateau would hold you, too, would preserve your arrangement like it preserves the walls, would let the insects move around you in their slow billions until you became a feature, a stone, another permanent presence on the ancient flat surface of a world that has completed its becoming.

You could stay. You are not sure you have a choice. The fractures go down and the insects come up and the thinness is in you now, the sense of being a surface over something else, something mineral and patient and old, something that does not change.

The plateau goes on forever.
