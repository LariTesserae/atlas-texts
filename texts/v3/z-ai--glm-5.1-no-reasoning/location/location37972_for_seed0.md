The darkness is not an absence. It is a weight, a thick and living medium pressed against the skin. You cannot see the horizon because the horizon does not exist here; there is only the sense of vast, low-lying flatness extending in every direction, felt through the soles of the feet rather than confirmed by the eye. This is a young land, recently torn from some subterranean incubation, its features still sharp and unweathered, though you would never know it from looking. There is no looking. There is only immersion.

The heat comes first—wet, oppressive, wrapping around the body like a soaked wool blanket left in the sun. It radiates upward from the ground and downward from the canopy above, a closed system of warmth with nowhere to vent. The air tastes of iron and chlorophyll, green and mineral, thick enough to chew. Condensation is constant here, not rain exactly, though that comes too in sudden apostrophic downpours that roar through the darkness and vanish. No, the primary water is passive, ambient—the sweat of the land itself. Every surface is slick with it. Every leaf wears its own lens of moisture, magnifying what little light might exist into accidental constellations that mean nothing. You reach out and your hand comes away dripping. The air perspires.

And the sound—god, the sound. It is not one sound but a thousand, layered and competing, a wall of biological noise so dense it becomes architectural. You can almost feel the vibrations settling against your ribs, finding the resonance of your sternum. Insect noise, first: a constant subsonic drone, the whine of wings and the scrape of chitin, cycling through frequencies that seem designed to bypass the ear and drill directly into the hindbrain. Then the vocalizations—hoots, screams, chittering, the percussive thock of something hard striking something hollow, over and over, rhythm without music. Something large moves through the undergrowth to the left, and the vegetable matter protests, cracking and hissing. Something else answers from the right. The sounds do not pause or cycle; they *layer*, building and collapsing in waves of acoustic turbulence that never fully resolve into silence. This is a place where quiet has never existed, where the concept of silence is a foreign import, meaningless as snow.

The vegetation is the architecture here, since nothing else builds. It is lush beyond reason, overwhelming in its productive fury, every square inch of available space colonized by something green and hungry. You move through it rather than through air—the ferns waist-high and slick, the vines dropping from above like nooses and ladders, the broad leaves shouldering against each other for the scant photons that filter down from whatever sun might exist above the canopy's black ceiling. The growth is recent, fresh, aggressive. This is not old-growth forest with its established hierarchy and patient decomposition. This is a riot, a landrush, every species throwing itself toward every niche simultaneously. Root systems tangle and compete just below the surface, shallow and grasping, and the ground beneath your feet is soft, giving, full of hollows where water has pooled in hidden cisterns of leaf and loam. You step and sometimes you sink to the ankle. You step and sometimes a spray of warm water hits your calf from some compressed sponge of moss releasing its load. The land is not yet compacted. It is still becoming.

And through all of this—the fauna. They are everywhere, in quantities that shift the atmosphere from environment to organism. You cannot see them but you know they are there by the sound, by the movement, by the occasional brush of something many-legged against your exposed skin. The canopy overhead rustles and dips with the weight of congregations. The undergrowth parts and re-forms around the passage of bodies large and small. Something whirs past your ear—beetle or bat or something without a name—and something else skitters away from your footfall with a wet, panicked sound. There is a constant sense of being witnessed, evaluated, surrounded. The denizens of this place have no fear because they have never learned to fear; the land is too new for the introduction of caution. They move with the manic energy of creation itself, eating, mating, dying, being eaten, the whole cycle compressed into a continuous present-tense roar of biological business. The ground beneath the leaf litter shifts with the motion of things burrowing and emerging, burrowing and emerging. The air is a highway for wings and gliding membranes. The water that pools in the crotches of branches and the bowls of flowers seethes with larval life, writhing, transforming.

There is a strangeness to the place that emerges slowly, not in any single feature but in the *totality*, the sense that the rules here are slightly askew. The darkness is too complete for a simple canopy. The sound is too layered for any known ecosystem. The growth is too fast, the life too dense, the energy too concentrated. This feels like a place that should not yet exist, a landscape still in the process of being born, still wet and unformed and deciding what it wants to be. The sharp edges of the young terrain—the sudden drops where the earth has cleaved, the ridges where something has pushed up from below, the fresh exposures of pale mineral substrate glimpsed only by touch—these suggest a geological youth that conflicts with the biological maturity all around. How can so much life exist in so new a place? The answer, felt rather than articulated, is that the life *made* the place, or the place *summoned* the life, or the two emerged together in some mutual ignition that is still burning itself out. The dynamism is not secondary. It is the primary fact. This landscape is a verb, not a noun.

There are no tools here, no structures, no evidence that anything has ever paused long enough to build. And why would it? Building requires stillness, planning, a gap in the noise. Here there is only the endless churning present, the press and pull of organism against organism, the wet heat and the dark and the sound that fills every available cranny of silence and keeps filling it until silence becomes imaginary, a memory of a place that never existed. You are in it now, and it is in you—the water on your skin, the spores in your lungs, the vibrations in your bones. The land does not care about you. The land does not notice you. The land is too busy becoming.
