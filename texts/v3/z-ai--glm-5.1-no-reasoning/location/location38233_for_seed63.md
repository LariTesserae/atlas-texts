# The Brood Chamber

The heat arrives before anything else. It does not radiate—it suffuses. The air is so thick with warmth that it presses against the skin like a hand, and breathing means drawing in a substance that seems more solid than gas. There is no sun to speak of. No sky. Only a darkness so complete that the mind begins to invent shapes just to have something to hold.

This is a room. Or it was, once.

---

The walls are close—close enough that if you stood in the center and stretched your arms, you could almost touch both sides. The space is roughly the size of a cathedral nave, though the ceiling sags lower than any architect would permit, and the corners have been swallowed by something that is not quite mineral and not quite tissue. The stone—if it is stone—has the color of rust dissolved in milk: a pale, streaky orange-brown that seems to shift when you look at it directly. Veins run through it, some thin as hair, some thick as a forearm, and they pulse. Slowly. A rhythm below hearing.

The floor is not flat. It undulates in shallow waves, frozen ripples that catch the heat and hold it. Walking here means navigating ridges that have calcified into ridges, into shelves, into shallow depressions that might have been pools once, long before the water left. Now they hold only a crystalline residue—mineral salts, perhaps, or something excreted by the walls themselves. The crystals are small and sharp and they crunch underfoot, though the sound is swallowed almost immediately by the density of the air.

---

There are no plants. There has never been a plant here. This place predates cellulose, or perhaps it exists in a lineage that never discovered it. Instead, the surfaces are covered—saturated—in a layer of life that belongs to no kingdom recognized by any biologist.

It is chitin. Mostly chitin.

The walls crawl.

Not in the dramatic way of horror—the frantic skittering of insects across tile. This is slower, more deliberate, and therefore worse. The movement is peristaltic, wave-like, the way an intestine processes food or a caterpillar walks. The surface shifts, and you realize that what you took for texture is actually density: thousands upon thousands of bodies packed so tightly that they form a continuous skin. They are the size of thumbnails—flat, pale, segmented things with too many legs and no visible eyes. They press against each other, against the walls, against the ceiling, and they move in a slow, coordinated circulation that suggests purpose. Respiration. The chamber is breathing through them.

They make almost no sound. A faint click, like a fingernail tapping glass, when one adjusts its grip. A whisper of shell against shell, a friction that never builds to a rasp because each body is slightly damp—warm and tacky to the touch, like fruit skin left in the sun. The cumulative effect is a low, wet susurrus that sits at the edge of perception, like tinnitus or the memory of rainfall.

---

The heat, again. It is impossible to escape. The source is the mass itself—the biological engine of all those bodies generates a thermal output that has no vent. The chamber is sealed, has been sealed for what must be geological time, and the heat has built upon itself until the air is nearly breathless. You can feel it in the walls. You can feel it rising from the floor in slow columns. And the creatures—they seem to need it. They move faster where the heat pools, slower where it thins. They arrange themselves in formations that suggest thermoregulation, clustering thick in the depressions, thinning along the ridgelines. They are optimizing something. Processing something.

There are openings in the walls. Not doors—there are no doors here, no architecture, no intention of human scale—but gaps where the stone has pulled apart along those pulsing veins. They are the size of fists, the size of heads, and from them, more of the creatures emerge in a slow, continuous stream. Or they recede into them. The traffic is constant, directionless, and it gives the chamber the feeling of a node—a place where passages meet, where the body of the larger structure funnels its processes through a single warm chamber.

You are standing inside something's organ.

---

And then: the larger ones.

They are rare—seen only two or three at a time—but they move through the dense carpet of smaller bodies the way whales move through plankton, parting the mass by sheer presence. They are the length of a forearm, thicker around, and their chitin has darkened to the color of dried blood. Their legs are longer, slower, more deliberate. They do not crawl the walls so much as patrol them, and when one passes near, the smaller creatures flatten and go still in a way that suggests not fear but deference. Acknowledgment. The hierarchy is invisible but absolute.

The larger ones make sound. Not often—once every few minutes, perhaps—and it is a low, resonant click that drops from somewhere in the body's middle, a percussive thunk that travels through the floor and the walls and the air and the sternum simultaneously. It is not communication, or if it is, the message is simple: *I am here. This is mine. Continue.*

The smaller creatures always continue.

---

There is nothing built here. No marks, no structures, no remnants of any intelligence that would think to shape a tool or leave a record. And yet the chamber is not accidental. Its proportions are too specific, its thermal dynamics too balanced, its population too precisely regulated. It was made—or it grew—according to a logic that does not require a mind to design it. The creatures are the architecture. Their bodies are the walls' immune system, their motion the chamber's respiration, their heat its metabolism. Remove them and the room would cool, the veins would still, the stone would become merely stone. They are the mechanism by which this place remains itself.

It is ancient. The erosion patterns on the floor—those frozen ripples—suggest long cycles of wet and dry, though the wet must have been something other than water. Some solvent, some secretion, some fluid that carried minerals through the veins and left the crystal residue in the depressions when it evaporated. That process has stopped now, or paused. The chamber is in equilibrium, holding its shape the way a sand dune holds its shape: through the continuous action of the forces that made it.

---

The darkness is the hardest part. Not because of what it hides—there is nothing here that hides, nothing predatory, nothing with eyes to see you—but because of what it does to time. Without visual reference, the slow pulse of the walls becomes the only clock. The click of the larger creatures becomes the only punctuation. You cannot measure distance or direction; you can only feel the heat on your face and the faint vibration under your feet and the occasional brush of shell against your ankle when a smaller one strays from its pattern.

It does not bite. None of them bite. They simply continue their circuit—up the wall, across the ceiling, down the other side, into the gap, out of the gap, over the ridge, into the depression, around the crystal, over the ridge again. A loop that has no beginning and no end, only the perpetual middle of a process that was old before the first root broke soil anywhere in the world above.

You are not part of the process. You are not food, not threat, not relevant. You are a warm body in a room that is already warm, a slight disruption in the thermal gradients that will be corrected within seconds of your leaving. The creatures flow around your feet like water around a stone.

The chamber does not notice you.

The chamber continues.
