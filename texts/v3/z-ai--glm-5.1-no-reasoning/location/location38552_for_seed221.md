You enter through a valve that wheezes shut behind you, sealing with a pressure lock hiss that is immediately swallowed. You are now inside the Cistern.

This is not a landscape in the traditional sense; there is no sky, no horizon, no ground that was ever born of tectonic shift. Everything—the walls, the floor, the ceiling, the very air—is made. It is a total environment, a pocket of constructed reality embedded deep within the industrial guts of some incomprehensible megastructure. The scale is intimate, claustrophobic—a single, vaulted chamber perhaps thirty meters across, shaped like the interior of a cast-iron lung. There is no world outside this room; there is only the room, and it is everything.

The temperature bites immediately, a deep, penetrating cold that sinks into the marrow. It hovers just above freezing, the kind of ambient chill that bleeds from iron that has never known sunlight. And yet, this space is defined by water. It is impossibly, suffocatingly wet. The atmosphere is a dense fog, a super-saturated vapor that condenses on every surface in real-time, weeping down the riveted steel walls in parallel streams. The ceiling sweats profusely, a million silver droplets forming, swelling, and falling in a continuous, synchronized drip that never varies by a millisecond. You can feel the water near the surface of your skin, dampness pressing in on you from all sides, though you never see a pool drain away; it simply condenses and weeps, caught in a closed-loop cycle that is locked in a frozen equilibrium.

The noise is a physical weight. It is deafening, a roaring, thudding, bone-rattling cacophony of industry. Beneath the floor grates, massive pistons pump viscous fluids through armored pipes. Gears the size of dinner tables grind against one another, their teeth interlocking with shuddering force. Steam vents scream in high-pitched, eternal whistles. The sound is so immense and layered that it ceases to be heard; it is felt, a constant vibration in the back of the teeth and the sockets of the eyes. And it never changes. The rhythm is perfectly, terrifyingly static. The clanks, the hisses, the roars occur in an exact, unvarying loop. It is a mechanized heartbeat frozen in a single, eternal stroke. Time does not pass here; it just grinds.

The light is a constant, bruised twilight. Dim, sickly-yellow incandescent bulbs are recessed behind heavy wire cages in the ceiling, casting a feeble glow that barely penetrates the thick vapor. Shadows are deep and permanent. There are no gradients of light, no flickering, no dawn or dusk. The illumination is stuck at a perpetual, struggling minimum.

In this unyielding, entirely artificial freezing purgatory, life should be impossible. But the fauna is overwhelming. The surfaces—every pipe, every rivet, every inch of dripping mesh—is covered in a dense, layered biomass of pale, chitinous creatures. They are the color of uncooked shrimp and wet concrete, blind and segmented, like a cross between deep-sea isopods and maggots, ranging from the size of a grain of rice to the length of a forearm. They cling to the walls in near-touching densities, thousands of them, layered three or four deep in places. They do not move from their spots. They do not hunt. They simply exist, their mandibles working rhythmically in sync with the deafening machines, feeding on the condensation, filtering the metallic vapor, absorbing the ambient vibration. The floor grates squelch underfoot—not with mud, but with the wet, brittle crush of these creatures. Their shells crack and pop, but the population is so dense, so utterly saturated, that a step taken is instantly filled again by the shifting, pale mass. They emit a foul, metallic heat that only barely cuts the freezing damp, a sickly warmth emanating from their writhing, static masses.

Vegetation is utterly absent. There is no green, no chlorophyll, no softness. Only bare, painted steel, sweating iron, and the pale, writhing carpet of industrial fauna.

The strangest aspect of the Cistern is its absolute stasis. The water falls, the machines roar, the creatures feed, but nothing actually happens. It is a landscape under construction that has abandoned the concept of completion. It is embryonic, frozen forever in the act of becoming, yet never changing. The water that falls from the ceiling seems to hit the ground and instantly evaporate back into the fog, a perfect, perpetual cycle with no entropy. The gears grind but show no wear. The blind things cling and feed but do not grow or die. It is a pocket of reality caught in a deadlock, an industrial diorama sealed in amber, repeating its single, deafening moment forever.
