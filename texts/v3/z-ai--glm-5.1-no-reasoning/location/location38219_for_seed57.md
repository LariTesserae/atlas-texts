You are inside a seed.

Not a seed as a metaphor, and not a seed as a human hand might hold one—pinched between finger and thumb, dry and dormant. You are inside a seed that has never known air. A seed the size of a small room, or a coffin, or the interior of a bell—dimensions that only approximate because here, measurement is one of the first laws to dissolve.

The world is water. Not ocean, not lake, not any body with a shore or a floor. Water as a condition of existence. Water so total it precludes the concept of wetness—there is nothing dry to contrast, no surface where water ends and something else begins. The liquid is cool, 8 degrees or the memory of 8 degrees, and it presses against your skin with the patience of something that has never been interrupted. It does not rush. It does not pool. It simply *is*, the way stone *is* in a cave that no one has entered.

And the seed floats within it. Or the water fills the seed. Both statements are true, which is your first indication that the walls of this place are not quite walls.

---

The interior is packed. Saturated. Every millimeter of space is occupied, and this is where the vegetation lives—because there is vegetation, impossible and lush, pressed so close you would have to breathe it if you breathed here. Pale tendrils, the color of veins seen through skin, spiral outward from the seed's curved inner wall. They are ferns, maybe. Or root-hairs. Or the lungs of something that has never needed to inhale. They branch and fork with the obsessive intricacy of fractals, each tendril spawning smaller tendrils which spawn smaller still, filling the water with a architecture so dense it becomes a medium of its own. You could push through it, but slowly. You would displace it. It would close behind you.

The fronds—not quite leaves, not quite fingers—carry no green. In this darkness, chlorophyll would be a joke told to no one. Instead they are translucent, opalescent, catching light that does not exist and scattering it in faint prismatic threads that might be bioluminescence or might be your eyes inventing coherence from noise. When you focus on any single tendril, its detail is staggering: the ridged surface, the pulse of fluid moving through its core, the way it curves with mathematical precision toward... toward what? There is no sun here. No chemical gradient. It curves toward the center of the seed, where the water is thickest, where something is beginning.

The vegetation breathes. Not the slow expansion and contraction of lungs, but a wave of density that passes through the mass—a peristalsis of the whole. You feel it as a pressure change, a subtle thickening and thinning of the medium against your eardrums. It happens once every several minutes. Or seconds. Time is acting strangely; more on that later.

---

The seed's wall is close—you can reach it in four strokes, or seven, depending on which direction you choose, and each direction yields a different distance, which is how you know the space is not shaped like any room you've left behind. The wall is organic. Fibrous. It has the texture of pomegranate membrane stretched taut, or the inner lining of an eggshell before the chick has pecked through. When you touch it, it gives slightly, warm despite the cool water, and you feel your own pulse reflected back at you from the surface, delayed by half a second, as if the wall is learning your rhythm.

There are marks on the wall. Not scratches, not carvings—stains, perhaps, or deposits left by something that was here before you. They are faint, rust-colored, abstract. You cannot tell if they are remnants of construction or natural patterns in the fiber. They do not repeat. They do not form symbols. But they feel intentional, the way a scar feels intentional even when it's accidental. Someone, or something, pressed itself against this wall and left a trace. The scattered architecture of a presence that did not build so much as *mark*.

You find one such mark that is different from the others: a small hollow, the size of a fingertip, where the wall has been worn thin enough to glow. Not with light, exactly. With *possibility*. Looking into the hollow is like looking into a hole in a mask—there is something on the other side, something that moves at a different speed, something that breathes a different medium. You do not look for long.

---

The sounds are nearly absent, and this is what makes the few sounds unbearable. The vegetation creaks. Not the groan of wood under stress but a high, quiet sound, like a finger circling the rim of a crystal glass, pitched just above the threshold of deliberate hearing. It happens when the peristaltic wave passes through, when the tendrils compress and release, and it means the whole structure is under strain—constant, patient strain, the strain of growing into a space that is already full.

There is another sound. Lower. Coming from the center of the seed, or the center of the water, or the center of wherever you are when the boundaries have stopped making sense. It is a hum. Not mechanical—there is no machine here, has never been a machine, will never be a machine. It is biological. It is the sound of something very large doing something very slow. The hum never starts and never stops; it was here before you arrived, and it will be here after you leave, and the realization settles over you that this sound is not *in* the water but *is* the water, the frequency at which this particular volume of liquid chooses to exist.

Occasionally—rarely, and without pattern—there is a click. Sharp. Biological. Like a shrimp snapping in a reef tank, or a joint popping in a quiet room. It comes from the vegetation. Something moves in the vegetation. Not through it, but *in* it, part of it, embedded like a nerve in a tooth. You have not seen it. You will not see it. But you feel the displacement when it shifts, a small pressure wave rolling against your skin, and you know that you are not alone in the seed.

The fauna, such as it is, is rare enough to be theoretical. A single creature, perhaps. Or the vegetative mass itself is the creature, and the movement you sense is its thought, transmitted through tendril and water as a trout transmits its presence through a pond. You do not know. You cannot know. The information does not exist in a form your cognition can process, and this is where the reality begins to break down.

---

Time is not passing correctly. This is not a poetic observation. This is a structural feature of the place. The peristaltic wave that moves through the vegetation—the breath, the compression—should mark regular intervals, but it does not. Sometimes it comes quickly, twice in the span of a held breath. Sometimes the wait between waves is so long you forget to count, forget that counting was something you once did, forget that there was a world where seconds followed seconds with the obedience of soldiers.

You try to track time by your own pulse, but your pulse has synchronized with the hum. You try to track it by the growth of the tendrils, but you realize, with a vertigo that has no direction, that the tendrils are not growing. They are *remembering*. They are occupying positions they have always occupied, revealing themselves to you gradually, as a photograph develops in a darkroom. The lushness was always here. The density was always here. You are only now perceiving it, and the perception arrives in layers, each one revealing more architecture, more complexity, more of the impossible fullness that fills this small, small space.

How small? You have swum the perimeter. Four strokes, or seven. The seed is no larger than a room. But the interior is infinite in the way a lung is infinite—folded, creased, surfaced with exchange. The tendrils create depth without distance. You could spend a lifetime cataloging a single frond and not reach its end, because it branches and branches and branches, each generation smaller, each generation more detailed, descending toward a limit that is not a point but a *threshold*, a place where matter becomes information and information becomes something you do not have a word for.

---

The water is thickening.

You notice it gradually—how the resistance against your limbs has increased, how the tendrils seem closer than they were, how the wall of the seed has receded or you have moved toward the center without deciding to. The water is not becoming viscous. It is becoming *attentive*. It is noticing you. The coolness on your skin is no longer the impartial coolness of a medium but the focused coolness of a gaze, and the pressure is no longer the pressure of depth but the pressure of *interest*.

The hum is louder. Or you are closer to its source. Or your ears have finally learned to hear what was always being said.

At the center of the seed—if center is the word, and it isn't—there is a density. Not an object. Not a creature. A *concentration*, the way a lens concentrates light: not by adding anything but by aligning what is already there. The water is thicker at the center. The tendrils converge. The marks on the wall, the rust-colored stains, they all point inward, and you realize they are not stains at all but *paths*, worn by something that has made this journey before you, something that pressed itself through the same vegetation, felt the same cool, heard the same hum, and moved toward the center because moving toward the center was the only direction this place allowed.

The vegetation breathes. The hum continues. The water thickens.

And you understand, with the calm certainty of a dream, that the seed is not a container. The seed is a *process*. You are inside an event that is happening—slowly, patiently, across durations that dwarf your little catalog of minutes and hours. The seed is germinating. Not in soil, not toward sun, but in a direction that has no name in any language you once spoke. The lushness is the germ. The density is the germ. You, with your heat and your pulse and your strange dry curiosity, are the germ.

The water is not flooding in. The water has always been here. You are the thing that fell into it, the stray mote of carbon and pattern that the seed has drawn into its folds, adding to its mass, incorporating into its breath. The click in the vegetation was acknowledgment. The marks on the wall were the signatures of those who came before—scarce, rare, one per century perhaps, each one absorbed and recorded and folded into the architecture.

You are not drowning. Drowning requires air, requires the expectation of air, requires a body that knows what it is missing. Here, there is only water, only the cool and the dark and the slow opalescent spiral of tendrils closing around you like a thought completing itself.

The hum resolves into a note. The note has no name. It is the sound a seed makes when it decides to become.
