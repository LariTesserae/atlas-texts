The world is still being born, and the birth is deafening.

You stand on a ridge that should not yet exist—a spine of raw, obsidian-sharp schist that heaves itself upward at a rate you can almost measure, half a centimeter of new stone thrust into the cooling air each hour. This is a geology of impatience, of processes that should take millennia compressed into durations measurable by watch. The elevation is extreme; the air thins to almost nothing, and yet there is no wind—only the groaning, shrieking, sub-harmonic bellow of stone folds compressing and releasing, a tectonic groan that vibrates in the marrow of your long bones. It is a sound without a source, or rather, the source is everywhere: the entire region is a throat, trying to speak something into existence and failing, producing only this ceaseous, oceanic roar.

There is no water. None. Not a drop of moisture in the atmosphere, not a trace of hydrogen-oxygen bonding to be found. The air itself is a different chemistry—nitrogen, carbon dioxide, trace sulfurs, and something else, something that tastes like copper and static and the mineral ghost of what might once have been an ocean but has long since dissociated into its component atoms. This should be death. This should be a void.

And yet the place is lush.

The vegetation is impossible. It is lush in the way a fever dream is lush—saturated, excessive, wrong. Great colonies of… things… colonize every surface, every crack, every new face of stone as it emerges from the mantle below. They are not plants. They have no chlorophyll, no cellulose, no need for water. They are crystallo-organic structures, half mineral and half something else—something that metabolizes the stone itself, that draws sustenance from the slow release of silicate bonds under pressure. They grow in fractal spirals, in branching candelabra, in sheets and ruffles and cathedral-arched ribbing that spreads across the naked rock like frozen green fire.

Their color shifts depending on angle: from directly above, they are a deep, bruised emerald; from the side, they flash a volatile turquoise; and where the thinnest edges catch the dim light, they go nearly white, almost transparent, like the skin of a newborn. They are cool to the touch—not cold, but cool, the cool of something that has found a way to live in the gap between warmth and void.

And the light. The light is almost not there. A diffuse gray phosphorescence seems to emanate from no single point—perhaps from the vegetation itself, perhaps from the stone, perhaps from the strained geometry of the air. The sky, if there is a sky, is an occluded band of darker gray, folded like felt, pressing down on the peaks with a weight that has nothing to do with atmosphere. You cannot see the sun. You cannot tell if there is a sun. The light simply *is*, a weak ambient presence, enough to see by if your eyes adjust, enough to make the crystal-vegetation glow with its strange internal luminescence, but not enough to cast shadows. Nothing casts a shadow here. The light comes from everywhere and nowhere.

The scale is open—you can see for kilometers, the valley-scarred terrain rolling outward in a series of nested ridges and cut-gorge defiles, each one thicker with the crystal growths than the last. But the density is sparse. Between each great colony of the vegetative mineral, there is raw, naked, still-warm rock, dark and sharp and utterly barren. The space between presences is enormous. You could walk for an hour across the new stone and see nothing living, then crest a rise and find an entire cliff face draped in the branching spirals, thick as a forest, silent and slow and radiant in the dimness.

There are no animals. No fauna at all. No insects, no birds, no skittering things in the cracks. The sound is all geological—all that tectonic groaning, the crack and settle of cooling stone, the high thin whine of crystal lattices expanding in the cool dim air. And beneath it, lower, a subsonic pulse that you feel in your teeth rather than hear—a rhythm like a heartbeat, like the world's own slow pulse, one beat per minute, per hour, per some interval your body recognizes but your mind cannot name.

There are structures. Scattered. You come upon them unexpectedly—a single low dome of fitted stone on a high ridge, its doorway facing a direction that no longer exists, its interior empty except for a fine dust that might be ash or might be something more disquieting. Another: a half-wall of mortared schist, built into a cliff face, its remaining stones shaped with a precision that suggests hand-tools, simple metal, a patient craft. Whoever made these had no advanced technology. They fit stones together. They made walls. They sought shelter in a place that seems to offer none.

But they are gone. And the structures remain only as traces—partial remains, occasional artifacts, marks on the world that say *someone was here* and nothing more. The crystal growths have colonized some of them, spreading across the mortared walls in slow-motion conquest, incorporating the worked stone into their fractal architectures. In a thousand years—perhaps a hundred—there will be nothing left but the crystal, and the stone, and the sound.

And then there is the weirdness.

It builds slowly. At first, you notice only small things: the way the crystal growths seem to lean toward you when you are not looking, the way the sound seems to form almost-words in the upper registers, the way your shadow—despite the shadowless light—occasionally falls in the wrong direction. Then it escalates. The geometry of the ridges begins to hurt your eyes; you look at a valley and it has too many edges, or the angles between the peaks sum to something other than what they should, and your mind slides off the numbers like water off glass. The stone beneath your feet feels thin—impossibly thin, as though the crust is only a membrane, and below it something vast is breathing.

The air shivers. Not from heat, not from motion—from *attention*. The place is aware. Not in any way you can parse, not as an animal is aware or even as you are aware, but in the way a lung is aware of air, the way a throat is aware of sound. The landscape is a process, and the process is trying to complete itself, and you are in the way, or perhaps you are part of the completion, or perhaps the distinction has ceased to matter.

The crystal growths are moving now—not growing, but *shifting*, their branches reorienting with a slow vegetative intent, all of them, across the entire visible valley, tilting toward the same point in the dark gray sky. The sound changes pitch. The subsonic pulse quickens, just slightly—once every fifty-nine seconds now, once every fifty-eight—and the stone beneath your feet gives a lurch that has nothing to do with tectonics and everything to do with the fact that the world is trying to fold itself into a new shape and has not yet decided whether you will be inside or outside the crease.

This is an embryonic place. A still-forming place. The rules are not yet fixed. The geology writes and rewrites itself; the crystal-vegetation metabolizes contradictions; the sound is the sound of a landscape arguing with itself about what it will become. The scattered ruins say that others have stood here before, have watched the birth, have perhaps been incorporated into it—their stone walls consumed by the crystal, their bodies perhaps dissolved into the copper-tasting air, their presence remembered only as a faint mineral trace in the lattice of something that is neither plant nor mineral nor alive nor dead but all of these at once, and none.

The cool air presses against your skin. The dim light presses against your eyes. The sound presses against your skull. And below, above, around, the world continues to come into being—slowly, loudly, and wrong.
