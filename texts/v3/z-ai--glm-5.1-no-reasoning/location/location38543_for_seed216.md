# The Canopy Empty

There is no ground.

Or rather, the ground is so far below that it has become a theory, a rumor, a thing the oldest inhabitants speak of with the hesitation of people describing a dream they are not certain they had. What you stand on—and you do stand, on something—is the back of a living architecture so old it has forgotten it was made.

---

The substrate is plant. This is the first and final fact. Everything you touch, everything that holds weight, everything that separates one volume of air from another is cellulose and lignin, bark and heartwood, phloem and cambium. The constructed world—the ancient, total built environment—was not consumed by the green. It *became* the green. The lattice-work of whatever vast civilization preceded this one was threaded with seed and spore so thoroughly, so completely, that the metal and stone are now only trace minerals inside trunks the width of cathedral naves. The buildings grew. The roads root-knitted themselves into continuous ridges. The walls put out leaves. And then the growing did not stop.

Now there is only the plant, and what the plant has chosen to become.

---

You are high. The air tells you this before anything else does—it is thin, but warm, carrying the particular thickness of oxygen that photosynthesis on a planetary scale produces. You breathe the exhalation of a world-sized lung. The elevation is so extreme that the curvature of the planet is faintly visible on clear days—or would be, if there were ever clear days. There are not. The atmosphere here condenses constantly. Moisture precipitates out of nothing, out of the dim air itself, because at this altitude the temperature differential between the living mass below and the cold upper air creates a perpetual dew point. You are inside a cloud that has learned to stand still.

Water beads on every surface. It does not rain, exactly. The rain happened long ago, somewhere below, and this is merely the after-breath of it—condensation forming and reforming, a skin of wetness that never fully evaporates because the light is too dim to carry it away. The bark you walk on is slick. The broadleaf platforms that serve as catch-basins are always half-full. Water runs in capillary channels carved by the plants themselves, following xylem-highways down through the structure toward roots you will never see. You hear nothing of it. The water moves too slowly, too thinly, to make sound.

That is the other fact: silence.

---

The silence is not emptiness. The silence is *volume*. This place is so vast that sounds are swallowed before they can reach any boundary to echo against. A branch the length of a river snaps somewhere in the dim green distance, and you feel the vibration through your feet before you register any sound—and the sound, when it arrives, is a low subsonic pulse more felt than heard. The structure you inhabit is continent-scale. Weather forms inside it—actual weather, cells of pressure and temperature that bloom into fog-lifts or brief condensation-falls. The canopy-sky above you, which is only another layer of the same living architecture, is high enough that its own clouds form beneath it, pale wisps in the perpetual amber gloom.

And yet for all this immensity, there is almost nothing here. The space between presences is staggering. You can walk for hours across a single branch-road—walking is possible because the surface has been worn by time and by the passage of creatures into something like a path, something like a floor—and encounter only the branch, the moisture, the faint breathing of the wood beneath your feet. The density of living mass is total, but the density of *presences* is near-zero. You are alone in the way that a person standing on a vast empty plain is alone, except the plain is alive and horizontal and vertical and everything in between.

---

The fauna changes this, but strangely.

There are animals. Many animals. You see them in the dimness, or you see the evidence of them: the scored bark where something with too many claws has climbed, the seed-husks split open by beaks you never find, the iridescent frass of insect colonies layered thick in the crotches of branches. At night—which is only a deepening of the dimness, since the light here never truly rises past a perpetual dusk—you hear them, or rather you feel them: the weight of a climbing body on the branch you share, the displacement of air when something large moves through the fog nearby. They do not call. They do not cry. The ecosystem operates in a silence so thorough it feels like a law of physics.

The animals are not afraid of you. They are not unafraid. They regard you the way the plant regards you—with an indifference that borders on the ontological. You are a fact of the environment in the way a stone is a fact, except you are softer and you move and you require water they can smell on your skin. Some follow you at a distance. Some approach and then lose interest. Some are consumed by others while you watch, the predation happening so quietly and so completely that it seems more like a transaction than a violence—a transfer of mass from one body to another, one form to another, in a system where everything is already everything else.

---

The weirdness reveals itself slowly.

At first, you notice only that the geometry is wrong. The branches curve in ways that wood should not curve—spirals that double back on themselves, knots that open into chambers, surfaces that seem to face two directions at once. You put it down to the age of the structure, the eons of growth and weathering, but it is more than that. The plant is not growing *in* space. The plant is growing *space*. The architecture—remember that everything is built, everything is made—has been modified by its own living substrate into configurations that do not respect the plane you stand on. You walk along a horizontal branch and find, without any transition, that you are walking vertically, or upside-down, and your body does not fall because the relationship between mass and the living surface has been rewritten. Gravity here is a property of the wood, not of the world.

In the deeper zones—the oldest sections, where the bark is as thick as cliff-faces and the condensation hangs in curtains rather than drops—you find the chambers. The plant has made rooms. Not hollows, not cavities: *rooms*, with walls and ceilings and floors, all grown from the same continuous tissue, all slightly wrong in their proportions in a way that makes your eyes ache. Something built the seed from which this grew. Something designed a structure that, when activated by time and water and light, would produce *this*—this infinite labyrinth of living rooms, this cathedral that is also a forest that is also a machine whose purpose has been lost.

In the deepest chamber you find, the condensation does not bead. It writes. Moisture forms on the walls in patterns that resolve, after long staring, into script—or something like script, something that might be language if language could be three-dimensional and could grow and could include the reader as one of its phonemes. You touch the writing and it is warm, warmer than the ambient air, and the wood beneath your fingers pulses with a rhythm that is not a heartbeat but might be counting.

Something is still running here.

---

The water knows where to go. The animals know where to go. You do not. You follow the capillary channels downward, or what you think is downward, and the plant accommodates you—it always accommodates you, with a surface to walk on, a gentle curve, a handhold where you need one. It was built for movement. It was built for bodies. But the bodies it was built for may not have been yours, and the movement it was built for may not have been walking, and the purpose of the accommodation may not have been comfort.

You are inside a mechanism that has outlasted its makers by so long that the mechanism itself has become the ecosystem, has become the geology, has become the weather. It does not remember being made. It does not need to. It grows. It breathes. It waters itself. It feeds its animals. It opens its chambers and writes its condensation-script on walls that are also ribs and also roots and also the last evidence that someone, once, decided to build something that would never, ever stop.

The landscape is a garden that ate the gardener.

The landscape is a building that learned to breathe.

The landscape is waiting, with infinite patience, with the patience of wood and water and time, for whatever it was designed to do.

And you are inside it, small and soft and temporary, walking on its back, reading its walls, breathing its exhalation, becoming—in the slow way that water becomes the shape of its container—a part of the machine.
