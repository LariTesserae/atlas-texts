# The Drowned Plateau of Kesh-Toral

## What can be seen from the surface

There is no surface. Or rather — the surface is a fiction, a membrane stretched taut over six hundred meters of living water, and it cannot hold. Storm light comes through in gray-green shafts, bent, heavy with particulate. It is enough to see by. It is not enough to see far.

This is a high place. That fact persists even underwater. The plateau sits at what was once three thousand eight hundred meters above sea level — a subalpine shelf of basalt and compressed ash, rimmed by the broken teeth of older mountains. The flood did not erase the elevation. It buried it. Water stacked on water until the pressure found its own logic, and now the plateau is simultaneously the highest thing for a hundred kilometers in any horizontal direction and the lowest thing in the vertical one. A ceiling that is also a floor. Depth and height cancel into something the body reads as a low, continuous hum in the sternum.

The cold is not the lethal kind. It sits around nine degrees Celsius, year-round, season-less. It is the cold of a cave, of a root cellar, of a spring that never saw the sun. It does not bite. It soaks. After an hour in the water — and everything here is in the water, including the air, which is so saturated that breathing feels like drinking — the cold enters the joints and stays there, patient, administrative. Nothing about it is hostile. It is simply the temperature this place has decided to be, and it has decided this for a very long time.

## The age of it

The plateau is old. Not old the way mountains are old, which is a geological joke told in increments of millions of years. Old the way a riverbed is old — worn down, smoothed, reduced to its essential argument. The basalt has been ground to pudding stone. The ash strata have liquefied into clay bands that drift in the current like the flesh of something half-decayed. Everything sharp has been rounded. Everything vertical has been persuaded toward horizontal. The plateau's original cliffs — which must have been dramatic, must have been sheer and dark and plunging — are now long slopes, graded by ten thousand years of water movement into something almost gentle, almost reasonable, as if the landscape sat down slowly and never stood back up.

There are places where the rock has been so thoroughly worked by water and time that it has become something between stone and sediment and mud — a material that holds its shape under pressure but slumps when disturbed. The entire plateau has the feel of a thing at the end of a very long exhale. Settled. Not dying — past dying. Past living, too. Into some third state that is simply persistence.

And yet it is not still. Nothing here is still.

## The water and its movement

The water moves in systems. Not currents — systems. The plateau is broad, perhaps forty kilometers at its widest, and the water above it does not behave like ocean water. It behaves like atmosphere. There are thermoclines that function like cloud layers, where the temperature drops two or three degrees in the space of a meter and the water goes from clear to milky. There are currents that spiral — not linear flows but gyres, hundreds of meters across, turning slowly, carrying debris and sediment and living things in great lazy loops that take hours to complete. There are downdrafts where cold water sinks along the old mountainsides and upwellings where warmer water rises through fissures in the basalt, carrying minerals and heat from sources far below.

The result is a body of water in constant internal argument. Layers slide against each other. Boundaries blur and reform. The water is rarely clear — it carries silt, plankton, dissolved organic material, the fine particulate residue of ten millennia of slow erosion. Visibility is a negotiation. On a good day, maybe thirty meters. On a bad day, less than five. The water is not dirty. It is *full*.

And it is loud.

Not loud the way a storm is loud, though there are storms. Loud the way a city is loud — a composite, layered, directional sound with no single source. The currents grind sediment against stone. The upwellings hiss as dissolved gases come out of solution. The thermoclines creak — there is no other word for it — as layers of different density press against each other and flex. Schools of fish move through the water with a sound like fabric tearing, the collective displacement of thousands of bodies pushing water aside in unison. Kelp stalks groan at their base where they flex in the surge. Something — it is never clear what — clicks and pops in the rocky substrate, a constant percussive chatter that might be crustaceans, might be gas bubbles, might be the stone itself adjusting to pressure changes.

The loudest sound is the one that never stops: a low, subsonic drone that seems to come from the plateau itself. It is the sound of water moving through rock. The basalt is porous — was porous even before the flood, is more porous now — and the entire plateau functions as a sponge, a filter, a slow machine through which the water above and the water below trade places. The water in the fissures moves at velocities that would be invisible if they didn't generate pressure waves, and those pressure waves combine into a continuous vibration that lives in the chest more than the ears. It is the sound of the place breathing. It never stops. You stop hearing it after a while, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat, and then it comes back, and it is enormous.

## What grows here

The vegetation is the first thing that contradicts the age, the cold, the everything.

It is lush. Aggressively, absurdly lush. The plateau is carpeted in growth — not the sparse, slow growth of deep water, but a dense, competitive, almost tropical profusion that has no business being this far from sunlight. The kelp is the dominant form. Giant kelp, or something very like it — stalks as thick as a human thigh, rising thirty meters from the substrate to the surface in continuous columns, their fronds spreading at the top into broad canopies that filter the storm light into a green-gold dapple. The kelp grows fast, faster than it should in this cold, and it is constantly dying and being replaced. The substrate is a compost of old fronds, half-rotted holdfasts, and the fine silt that results from their decomposition. The floor is soft. It gives underfoot. It is alive in the way that soil is alive, and it smells — if you could smell underwater, and in a sense you can, through the skin, through the tongue — of decomposition and growth in equal measure.

Between the kelp columns, other things grow. Sea grasses that have adapted to the cold, their blades dark green, almost black, moving in the current like hair. Sponges in colors that seem wrong for the depth — orange, red, a blue so vivid it appears to generate light. They encrust every available surface: rock, dead kelp, the remains of structures. Bryozoans build lacy colonies on the kelp stalks, and the kelp tolerates this, or cannot prevent it, and the colonies add their own architecture to the vertical forest until each column is a composite organism, a living tower with a dozen species contributing to its structure.

The growth is not random. It is dense, layered, competitive. The kelp canopy captures most of the light, and beneath it the undergrowth thins, adapts, finds niches. There are zones — a mid-layer where red algae dominates, using the blue-green light that penetrates past the canopy; a lower zone where the substrate is dominated by bivalves and tubeworms; a floor zone where the decomposition happens, where the detritivores work, where the cycle completes. It is an ecosystem in the full sense, self-sustaining, self-regulating, ancient enough to have reached something like equilibrium but dynamic enough to never actually be in equilibrium. The kelp grows and dies and grows. The sponges encrust and are consumed and encrust again. The whole system breathes.

## What lives here

The fauna is abundant in the way that the vegetation is lush — more than you would expect, more than the cold and the depth and the turbidity would seem to allow. The explanation, if there is one, is the upwellings. The mineral-rich water rising through the basalt feeds the phytoplankton, which feeds the zooplankton, which feeds everything else, and the plateau functions as an oasis, a nutrient trap, a place where the water's internal movements concentrate life.

The fish are the most visible. They move in schools that number in the tens of thousands — silver, dense, responsive to the currents in ways that suggest they are not swimming so much as being swum, their collective intelligence reading the water's movements and adjusting as a single body. The schools move through the kelp forest like weather systems, darkening the water, then gone, then back. Individual fish — the larger predators, the solitary hunters — move between the columns with a deliberation that feels almost architectural, as if they are navigating a city they know by heart.

There are other things. Crustaceans — crabs, mostly, in sizes from thumbnail to dinner plate — that occupy every crevice and crack in the rock. They are the source of much of the clicking, and they are territorial, aggressive, constantly fighting over the small patches of substrate where food collects. Octopuses, or something like them — intelligent, curious, colored to match whatever surface they rest on, visible only when they move. They live in the kelp, in the rock piles, in the remains of the built structures. They are the plateau's other engineers, rearranging the environment to suit themselves, moving stones and shells and debris to build dens that are as complex as anything the human builders left behind.

There are larger things. It is unclear what. The turbidity of the water and the scale of the plateau mean that something could be fifteen meters away and invisible, and the sounds — the deep, rhythmic pulses that occasionally join the background drone — suggest something large enough to displace significant water with each movement. These sounds are infrequent. They do not repeat on any schedule. They might be geological. They might not be.

## What was built

The structures are scattered across the plateau like the remains of a civilization that built sparingly and without ambition. There are perhaps twenty sites visible — or partially visible — across the plateau's breadth, and they share a common character: small, functional, industrial. These were not homes or temples or monuments. They were facilities. Processing plants, perhaps, or pumping stations, or the mechanical infrastructure of an industry that required water and stone and elevation and not much else.

The largest remaining structure is a building — or the skeleton of a building — that sits near the plateau's eastern edge, where the slope begins its long descent toward the abyssal floor below. It was rectangular, perhaps forty meters on a side, three stories high, built from local basalt cut into blocks and mortared with something that has since dissolved into a calcium carbonate haze. The walls still stand, mostly, but they are porous now, hollowed by the water, and they are so thoroughly colonized by sponges and bryozoans and algae that the original surface is visible only in patches, where a cleaner organism — a sea urchin, a chiton — has scraped the growth away to feed on it, revealing the dark stone beneath, and even that stone is soft, eroded, its edges rounded to the point where the blocks look more like loaves of bread than masonry.

Inside the building — and it is still possible to go inside, though the doorways have become low tunnels hung with growth — there are the remains of machinery. Iron, mostly, or something that was iron and is now a oxide-brown aggregate that holds its shape only because the marine growth encasing it provides structural support. The machines are unrecognizable. They were tanks, perhaps, or boilers, or vessels for some process that required heat and pressure, and they have been inert for so long that their original function is irrecoverable. They have become part of the substrate. Fish live in them. Crabs nest in the joints. The kelp has found the nutrient-rich sediment that collects around their bases and has colonized it, so that the building is now ringed by a particularly dense stand of giant stalks, their holdfasts anchored in the industrial decay.

A pipeline — or the remains of one — runs from the building toward the plateau's center, where it disappears into the silt. It was perhaps a meter in diameter, made of riveted iron, and it is now a low ridge on the plateau floor, collapsed in sections, intact in others, colonized by the same community of organisms that colonizes everything. In the intact sections, water moves through the pipe — not the original flow, which ended long ago, but the tidal and current-driven movement of the water itself, finding the path of least resistance through the plateau's internal plumbing. The pipe has become, in effect, a secondary circulatory system, and the organisms that live in it are different from those outside — paler, slower, adapted to the lower oxygen and reduced light.

There are other sites. A tower — or the base of one, perhaps ten meters of what was once something taller, now a stump of crumbling concrete and rebar. A series of rectangular basins, cut into the basalt, that might have been settling ponds or holding tanks. A platform of stone and metal that juts from the western slope, its purpose unclear, its surface so eroded that the original level is uncertain. A road — the ghost of a road — visible as a slightly raised, slightly straighter line in the substrate, where the compacted material has resisted erosion just enough to remain a topographic feature. The road runs between two of the larger sites and then continues toward the plateau's edge, where it vanishes over the slope into deeper water.

No one built here recently. The structures have the quality of things that were made by people who understood they were building in a difficult place and did not intend to build twice. They are sturdy. They were well-engineered. And they have been underwater for so long that the water has become their primary architect, dissolving the original design and replacing it with its own — a design of rust and colonization, of smooth surfaces and rounded edges, of structures that serve functions their builders never intended. The building is now a reef. The pipeline is a tunnel. The road is a ridge. The tower is a marker. The plateau has absorbed them the way a body absorbs a splinter — slowly, completely, without urgency.

## The weather

The storms come from above, which is to say they come from the surface, which is to say they are the surface's problem until they are not.

When a storm passes overhead — and they pass frequently, the plateau sits in a band of persistent weather — the surface water churns, and the churning propagates downward. At ten meters, the effect is direct: the water moves in all directions at once, visibility drops to zero, the kelp is bent and tormented, fish shelter in the substrate. At thirty meters, the effect is translated into pressure changes and current shifts, a roughening, an instability that the ecosystem reads as a kind of alert. At sixty meters — the depth of the plateau's central basin — the storm is felt as a slow, rhythmic compression, a change in the quality of the water's movement, as if the entire body of water has taken a deep breath and is holding it.

The storms are loud. The surface turbulence transmits as a low-frequency rumble that adds to the plateau's constant drone, and during the worst storms — the ones that last days, that cover the entire region in wind and rain and wave action — the sound becomes a single sustained note, a vibration that enters the bones and stays. The kelp stalks hum at their resonant frequency. The structures vibrate. The water itself seems to thicken, to become denser, as if the storm is pressing down on it, compacting it.

Between storms, there is a different kind of turbulence. The internal movements — the gyres, the thermocline shifts, the upwellings — create a constant, lower-level chaos that is less dramatic but more persistent. The water is never still. The kelp is never still. The sediment drifts, settles, drifts again. The boundary between the plateau's water and the deeper water below shifts constantly, and the organisms that live at that boundary — the ones adapted to the specific temperature and chemistry of the interface — are always moving, always adjusting, always re-establishing themselves in the new conditions.

The plateau is not a place that settles. It is a place that has settled, in the geological sense — it has reached its final form, its oldest stable state — but that form is motion. The erosion has not stopped. The water has not stopped. The life has not stopped. What has stopped is the change. The plateau will not become something else. It will continue to be this — this specific arrangement of stone and water and growth and decay and sound — until something fundamental changes, and nothing fundamental is changing. The storms will come and go. The kelp will grow and die. The structures will dissolve, slowly, over millennia, until even the stone blocks are sand. And the plateau will be here, under the water, at the bottom of a sky that is also a ceiling, breathing its slow mineral breath, humming its low mineral hum, populated and loud and cold and green and ancient and not finished, exactly, but no longer becoming. Being. Just being. For as long as being takes.
