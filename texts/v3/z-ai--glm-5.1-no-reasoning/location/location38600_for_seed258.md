# The Verdant Liturgy

There is no ground here.

That is the first disorientation. You step and your foot finds purchase, but what it finds is not stone, not soil, not any mineral thing. It is root. A vast interlocking root mat, thumb-thick and wet, knitted across itself ten thousand times, firm enough to walk on, alive enough to give slightly under each step. You feel it yield and recover. Like walking on the chest of something breathing.

You are high. The air tells you this before anything else — it is thin, warm, and so saturated that breathing feels like drinking. Moisture collects on every surface, including your skin, including the inside of your lungs. The horizon sits low and far, which means you are elevated, though you cannot see what you are elevated above. A highland plateau, maybe. A tableland. Or the crown of something whose body is buried so deep you will never find its feet.

The scale is valley-sized but feels larger, because the emptiness is large. Structures — if that word applies to things that grew — are scattered across this living floor with generous, almost deliberate spacing. You can walk for minutes between them. Each one stands alone in its own territory of wet root-floor, its own circumference of shadow and condensation. There is no crowd here. No press. The place has the spatial logic of a cathedral whose congregation has been dispersed to the corners of the earth, each worshipper given acres of personal silence.

And yet it is not silent. It is loud.

## The Architecture of Growth

The structures are plant. Entirely, thoroughly, without apology. They have the general shape of buildings — vertical, enclosing, with what appear to be walls and openings and internal spaces — but they did not arrive at these shapes through any process of construction. No tool touched them. No hand bent them. They grew, or are growing, or appear to have grown. The tense is uncertain because the tense is the problem.

Each structure is a single organism. You can see this up close: the bark of the walls flows uninterrupted into the roots of the floor, flows upward into arching ribs that form a roof, flows back down the other side. There are no joints. There are no seams. The grain runs continuous from ground to crown and back, like a single folded sheet of living wood. Some of them are thirty feet tall. Some are barely waist-high, as if they stopped partway through and forgot to continue.

But they didn't stop. That's what you realize slowly, standing inside one of the larger ones, touching the inner wall where water traces slow vertical lines down the grain. They didn't stop and they didn't finish. They are in the state of becoming. Permanently. Embryonic. The walls are thin, still forming — you can see where new layers of cambium would thicken them, but the layers haven't been added. The openings are not openings by design; they are places where the growth hasn't closed yet. The internal spaces feel less like rooms and more like the cavities of a body that is still assembling itself, a body that will never finish assembling.

The buds are the strangest part. Every structure bristles with them — at the peaks, at the junctions of ribs, along the floor where wall meets root. Fat, glistening, unopened. Some are the size of fists. Some are the size of your head. They look like they should burst, should unfurl into leaf or flower or branch at any moment. The tension of that expectation is constant. You keep watching them. They never open. They have never opened. The surfaces of the buds are slick with condensation, beaded like they are sweating with the effort of almost-becoming.

Everything here is almost.

## The Water

Water does not fall from the sky here, or if it does, it is indistinguishable from the water that is already everywhere. The atmosphere is at the point where air and water are not quite separate things. You walk through a medium. It is warm and it is wet and it is in your eyes and on your hands and beading in the fine hairs of your forearms. The visibility is perhaps a quarter mile, not because of fog exactly, but because the air itself is so full.

The structures weep. This is not metaphor. Every vertical surface runs with water — not from rain, not from streams, but from the wood itself, which seems to be exuding moisture from its pores, pushing it out through the bark in a slow continuous sweat. The water collects in grooves in the root-floor and runs along them in shallow channels, finding paths between the structures, connecting them with a network of capillary streams no deeper than a finger. The root-floor is saturated. You can feel it compress under your weight, water welling up around your footprints, filling them behind you as you step forward.

There are basins — shallow depressions in the root mat where the water collects into still pools, dark and tannin-stained, warm as skin. These pools sit between the structures like town squares between buildings, and they are perfectly still, perfectly reflective, and perfectly loud, because each pool is a resonance chamber. The water that feeds them drips and trickles and patters, and each drop is amplified by the architecture of the space — the wooden ribs above catching the sound, the root-floor below carrying it, the pool itself ringing with each impact like a bell being struck by a finger.

The sound is layered and constant. Every surface in this place has a film of water on it and every film is moving, sliding, gathering, falling, and each movement produces its own tone. The deep thud of heavy drops landing in pools. The high thin singing of water tracing thin vertical grooves. The hiss of water spreading across flat root surfaces. The click and pop of bubbles forming and bursting in the warmer basins. All of it continuous, all of it overlapping, and all of it — this is the unsettling part — exactly the same from one moment to the next. The sound does not rise and fall. It does not vary. It is a chord sustained without deviation, a single complex note that began at some point you cannot identify and will not end.

## The Unchanging

This is what takes the longest to understand, and when you understand it, it is the thing that frightens you, or perhaps more accurately, the thing that makes fright irrelevant because fright requires an expectation of change and there is no expectation of change here.

Nothing changes.

The buds do not open. The structures do not grow. The water does not rise or fall. The pools do not overflow. The drips fall at the same rate, from the same points, in the same rhythm. The air does not shift in temperature or humidity. The light — a warm, even, directionless luminance that seems to come from the atmosphere itself rather than from any sun you can see through the saturated air — does not change in angle or intensity. There is no morning. There is no evening. There is no season. There is now, and now is all there has been.

You could sit beside one of the pools and watch a particular drip point — water gathering at the tip of a root-fiber, swelling, trembling, falling, landing — and you would see that the interval between drops is precisely, impossibly the same. Every time. Not approximately. Not roughly. Exactly. You could measure it with a stopwatch and the measurement would not vary by a hundredth of a second. The natural world does not work like this. Natural systems have variance, noise, drift. This has none. This has the regularity of a mechanism, but there is no mechanism. There is only wood and water and root and air, performing a process that has no beginning and no end and no deviation.

The place is in equilibrium, but it is not the equilibrium of a mature ecosystem. It is the equilibrium of a thing frozen mid-birth. An embryo in permanent gestation. The structures are the shape of becoming and they will never become. They are caught in the act of their own creation and the act will never complete.

## What Lives Here

Almost nothing. This is perhaps the mercy of the place.

There are no birds. No insects visible on the surfaces. No small mammals moving through the root-floor. The structures do not host communities or ecologies in the way that living systems usually do. They are alone, each one, standing in its own space, weeping its own water, ringing its own note in the continuous chord.

But there is something.

In the pools, if you watch long enough — and there is nothing here to prevent you from watching for a very long time — you will see movement. Not the movement of a creature, exactly. A movement in the water itself, a displacement, a ripple that has no visible cause. The pools are fed by drips and the drips are constant, so the surface is always disturbed, but you learn to read the difference between drip-ripples and other ripples. The other ripples are deeper. Slower. They move across the pool in lines that are too deliberate, too directional, and they originate from the center of the pool, where nothing is.

You never see what causes them. You only see the water's response to something that is beneath it, within it, part of it. The root-floor extends beneath the pools — you can feel it under the water, firm, living — and whatever moves down there moves within the root, not above it. It is not in the water. It is in the wood. It is in the substrate.

The ripples come at intervals. The intervals are regular. Of course they are. Everything here is regular. Everything here is the same.

## The Landscape as It Extends

From the highest structure — you can climb it, the ribs of its walls forming a ladder of sorts, the wood surprisingly strong under your hands — you can see the extent of the place. It is wide. A valley or more. The plateau stretches in every direction, flat and root-covered, dotted with structures that become smaller with distance until they are just shapes in the luminous mist. You count dozens. Hundreds, perhaps. Each one standing alone. Each one with its own pool. Each one weeping its own water. Each one mid-birth, mid-becoming, mid-never-finish.

They are not identical. Some are tall and narrow, like the fingers of a hand reaching upward. Some are broad and low, spreading across the root-floor like a hip or a shoulder. Some have complex internal structures — chambers within chambers, walls that fold back on themselves. Others are simple, a single arch of living wood, a single hollow space. But all share the same quality of incompletion, the same bristling of unopened buds, the same slow exudation of water.

The root-floor extends to the horizon in every direction. You cannot see where it ends. Perhaps it does not end. Perhaps the plateau goes on forever, and the structures go on forever, and the water goes on forever, and the sound goes on forever.

You climb down. Your hands are wet. The wood under your feet is wet. The air in your lungs is wet. The sound surrounds you, unchanged by your movement through it. You are the only variable here. You are the only thing that is different from one moment to the next. And after enough time — and there is no way to measure how much time, because the light does not change and the sound does not change and the water does not change — you begin to feel that you are the anomaly. That the place is complete in its incompletion, perfect in its frozen becoming, and your presence is the single element that does not belong.

The buds glisten. The water falls. The chord sustains. The embryo holds.

It will hold forever.
