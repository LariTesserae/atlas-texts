To enter the Hanging City of Kael is to step inside a throat of stone.

The space is small—cathedral-sized, no more. A single vast chamber in the earth's crust, a rounded dome of primordial rock perhaps forty meters across and thirty high. That is the entire world of this place. There is no horizon. There is no sky beyond the cracked and gasping chimneys in the ceiling. The walls are the boundaries, and they are close enough that you can always feel their presence at the edge of your vision, pressing in like the ribs of some sleeping leviathan.

**The Terrain and Its Age**
The rock here is unimaginably old. It is the color of dried marrow and compressed ash—striped in bands of rust and pale, chalky white. This is a landscape at the end of its life. Three billion years of tectonic shuddering and mineral sweating have left it utterly exhausted; it has settled into its final, permanent form. There are no sharp edges left, no jagged outcrops. Everything has been worn to a smooth, almost fleshy roundness by the patient, infinite friction of deep time. The stone sags. It billows. The stalactites hanging from the ceiling have grown so long and heavy they have begun to merge with the stalagmites below, forming thick, squat pillars that look like the legs of some colossal, buried table. The floor is a gentle, undulating basin of this same smooth stone, worn into shallow terraces and ledges by the slow drip of epochs. 

**The Absence of Water**
There is no water here. Not a drop. Not a puddle, not a smear of moisture on the stone. The air is completely dry, pulling the moisture from your lungs with every breath, leaving a taste of metal and ancient dust on your tongue. Yet this is not a dead place. It is merely a place that has forgotten water, moving on to a different, stranger chemistry. What percolates down from the rock above is a thin, viscous sap—a mineral-rich, non-aqueous fluid the color of pale amber. It seeps from the porous ceiling at a rate of a few drops an hour, pooling in shallow basins carved into the stone over millennia. It is the blood of this closed system, and everything here drinks from it.

**The Lushness**
Around these amber basins, the vegetation erupts. It is terrifyingly lush, a suffocating, fractal density of life that makes the most of the warm, still air. But it is the lushness of the deep sea, or of a greenhouse left to run wild. There are no leaves—there is no light to catch. Instead, the flora is built from thick, waxy fingers of pale green and bruised purple, reaching upward in dense thickets. Shelf-like fungi, broad as dinner tables, layer the lower walls in cascading terraces, their edges ruffled and dripping with the amber sap. Veins of pale, fibrous roots crisscross the rock, binding the ancient stone in a tight, suffocating embrace. You cannot walk without brushing against them; the space is a tightly packed garden of fleshy, seeking growth, layered three deep in places, fighting for the warmth radiating from the stone.

**The Urban Fabric**
The people of Kael have not built their city *on* the stone; they have woven it *into* the vegetation. Their architecture is primitive, built of hand-split fungal shelves and the cured stalks of the largest growths. The dwellings are spherical, woven like heavy baskets, and wedged between the giant supporting pillars of rock, or suspended from the ceiling by thick ropes of fibrous root. They are near-touching, layered upon one another in a dense, three-dimensional lattice. It is a hamlet of perhaps fifty structures, a vertical shantytown of woven pods and hanging walkways, organized not by streets but by the logic of the vegetation. You climb to move through Kael. You pull yourself up by the guide-ropes, you step across swaying bridges of lashed stalks, you squeeze between the clustered homes. The construction is simple, pre-metal, tied and wedged and stuffed with dried moss, but it is secure. It has to be. The drop to the basin floor is long, and the amber pools below are shallow.

**The Warmth and the Dimness**
Heat rises from the floor, a deep geothermal sigh that keeps the chamber at a humid, comforting warmth, like a nursery or an incubator. The light is almost nonexistent. The only illumination comes from the vegetation itself—a thick, slow bioluminescence. The fungal shelves glow with a dim, rust-colored mold-light, and the tips of the pale stalks pulse with a bruised, violet phosphorescence. It is enough to see by, but only just. The depths of the chamber are lost in a permanent, warm gloom. Shapes are suggested rather than revealed. The far wall is a rumor, a shadow behind a veil of hanging roots.

**The Abundant Fauna**
In this dim warmth, the fauna thrives. The air is never still with the sound of chitin and soft bodies. Pale, eyeless insects the size of a human thumb click and scuttle along the fungal shelves, sipping at the amber sap. Plump, slow-moving things—like furry, six-legged slugs—graze on the moss, making a wet, smacking sound as they feed. Higher up, in the woven city, small, warm-blooded creatures with leathery wings and blunt, grasping claws scurry along the walkways, stealing scraps of dried stalk-pith from the thresholds of the homes. There are many of them, a living, skittering tide that fills every crack and hollow of the chamber. They are not aggressive, but they are ever-present, a constant, bustling biomass.

**The Sound**
The noise is surprising. In this sealed, stone throat, every sound is amplified and trapped. The air hums with a low, resonant throb—the collective vibration of a million clicking mandibles, the wet tearing of the slugs feeding, the creak of the swaying basket-homes. The city is loud with the sound of industry: the rhythmic *thock-thock* of stone axes shaping fungal shelves, the scrape of woven baskets dragged across rock, the low, continuous murmur of the inhabitants' voices. The chamber never silences. It roars with the close, intimate noise of life packed too tightly into a single, warm space.

**The Rules of the World**
Time moves slowly here. The inhabitants live long, measured lives. A generation passes while a single stalactite grows a centimeter. There is no urgency. The warmth is constant, the food is abundant, the chamber provides. The only rule is density. The space is fixed, the stone unyielding. Every birth requires a death to make room. Every new structure requires an old one to be dismantled. Kael is at capacity, a closed loop of warmth, flesh, and amber sap. The people move with a deliberate, careful grace, never rushing, always conscious of the weight of the bodies around them, the nearness of the stone, the slow, endless pulse of the ancient earth beneath their woven floors.
