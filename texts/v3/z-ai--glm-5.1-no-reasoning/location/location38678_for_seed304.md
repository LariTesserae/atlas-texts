The weeping begins at first light.

Not from the sky—which remains a steadfast pallid grey, neither threatening rain nor offering sun—but from the walls themselves. The settlement of Veren occupies a single, steep-sided hollow in the lowlands, a tear in the earth no larger than a cathedral nave, and every surface within it sweats. The rock here is young. You can feel it in the way the stone refuses the chisel, how it fractures in sharp, unweathered shards, its edges yet to be softened by time. The strata are still rising, still pushing themselves above the water table like a gasp of breath held too long, and the earth cannot contain what it holds. Water beads on every vertical face, runs in capillary threads along the mortar, drips from the lips of carved drainage grooves into channels cut ankle-deep into the flagstone floor.

There is no room here. That is the first thing a visitor understands—not through thought but through the body, which must constantly adjust. The hollow is packed, saturated with presence. Buildings lean against rock walls and against each other, their timber frames wedged into fissures, their foundations sunk into crevices where the stone split during emergence. Walkways span the gaps between structures, sometimes only a plank's width, crossing over the channels where the weep-water collects. Roof eaves nearly touch across the narrowest passages. A person cannot stand in Veren without being touched by it: the damp sleeve of fog, the brush of lichen-crusted wall, the mist that rises from the channel and settles on skin like a cold benediction.

They built upward because they could not build outward. Three tiers of construction lean into the curving walls of the hollow, connected by ladders and carved steps and, in one instance, a kind of wooden cage that two people must crank together to raise or lower. The buildings are timber and rough stone, mortared with a pale clay that the weep-water slowly dissolves, so that there is always somewhere being re-mortared, always a section of wall being scraped clean and re-sealed. Hand tools lie everywhere—stone hammers, wooden trowels, chisels of sharpened bone. No metal. The damp eats it within a season, leaves it orange and crumbling, and so the people of Veren work with what holds: wood, bone, stone, clay, fiber. The architecture is dense and layered, additions leaning on additions, a second floor cantilevered over a first, a storeroom wedged beneath a staircase, a sleeping loft that is also the only passage to a neighbor's door. There is no wasted space. There is barely any space at all.

The vegetation is sparse but specific. Where the weep-water runs heaviest, a kind of pale reed grows in thread-thin stands, no taller than a knee, its fibers harvested for cordage and rough cloth. Veins of something like moss—not quite moss, too silvery, too dry to the touch despite the wet—trace the higher walls where the stone is newest and sharpest. They do not spread. They only follow the fresh fractures, as if seeded by the breaking of rock itself. In the deepest channel, where the water pools before finding some subterranean exit, there grows a single plant that the people call hardroot—a thick, pale tuber that pushes above the waterline only to flower, producing a small white bloom that lasts a single day before dissolving into the mist. The people harvest it carefully, leaving half in the water, taking half for food. They do not explain why. It is simply done.

The fauna are rare and strange. There is a kind of eyeless crustacean, pale and soft, that lives in the channels—vermin, the people call it, though they do not treat it as a pest. The creatures eat the accumulation of plant matter and clay that would otherwise clog the waterways, and in return they are left alone. Occasionally one is found dead in a channel, and it is scooped out and added to the mortar mixture, its shell lending a faint pinkish resilience to the clay. No one remembers when this practice began. It is simply how mortar is made. There are also insects—tiny, nearly transparent things that congregate where the weep-water runs, forming delicate living skeins across the channel surfaces. They make no sound. They have no visible purpose. But when the water slows or the channels clog, the insects vanish first, and the people have learned to watch for their absence.

Beyond these, nothing lives in Veren that was not brought. A few hardy birds sometimes descend into the hollow, drawn by the insects, but they do not stay. The people keep no animals. There is no room for them, and the wet cold seeps into the lungs of anything not adapted to it.

The temperature sits at the edge of discomfort—cool enough that a body in motion stays warm, but stillness invites the chill. The people dress in layers of woven reed-fiber and skins treated with the pale clay, which seals against the damp. Their movements are economical, shaped by the tight passages and the constant presence of water. There is no running in Veren. There is barely room to walk two abreast. And so the settlement moves at a single, deliberate pace, a kind of walking meditation enforced by architecture and geology.

Sound in Veren is an intimate thing, kept close by the walls, absorbed by the damp. The weep-water provides a constant background—a thousand small drips, the whisper of water running in channels, the deeper gurgle where channels meet and descend into the rock. Against this, human sounds are muted: the tap of a stone hammer, the murmur of voices too close to need volume, the creak of timber adjusting to the slow pressure of the still-rising stone. There are no echoes. The wet walls swallow them. A shout in Veren would be a desperate, private act, heard only by those within arm's reach and the rock itself.

And yet the place is not still. Processes continue without pause. The stone rises—a millimeter a year, perhaps, but enough that the buildings shift, that doors stick, that channels must be recut. The weep-water swells and recedes in some rhythm tied to tides or seasons or something deeper, something the people track by marking the highest waterline on a central post each morning. The hardroot flowers and dissolves. The mortar dissolves and is replaced. The lichen-thing traces new fractures. The insects thin and thicken. The crustaceans clean and die and are added to the walls. There is a continuous present-tense to Veren, an ongoing negotiation between the people and the water and the young stone that will not stop emerging.

The people do not speak of why they stay. Perhaps they no longer remember. Perhaps the question has become meaningless, like asking a body why it remains in its skin. They tend the channels. They re-mortar the walls. They harvest the hardroot and leave half. They watch the insects. They crank the cage-lift and climb the ladders and press through the narrow passages where the walls sweat against their shoulders. At night, in their dense-packed sleeping lofts, they listen to the weeping of the stone, and it is not a sound of grief. It is only what the world does here: rising, breaking, releasing what it held, becoming itself by increments.
