The world is a vast, slanted emptiness—a continental shelf of mineral crust that tilts upward toward an imperceptible zenith, reaching an elevation so extreme that the air thins into something more like a warm, faintly luminous vacuum. There is no water here. None at all. The ancient seas, if they ever existed, departed so long ago that even the memory of moisture has been baked out of the stone. What remains is a desert of crystalline halite and iron oxide dust, a salt-field that stretches to every horizon in bands of pale amber and rust, warm beneath a normal but depthless light. The sun—or whatever stable star serves this place—casts shadows that are sharp and long, but there is no atmospheric scatter to soften them; the light simply is, functional and unremarkable, illuminating the scale of things without comment.

And the scale is staggering. The terrain is not flat but rather a slow, planetary incline, a rise so gradual it takes days of travel to notice the ascent, yet so extreme that standing at the low-edge and looking upward reveals the curvature of the world itself—a wall of stratified geology rising like a bruised sky. The formations are weathered into their final shapes. Erosion has finished its work here and moved on. The buttes and mesas are not crumbling; they are settled, balanced, in a state of long-equilibrium that implies they have looked exactly this way for geological ages. Wind has carved them into smooth, muscular forms—hollows and arches, natural amphitheaters of rust-colored stone—and then stopped, as if the wind itself grew tired and lay down among the salt flats to sleep.

Into this vast and patient emptiness, someone has built.

The city clings to the ascending terrain like a slow stain. It is not a desperate settlement or a frontier outpost; it is organized, layered, deliberate—a town that has metastasized into infrastructure. The architecture follows the logic of the incline: buildings are terraced into the rock, their foundations cut directly into the warm stone, their walls made of the same iron-heavy aggregate that surrounds them, so that from a distance the settlement appears to be a denser, more geometric version of the landscape itself. There is no ornamentation. Every surface serves a purpose. Pipes and conduits run along the exterior of structures like exposed veins, carrying something that is not water—some industrial solvent, perhaps, or a heated mineral slurry used in whatever process the machines are here to perform.

Because there are machines. This is a place of engines.

The sound tells you before the sight does. The landscape is mostly quiet—profoundly, oppressively quiet, the kind of silence that makes you aware of your own pulse—but beneath that silence, threading through it like a low-frequency hum, is the mechanical breath of the city. Factories at the higher elevations run on some internal combustion that requires no oxygen from the thin air; their exhaust is a pale heat-shimmer that distorts the horizon above the settlement. Gears turn. Pistons compress and release. The rhythm is glacial. A single cycle of the great engines takes perhaps twenty minutes from start to finish, so that the sound is not a clatter but a groan—a continental sigh that rises and falls like a tide of pressure. Electrical infrastructure hums at frequencies just below conscious perception, felt in the teeth more than heard. The town is electrified, but the current is slow, the voltage patient, as if the power itself has adapted to the tempo of the place.

What is being produced here? It is difficult to say. The factories emit no smoke—only that wavering heat—and the conveyance systems that link one building to the next carry sealed containers of something dense and dark. The product, whatever it is, moves uphill. The entire city is a slow pump, drawing material from the lowlands and processing it upward toward the extreme elevation, where the air is so thin that sound barely travels and the machines seem to operate in a vacuum of pure function. Perhaps they are mining something. Perhaps they are refining the stone itself, extracting some mineral essence that can only be liberated by this particular combination of warmth and pressure and absence. The purpose feels ancient and self-justifying. The industry is not for anyone; it simply *is*, a process that began before memory and will continue after indifference.

And yet the place is not empty of life.

The fauna are the strangest feature of this already-strange world. They are not biological in any familiar sense—nothing with cellular structures or aqueous chemistry could survive here, where there is no water and the medium of exchange is something else entirely. What lives here lives on the margins of the industrial process, drawn to the waste-heat and the mineral exhaust the way desert insects might be drawn to moisture. They are chitinous and mineral-themselves, creatures of articulated shell and crystalline joint, their bodies warm to the touch and faintly resonant—when they move, their carapaces click and scrape, producing sounds like small geological events. They are abundant. They thrive. They cluster around the warm outflow pipes of the factories in dense, clicking colonies, their multifold limbs finding purchase on the smooth stone, their mouthparts—if that is the right word—scraping at the mineral residue that condenses from the industrial exhaust. They are not large; the biggest are perhaps the size of a human hand, the smallest like grains of animated sand. But there are thousands of them, millions, an ecology built on the waste of a process no one remembers starting.

They are strange. Not hostile—there is no aggression in them, no more than there is aggression in a moth drawn to light—but strange in the way of things that have evolved for a world that itself is strange. Their movements are slow, matching the pace of the engines. When a factory cycle completes and a burst of heat ripples outward, the nearest creatures turn toward it in unison, a ripple of attention that passes through the colony like a wave through water. They communicate by vibration, tapping their shells against the stone in patterns too complex and too slow for human perception—conversations that take hours, carried through the warm rock itself. They have a society, perhaps. A purpose. They are not pests; they are citizens of a world that was made for them as much as it was made for the machines.

The weirdness accumulates in the details. The light here, though normal in intensity, has a quality of *flatness*—as if it has traveled so far through the thin atmosphere that it has lost its ability to model form, rendering everything in a uniform illumination that makes depth difficult to judge. The salt flats shimmer with a faint iridescence that has no apparent source, as if the mineral itself is generating light at a frequency just beyond comfortable perception. At the highest elevations, where the city gives way to bare rock and the incline becomes almost vertical, there are formations that look too organized to be natural—hexagonal columns of some dark basalt-like material, evenly spaced, rising to identical heights, their tops flat and faintly warm, as if they are the stumps of something that was once much taller. No one in the city seems to know what they are. No one seems to care. They are simply part of the landscape, the way a mountain is part of a landscape: present, inert, irrelevant.

Time moves strangely here, or rather, it moves at all—that is the strangeness. In a world of such minimal flux, where the engines take twenty minutes to complete a cycle and the creatures speak in vibrations that last for hours, where the erosion has finished and the climate has settled into a permanent warm stillness, change is so gradual that it becomes invisible. A visitor might perceive only stasis: the city frozen, the desert empty, the machines running their endless slow loops. But stay long enough—stay for a year, for a decade—and you would begin to notice the patient processes. The mineral residue accumulating on the outflow pipes, millimeter by millimeter, until a factory must be shut down and cleaned, a process that takes weeks. The colonies of shell-creatures migrating slowly uphill, following the warmth as the lowland factories age and their output diminishes. The salt flats receding by centimeters per century as some slow chemical reaction pulls the minerals deeper into the substrate. The city itself shifting—not collapsing, but settling, its terraced foundations adjusting to the infinitesimal give of the stone beneath, walls leaning a fraction of a degree further into the incline, a process so slow it can only be measured by the ancient survey markers set into the rock at the settlement's founding, markers that no one alive remembers placing.

This is the world: a warm, dry, tilted vastness where industry functions as geology, where life has learned to drink from the exhaust of engines, where the silence is so deep it becomes a texture—a thing you can almost feel pressing against your skin. It is a place that has arrived at its own equilibrium and will remain there for geological ages, the machines turning, the creatures clicking, the salt flats gleaming under flat light, the whole slow system breathing in and out with the patience of a world that has nowhere else to be.
