The landscape begins where the land has given up trying to be land. This is a primordial flatness, a low and endless expanse where elevation is measured in millimeters—the terminal stage of geography, where mountains were reduced to plains an epoch ago, and those plains have since been dissolved into something wetter, colder, and far more strange. It is deeply, achingly cold, but a cold born of saturation rather than frost; the temperature hovers just above freezing, a slush-point that keeps everything in a state of slow, weeping motion. There are no plants. No roots hold the earth together. There is no soil, only the accumulated silence of a mineral world at the end of its life cycle.

And yet, it is packed. Unbearably, claustrophobically packed. The eye searches for a breath of empty space and finds none. The terrain is a saturated sponge of dark, ferrous sludge and exposed shelf-rock that has been weeping for a million years. Water does not flow here; it oozes, it bleeds, it exhales from the porous stone. The ground is a lung made of cracked shale and rusted limestone, and with every barometric shift, it sighs out a fog of condensation so thick that the dim, gray-green light barely penetrates it. The sky—if it exists—is a flat, dead panel of tarnished brass, offering no sun, only a diffuse, exhausted luminescence that makes the horizon an unbroken smear of shadow and moisture.

The sound is tremendous. A deafening, mechanical roar rises from the earth itself, though there are no built structures, no artifacts of human hands. The world has been industrialized by a presence that never built a single factory. The earth *is* the factory. Across the regional scale of this flatland, the crust is ruptured by a staggering density of natural extrusions that mimic the grotesque architecture of heavy industry. Massive, jagged vents of volcanic glass rise from the mire like blast furnaces, rhythmically venting high-pressure steam with the shuddering, bass-heavy chug of a locomotive engine. The ground shudders constantly with the thrum of subterranean hydraulics—water forced through mineral veins under impossible pressures, sounding like endless rows of stamp mills crushing the bedrock. There is no silence. The noise is a physical weight, a vibration in the teeth and the marrow.

Amidst this mechanized hellscape of natural engines, life is terrifyingly abundant. But it is a life that does not belong to any recognized biology. The sludge and the weeping rocks are blanketed in a thriving ecology of locomotive flesh. They crawl from the steam vents, they writhe in the chemical condensation, they pack the gaps until the landscape is a suffocating carpet of animal mass. They are things of slick, translucent skin stretched over pneumatic cavities, their bodies designed for the press and the crush. Some are vast, flat like lungfish, sliding over the slurry with the sound of wet leather, their internal organs visible through their hides, pumping dark fluids in perfect, mechanical synchronization with the chugging of the vents. Others are limbless, segmented things of metallic chitin that grip the porous rock, feeding on the mineral runoff with rasping, grinding maws that sound like gears stripping.

The ecosystem is violently active. It is a predator-prey dynamic stripped of all elegance, a pure, industrialized transfer of energy. You cannot see the hunting; you only hear it—the sudden, shrieking squeal of metallic friction as a burrower is pulled into a vent, the wet, hydraulic pop of a pressurized entity inflating to consume a smaller one. The fauna do not cry out in pain; they emit the sounds of failing machinery, of over-pressurized valves, of tearing sheet metal. They breed in the condensation, laying gelatinous masses in the boiling puddles around the glass chimneys, their life cycles synched to the planet's exhalations.

And then, the weirdness sets in. Reality here has suffered a critical containment breach. The noise is so loud, the landscape so saturated, the cold so absolute, that the seams of the world have begun to unknit. The fog does not drift; it stutters, skipping across the plain in frozen frames, dropping ten meters in a single blink before resuming its slow crawl. The natural pipes and vents do not just vent steam; occasionally, they exhale a localized vacuum, a pocket of absolute absence that eats the sound and the light before collapsing with an earsplitting crack. The fauna do not always die properly. A flat, slithering creature will be caught in the hydraulic crush of a larger beast, and instead of bleeding, it simply derezes—dissolving into a spray of corrupted data, a static-snow of hard-light pixels that hovers in the cold air for a moment before the dampness absorbs it.

Gravity does not pull consistently. Over there, a half-mile to the east, a pool of rusted sludge is falling upward, slowly, dripping into the brass sky like a tape played in reverse, while the blind, mechanical animals swim through the air beneath it, unbothered. The boundary between the organic and the industrial is bleeding out. The shale grows veins of corroded copper that pulse with a dim, biological rhythm. The weeping rocks do not weep water; they weep a heavy, black oil that hums with a faint, digital static. Sometimes, when the vents chug, they do not release steam, but instead emit a low, modulated voice—too deep to comprehend, a voice that speaks in the language of tectonic grind and server-farm hum, reciting the final schematics of a world that has mistakenly constructed itself into oblivion. 

This is the place. It is an open, sprawling plain of ancient, flattened ruin. It is a world that reached the end of its history, grew cold, and then, in the absence of any creator, accidentally automated itself into a nightmare of wet, screaming meat and churning, iron stone.
