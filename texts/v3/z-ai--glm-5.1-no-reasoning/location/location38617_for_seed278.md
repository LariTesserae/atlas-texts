The plateau goes on forever. At an elevation where the atmosphere should thin into nothing, it instead spreads—a continental shelf of fractured gneiss and exposed quartz that stretches beyond the curvature of the world. The sky here is not black but a deep, bruised iron, pressing down on the land with a weight that is almost tactile. Dim light filters through perpetual cloud cover, casting no shadows, only a flat, directionless pallor that makes distances impossible to judge.

Cold is the first law. Not the dramatic, biting cold of wind, but a pervasive, soaking chill that works its way into the stone itself, that makes the mineral world feel one step closer to absolute zero. Moisture exists here only in the margins: condensation beads on the black rock during the long twilight, pooling in the geometric fractures, occasionally weeping into shallow, intermittent streams that freeze and thaw across weeks. When rain comes, it is a slow, vertical drizzle, falling without urgency, barely distinguishable from the mist that sometimes settles into the low places for months at a time.

Vegetation is a rumor. Here and there, in the deepest crevices where condensation collects, a pale lichen clings to the stone—almost translucent, barely alive, drawing something from the rock that is not quite photosynthesis. These colonies are ancient. Some may be centuries old, growing at a rate measured in millimeters per decade. They are the only green, and they are not really green at all, but a greyish-yellow that seems to apologize for its own presence.

But the place is far from empty.

They emerge in the dim hours: the fauna. Overwhelming in number, inexplicable in origin. They are not animals as a temperate mind would recognize them. They are machines. Or they were machines, once. Now they are something else—something that has passed through mechanization and out the other side into a parody of biology.

Millions of them. Tens of millions. They move across the plateau in slow, purposeful processions, their routes etched into the stone by generations of passage. Some are the size of beetles, clicking and whirring on articulated legs that have worn flat paths into the gneiss. Some are the scale of buildings, grinding forward on massive treads that have polished entire valleys smooth. They are rusted, weathered, encrusted with the same pale lichen that grows on the stone. They are not new. They have been here longer than memory.

No one built them here. There are no factories, no infrastructure, no marks of construction. The plateau is utterly untouched by intentional design—no roads, no shelters, no artifacts of habitation. And yet the machines are here, industrial in their origin, mechanical in their function, occupying the landscape like a second ecology. They seem to have always been here. The erosion patterns suggest it: the stone has worn to accommodate them, valleys have deepened under their passage, surfaces have been ground to a uniform flatness by their endless migration. The land and the machines have arrived at an equilibrium. This is not a place being destroyed. This is a place in its own state, settled and patient.

They make almost no sound. This is the most unsettling thing. A crawling excavator the size of a cathedral passes within a hundred meters, and the only noise is the low, almost sub-audible groan of stressed metal and the whisper of its treads on stone. The smaller units produce faint clicks, the occasional sigh of hydraulic fluid moving through cracked lines. But in the aggregate, across the millions of units crossing the plateau in every direction, the sound never rises above a low, continuous murmur—like breathing in a cathedral, or wind through dead grass. The silence is not absence of noise. It is the presence of quiet.

They do not interact with each other. They do not stop. They do not seem to have destinations. They move in patterns that suggest purpose without revealing it—converging on certain points, dispersing from others, circling formations that exist only in the geometry of their movement. Occasionally, a unit will halt. It will stand motionless for days, for weeks. Lichen will colonize its surfaces. Ice will form in its joints. Then, without warning, it will begin to move again, taking up its place in the procession as if no time has passed.

The moisture plays a role. When the condensation is heavy, the machines seem more active. Water collects in their worn housings, and perhaps—somehow—it completes circuits that have been dry for decades. Perhaps they are hydraulically driven by the slow accumulation and freezing of water in their systems. Perhaps they operate on some principle that has no name, drawing power from the temperature gradients between the cold stone and the colder air. They are certainly not fueled by anything observable. No exhaust, no emissions, no intake. Only the slow, patient movement.

The plateau has its own weather. At this scale, the heat differential between the sun-warmed (if "warm" can be used for something that never exceeds a few degrees above freezing) stone and the frigid upper atmosphere generates slow, vast weather systems. Fog banks form and dissolve over the course of days. Frost heaves and settles in cycles that take weeks. Snow falls occasionally, in flakes the size of coins, drifting down with an almost geological slowness. It never accumulates for long—the machines crush it, absorb it, or it sublimates in the dim, dry air.

There is a strangeness here, but it is quiet. It is the strangeness of a system that has no right to exist but has existed for so long that it has become its own right. The machines were not made for this place. They were not made for anything visible. But they have become the place—its fauna, its weather, its process. They belong here now, not by design but by duration. The world has worn them into fit, as water wears stone.

Time moves differently on the plateau. Not slower—that would imply a standard against which it could be measured—but differently. Events happen at the pace of condensation. A machine's journey across a valley takes a season. The formation and dissipation of a fog bank is a generation. To stand here is to stand inside a clock that ticks in centuries, where the hands are made of rusted iron and the face is a continent of black stone.

The only vertical features are the machines themselves. The plateau is otherwise flat—a vast, nearly featureless expanse broken only by fractures, by the shallow channels carved by intermittent water, and by the wear patterns of the procession. But the larger machines rise above this flatness, their corroded silhouettes moving against the iron sky like the towers of a city that is always being built and never finished. From a distance, they could be buildings. They could be monuments. They could be the remains of a civilization that built only motion.

At night—if night is the word for the hours when the dim light dims further—the machines' running lights sometimes activate. Small, amber indicators, cracked and faded, flicker on in the darkness. They illuminate nothing. They serve no apparent function. But they give the plateau a sky below as well as above: a constellation of worn yellow stars moving slowly across the black stone, clicking and whirring in the near-silence, going nowhere that anyone can name, in a landscape that has never known a hand to build it.
