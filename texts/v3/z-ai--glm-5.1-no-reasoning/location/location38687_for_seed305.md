The plateau goes on forever. It is a continent of uplifted basalt and gneiss, so newly thrust from the planet's crust that its edges still carry the fracture-patterns of the tectonic violence that birthed it—sharp, unweathered ridges that have not yet learned to be gentle, escarpments that drop away into altitude without the softening of talus or the forgiveness of time. The scale is difficult to accept. Stand at the edge of the Karst-span and the horizon is not a line but a wall of atmosphere, the sky compressing into a faintly luminous band where the sheer vastness of the plateau creates weather of its own—slow, immense pressure systems that take weeks to rotate. 

Everything here is bright. The altitude is so extreme that the atmosphere thins to something nearly absent, and the light arrives without mediation—raw, actinic, possessing a weight and clarity that feels like pressure against the skin. There is no soft shadow. Every edge is a razor division between illumination and darkness, and the dark is absolute, a thing carved from the stone itself. 

It should be a desert. It is not. 

The cold is the first truth of this place—a grinding, omnipresent cold that does not bite but presses, slow and patient, working its way into the marrow. The temperature sits permanently near freezing, sometimes below, sometimes just above, and this narrow band of thermal possibility creates the strange hydrology that defines the landscape. Moisture arrives as condensation—vast quantities of it, pulled from lower altitudes by the plateau's own weather systems, condensing against the cold stone in the mornings as a heavy dew that soaks everything, running in thin sheets down the sheer faces, collecting in the infinite small depressions of the young rock. Some days bring a thin, intermittent rain, cold as the stone it strikes, rarely lasting more than an hour. Some days bring fog so dense it obliterates everything, a cold wet breath held against the plateau's surface. And then the fog lifts and the light returns and everything is burning bright again, the moisture already evaporating, the stone steaming.

Into this paradox—cold and wet and young and bright—something has erupted. 

The vegetation is impossibly lush. Not the lushness of tropics, but something stranger: a vast and dense biological engine adapted to cold and light and intermittent water, grown so thickly across the plateau that in places the underlying rock is invisible beneath meters of accumulated biomass. The dominant forms are moss-like, but moss scaled to the landscape itself—dense cushions and hummocks and towering mounds of photosynthetic tissue in shades of deep green, burgundy, near-black, their surfaces coated with fine dew that catches the light until the whole plateau seems to glitter. Between the moss-growth, tall reed-like structures rise in thickets, their stalks segmented and cold-hard, their tips crowned with seed-heads that catch the condensation and channel it down to their roots. There are creeping vines that bind the hummocks together into a continuous mat. There are lichen-like crusts that encrust every vertical surface in stratified layers, each a slightly different shade, building slowly outward into the bright air. Nothing here is bare. The density is overwhelming—every cubic centimeter of surface is occupied, every crack filled, every surface claimed. The vegetation does not grow; it *saturates*.

And beneath it, through it, around it, the sound.

The plateau is loud. Not with animal calls or wind—though there is wind, a constant low-frequency moan as air moves across the sheer edges and through the reed-thickets—but with something else. The earth itself is resonant. Somewhere below the young stone, deep in the formation that thrust this land upward, there are engines. Not built engines—nothing here is built, there are no artifacts, no structures, no marks of any hand—but *geological* engines, vast chthonic systems of heat and pressure and mineral reaction that produce a rhythmic, industrial throb. The sound rises through the basalt like the heartbeat of something immense and slow. It is percussive, regular, mechanical in its precision: a deep compression-pulse followed by a longer exhalation, over and over, a frequency just above the threshold of hearing but felt in the chest and the teeth. The plateau is a machine. Not a metaphor. The geochemistry here has produced something functionally identical to industry—autocatalytic mineral reactions in deep vents, thermal cycling chambers of stone that drive fluid through fissures at pressure, natural turbines of magma and gas that rotate on timescales of centuries. The land produces. The land *processes*. There is no design, but there is function, and the function is vast and ongoing and loud.

And it is populated.

The fauna is the hardest thing to accept. The plateau teems—swarms, overflows—with animal life, and the sheer volume of bodies is staggering. The primary inhabitants are insects, but not as they are known elsewhere. They have adapted to the cold through a chemistry that is almost certainly impossible: their hemolymph contains a natural antifreeze of such concentration that they remain active at temperatures that should kill them, and their metabolisms run hot, fueled by the sugars produced in unimaginable quantity by the moss-forests. They are large—the largest the size of a human fist—and they move in coordinated masses that number in the billions. From any vantage point, the air is thick with them: slow-drifting clouds of dark-winged forms, their wingbeats adding a lower harmonic to the plateau's mechanical drone, their bodies catching the light in ways that make the air itself seem to shimmer and clot. They crawl across every surface in dense living carpets, feeding on the vegetation, on each other, on the condensation. They fill the reed-thickets until the stalks bend under their weight. They cluster at the seeps and condensation-pools in writhing masses that ripple with some collective motion. The sound of their feeding is a constant wet crackle, like static or low flame.

Larger forms move through the moss-forests—something like arthropods, something like mammals, chitinous and furred both, their body-plans wrong, adapted to pushing through dense vegetation in freezing temperatures. They are grazers and they are many, herds of them visible from the plateau's edges as dark slow motion through the green, their calls deep and tonal, vibrating in the chest. Above them, something flies—too high to see clearly, casting shadows like small clouds against the bright stone, circling on the plateau's own weather, patient, vast.

And the strangeness. It accumulates. The insects do not merely die—they dissolve, their bodies liquefying almost immediately upon cessation of movement, their antifreeze-rich fluids soaking into the moss and reeds and being absorbed so quickly it seems they were never there. The vegetation grows toward the sound of the geological engines, its growth-patterns aligning with the pulse, orienting toward the deep rhythm as though toward light. The condensation has a taste—not of water but of mineral, metallic, slightly sweet, as though the fog carries something more than H₂O. The light does not behave correctly at the plateau's edges; it bends, fractionally, refracted by the sheer volume of winged life in the air, creating faint halos around the sharper ridges, making the distant escarpments shimmer as though heated. The cold does not slow things as it should. Everything here moves at the same patient speed—the growth, the dissolution, the pulse of the engines, the drift of the weather. It is as though the plateau exists in a different relationship with time, its processes not slower or faster but *calibrated differently*, each one taking exactly as long as it needs, no more, no less, all of them synchronized to some deeper clock.

Nothing here is built, but everything is *organized*. The vegetation and the animals and the geological processes and the condensation and the light form a single integrated system so densely interconnected that it is impossible to say where one ends and another begins. The insects pollinate and dissolve and feed. The vegetation transpires and condenses and channels. The engines pulse and heat and drive the cycle of moisture from below. The cold preserves and limits and selects. The light powers it all, relentless, indifferent, absolute.

This is a place that does not know you are here. It does not know anything. It is not aware. And yet it *functions*—more efficiently than any built system, more completely than any designed machine, a continental engine of biology and geology and physics running on its own slow logic, its own deep time, its own patient and overwhelming momentum. 

You stand at the edge and the light hits you and the cold presses in and the pulse rises through your feet and the air is thick with wings and the horizon is a wall of luminous atmosphere and the moss stretches away forever, dark and wet and glittering, and the sound is everywhere, and nothing stops.
