The land above is honest about its dryness. Cracked clay, blackthorn, gravel the color of old bone, heat standing above the rock like a held breath. But there is a slit in the bedrock, a little over a meter wide, that breathes. On cool mornings a wisp of steam rises from it and unravels in the wind — a plume with no fire under it, and from a distance it looks like the ground is quietly smoking.

Steps go down into the slit. Someone cut them a very long time ago: each tread has worn into a shallow basin, the chisel marks gone soft beneath a skin of mineral, so that the descent feels less like a staircase than like a bedrock streambed that happens to fit feet. There are thirty-one of them. Around the twelfth, the air changes — the cool draft off the plain becomes something wet and warm as the inside of a mouth, and sweat arrives all at once, everywhere.

The chamber is one room. That is the first thing to understand about it: a single vault, the size of a small chapel, every wall in view at once, the ceiling lost in a soft gloom maybe thirty meters up. Far above, a rift in the crown admits one column of light — pale, slanted, and slow; over the course of a day it crawls across the floor like the hand of an enormous clock. At noon it falls on water. The rest of the time it lies on stone, or on green, or on nothing, and everything outside its width lives in a permanent late dusk.

The second thing to understand is the quiet. Not silence — silence is an absence, and this is a material. The room is upholstered with hush: moss, guano, damp fungal mat, stone gone soft with age. Sounds are born here and swallowed at birth. A drop falls somewhere in the dark and you wait nine seconds for the next, and the waiting turns out to be part of the sound. At the low end of the chamber sits a pool, ten meters across, fed by a thread of water that leaks out of a crack with a sound like a whisper repeating one word. The pool is warm as blood, and that is not a figure of speech — put a wrist in and your body will be briefly unsure where it ends. A pale throat of deep water occupies its center. At the far end the water simply leaves, sinking into a gravel bank, going down into rock to wherever pockets of water go in dry country. This pool is one of those pockets — the visible one, the one that made a room around itself.

The cave has weather. The rock here lies along a warm seam, which is why the whole chamber runs hot while the plain above swings between furnace nights and cold nights; and because the inside stays warm and wet while the outside cools, the rift above exhales steam at dusk and dawn, and inhales dry air at midday. Once a day the system produces rain: near the shaft the ceiling chills as night air creeps down, the rising damp meets it, and at dawn the ceiling rains — fat, slow drops, for perhaps twenty minutes, over a zone about the size of a threshing floor. Under that zone the green lives.

And the green is real, though it keeps to its bands. Directly below the shaft, ferns stand waist-high in a crowded cone, layered frond over frond, moss upholstering the stones between them like furniture no one is allowed to sit on. Liverworts sheathe the walls in the drip-line. A bright algal fringe rings the pool's warm shelf. And then there is the fig.

The fig is rooted on the rim above, out in the thorn and gravel, a surface tree like any other — except that decades ago it sent an aerial root down through the rift, and the root is now as thick as a thigh at the crown of the chamber, tapering through forty feet of dark to a tip pale as a nerve. It drinks from the cave's breath and, some say, from the spray of the pool, and it has made the hollow its own cistern: a tree of the dry upland secretly plumbed into the underworld's water. Now and then it drops a fruit. A fig falls the whole way, strikes the scree, and before it stops rolling the crabs and beetles are on it. By dusk it is a memory.

For all its hush, the room is crowded the way a jar is crowded. The wall under the high vault is furred with guano, and under that with fungus — pale caps, ghost-thread mycelium — and under that with moths, and crickets beyond counting, motionless, antennae laid flat, so densely packed that the wall seems to be breathing only because they are on it. Snails graze everywhere in the darker margins, shells thin as frost, the animals' slow pulse visible through them; they live off the film of microbes the warm spring paints onto everything wet. Long-legged spiders wait among them with the patience of furniture. A snake loops in the fig root like a knot no one tied, taking its share of the dusk. Over the pool the ceiling is beaded with faint blue-green lights — gnat larvae, each under its small thread of silk — and all evening the midges rise toward them, so that in this half of the room the sky is indoors, dim, and hunting.

There were people once, briefly. Besides the steps: a niche cut near the pool, empty, with a soot shadow above it and three curved shards of fired clay beneath — the lamp that burned here is older than any metal thing within a hundred miles. A groove worn glassy into the lip of rock over the water, where a rope hauled up a thousand thousand buckets. A fallen cairn of water-rounded stones, mineral-cementing back into bedrock. Nothing else. The cave is taking all of it back at its own pace, furring the chisel marks, sending snails to graze across the carved names of hands, and there is no hurry in it, because nothing here is new, and nothing is falling apart either. The collapse that opened the rift is ancient; the guano cone has had ten thousand generations of tenants; the balance has been paid and repaid longer than any memory in the country above.

The rules are simple enough to state. Everything in this room lives at the meeting of three rivers: light from the shaft, which feeds the fig, the ferns, the algae; guano from the bats, which feeds the fungus, the moths, the crickets, the spiders; and the warm film of the spring, which feeds the snails, the shrimp, the pale blind minnows in the pool. Density is the consequence — three economies in a space the size of a nave, everyone within arm's reach of everyone else. Which produces the final law, the one you can feel in your skin: in a room this small, everything that hunts must be patient, and everything hunted must be still, and so, mostly, they are all still together. The snake and the frog spend their whole lives four feet apart. The hush isn't emptiness. It's a truce.

At dusk the light column thins and slides off the water. The rift reverses and the hollow begins to exhale, steam threading up into the first cool of the plain. Then the bats go — thousands, and the loudest event of the day is a sound like a scarf drawn through a ring, over in ninety seconds. The glow over the pool becomes the brightest thing in the world, for whoever is left to see it. And at dawn comes the rain from the ceiling, twenty minutes of fat drops onto the ferns, and for about a minute after it ends the frogs tap — a soft, dry note, seven or eight times, never more — and then the room is quiet again, and does not explain itself.

Above, on the plain, the steam stands up out of the ground into the sunrise. Nobody reads it. The hollow keeps its own time.
