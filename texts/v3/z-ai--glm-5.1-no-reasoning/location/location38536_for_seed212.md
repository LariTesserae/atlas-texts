This is a place where vegetable matter has forgotten it is supposed to be still.

It is a low basin, a wide and shallow geographical depression that should collect water into a languid swamp, but the water here is a passing ghost. The moisture exists only in fitful exhalations—condensation weeping from the undersides of leaves at irregular intervals, brief and torrential rains that fall in sheets from a clear, warm sky and are drunk by the ground within minutes, leaving only the scent of wet cellulose in the air. The earth is barely earth at all; it is a rhizomatic loam, a woven mat of roots and tubers so densely interlocked that one cannot step without stepping on architecture.

Because this is architecture. The city is not built from plants; the city *is* plants. There is no stone, no brick, no smoothed timber, no tool-mark upon any surface. There are only the constructions of pure, unbridled, terrifying botany. Towering banyan-like structures spread their secondary roots across acres, forming colonnades and archways and tenement-thick walls. Vines cable everything together in a tension network so tight that the spaces between are more felt than seen. It is a metropolis of photosynthesis, a labyrinth of living mass that stretches outward across the low valley, replicating the logic of streets and plazas and amphitheaters without ever having known the mind of an architect. The sheer density defies comprehension. To look in any direction is to see surface upon surface, layer upon layer of overlapping leaves, bark, petioles, and thorns, all pressing near-touching, all fighting for the abundant light that streams down in thick, golden beams from a sun that feels too close, too warm, like a lamp left burning over an incubator.

It is a young landscape. Despite the monumental scale of the plant-city, nothing is mature. Everything has the sharp, desperate edge of early formation. The bark is thin and tears easily. The leaves are tender, translucent in the light, shot through with veins that pulse visibly. The surfaces are fresh, un-weathered, un-eroded. The few trickles of water that manage to pool in the hollows of root-joints carve quick, sharp channels through the loam before being swallowed, leaving incised grooves that reveal the pale, wet white of new root-tips already reaching to fill the wound. The place has the energy of a sprouting seed, magnified to the scale of a town—frantic, impatient, raw.

And it screams.

The sound is the first thing that breaks the mind. There are no animals here—no birds, no insects, no amphibians to fill the air with their calls. Yet the noise is a physical weight, a suffocating, deafening roar. It comes from the plants themselves. The stalks grow so fast you can hear them elongating—a deep, groaning creak that reverberates through the tensile walls of the green city. Vines twist and wind with a wet, grinding squeal, fiber sliding against fiber under tremendous pressure. Pods burst with sharp retorts like cracking joints. The massive leaves, some as broad as market stalls, fan the warm air with low-frequency whomps that blend into a continuous, thunderous pulse. The condensation drips in unpredictable, arrhythmic syncopation onto hard surfaces, adding a violent, staccato rattle to the drone. It is an anthill of acoustic violence, the sound of a thousand simultaneous construction projects built with no plan and no peace.

But the sound is merely the herald of the place's true horror: the instability. The dynamism here is not seasonal, not diurnal—it is constant, chaotic, a Solaris-like churn of creation and un-creation that refuses to hold a shape for more than moments. Step into what seems like a broad, vine-arched boulevard, and within the time it takes to traverse it, the walls are already closing. The tendrils reach out, feeling the space, replicating the geometry of your passage into new architecture. A plaza is clear one minute; the next, a massive succulent stalk, thick as a silo, erupts from the loam, its flesh still translucent with birth-fluids, blocking the path. An entire building—a dome of interlocking fronds that might have been a temple or a granary—collapses into rot as a blight passes through it in seconds, only for the rot to be immediately consumed by a carnivorous moss that patches the hole with a new, darker wall. The city is building and demolishing itself with you inside it, without reference to you, a closed loop of biological ambition that will not stop.

The weirdness sets in when you try to find a boundary. You push through a curtain of hanging moss, and find yourself... somewhere identical. The geometry refuses to resolve. Paths curve and split, but the splits are not forks in a road—they are splits in the plant itself, one stem dividing into two living corridors, each equally valid, each equally real. You see a distant grove of pillar-cacti, and walk toward them for an hour, only to find they are now behind you, without having moved. The leaves seem to orient toward you, not just toward the light, following your passage with a slow, grinding rustle that adds another voice to the deafening chorus. You find structures that look like rooms—hollow boles with shelves of shelf-fungus, basins of collected rainwater, hammocks of woven vine—and they feel arranged. Purposeful. But there is no purpose here. There is no animal to inhabit the room, no tool to rest on the shelf. The architecture is a mimic, a reflection of function with no function to reflect, as if the plants have dreamed the shape of a life that does not exist.

The light becomes the enemy of certainty. The warm, golden light falls evenly on everything, but the density of the growth creates an underworld of shadows just a few feet in. The boundaries between zones blur; a vine-wall becomes a passage becomes a wall again. Surfaces are slick with condensation, and every drip changes the shape of what it falls upon.

Time is meaningless in the green city. You might find a pocket of stillness—a small, enclosed grotto of ferns, wet with a fresh rain, the roar of the outside growth muffled—and sit for what feels like hours. When you emerge, the entire district around you has regrown. You cannot go back. The grotto is gone, its geometry subsumed by a new growth of tube-like stems. The erosion patterns have shifted. The sounds have changed pitch. The city is not a place you are in; it is a process you are witnessing, a turbulent engine of vegetable matter that will not remember you were here.

There are no animals. There is no technology. There is no stone. There is only the plant, the damp, the heat, the light, the roar, and the awful, accelerating churn of a living world that is building itself out of nothing, toward nothing, with all the manic energy of a fever dream.
