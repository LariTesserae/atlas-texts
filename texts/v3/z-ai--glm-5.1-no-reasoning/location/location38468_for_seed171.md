You do not walk here. You are absorbed.

The plateau came into existence only moments ago—or epochs ago; time thickens at this altitude and cannot be counted reliably. It is a high place, though the word "mountain" implies an erosion, a wearing-down into shape. There has been no wearing here. This terrain is still arriving, still extruding itself upward from some inexhaustible geological throat, a continent-scale mesa birthing itself into a cool, dim firmament. The rock—if it can be called rock—is raw, unweathered, a dark mineral paste that hasn't yet decided on its crystalline structure. It is warm and yielding underfoot, like the flesh of a gum just cut, pushing upward at a pace the eye cannot track but the body can feel: a constant, imperceptible surging. You are standing on a geology that is still in the act of becoming, a pure and massive emergence the size of a small world.

And before the stone can even harden, the plant-life takes it.

This is not a landscape with plants; it is a landscape that *is* plant. The vegetation is total, an existential saturation. There is no gap, no bare patch, no breathing room between presences. The moment the raw stone pushes into the cool air, it is colonized by a pale, rhizomic substrate—a living felt of mycelium and taproot that weaves the soft geology together before gravity can pull it apart. The flora does not grow from the ground; it is the ground. Every surface, every cliff-face, every horizontal plane is a thick, pulsing mat of interlocking vegetative mass. Towering structures rise from the plateau—not built, but grown, though "grown" implies a passive, organic patience. These are aggressive, engineered structures. Massive trunk-columns, hundreds of meters wide, buttress the edges of the plateau, their bark fused into load-bearing walls. Canopy-vaults arch overhead, so densely interwoven that they form a vaulted ceiling, a vegetative architecture that braces the very sky. The plant has become the substrate, the scaffolding, the edifice. It is a pure-plant ecosystem, and it fills all available space, pressing in from every direction, leaving no void unfilled.

It is dim beneath this endless ceiling. The light that filters down from the gray, high-altitude sky is a thin, greenish luminescence, absorbed and reflected by the layered canopies above. It is a perpetual twilight, a cool, aqueous dusk that makes the air feel heavy and slick. And the air *is* slick. The atmosphere is saturated, condensing. At this altitude, the air pressure should be thin, but the vegetation breathes out such immense volumes of vapor that the sky sweats. Water is everywhere, though it does not fall as rain. It weeps. The colossal trunk-columns run with continuous tears, their bark channeling rivulets down into the raw stone. Condensation beads on every leaf, every filament, every tendril, dripping in an endless, rhythmic percussion. The water table is the surface; you cannot dig a centimeter without hitting warm, seeping water. The stone itself weeps, squeezed by the roots that grip it, bleeding moisture from pores that haven't yet sealed. It is a marsh at the edge of the sky, a high-altitude bog suspended on a fist of rising earth.

And the sound. The sound is annihilating.

It is not the sound of wind, for the plant-mass is too dense for wind to move. It is not the sound of animals, for there are no animals here, no fauna to bark or cry or rustle. It is the sound of the process itself. The plateau is still rising, and as it rises, the raw stone groans and stretches, a subsonic tectonic bellow that vibrates in the marrow of your bones. The vegetation creaks under the strain of its own impossible mass, the trunk-columns grinding against each other as they adjust to the shifting ground, a long, slow, agonizing shriek of compressed cellulose. The weeping water pings and drums against the fungal mats, a staccato rhythm layered over the deep groan of the earth. And above it all, the breathing: the entire vegetative mass is respiring, a vast, continental-scale exhalation of vapor and oxygen, a hiss and rush of gas moving through stomata by the trillions, a white noise so immense it becomes a wall. It is deafening. You cannot hear your own voice. You cannot hear your own heartbeat. The sound presses against your eardrums like a physical weight, filling your skull until you are not hearing it but becoming a part of it, a small, vibrating node in an infinite resonant field.

There is a strangeness here, subtle but inescapable. The vegetation is too efficient, too precise. The trunk-columns do not merely grow; they are *engineered*, their internal structures a lattice of hexagonal vessels that distribute water and mechanical stress with a mathematical perfection no blind evolution produces. The rhizomic substrate does not simply colonize; it *computes*, routing nutrients and moisture along pathways that shift in real-time in response to strain, as if the entire plant-mass were a single, vast, slow-thinking processor. This is post-modern technology, biology pushed beyond the organic into something that current physics can describe only partially: a nanoscale orchestration of carbon and water, a living superstructure that treats the laws of thermodynamics as suggestions. The roots do not merely grip the stone; they *alter* it, depositing minerals in patterns that reinforce the emerging geology, literally growing the plateau into stability. It is a feedback loop of such complexity that it blurs the line between growth and manufacture. The architecture has no architect, but the architecture is *intentional*.

There are no artifacts here. No constructed structures, no made things. And yet, the place feels *designed*. The canopy-vaults channel the condensation into precise watercourses. The trunk-columns are spaced with a rhythm that is too regular to be accidental. The raw stone beneath rises in patterns that mirror the root-networks above, as if the earth is being sculpted by the life that grips it, a chisel made of cellulose and patience. This is an undeveloped world, untouched by hands, but it is not unshaped. It is shaping itself.

Stand here—if you can find footing on the yielding, weeping stone—and feel the continent rise beneath you. Feel the deafening hum of a world being built, not by tools, but by the relentless, mathematical pushing of root and rock and water. Watch the dim green light filter through the canopy-vaults, illuminating the endless, dripping walls of living architecture. Breathe the thick, wet air, saturated with the exhalations of a trillion breathing pores. You are inside a process. You are standing in the throat of a living engine, a high-altitude cathedral of raw geology and total vegetation, and it is still coming into being, and it is deafening, and it is vast, and there is no room for you here, and yet here you are, absorbed into the weeping, growing, roaring mass of it, and it will not stop for you.
