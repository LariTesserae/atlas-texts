# The Drowned Steppe-Cities

The first thing you notice is the weight of the water.

Not the cold — it's warm, bathwater-warm, the kind of warmth that doesn't register until you've been in it long enough to forget air existed. The sea here holds its heat like a complaint. It presses against everything equally: the stone, the growing things, the slow bodies that drift through the greenish murk. You feel it on your skin as permission, almost. The world has already decided to be submerged. Resistance was never an option.

---

## The Geometry of Emergence

This place is still becoming.

That's the strangest thing. Not the water, not the warmth, not even the way light arrives in thick columns that seem to hold their shape like architectural features. The strangeness is that the ground beneath you isn't finished. The plateau — if you can call it that, this vast highland submerged beneath thirty, forty, sixty meters of warm ocean — has the texture of something still setting. The basalt is soft. Not soft like mud, but soft like clay that hasn't been fired, like bone that hasn't calcified. You can press your thumb into it and leave an impression that slowly, over hours, fills back in as if the stone is breathing.

Fissures vent warm mineral water, but not with the violent enthusiasm of hydrothermal vents. These seep. They exude. The water from below is only slightly warmer than the water above — maybe a degree, maybe two — and it carries a taste of iron and sulfur and something else, something without a name, a mineral that hasn't been categorized because the geology here doesn't follow catalogues. The stone is riddled with vesicles and channels, a three-dimensional sponge of proto-rock, and through these channels the deep water circulates with the patience of a circulatory system that has nowhere to be.

Erosion hasn't begun. The water shapes nothing because the stone hasn't yet decided what it is. It's pre-erosion — the landscape exists in that brief window between formation and dissolution, and it may stay here for geological ages, this soft highland under its burden of warm sea, because the processes that would carve it are the same processes that are still building it. The plateau grows upward at roughly the rate the water would wear it down. A standing wave of geology.

## The City That Was Planted

They built before they knew what they were building.

The settlement sprawls across a section of the plateau where the stone is slightly more consolidated — still soft, but holdable, workable. The architecture is primitive in the truest sense: *primus*, first. These people — if they are people; they might be something adjacent, something that adapted to submersion so long ago that the distinction is academic — they work with hand tools. Stone on stone. No metal. Their cutting implements are made from the harder nodules found deeper in the plateau, chert-like inclusions that the soft matrix releases reluctantly. They chip these into edges, and with these edges they carve.

But carving is the wrong word. *Sculpting* is closer. *Planting* might be closest of all.

The buildings are grown as much as constructed. The soft stone yields to sustained pressure, and the builders have learned that if they work a channel into the rock and maintain it, the stone doesn't fully close back up. It accommodates. Over generations — and they measure time differently here, in the slow cycles of water temperature and bioluminescent bloom — they've carved out chambers, passages, courtyards. The settlement has the organic quality of a coral reef, but it's not accretive. It's subtractive. They've removed stone to make space, and the stone, in its unfinished state, has accepted the removal.

Doorways are rounded, smoothed by the passage of bodies over years. Walls bear the dimpled texture of thumbwork. Ceilings are low — the stone above hasn't been fully committed to being a ceiling yet, and the builders leave it thick, trusting the pressure of the water to hold it in place. Rooms connect without corridors, one chamber opening into another like the chambers of a heart. There is no straight line in the city. Everything curves, accommodates, breathes.

The effect is intimate. The density is not the density of a modern city — stacked and segregated and efficient — but the density of a nest. Everything touches. Walls are shared because walls are still becoming walls, and the boundary between your chamber and your neighbor's is more a suggestion than a fact. Sound carries through the stone as a low thrum, more felt than heard, and you can know your neighbor is home by the subtle vibration of their movement propagating through the soft rock.

## The Vegetation: A Forest in Solution

The lushness here is not the lushness of land.

There is no soil. There is no sunlight worth speaking of — the water above absorbs most of it, and what reaches the plateau comes as a dim emerald glow, diffuse, sourceless, as if the water itself is the light. And yet: growth. Abundant, dense, layered growth that turns the city and the plateau into something more like a thicket than a settlement.

The plants — if they are plants; they photosynthesize, or something like it, using the dim light and the chemical gradients that seep from the stone — are mostly thalloid. Sheets and ruffles and fans. No stems to speak of. No roots. They attach directly to the stone, and the soft stone accepts them, the tissue of the plant mingling with the tissue of the rock in a way that makes the boundary unclear. Some of the larger growths seem to be holding the buildings together, their flat leaf-bodies spanning cracks, sealing chambers, reinforcing arches. The builders encourage this. They plant by pressing a fragment of growth against a surface and waiting. The vegetation does the rest.

The canopy — if it can be called that when there is no sky — rises in layers. Close to the stone, a turf of small round pads, coin-sized, olive and rust, overlapping like scales. Above them, the mid-layer: fan-shaped fronds a meter across, translucent, their edges rippling in the slow thermals, filtering the dim light into something even dimmer and greener below. And above that, the towers: great ribbon-like organisms that extend upward five, ten meters, swaying in the almost-imperceptible currents, their surfaces catching what little light remains and reflecting it downward in shifting geometric patterns.

Walking — swimming — through the settlement, you pass through these layers. The pads brush your feet. The fans shade your head. The ribbons tower overhead like the columns of a nave, and the light that filters through them moves like the light through stained glass in a cathedral at the bottom of the sea.

It smells — and the water does carry scent, dissolved and diffuse — of chlorophyll and iron and the clean ozone of slow metabolism. The smell of a place where nothing has died recently because everything is still busy being born.

## The Fauna: Absence as Presence

The animals are rare, and their rarity makes them significant.

Mostly, they are small. Pale things, eyeless or nearly so, that move along the stone-plant interface eating whatever they find there — detritus, growth tips, each other. They have the simplified body plans of creatures that evolved in a stable environment with few threats and fewer opportunities. Some are crustacean-like, with too many legs and a tendency to freeze when disturbed. Others are gelatinous, barely differentiated from the water they swim through, visible only when they cross a shaft of light.

The one large creature you might encounter — you might not; there seem to be only a handful in the entire region — is something the builders have a name for that translates roughly to "the one who was already here." It's a grazer. Four meters long, flattened, with a mouth like a sand dollar and a body like a mattress that has learned to swim. It moves through the vegetation with the unhurried confidence of something that has never been preyed upon, cropping the fan-fronds with a sound like cloth tearing, very slowly. When it passes through the settlement, the builders pause in their work and watch it. Not with fear. With something closer to recognition. It was here before the city. It will be here after. It is part of the slow process that made the plateau and that the plateau is still becoming.

There are no predators. The ecosystem is too young, too simple, too warm and stable to have developed the arms race of predation yet. Everything eats the vegetation or the detritus or each other on a scale so small it hardly counts. The big creature eats plants. The builders eat plants and the small crustacean things. Nothing eats the builders. This fact shapes the city more than any tool or technique. A city without walls. A city where the doors are open and the stone is soft because there is nothing to keep out.

## The Light and the Sound

Dim. The light is dim.

Not dark — dark would be simpler. Dark you could fill with imagination. This dimness is specific. It's the light of a place that is receiving just enough energy to see, and no more. Your eyes adjust, and adjust again, and then there's no further adjustment possible. You see in greens and grays. The reds are gone, absorbed in the first meters of water above. The world is monochrome the way an old photograph is monochrome — not because color isn't there, but because your instruments can't detect it.

The light has texture. It comes from above, but above is not a direction here; it's a condition. The water above is lighter, that's all, and the lighter water presses down against the dimmer water, and the boundary between them shifts with the tides and the seasons and the slow metabolic rhythms of the vegetation. Sometimes the light brightens, and you can see the full extent of the city — the curving walls, the planted chambers, the tower-ribbons swaying — and it's beautiful in the way that a thing can be beautiful when you weren't expecting beauty. Sometimes the light dims further, and the city contracts to the range of your outstretched hand, and the stone breathes against your fingers.

Sound: the water carries it, but there's little to carry. The settlement is quiet. The tools are stone on stone, which underwater is a muffled click, more tactile than auditory. The vegetation rustles in the currents, but the currents are slow, and the rustle is a whisper. The big grazer makes a low grinding sound as it feeds, and this carries for hundreds of meters, a sustained baritone drone that becomes the background music of whatever section of the city it's visiting. The builders vocalize — they must, they have language — but their voices are adapted to water, low and resonant, more like whale song than speech, and they use them sparingly. A phrase to greet. A phrase to coordinate. Long silences in between.

The dominant sound is the sound of the seeps. The mineral water venting from the stone. It doesn't hiss or roar; it hums. A low constant tone, different in each chamber because each chamber's stone has a different density and a different flow rate, and the combination produces a different note. The city is tuned like an instrument. The builders didn't design it that way, but they live within it, and some chambers are favored because they sound right, and others are avoided because they don't. Music, here, is architecture.

## The People and Their Tools

Their technology is the technology of patience.

They work with stone. They work with the plants. They work with the water itself, which they've learned to direct through channels and chambers to shape the soft rock faster than it would shape itself. They have no metal. They have no fire — there is nothing to burn and no air to burn it in. They have no wheels, no pulleys, no levers except the levers of their own bodies, which are strong in the way that bodies become strong when they move through resistant medium all their lives.

Their tools are cutting stones, grinding stones, stones for pressing, stones for scoring. They've developed a kit of maybe fifteen distinct tool forms, each suited to a specific task, and the knowledge of how to make and use them is passed from hand to hand. There are no specialists. Everyone builds. Everyone plants. Everyone maintains. The city is a communal organism in a way that goes beyond metaphor; the labor that sustains it is as distributed and undifferentiated as the labor of cells in a body.

They harvest the vegetation for food, taking the growth tips of the fans and the pads, which regrow quickly in the warm nutrient-rich water. They harvest the small crustacean-things by laying flat stones as traps; the creatures shelter under stones, and the builders simply lift the stones and collect what they find. They harvest the grazer, occasionally, when one of the great creatures dies its slow natural death, and they treat this as both a gift and a mourning, and they use every part.

They make fabric from the ribbon-towers, processing the long strips through a method of soaking and pressing that produces something like felt — dense, warm, water-resistant. They wear it wrapped around their bodies, not for warmth but for protection against the occasional sharp edge of unworked stone and the minor abrasions of daily life in a world of growing things.

They count time by the light. The cycles are slow — the dimming and brightening above them happen over durations that would correspond to months in a surface world, though they have no concept of months. They have "long light" and "short light" and the gradations between, and they plan their building and planting accordingly. Some chambers are workable only in long light, when the stone is slightly softer. Some plantings take only during short light, when the growth is more conservative and more likely to hold.

## The Weirdness at the Center

And yet.

There's something wrong. Or not wrong — that's a judgment. There's something *other* about this place that doesn't resolve into any of its components.

The stone is too soft. The plateau is too high. The water is too warm and too shallow for this depth, and the depth is too great for this warmth. The vegetation grows too well for the light that feeds it. The ecosystem is too simple for its stability. The city is too functional for its primitiveness. Each of these can be explained — geological oddities, chemical gradients, evolutionary contingencies, accumulated traditional knowledge — but together they form a pattern that feels intentional, as if the place were designed to be exactly this: a warm shallow sea on a high soft plateau, with a city of stone and plant in the dim green light, inhabited by patient people with simple tools, changing almost imperceptibly across long durations.

The builders have a story about this. They say the plateau is a thought that the water is thinking. The stone is the thought becoming solid. The vegetation is the thought becoming alive. The city is the thought becoming known. They say this without any particular reverence or mysticism, the way you might describe the water cycle or the tides. It's just how it works. The world is thinking, and they live in the thinking, and their own slow work — the carving, the planting, the maintaining — is part of the thought.

You can feel it, sometimes, in the pause between one breath and the next. The whole place is holding still. Not frozen — holding. The way a held breath holds. The way a thought holds before it becomes a word. The plateau, the water, the city, the green dim light, the hum of the seeps — all of it waiting with the patience of something that has nowhere to go because it's already where it needs to be.

The water presses against your skin. The stone breathes under your hand. The fans ripple overhead. And somewhere, in the slow warm darkness of the deep-embedded channels, the world is still making itself.
