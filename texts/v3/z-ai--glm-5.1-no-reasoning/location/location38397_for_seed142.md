The plateau of Uval-Kth extends farther than the eye can trust, a vast, high-altitude shelf of mineral crust suspended three kilometers above the basalt foundations of the world. Here, at the end of geology, everything has settled. The mountains that once fractured the sky were worn down billions of years ago; their dust compressed into the flat, iron-hard pan that now stretches to a horizon perpetually blurred by warm, stagnant haze. It is a primordial flatness, a landscape that has survived into its absolute final form. Nothing grows. Nothing flows. The world has exhausted its gradients, leaving only an eternal, breathless equilibrium.

The warmth is omnipresent and utterly dry, radiating upward from the fused silica of the ground and downward from the dense, insulating atmosphere. It is a temperature that possesses the sickly consistency of a low-grade fever—uncomfortable against the skin, yet lacking the kinetic violence of true heat. There is no wind to carry it away. The air hangs in thick, stratified layers, trapping the warmth in a permanent, still oven. 

Punctuating this boundless, flattened expanse are the Settlements. They rise from the crust like massive, rectilinear blisters—dense, multi-tiered infrastructures of blackened iron and soot-stained brick. They are towns built vertically out of necessity, their footprints kept compact, their walls layered upward into the murky dark. Within these grids of organized construction, the architecture is purely industrial. Rows of narrow ventilation stacks, the skeletal arms of cranes locked permanently in place, and massive gear housings define the skyline. The streets are deep, shadowed canyons cutting through the built environment, yet they feel entirely empty. The density of construction is offset by a profound sparseness of presence. There are no crowds, no bustling markets—only the sheer, overwhelming weight of engineered mass taking up space.

Light barely exists here. A deep, bruised twilight permeates everything, soaked up by the dark stone and the soot that clings to it. The only illumination comes from the Settlements themselves: the faint, sulfurous glow of sodium vapor barely piercing the gloom, and the occasional molten-orange fissure of a blast furnace bleeding heat into the dark. It is a world of outlines and shadows, where the far edges of the plateau dissolve entirely into the black air. 

Yet, for all its industrial might and thermal exhalation, the plateau is nearly silent. The acoustic landscape is defined by what it lacks: the absence of wind, the absence of water, the absence of voices. The only sound is the subsonic thrum of immense subterranean engines—mechanical hearts buried deep beneath the iron foundations of the towns. It is a frequency felt in the marrow rather than heard, a constant, low-frequency shudder that cycles endlessly. Occasionally, the stillness is broken by the rhythmic, muffled clank of a piston stroke in the mid-distance, the sound traveling through the dense air before being swallowed by the vast emptiness. 

Fauna is a statistical anomaly here. Once in an age, something is seen—a pale, multi-limbed shape scuttling along the rim of a cooling runoff channel, or a leathery, eyeless glider riding the faint thermal vents between the factory stacks. These creatures are vestigial, built of chitin and raw survival, moving with mechanical, hunger-driven purpose. They do not belong to an ecosystem, for there is none; they are solitary glitches, wandering anomalies that drift through the warm dark and vanish just as silently.

The strangeness of Uval-Kth settles into the bones the longer one stands in its warmth. It is a place built around the mechanics of a life that seems to have already departed. These colossal engines, these towering smokestacks and layered industrial grids—they do not extract, refine, or produce anything observable. There are no cargo trains leaving the plateau, no ships on the horizon, no raw materials being pulled from the crust. The factories run to run. The heat is generated to maintain the heat. The machines have settled into a closed loop, a mechanical metabolism perfectly adapted to a world where time has stopped. 

The few inhabitants—human or otherwise—seem less like citizens and more like yet another mechanism in the infrastructure. They move through the dark, warm streets with a slow, deliberate inevitability, their footfalls adding a soft, rhythmic percussion to the endless engine thrum. They maintain the machines because the machines must be maintained. They do not speak. They do not look toward the horizon.

There is no erosion left to shape the land, no water to carve new paths, no life to break the stone. The plateau exists in a frozen equilibrium, a perfectly sealed system of warmth and industry and dark. It is the final, stable state of the world—sitting quietly in the black, endlessly cycling through its simple, purposeless motions, waiting for an end that the mathematics of the place promise will never come.
