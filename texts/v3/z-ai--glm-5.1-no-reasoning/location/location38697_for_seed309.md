# The Churn

You descend. Not through a tunnel — through a throat.

The stairs are roots. Not root-covered, not root-flanked: *root*. Each step is a knee-buckling warp of lignin laid down in seasonal pulses, growth rings rippling under your boots like compromised muscle. The railing is bark. The walls are bark. The air tastes like bark — that astringent, tannic dryness that means something enormous is respiring around you. You are inside something that has been alive longer than the settlement it swallowed.

---

The settlement is called Vask, though no one built it. Vask *grew* — or more precisely, Vask was *grown into*. Sixty years ago this was a factory floor. The Ghent Produce Company sank it below grade to maintain humidity for mushroom cultivation: concrete bins, irrigation channels, steam pipes, the whole rational apparatus of industrial food. They operated for eleven years. Then the bluestain spores arrived, and the mycelium, and then the vines, and then the things that came for the vines. The company boarded the entrance. The city forgot the basement.

The basement did not forget itself. It kept growing.

Now Vask occupies the space of a modest cathedral — a single vaulted chamber roughly sixty meters long and twenty wide, with alcoves and side rooms that branch into darkness where the original factory infrastructure once stood. The ceiling is a latticework of root and vine so dense it reads as solid, though condensation drips through the gaps in a rhythm that is almost musical. Almost. The intervals are wrong. They follow no pattern you can anticipate, and the waiting between drops builds a small anxiety in the chest that never quite resolves.

The floor is the only surface still recognizably artificial — concrete, cracked and heaved, with slabs tilted at angles that collect the drip-water into scattered pools no larger than a man's hand. The water is brown. It is always brown. It smells like soil and iron and something sweeter underneath, something that reminds you of the inside of your own wrist.

Between the pools, the concrete is carpeted in moss so thick it muffles footsteps. You can walk without making a sound if you try. You will not want to try. The silence here is not peaceful. It is attended. When you go quiet, you can hear the chitin.

---

The fauna are the true architecture of Vask. Not the roots, not the vines — those are substrate, those are *furniture*. The animals are the walls, the doors, the processes. They are overwhelming in the way that weather is overwhelming: not aggressive, simply *inexorable*, too much and too many to negotiate with, a condition of the environment rather than a presence within it.

The beetles come first. Carabids, fist-sized, with elytra the color of petrol spills. They move in lines — processional, purposeful, carrying fragments of leaf-litter and mycelial mat between their mandibles like workers carrying bricks. You will see hundreds in the first minute. Thousands in the first hour. They do not bite. They do not startle. They walk over your boots if you stand still long enough, and their tarsal claws catch in the fabric with a sound like tiny Velcro tears, and you will feel the vibration of their movement through the moss before you see them, a low shivering in the pads of your feet, like standing on a phone set to vibrate.

The beetles maintain Vask. They clear the root-choked channels that were once irrigation pipes. They prune the fungal blooms that threaten to choke the air exchanges. They compost the dead. You will find their midden-heaps in the alcoves — knee-high cones of processed organic matter, steaming faintly, seeded with the pale threads of new mycelium. The whole settlement is a garden, and the beetles are the gardeners, and they have been at it long enough that the garden has reached a kind of equilibrium: settled, weathered, in-its-own-state. The roots no longer crack the remaining concrete. The moss no longer expands its range. Everything holds where it is, in the shape that it has found, like a river that has finished carving its bed.

The spiders come second. Orb-weavers with bodies like ripe plums and leg-spans wider than your open hand. Their webs lace the upper vault — root-gap to root-gap, vine to vine — and they catch the insects that the beetles do not manage: the fungus gnats, the drain flies, the tiny pale moths that spiral up from the compost heaps in slow columns that catch what little light there is and make it visible as particulate fog. The webs are everywhere overhead. You will walk through them. You will feel the silk on your face, your neck, and you will taste the adhesive on your lips, and it will taste like the smell of the brown water, sweet and metallic, and you will understand that the spiders' silk is the same chemistry as the sap that bleeds from the wounded roots, and you will not know what to do with that understanding.

The spiders do not bite either. Nothing in Vask bites. *Nothing needs to.*

---

The third layer of fauna is the one that makes the sound.

Crickets — but wrong. Too large, too numerous, too *rhythmic*. Their stridulation is industrial in its regularity, a churning pulse that fills the chamber at a volume that makes conversation difficult and thought scattered. It is not the lazy singing of a meadow at dusk. It is the racket of a factory floor, of looms and presses and stamping machines, of mechanized production running in three shifts. The crickets are arranged in colonies throughout Vask — in the irrigation channels, in the gaps between tilted concrete slabs, in the hollow butts of the largest roots — and each colony pulses at a slightly different frequency, producing a phase-shifted drone that seems to move through the space like a slow wave, like breath.

You will feel it in your sternum before you identify it as sound. You will think your heart has changed its rhythm. It has not. The crickets have simply become the heartbeat of the space, and your body has mistaken their urgency for its own.

This is normal. This is Vask.

There are other animals — the centipedes that patrol the root-walls like security convoys, leg-rhythm tapping against the bark in a syncopation that undercuts the cricket drone; the pale eyeless salamanders that slip through the moss with a fluidity that makes them seem liquid rather than flesh; the moths, always the moths, in their spiraling columns, raising from the compost heaps like slow smoke from a fire that never catches. But you will not notice these at first. The beetles, the spiders, the crickets — these fill the attention to capacity. The rest emerge only after you have been here long enough to stop flinching.

---

The light in Vask is not absent but *reluctant*. It comes from two sources: the gap at the top of the throat-stairs, where grey daylight filters down in a shaft that narrows to nothing before it reaches the floor; and the bioluminescence of the fungal mats that cling to the root-walls in patches the size of dinner tables. The fungi glow a dim, arterial blue — not the cheerful phosphorescence of storybook caves, but something closer to the light of a screen in a dark room, a light that illuminates without warming, that makes colors wrong. Under the blue glow, the moss looks black. The beetles look metallic. Your own hands look borrowed.

The city above — you can hear it, sometimes, when the cricket-churn dips between pulses. The rumble of trams. The hydraulic sigh of the factory quarter. The muffled percussion of construction. It is strange to think of streets and schedules up there, of coffee and conversation, while down here a parallel city operates with equal complexity and zero reference to human design. Vask is urban. It has infrastructure, density, organization. It has districts — the compost sector, the nursery alcoves where pale root-tips extrude from wounded bark like tongues from mouths, the spawning channels where the salamanders congregate in writhing masses that the beetles carefully circumnavigate. It has traffic. It has labor. It has a kind of governance, if the processional lines of beetles and the territorial spacing of spider-colonies can be read as such.

But it does not have you in mind. This is the strangeness of Vask, the quality that presses against the back of your thoughts like a thumb against a bruise: it is a city that does not care whether you are in it. It does not adapt to your presence. It does not accommodate or exclude. You are here in the way that a stone is in a river — the water moves around you, the processes continue, and if you were gone, the current would close over your absence with less disturbance than a beetle makes stepping over your boot.

---

There is damp. Not rain — there is no sky for rain — but the condensation that weeps from the root-ceiling in the brown-water drops, and the mist that rises from the compost heaps when the cricket-churn raises the temperature, and the slick microbial film that coats every surface below knee-height, making the concrete treacherous and the moss sodden. Your clothes will dampen. Your skin will prickle. The humidity is a weight, a presence, another body in the room with you, pressing close, breathing with you.

And the whole place breathes. The roots expand and contract with a cycle so slow you feel it only as a change in pressure, a fullness and an emptiness in the air, like the chamber itself is a lung. The cricket-churn rises and falls with this cycle. The beetles move faster during the exhale. The spiders rebuild during the inhale. The moths spiral upward during the transition between. Everything here is synchronized to a rhythm that preceded the factory, preceded the city, preceded perhaps the very concept of a human witness — a vegetal biorhythm as old as the first root that found darkness and decided to grow into it.

You are inside that rhythm now. You are inside the Churn.

Your heartbeat has synced. You haven't noticed yet.

---

When you leave — if you leave — the throat-stairs will feel steeper than they did on the way down. The roots will feel more like walls. The daylight at the top will be an assault, a violence of clarity, and the city above will seem flimsy, temporary, a film of human intention stretched over a deeper and more durable churn that is always running underneath.

The beetles will not follow you to the surface. The crickets will not chase. The throat will close behind you with nothing more dramatic than a change in air pressure and the cessation of a sound you did not realize was still present until it stops.

You will carry the humidity in your clothes. You will carry the blue glow behind your eyes. You will carry the rhythm in your chest, and for days — weeks — your own heartbeat will feel slightly borrowed, slightly *grown*, slightly as though it belongs to a body that is not entirely yours.

This is normal. This is what Vask does.

The Churn continues.
