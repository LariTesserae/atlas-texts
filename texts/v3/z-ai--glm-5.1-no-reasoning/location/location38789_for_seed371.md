The gorge is barely thirty meters from wall to wall, a narrow wound cut fresh into the flank of a mountain that is still deciding whether it wants to exist. The rock is raw—gneiss and feldspar sheared at angles so sharp they look machined, free of the rounding that millennia of ice and wind would impose. This is young stone. It was born yesterday, geologically speaking, thrust upward by tectonic violence and now standing in extreme, breathless elevation, its walls climbing vertically out of sight into a darkness that never fully relents.

There is no sky here. Or rather, there is, but it is so far above the slot of this gorge that it shrinks to a charcoal smear, a slit of dim grayish light that provides no illumination, only a suggestion of the weight of the atmosphere above. The true light comes from below. 

Across the floor of the gorge, in a braided, chaotic lattice, run iron pipes. They are the only horizontal things in this vertical place—rough-cast, riveted, six inches to two feet in diameter, branching and rejoining in industrial illogic. Steam vents from pressure valves at irregular intervals, and it is this venting that provides the illumination: behind each valve, a small mesh cage holds a chunk of calcium carbide that burns with a fierce, trembling white flare as the moisture-rich steam hits it. The light is industrial, stuttering, throwing hard shadows that leap and collapse. The pipes sweat. The rock sweats. Everything is damp.

Not wet—damp. The kind of ambient moisture that never accumulates into a puddle but coats every surface in a film that never dries. Condensation weeps down the rock faces in slow, uneven trails, pooling in the sharp divots and fresh gouges of the young stone, feeding what grows here without ever raining. There is no rain in this gorge. There is only the ceaseless exhalation of the pipes and the cold breath of the mountain, meeting to form a humid atmosphere that hovers at the threshold of drinkable.

And what grows here is extraordinary.

The vegetation has claimed every surface that does not burn. Thick, rubbery vines of a deep chlorophyll black—so dark they seem to drink the carbide light rather than reflect it—climb the pipe networks and the rock walls in equal measure, indifferent to the distinction between the made and the geological. They do not merely grow; they surge. You can watch them move. Not in the patient, imperceptible creep of normal plants, but in a restless, searching oscillation, tendrils extending several centimeters in the hours between steam-vents, then retracting when the heat comes. Between the vines, a dense carpet of something like moss but tougher, more like felted wire, covers the stone in layers so thick the original rock is invisible beneath. It is olive-drab at the surface, shading to a bruised burgundy where the moisture collects. Small, fibrous cups face upward, catching the condensation, opening and closing with an audible papery rustle.

Everything here is loud. The sound is relentless and comes from everywhere at once: the deep, thudding pulse of fluid moving through the pipes under extreme pressure; the sharp, shrieking exhale of the steam valves, each one cycling on its own inscrutable timer, some every few seconds, some every several minutes; the creak and groan of the pipes expanding and contracting with temperature shifts; the wet, tearing sound of the vegetation adjusting its grip. It is a mechanical roar filtered through organic acoustics, a constant tidal noise that fills the narrow gorge from wall to wall, leaving no silence, no gap in which to think clearly. The sound bounces off the young, sharp rock and comes back harder, stripped of echo-softness, all edges.

And nothing with a spine or a heartbeat lives here. No insects click in the moss. No birds call from the ledges above. The only movement is the mechanical and the vegetal, and they are so entangled now that they have become a single system. The pipes were laid—by whom, for what purpose, there is no artifact here to say—and the vegetation came, and neither has won dominance. Instead, they have accommodated. Root systems have wrapped around valve housings and grown into the rivet gaps, and in places the pipes have heated the vines enough to scar them into hard, black calluses that then served as anchor points for new growth. The steam has killed what it touched and fed what survived. The system destroys and creates itself continuously, a turbulent metabolism that tears at its own infrastructure and builds over the wounds.

Where a pipe has burst—a section blown out by pressure, a joint corroded by the constant damp—the vegetation has grown fastest, drinking the enriched runoff, creating a knotted, tumorous bulge of new growth that itself forces more strain on the adjacent pipe sections, creating new bursts, new feed points. The gorge is a self-accelerating loop, an engine of growth-powered destruction, running without oversight in the cold, damp dark.

At the floor of the gorge, where the pipes lie thickest and the steam rises in columns that the carbide lights turn to columns of illuminated vapor, the noise is loudest and the growth is densest. You have to push through the vines to walk. They do not resist, exactly—they give, springy and slick underfoot, then close behind you, erasing your passage within minutes. The condensation soaks through any fabric in moments. The cold is a damp cold, barely above the temperature where breath would fog, if there were enough light to see breath.

The walls of the gorge narrow further as you move along the floor, the pipe network growing denser, the steam-vents more frequent, until the space becomes almost cave-like, a horizontal crack in the mountain's side where the industrial and the botanical have fused into a single breathing, clanking, rustling mass. There is no end to it that anyone has found. The pipes keep coming, from somewhere deeper in the mountain, and the vegetation keeps eating them, and the steam keeps venting, and the dark keeps pressing down from above, and the water keeps weeping from the stone, and the whole small, tight, enclosed place shudders with the effort of its own turbulent, unceasing becoming.
