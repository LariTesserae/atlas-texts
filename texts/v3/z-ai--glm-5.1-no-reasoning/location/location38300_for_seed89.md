The throat of the world narrows here, compressing the sky into a distant, bruised slit far above. You are deep inside a cirque—a cathedral-sized amphitheater of ancient, weathered stone, walled in on all sides by extreme elevations. The rock is old, its edges long since surrendered to eons of wind and patient frost. It is settled, balanced, rounded into vast, sloping shoulders and sweeping groins of granite that feel less like carved shapes and more like the slow exhalations of the mountain itself, frozen in time.

Despite the crushing altitude, the air is strangely warm, heated by geothermal breath seeping from below and trapped within the sheltered stone bowl. Against this warmth, a constant, slick dampness clings to everything. It is not the damp of open water, but of ceaseless condensation. Moisture exhales from the porous rock, gathers into heavy beads, and weeps down the granite in a perpetual, sluggish tear-track. At the lowest point of the cirque, where the walls pinch together to form a narrow, slot-like throat, these tears converge into a thin, black rivulet that slides away into the abyss. Occasionally, a faint, warm mist drifts through the chamber, clinging to the stone like a living shroud.

The light is almost nonexistent. The world above is reduced to a pale, anemic ribbon of grey, far too weak to reach the floor of this natural cathedral. What little illumination exists is stolen and hoarded by the teeming life that absolutely saturates this place. The vegetation is sparse—there is no soil for roots, no sustained sunlight for leaves—but the fauna here is overwhelming, a dense, layered biomass that defies the darkness and the altitude.

Every square inch of the stone is claimed. The walls are thickly armored with vast, overlapping colonies of blind, pale isopods, their chitinous plates clicking and rustling as they graze on the microscopic fungal blooms that thrive in the warm condensation. Between them, in the crevices and damp hollows, clusters of finger-thick, bioluminescent grubs pulse with a feeble, sickly green light. It is this ghostly glow that allows you to perceive the sheer, claustrophobic density of the space. The air is thick with the flutter of pale, leather-winged moths, their bodies the size of a human fist, navigating the warm updrafts with low, throbbing hums.

The sound is immense, a suffocating tide of biological noise that echoes against the ancient stone. It is a continuous, vibrating drone composed of millions of tiny sounds: the wet, scraping mandibles of the isopods; the rhythmic, squelching contraction of the grubs; the papery rustle of moth wings brushing against the damp rock; the steady, metronomic *drip-drip* of condensation falling from the high arches into the rivulet below. It is loud enough to feel in the chest, a low-frequency hum that vibrates in the marrow of your bones.

There are no human artifacts here. No carved steps, no spent brass, no discarded tools. The only technology is biological: the precisely evolved mandibles of the insects, the heat-seeking pits of the moths, the silken threads woven by blind spiders the size of plates, webbed thick across the narrower passages to catch the constant rain of condensation and the hapless flutterers.

The rules of this place are simple, ancient, and merciless. The mountain provides stone and warmth; the stone weeps water; the water feeds the fungi; the fungi feed the teeming masses of chitin and flesh. It is a closed loop, a slow, grinding cycle of survival that has existed in perfect, weathered balance for millennia. The dynamic is glacial—populations rise and fall over centuries, the rivulet carves a millimeter deeper every thousand years. Nothing here is in a hurry.

You stand in the warm, wet dark, pressed close by the encroaching walls, surrounded on all sides by the clicking, rustling, pulsing presence of overwhelming life. The air tastes of calcium and warm moisture. The darkness breathes. And the slow, patient heartbeat of the mountain drums on, indifferent and immense.
