The Greenworks is a chamber, though "chamber" is a word that implies a harshness of boundary that does not exist here. It is a space roughly the size of a cathedral nave, but organic, enclosed by the interlocking bodies of colossal bamboo-like grasses and the sprawling buttresses of fungal-rooted ancient ferns. There is no mineral substrate—no soil, no stone, no earth. To step into the Greenworks is to step onto a living mesh of root and rhizome, a woven floor that yields like damp loam beneath your feet. 

Water is the lungs of this place. It does not flow; it exhales. The atmosphere sits close to the dew point, perpetually saturated, and every surface weeps. Moisture condenses on the waxy cuticles of overhead leaves, gathering into trembling orbs that hang, heavy and lens-like, before releasing in a constant, arrhythmic percussion. It is a loud place. The sound is not chaotic, but it is overwhelming—a low, rhythmic thumping of hydraulic pumps beneath the floor matting, the hiss of pressurized sap forcing through xylem, and the steady, resonant *drip-splat* of the weeping canopy. It drowns out footsteps and softens the edges of thought. 

Vegetation is not an inhabitant of the Greenworks; it is the architecture, the infrastructure, and the citizenry. The space is packed, saturated with life leaving no void. Fleshy vines thick as a human thigh spiral around hollow stalks. Fungal shelves layer the lower darkness, and mosses fill every remaining crevice like green mortar. There is no sky, only overlapping tiers of leaf and stem, filtering the normal, ambient luminescence—radiating from bioluminescent algae in the water veins—into a diffuse, sterile green-white glow that leaves no shadows, only depths.

Scattered throughout this total biological occupation are the remains of an older, colder intent. Sheets of riveted brass, green with verdigris and weeping rust-water, are bolted into the plant-flesh. Iron pipes, swallowed by expanding bark, still shudder with the deep, slow sigh of pressurized fluid. A single, massive boiler—shaped like a beetle's carapace—sits crucified by root-tendrils in the center of the chamber, its access hatch yawning. Out of its rusted grates, the local flora has forced a thick, rigid stalk, transforming the machine into a flower pot. The industrial is not dead here; it has been colonized, but it still functions, repurposed by the ecosystem. Copper roots tap into old power lines, and the thumping pumps beneath the floor are driven by the slow conversion of sugar to torque, a mechanized heartbeat run on chlorophyll. 

The Greenworks changes, but with the patience of wood. It is a place of slow dynamic, of barely perceptible gradients. A brass gear might take a decade to halt, choked by the gradual tightening of a vine. An entire walkway might collapse as a support stalk rots from within, but it happened over seasons, settling inch by inch. The air is warm, thick with the humidity of compost and the sharp, metallic scent of ozone from the old generators. 

Fauna is almost entirely absent. There are no grazing herds, no chittering insects, no birds to eat the seeds. The plants have no predators. They fill every niche, consume every resource, and wage their wars entirely through growth and suffocation. If there are animals at all, they are singular, creeping things—perhaps a gelatinous, eyeless mantis the size of a dog, wandering the upper catwalks, scraping lichen from the steaming pipes, so rare it feels like a hallucination.

The strangeness of the Greenworks creeps up on you. It is felt in the rhythm of the pumps, which syncopate with the dripping water to form a low, thudding music. It is seen in the way the light catches the hanging droplets, turning them into optical lenses that reflect not the canopy above, but old blueprints—schematics for engines that have never been built, flickering in the water's surface before the drop falls. It is a closed loop, a biological factory with no product, a perfectly balanced engine of moisture and green that exists solely to maintain its own slow, heavy breathing.
