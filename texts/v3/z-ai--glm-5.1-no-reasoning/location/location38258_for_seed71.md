The plateau goes on forever. It is the oldest thing you will ever stand on—a continental shield of stone so ancient that the concept of age has worn flat alongside it. The elevation is staggering, pushing up into the thin, freezing stratosphere, yet there are no dramatic peaks left. Erosion won billions of years ago. The mountains that once scraped the sky have been ground down to an imperceptible, planetary swell, a near-flatness that nonetheless sits miles above the sea. Because the scale is so vast, the horizon curves away from you in all directions. You can see the weather coming three days in advance—immense, slow-roiling fronts of violet-grey cloud that drift across the expanse like the breath of a sleeping god.

It is cold. Not the biting, howling cold of a blizzard, but a profound, absolute chill that sinks through the soles of your boots and sets up residence in your marrow. The air is thin and mostly quiet, save for the low, ceaseless hiss of wind dragging across billion-year-old bedrock. When the intermittent rains come, they fall softly, without thunder, pooling in the shallow, primordial divots of the stone before slowly seeping away into the endless dark. 

And yet, the place is lush. Unbelievably, impossibly lush. 

Life here is entirely botanical, operating on a scale and logic that makes a mockery of the animal kingdom. There are no beasts, no insects, no birds. No chattering, no hunting, no fleeing. Just the slow, relentless pulse of vegetation. The floor of the plateau is carpeted in immense, spreading mats of crystalline mosses and sprawling, fibrous lichens. They are not green; the cold and the altitude have bred them into shades of deep indigo, bruised magenta, and iridescent black. They grow thick and heavy, trapping the condensation and sparse rain in their micro-root networks. Between these mats are vast stretches of naked, smoothed stone, creating a sparse rhythm across the continent—continents of life separated by oceans of bare, glinting rock. 

The ecosystem is active, but on a glacial, hypnotic timetable. You can watch the processes if you have the patience. The moisture cycle breathes: mist gathers in the afternoons, coalesces into a quiet, persistent drizzle, and is immediately drank by the thirsty moss. The flora breathes in the damp and exhales a faint, luminescent vapor at twilight. Spores are released in invisible, shifting tides that catch the updrafts and migrate across the plateaus. It is an ecosystem in constant, feverish motion, but completely silent.

Scattered across this unimaginable distance, at intervals that require days of walking to traverse, are the settlements. They are primitive, built of stacked stone and hand-carved timber dragged from the bizarre, sprawling root-systems, but they are undeniably urban. Towns and cities, organized in rigid, concentric circles, built to survive the elements. Their walls are thick, their doors low. But they are utterly devoid of animal life. No humans, no beasts of burden. The infrastructure is layered, showing generations of primitive construction—walls buttressed by crude stone, walkways reinforced with hand-split wood. Smoke does not rise from the chimneys. The workshops hold primitive tools: stone blades, bone awls, simple hand-looms. It is a civilization built by hands that are no longer here, or perhaps, hands that were never quite human to begin with.

Because here, at the very edge of the world, reality breaks down.

It starts at the periphery of your vision. A stone path will appear to double back on itself, looping into the sky before seamlessly reconnecting to the ground. The heavy stone walls of the empty cities occasionally lose their solidity, flickering into a state of translucent, crystalline lattice before settling back into dead weight. Distance is a suggestion. A cluster of amber-colored, bulbous flora might be a hundred feet away, but stepping toward it brings you instantly to its perimeter, the intervening space folding like wet paper. 

Stand still in the quiet, and you will feel the ground breathe—not the metaphorical breathing of wind over stone, but a literal expansion and contraction of the rock beneath your feet. The ancient erosion has worn the world so thin that the boundaries between the physical and the conceptual have rubbed away. The cold doesn't just lower your temperature; it lowers your density, making you feel as though you might drift up into the mist. 

In the empty towns, the doors stand open, leading not into rooms but into deep, thumping pockets of humid jungle that exist only on the other side of the threshold. The primitive tools left on the workbenches occasionally shift materials—a copper chisel slowly oxidizing into a length of petrified wood, a stone hammer turning into a solid block of condensed fog. 

There is no one here to witness it. There is only the endless, slow rain, the shivering indigo flora stretching toward the pale, normal light of a distant sun, the abandoned stone cities standing like monuments to a forgotten geometry, and the vast, profound quiet of a world that has lived so long it has begun to forget the rules of its own existence.
