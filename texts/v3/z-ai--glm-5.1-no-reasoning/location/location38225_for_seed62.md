The plateau came into the world only yesterday, geologically speaking—thrust upward along a fault line that still hums with subsonic tension, its basalt shoulders sharp-edged and unweathered, its cliffs dropping away in clean, angular fractures. This is a young landscape, a place where the stone has not yet learned to be round. The ridgelines carry the crisp geometry of broken glass, and the valley floors are littered with raw talus that hasn't had the millennia to settle into soil. And yet life has arrived with a ferocity that feels almost impertinent, as if the forest refused to wait for permission.

Everything here is overgrown. The lushness is aggressive, almost overwhelming in its density, a green so saturated it hurts the eyes under the hard, white light of the high-altitude sun. Ferns elbow each other for footing in the crevices of newborn rock. Mosses carpet every horizontal surface in thick, absorbent layers, drinking from the condensation that forms each dawn when the cool air meets cold stone. Bracket fungi fan out from every trunk in overlapping shelves, and lianas drape from branch to branch in curtains so thick they become walls. The vegetation has no subtlety—it fills every available niche, climbs every surface, layers itself in canopies and understories and ground-covers until the space between living things is measured in millimeters. You cannot walk here; you can only part the green and hope it closes behind you.

And the light. The light is tremendous. At this elevation the atmosphere is thin, and the sun falls without diffusion, without mercy. It bleaches the upper canopy to a pale, almost yellow-green and casts shadows so sharp they look cut from black paper. In the morning, when fog rolls through the valley, the sunlight catches every droplet and turns the air itself incandescent—a white glow that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere, as if the forest is lit from within. On clear afternoons you can see for miles across the plateau's rolling expanse, the dense green broken only by the dark lines of cliff and the distant sparkle of water, and the world feels enormous, open, telescopic.

But for all this visual abundance, the silence is profound. The density of the vegetation absorbs sound—the moss, the wet leaves, the spongy humus of the forest floor all act as acoustic dampeners. There is no wind at this level most days; the air sits still and cool against your skin, and when a branch falls it does so in perfect quiet, vanishing into the undergrowth without a sound. Occasionally you might hear the slow drip of condensation making its way from leaf to leaf, each drop finding the next surface with a faint, papery tick. Once an hour, perhaps, something moves through the canopy above—some solitary creature, rare and shy, displacing a frond as it passes. But you will not see it. The fauna here are ghosts: occasional rustlings, half-glimpsed shapes, the sense of being watched by something that has no interest in being known. A single species of pale moth seems to be the only animal comfortable with visibility; it clings to the trunks in the brighter hours, its wings marked with patterns that almost resemble printed circuitry.

Because this forest, for all its primal exuberance, is not untouched. The built world has been here, and left its marks—the question is when, and where it went.

Scattered throughout the plateau, half-swallowed by the aggressive vegetation, are the remains of industrial infrastructure. A smelting chimney rises from a cleft in the rock, its black brick obscured by climbing vines but its silhouette still sharp against the sky. A gantry of rusted steel spans a narrow gorge, its walkway buckled but still passable, the rivets blooming with orange corrosion that drips down onto the ferns below. Small structures—monitoring stations, perhaps, or pump houses—sit in clearings their builders must have carved from the forest, but the clearings are shrinking as the green presses back in. Windows are clogged with moss. Doors hang open on corroded hinges, and inside, the machinery sits quiet and cold: generators the size of automobiles, their housings flaked with rust; control panels with dials that have settled at zero; conveyor belts snapped and draped across the floor like shed skin. Whatever was being extracted or processed here, it has stopped. The industry has gone elsewhere, leaving only these hollow shells, these metal bones for the moss to grow upon.

The strangeness of this place accumulates slowly, the way condensation gathers on cold stone. It is not overtly surreal—no floating rocks, no impossible geometries—but something about the convergence feels wrong. The vegetation is too lush for the available water. The condensation should not support this density of life, and yet the forest is bursting with it. The air tastes faintly metallic when you breathe deep, and the moss in certain clearings has a curious shimmer, a silver-green iridescence that does not occur naturally—or at least, not in any naturally occurring moss you have seen. Near the industrial sites, the growth is thickest and most vivid, as if the plants are feeding on something left behind in the soil. Old pipes, cracked and root-warped, seem to have been deliberately opened, their contents long since absorbed into the earth. Whatever the factories were processing, the forest has ingested it, and the forest has thrived.

Time moves strangely here, or perhaps it simply moves slowly. The dynamic of the plateau is glacial, patient, vegetal. You can sit for an hour and watch a single fern frond slowly, almost imperceptibly, unfurl. The light shifts in increments so fine you cannot track it—only realize, suddenly, that the shadow has moved. The condensation forms and drips and forms again on a cycle that takes all morning to complete. Even the rust on the machinery seems to advance by millimeters per year, and the vines that grip the smokestacks tighten their hold over decades. This is a place that does not hurry, that does not need to. The forest has time. The stone has time. The machines, in their quiet degradation, have nothing but time.

And so you stand in the bright, bright clearing where a pump house sags under its burden of moss and creeper, and you listen to the silence, and you feel the cool damp air on your skin, and you watch the silver-edged leaves turn very slowly toward the sun, and you understand that something is being grown here. Not harvested. Grown. The factories were never extracting. They were preparing. And whatever they were preparing the ground for, the forest is almost ready.
