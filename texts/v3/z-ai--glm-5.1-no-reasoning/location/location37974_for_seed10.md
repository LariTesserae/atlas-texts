The plateau goes on forever, and it is ending.

From a distance, the landscape appears flat—a primordial tabletop of such extreme antiquity that the very concept of altitude has been worn away. Only when you stand upon it do you realize the staggering elevation, two miles high, where the air is so thin it barely holds together. This is the end of geology. The erosion metric has reached its absolute maximum; the mountains that once stood here were ground to dust a billion years ago, and that dust has been pressed into a perfectly level, cold expanse. There are no sudden cliffs, no jagged peaks—only an imperceptible, glacial tilt toward a distant, invisible sea.

It is cold here. A deep, marrow-seeping cold that hovers just above freezing, never quite committing to ice, but refusing the mercy of warmth. The sky is perpetually dim, choked with a thick, bruised atmosphere that filters the distant sun into a diffuse, pale bruise. The light does not cast shadows; it just oozes across the landscape, making it difficult to tell where the ground ends and the sky begins. And the space is vast—town-sized scatterings of presences separated by vast, echoing stretches of near-nothingness. The emptiness is a physical pressure, a sparse distribution of matter that makes the landmarks feel like solitary actors on an infinite stage.

The ground does not crunch beneath your feet; it squelches, then yields, then breathes. The earth is entirely a fiction. The substrate is plant. This is a pure-plant ecosystem, a total biological occupation where vegetation has long since consumed the mineral world and become the mineral world itself. Beneath your feet lies a kilometers-deep mat of ancient, compressed mycelium, peat, and living rhizome—a woody, waterlogged loam that serves as the only solid ground. There is no stone, no dirt, no sand—only the endless, lateral body of the plant kingdom, so old it has forgotten it is alive.

And it is wet. Good god, is it wet. The water table is so high that the plant-substrate is perpetually saturated, a colossal, high-altitude sponge. You do not walk here; you wade. The surface weeps. The stubs of broken rhizomes and the severed faces of the peat constantly bleed clear, cold water. The atmosphere is near condensing, a heavy fog that drips from nothing, condensing on the bristles of the vegetation before plummeting back to the spongy mass. Every breath feels like drinking. 

It is deeply, entirely untouched by human hands. There are no structures, no artifacts, no marks of individual construction. And yet, it is industrial.

The plant itself has industrialized. 

Over eons of hyperactive, turbulent evolution, the flora here has scaled up into mechanized, factory-like macro-organisms. Giant, chambered gourds the size of warehouses rise from the bog, their husks black and iron-hard. Inside them, turbo-charged biological engines of decomposition and synthesis run night and day. Flora act as bellows, as pistons, as centrifuges. Thick, fibrous root-networks operate like electrical grids, pulsing with bioluminescent charge, routing chemical energy across the plateau in a scaled, systematic production of biomass. The air is thick with the exhaust of this biological industry: spores, sweet-smelling alcohols, and the low, throbbing hum of a million organic turbines. There are no made artifacts, but the landscape itself is a mechanized plant-factory, running on the cold, wet fuel of the high plateau. 

It is loud. The sound is a physical assault, a chaotic, turbulent roar. The din is everywhere—the gurgling hiss of steam venting from pressure-valve bromeliads, the rhythmic, deafening thud of hundred-ton seed-pods snapping shut, the deep, subsonic thrum of the mycelial grid vibrating with power. It is a storm-state of biological creation and destruction, an anthill-energy of hyperactive, automated life. The sheer volume masks the silence of the empty spaces between the factory-groves.

Through this mechanized wetland swarms the fauna, abundant and bizarre. The animal kingdom here has been wholly subjugated to the plant's industrial complex. There are no predators in the traditional sense; there are only maintenance biomes. Swarms of blind, pale arthropods the size of dogs scuttle across the bog, their mandibles designed solely to clear debris from the intake valves of the giant gourds. Gelatinous, multi-winged avians ride the steam-exhausts, their bodies functioning as heat-sinks, cooling the factories while they feed on the boiling, sugary sap that bleeds from the machinery. The animals are the moving parts of the engine, automated and relentless.

And the reality here is breaking down. The extreme age, the hyperactive biological industry, and the constant cycle of wet decay have rotted the fabric of spacetime. The landscape is subject to a profound, creeping weirdness. The thick, dim fog refracts time and space; sometimes, walking toward a distant factory-grove, you find you have been walking for hours but the structure remains just as far away, or you suddenly realize you are standing at its base, having covered miles in a single step. Gravity occasionally stutters, losing its grip for a fraction of a second, making the weeping water hang in the air like cold glass beads before crashing back down. 

Mirages are not optical illusions here; they are temporal echoes. You will see a forest of dead, rust-colored stalks smoldering in the fog, only to approach and find the living, wet machinery. The boundaries between organisms blur—a flock of the maintenance-arthropods might suddenly fuse at the edges, their carapaces melting into a single, squirming gestalt that plugs a hole in a ruptured root-line, dissolving back into individuals once the pressure is sealed. The constant, loud thrumming of the plant-factories seems to vibrate at the resonant frequency of reality itself, shaking the laws of physics loose like rust from an old pipe.

This is the terminal bog. It is the oldest, highest, wettest, coldest, and most chaotic place imaginable—a sparse, deafening, automated wilderness where the ancient earth has been replaced by a screaming, engine-like weed, and the very structure of existence is dissolving into the damp, cold air.
