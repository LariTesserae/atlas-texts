To enter the Sunken City of Oram is to descend into a throat of stone. The surface world is left behind at the rim, where the land simply falls away into a vast, bowl-shaped depression—a crater so ancient its original violence has been entirely forgotten, its edges softened by eons into gentle, rolling slopes. Down here, nearly a mile beneath the horizon, the light is a perpetual twilight. The sun is a rumor, a pale gray smear in the overcast sky that rarely breaches the thick, cool air trapped in the basin. It is always cool here, a damp, mineral chill that seeps through clothing and settles into the bone, smelling of crushed limestone and old, silent dust.

There is no water in Oram. The paradox of the place is its dryness; it is a cavernous bowl stripped of all aquifers, a desert of stone and ash where no rain has fallen for epochs. And yet, it is impossibly lush.

The vegetation here long ago abandoned the need for rain. The basin floor and the sprawling, flattened ruins of the city are choked with mycelial forests—vast, interconnected webs of bioluminescent fungi and deep-rooted lithophytes that drink directly from the stone. Towering pillar-mushrooms, thick as ancient sequoias, rise from the cracked pavements, their caps a deep, bruised indigo that drinks in the dim light. Between them, beds of rubbery, dark-green moss creep over the toppled walls and cobbled streets, drawing moisture from the very air through microscopic mineral cracks. The roots of this fungal-plant symbiosis have pulverized the bedrock over millions of years, turning the lowest levels of the city into a spongy, nutrient-rich loam of pulverized concrete and chitin. It is a lushness born of patience, a biology that operates on geological timescales.

The city itself is the definition of primordial. This is not a ruin; it is a settled landscape. The buildings—constructed by hands so long gone they have left no tools, only structures—have been eroded by wind and root into smooth, undulating forms. The sharp angles of the architecture have been worn down to stumps and hummocks. Multi-tiered terraces, agora squares, and high avenues are now just variations in the topography, barely recognizable as the work of minds. The layered construction stretches for miles, a mid-range expanse of low, flattened hills and shallow valleys that mark where grand boulevards once ran. The scale is vast, but the density is strangely sparse; the spaces between the buildings are wide, filled with drifts of pale, desiccated dust and the slow creep of fungal mats, lending the emptiness a quiet, breathable dignity.

But the silence is an illusion. Oram is deafening.

The sound is inescapable, physical, and unrelenting. It is not the sound of water, nor of wind, but the combined roar of Oram’s fauna and the landscape itself. Millions of creatures call this basin home, and their collective voices bounce off the basin walls, amplified and trapped by the acoustics of the subterranean bowl. The primary sound is a deep, subsonic thrumming, a vibration felt in the teeth and the chest cavity, produced by massive, blind litho-beetles that grind the porous stone beneath their mandibles to extract trace minerals. Over this bass line is the shrill, endless chirring of arm-sized, multi-legged scavengers that skitter through the fungal groves, their carapaces clicking and rasping as they navigate the dark.

Above it all is the wind. The thermal gradients in the cool, deep bowl create slow, massive air currents that roll through the ruined streets. As the wind passes over the hollow, eroded doorways and the porous, honeycombed stone of the worn buildings, it creates a constant, howling drone, a choir of aeolian tones that ranges from a low bellow to a high, keening whistle. It is so loud that conversation is impossible; the creatures of Oram communicate through seismic tapping and pheromone trails, their vocalizations lost in the acoustic wash of the city. 

The sheer volume masks the profound stillness of the place’s dynamic. Oram is a world at the end of its history. The slow-growing fungal forests take centuries to spread a single meter; the erosion of a building’s facade is measured in millennia. The fauna move with a strange, deliberate sluggishness, conserving energy in the cool, waterless air. There are no sudden shifts here. The shadows lengthen and retract with a creeping patience; the drifts of dry dust migrate across the avenues over the course of generations. 

Everything here has already happened. The city has been built, abandoned, overtaken, and flattened. It exists now in its final, ancient form—a perfectly balanced ecosystem of stone, spore, and song, lying quietly at the bottom of the world, deafeningly loud, breathtakingly lush, and moving at the pace of a heartbeat stretched across a million years.
