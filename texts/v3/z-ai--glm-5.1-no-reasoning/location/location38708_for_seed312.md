# The High City of Ashvast

At elevations where the air thins to something the lungs must work for, a city sprawls across the spine of a young mountain range. The peaks here are still sharp-shouldered, their granite teeth not yet ground down by time. Frost heaves and thermal fracture still shape the ridges yearly—crack, split, tumble—so that the slopes below the city are always acquiring new scree. The mountains feel unfinished. Rubble and talus accumulate in fresh aprons. In another million years this would be rounded foothill country, but that is a long way off, and the city is here now.

---

## The City Itself

Ashvast is built dense. That is the first thing you notice from the approach—how the structures crowd each other like lichen on a stone, like barnacles, like cells packed membrane-to-membrane. There is almost no gap. Walk any street and your elbows brush walls on both sides; the overhangs of upper stories nearly meet overhead, leaving a ribbon of grey sky. The builders worked upward when they ran out of lateral room, stacking timber-framed tiers on stone foundations until the whole district terraces up the mountainside in a ziggurat of domestic architecture.

The technology is hand-scale. Stone and timber and bone. Mud mortar. Thatch where the slope permits, wood shake where it doesn't. Iron is known but scarce—mostly for cutting tools, not structural work. The load-bearing is all compression: thick walls, small windows, low doorways you duck through. Rope is everywhere. Hemp and sinew and braided wool, running through the city like connective tissue—hoists, ties, clotheslines, bridge cables, anchor lines for the stacks that sway in the high wind.

There are no roads wide enough for carts. Everything moves on human backs or the backs of the goats, and the goats are better at the stairs. Paths thread between buildings, some no wider than a man's shoulders, turning at odd angles where someone built out their wall or added a lean-to against a neighbor's already-crowded face. The city grew organically, without plan, and the result is a labyrinth that only locals navigate without hesitation. Visitors are lost within a hundred steps.

Bridges connect the second and third stories across the narrow lanes. Some are proper built things with rails and planking; others are just boards laid from window to window, used so often they've worn smooth and stay in place by habit and mutual understanding. You could cross half the city without touching the ground if you knew the route.

---

## Water

Rain comes and goes. The city sits in a belt of intermittent weather—storms roll through every few days, depositing a quick drenching, then the clouds tear apart and the sky goes pale and enormous. There is no river. Instead, water is gathered. Every rooftop funnels to a cistern. Every cistern is shared by the cluster of dwellings around it, governed by old arrangements that have the force of law. Some cisterns are stone tanks big as rooms, built into the ground floor of a building and drawing from catchments on three different roofs. Others are clay jars in a row along a ledge, each household's private store.

Condensation is a serious source. The high air is often damp even when it isn't raining. Stone walls weep in the early morning. Wooden troughs and carved channels guide the moisture downward. The city is full of these small catchments—grooves worn into lintels, shallow depressions in flat rocks, anything that will collect the slow cold drip of fog coming off the peaks. It is not enough for luxury, but it is enough. People are sparing with water. They know its paths the way they know the streets.

Snowfall is light here—the elevation is high but the air is too dry for heavy accumulation. What falls is usually a fine dry powder that the wind strips off exposed surfaces within hours, leaving it packed only in crevices and shadowed niches. The city's northern aspects hold snow in their angles; the southern faces are bare stone within a day of any storm.

---

## The Cold

It is always cold. Not the killing cold of the absolute peaks, but the cold that lives at the boundary where human effort can just stay ahead of it. You feel it in the stone floors—never warm, even in the rooms where fires burn. You feel it in the air that enters your lungs like a swallowed stone. Breath is visible from dawn to dusk and most of the night. Frost forms on any surface that doesn't have a body or a fire nearby.

The city compensates by crowding. Bodies are warmth. The dense construction is not merely a response to limited space—it is a thermal strategy. Shared walls mean shared heat. The innermost rooms of the deep buildings, those with no exterior face at all, are the warmest, and these are the valuable rooms, the ones families hold onto for generations. The outer rooms are for storage, for summer use, for the poor. Insulation is straw and wool and animal fat smeared on walls in thin layers. Every gap is stuffed. Every door has a curtain behind it, often two.

Fires burn in clay-lined pits, never on open hearths—the wind would take the heat straight away. The smoke exits through channels that wind through walls, warming the stone on their way out. Some buildings have such elaborate smoke-paths that the construction seems more chimney than house. The fuel is scrub wood, dried dung, and a kind of peat that workers cut from alpine flats a half-day's climb below. The peat smolders long and slow. A single bank of it can warm a room through a night.

## Fauna

The city is full of animals. They are not pets in the modern sense—they are working partners and competitors and co-occupiers, and the line between those categories is thin.

Goats are the backbone. The Ashvast goat is a small, hardy breed with legs like stilts and hooves that grip stone the way hands grip a rope. They go everywhere. You find them on third-story ledges, in basement stores, on the narrow bridges between buildings, standing on things that seem too small to hold them. They are milked and sheared and occasionally eaten, but mostly they are tolerated and worked—they carry loads up stairs no human wants to climb twice, they find paths through the maze that their herders then learn to follow, they stand watch in a way that isn't quite guarding but is certainly alert.

Dogs are rarer. A few of the large mountain type, used for pulling and for their warmth when they sleep against you. They are not numerous because they require meat. Goats eat what people eat, more or less; dogs need more.

Birds are everywhere. The city's ledges and rooftops and overhangs host thousands—pigeons and choughs and a small dun-colored falcon that nests in the upper stories and hunts the lower ones. The birds are semi-domesticated, harvested for eggs and occasionally for meat, but mostly they are just present, another layer of life in the stack. Their guano is collected. Nothing is wasted.

Rats and mice and similar small things live in the lower depths, in the gaps between foundations, in the spaces too small for anything else. They are hunted by the falcons and by the cats—there are cats, thin and cold-resistant, owned by no one, fed by everyone who wants the rodents kept down. They move through the city like ghosts, known but not named.

Insects survive in the warm zones, near the fires and in the straw insulation. Beetles and moths and a kind of silverfish that eats the mortar if given the chance. Lice and fleas are a given. The people scratch. This is not remarked upon.

Outside the city, in the crags and the thin vegetation above treeline, there are larger beasts—mountain cats, a type of shaggy ox that grazes the sparse alpine growth, hares that turn white in winter. But these are the province of hunters who go out and return, not the daily texture of the city itself.

---

## Vegetation

The city is above treeline. Below, some distance below, there are forests—stunted conifers that thin and shrink as you climb until they are just dark bushes hugging the ground, then nothing. The city sits in the nothing.

But life persists. Lichen covers every stone surface that isn't actively walked on or burned. It comes in grey and pale green and a startling orange that appears on the north-facing walls after rain. Moss grows in the wet channels and the shady crevices and anywhere the condensation is consistent. These are not decorative—they are structural. The lichen binds the stone, slows the erosion, fills tiny gaps. The city is partly held together by its moss.

A few hardy plants are cultivated in sheltered spots—window boxes lined with wool, rooftop beds walled against the wind. Small bitter greens, a type of dwarf onion, medicinal herbs. There are stories of grain grown in sun-warmed stone troughs, but it is mostly the greens that you see, and those mostly in the richer quarters. Below the city, on the slopes where the air thickens and warms just enough, there are terraced fields—barely more than shelves cut into the scree and filled with hauled soil—growing a tough barley and a root vegetable like a small pale carrot. The fields are the city's true edge, its real border. Below them is the mountain. Above them is Ashvast.

## Atmosphere and Light

The sky is large. This is what the elevation gives you—a sky that dominates, that presses down and opens up simultaneously, that makes the horizon feel close because you can see so far. On clear days the world below is a map: valleys like wrinkles, distant ranges like cloud, the sky's reflection in lakes too far away to ripple.

The light is clean and thin. Colors are slightly washed, slightly blue. Shadows are hard-edged because the air lacks the diffusion of lower elevations; you stand in sun and feel the warmth, step into shade and feel the cold enter like water. The sun is strong when it's present—burn is a known problem on exposed skin—but it is often not present. Clouds move through quickly. The weather changes by the hour, or seems to.

Twilight is long. The sun goes behind the peaks an hour before it would set on flat ground, and then there is a slow dimming where the sky holds light even as the city is already in shadow. This is when the cold sharpens. People draw inside. The bridges and external stairs empty. The city turns inward, and from above you would see it as a pattern of orange points—the fire-glow from small windows—appearing one by one in the grey mass of the structures.

Night is enormous and quiet and very cold. Stars are thick and bright, thick enough that you understand why the people here navigate by them. The moon casts sharp shadows. The wind, if there is wind, makes the city creak and groan on its older joints, the rope lines sing, the shutters bang until someone fastens them.

---

## Sound

Mostly quiet. The city is too high for the ambient noise of lowland life—no insects loud enough to hear, no distant water, no traffic. What sound there is is close and specific: the slap of a goat's hooves on stone, the murmur of a conversation in the next room (the walls are thin but the speech is quiet, habitually quiet, people keep their voices down in the cold), the crack of a fire, the creak of a rope under strain, the wind. Always the wind, which is not loud but is constant, a low presence that you stop hearing until it stops, and then the silence feels wrong.

The city has no industry loud enough to echo. No hammers ring all day—metalwork is done in short careful bursts. No mills grind. The sounds are all human-scaled: footstep, cough, laugh, the specific clatter of a wooden bowl being set down, the scrape of a door curtain being pushed aside. In the deep interiors you can hear your own pulse. In the exposed outer reaches you can hear the city breathing—thousands of small sounds overlapping into a rough quiet hum, the sound of a place where life is dense but restrained.

---

## The People

They are cold-adapted, compact, broad in the chest. They dress in layers of wool and leather and felt, always in layers, shedding or adding as they move between inside and out. Color is limited—natural dyes produce muted earth tones, and the occasional brighter thread is a mark of someone who traded for it or made it special. Faces are weathered. Hands are rough.

They know their city the way you know your own body—intuitively, in detail, without needing to think. They know which stair is loose, which cistern is full after a dry week, which neighbor leaves their fire unattended. They know the paths. The city is documented nowhere. There are no maps. If you need to find something, you ask, and you are given directions in terms of who lives where—"below Hesta's cistern, through the grain-room, the door with the bone latch." It is an oral architecture, a city held in living memory.

Governance is local and layered—each cluster of buildings around a cistern manages itself by consensus and custom, and disputes between clusters are handled by a council of elders that meets when needed and not otherwise. There is no central authority. There is no police. There are expectations, and the community enforces them through inclusion and exclusion, which is enough.

Children are everywhere, climbing things that alarm visitors, running along edges that have no rails, learning the city by body. They are put to work early—carrying water, minding goats, tending the peat fires. They are valued. The city needs hands.

---

## The World's Rules

Things change slowly here. The mountains are young but the city is older than any living memory, and it changes by accretion—a room added here, a wall reinforced there, a path closed when someone builds across it, a new bridge when the old one finally rots through. There are no demolitions. Things are built on top of, around, through. The city gets denser and slightly taller and more layered with each generation, like the lichen it resembles.

The cold sets the pace. Everything takes longer because hands are stiff, because fuel must be managed, because going outside requires preparation. The people are patient. They have to be. A task that takes an hour in warmth takes two here, and the margin for error is thinner—lost fingers to frost, fires that go out in the night, stores that run low before the next trade caravan comes up from the valleys below.

Trade is occasional. The outside world exists—there are settlements in the lowlands, other peoples, other ways—but contact is sparse. The city is self-sufficient in what matters and austere in what it isn't. Luxuries come up the mountain on the backs of porters and are expensive: metal, salt beyond what can be got from the sparse game, cloth finer than local wool, finished tools, sometimes a book or a strange object that no one knows the use for. The city sends down wool and dried meat and a particular lichen that produces a dye the lowlanders want. The exchange is infrequent and vital.

---

## Standing in It

You stand on a bridge between two buildings—wood worn smooth by traffic, rope handrails cold under your palms—and you look out. The city falls away below you in a cascade of dark rooftops and pale stone walls, punctuated by the small bright rectangles of windows where fires burn. Goats move on a ledge three stories down. Someone is pouring water from a height, the stream catching light for a moment before it vanishes into shadow. The air smells of smoke and cold stone and the faint animal musk of bodies living close.

Above: the peaks, still sharp, still growing, pushing the sky upward. Below: the terraced fields, tiny green shelves, and then the long drop into the valleys where the air is thick and warm and foreign. The wind touches your face. It carries no scent but its own cold.

This is Ashvast. It is enough.
