To stand here is to stand at the bottom of a throat. The land is ancient beyond the measure of strata, eroded not by time but by the sheer weight of its own existence. This is a primordial plain, a continental expanse so old that the mountains dissolved into memory billions of years ago, leaving behind a flatness so absolute it hurts the inner ear. There is no horizon, only a suffocating, dim maroon twilight that bleeds down from an overcast, condensing sky, erasing the boundary between air and earth. The scale is planetary; weather systems the size of ancient oceans roll overhead like slow, bruised viscera, breathing a wet, tepid warmth across the endless flat.

It should be empty, this end-of-all-places. Instead, it is packed to bursting. The low elevation means the water table is the ground itself. You do not walk on soil; you wade through a lukewarm, shallow braiding of braided streams, seeps, and weeping marshes that stretch in every direction without a single dry patch to rest upon. The water is not still. It percolates, bubbles, and weeps from the smooth, flat stones that lie just beneath the surface, stones so worn by eons of patient water that they feel like the polished joint-sockets of some colossal, buried titan. 

Vegetation is sparse, but what exists is inescapable because the plain is saturated—there is no room between presences. The water is choked with a single, relentless kind of life: a thick, dark amber filament, like wet hair or brittle fungal mycelium. It mats the surface of the shallows, binding the water into a viscous soup. It climbs the weeping rocks in damp, shaggy pelts. It is the only thing holding the world together, and it covers everything in a suffocating, biological felt. 

And the sound. God, the sound. It is deafening, an inescapable, skull-rattling roar that is born from the sheer density of the place. It is the sound of a trillion slow-dripping seeps, the subsonic groan of continental water shifting under its own weight, the hydraulic wheeze of the atmosphere squeezing out condensation like a soaked sponge. It is a white noise so profound it borders on silence, a deep, wet tinnitus that vibrates in the marrow and makes your own thoughts feel like they are spoken through a waterfall. 

Rarely, a creature moves through the amber muck—a pallid, eyeless thing, perhaps a large amphibian or an arthropod of terrifying, flaccid size, slipping through the filaments with the blind, solitary purpose of a deep-sea scavenger. They are phantoms, their splashes instantly swallowed by the roaring acoustics of the plain. 

But it is the turbulence, the reality breakdown, that makes this place a Solaris-like nightmare. The world is hyperactive at its fundamental seams. The deep age and the saturation of water have caused the laws of physics to fray into a wet, chaotic slurry. Distance is unreliable; a weeping rock a few yards away might take hours to reach, or you might suddenly find yourself standing on it without having moved. The water flows backward in places, defying gravity, pooling in hovering, basketball-sized spheres that drip upward into the maroon twilight before collapsing with a sonic boom. 

The density of the water and the filaments seems to have created pockets of temporal stagnation. You might see the ripples of a step taken by one of the rare beasts hours before the beast itself arrives. The atmosphere, thick and dim, occasionally condenses into solid, glass-like panes that reflect the marsh from impossible angles before shattering into warm rain. It is a world in a constant state of creation and destruction, a primordial soup at the end of time, furiously stirring itself in a final, deafening paroxysm before it finally, entirely, goes under.

There are marks of something else, once. Here and there, half-submerged and consumed by the amber muck, are the faintest remains of built things. A single, flawless geometric slab of an unknown ceramic, worn smooth by the water, used now as a weir by the filaments. A line of immense, perfectly spaced monoliths that have been ground down by the eons until they are mere ankle-high stumps in the marsh, their purpose obliterated. No tools, no technology remains. Only pure, relentless, suffocating biology and water. 

This is the ultimate destination of all matter: a warm, deafening, flat ocean of weeping stone and wet hair, crowded with itself, restless to the point of madness, waiting at the edge of reality for a dissolution that will not come.
