The land has been dying for longer than anything has lived here. It is not a violent death—erosion reached its maximum statement eons ago, and now the terrain exists in its final, absolute form: flat,妥协, and almost perfectly horizontal, stretching outward in every direction until it dissolves into a dim, aqueous haze. The elevation barely varies. What were once mountains have been reduced to gentle swells and depressions no taller than a kneeling person, worn down to nubs of quartz and feldspar after billions of years of patient water. This is the geography at the end of time, where the planet has been scoured down to its most intractable skeleton.

And yet, it is dripping.

Water is everywhere, rising from below rather than falling from above. The ground is saturated to the point of being a membrane; step heavily and the slate-colored soil weeps, releasing thin runnels that trace the micro-topography before pooling in the broad, shallow depressions that serve as this landscape's only features. The air condenses constantly. Moisture beads on every surface—on the flat rocks, on the scattered artifacts, on the carapaces of living things. It is cool, this water, perhaps five or six degrees above freezing, carrying that particular mineral sharpness of liquid that has spent geological time percolating through stone. There are no rivers, per se, but the entire landscape is a slow, lateral seep, moving so gradually that direction becomes meaningless. The standing pools reflect nothing; the light is too dim, the sky too uniform a pewter.

There is no green. No brown of bark, no rust of autumn, no black of rot. Vegetation simply does not exist here—not as this world understands life. The biological vacancy is total and ancient. Whatever epoch permitted plants has been scoured away with the mountains, leaving a world of mineral, liquid, and movement.

The movement belongs to them.

The fauna is abundant, and this is the landscape's central contradiction: a dead world thick with animal life. They come in two broad forms, both of which seem to have evolved in the absence of any vegetable basis for the food chain. The first are the grazers—creatures the size of large dogs, low-slung and six-legged, their hides the same slate-gray as the wet stone. They move in loose herds of twenty or thirty, their many feet making soft, rhythmic splashes as they cross the shallow water. Their mouths are broad and flat, designed for scraping. What they scrape is the biofilm—a living film of single-celled organisms and mineral deposits that coats every submerged surface. It is faintly iridescent, this film, shifting between oil-slick colors when the dim light catches it at certain angles. The grazers exist in a state of constant feeding, moving slowly from one wet stretch to another, their jaws working in that same relentless rhythm. Their eyes are small and vestigial; they navigate by touch and by the chemical signatures of the water.

The second form of life is predatory, and it is the source of the sound.

The sound is deafening. It never ceases.

They call them the resonators, though no one is here to call them anything. Vaguely eel-like creatures, three meters long, with elongated bodies built for the deeper channels that vein the landscape. Their skin is translucent and faintly luminous, casting a sickly bioluminescence that provides the only light source after the dim daylight fades. They possess a specialized organ—a chambered structure of hardened cartilage and thin, resonant bone—that they use to produce sound. The purpose is communication, navigation, and hunting; the sound penetrates the muddy water, reflects off the dense substrate, and returns to their sensitive hearing as a detailed image of their surroundings.

But the resonators are many, and they are constant. Hundreds of them occupy the deeper seeps, and their combined vocalizations produce a wall of sound that is physically overwhelming. It is not a single note but a complex, layered drone: deep bass vibrations you feel in your chest, mid-range tones that make your teeth ache, and higher frequencies that seem to drill toward the center of the skull. The sound reverberates off the flat stone, amplifies in the shallow water, and reflects off the low, permanent cloud cover. There is no echo because there is no silence in which an echo could exist—only an ongoing, all-consuming harmonic assault.

Against this auditory backdrop, the scattered remnants of building stand like wounds. They are primitive structures, crude things of stacked stone and fitted slate, none larger than a single room. The technique is consistent: flat stones levered into position, gaps filled with smaller chips, no mortar, no binding agent. Whoever made them understood only the simplest principles of construction, and yet they endured, these huts and shelters and windbreaks, because there is nothing here to tear them down. The wind is mild. The water is patient but not aggressive. The animals ignore them entirely. They persist as faint marks of habitation, scattered kilometers apart, each one containing perhaps a scraped depression where someone slept, or a few worked stones that might be tools—chipped edges, comfortable grips, nothing more sophisticated than what a clever hand could produce from the local materials.

Who built them is unclear. Whether they lived in the sound or fled from it is unclear. Whether the biofilm sustained them or they came here for other reasons is unclear. The structures are too old for any answer but the landscape itself.

The landscape offers only this: water moving slowly across stone worn flat before the first memory. Creatures that eat scum and sing in frequencies that shake the air. Shelters built by hands that have long since returned to the mineral matrix from which they emerged. A cool, dim world at the end of erosion, where nothing new will ever grow, but the water keeps rising and the sound keeps coming and the grazers keep moving and the resonators keep singing their deafening, incomprehensible song.

The sound never ceases.
