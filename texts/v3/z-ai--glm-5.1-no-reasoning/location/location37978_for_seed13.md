The city stands at the roof of the world, where the air is so thin and cool it feels like drinking cold water simply to breathe. It is a place of sharp, young geology—basalt escarpments and fresh, unhewn granite that thrust upward from the earth as if the mountain itself were still mid-birth, its features crisp and unrounded by time. There is no slow crumbling here, no gradual wearing into gentle hills. The stone is angular, defiant, split by deep crevices and sudden ravines that cut like knife-wounds through the high plateau.

Between these sharp ridges, the city is layered. It is an urban place, dense with organized construction, yet it is sparse in its population. The built environment outnumbers the builders. Tiered stone platforms cascade down the leeward side of the central peak, connected by stairways carved directly into the living rock. The structures are primitive but immense: dry-stone walls fitted with obsessive precision, heavy timber roofs clad in thick, damp moss. There are no forges here, no smelting, no metal beyond the occasional copper pin or soft-iron chisel. Everything is made by hand, from the materials the mountain provides—stone, wood, fiber, and the pale, elastic bark of the high-altitude cedars that cling to the lower slopes.

And yet, for all this construction, the city feels half-empty. The platforms and plazas stretch wide, and the distances between buildings are considerable, governed not by shortage of space but by some older principle of arrangement—a geometry of separation, as though each structure must breathe, must maintain a specific distance from its neighbors. You can walk for minutes along a paved path of flat, fitted stones without passing another soul. The openness is regional; the city extends far, sprawling across the ridge-system and into the adjacent valleys, a settlement the size of a modest kingdom but inhabited like a scattered village.

Vegetation has claimed the in-between spaces. Lush, almost alarmingly so for this altitude and chill. The damp sees to that. Moisture collects here in a peculiar way—there is no proper rainy season, no predictable storms. Instead, water manifests as a constant, ambient presence. Fog presses against the city most hours, condensing on every surface. Droplets bead on the stone walls, run in slow trickles down the stairways, pool in the shallow channels carved into the plaza floors. It is not wet enough to flow, not dry enough to evaporate. The water simply accumulates, persists, dampens everything to the touch. Mosses grow in thick, vivid cushions—emerald and jade and almost black where the shadows lie deepest. Ferns unfurl from every crevice, every joint between stones. On the wider platforms, where soil has gathered in the shelter of walls, tall grasses and broad-leafed herbs grow in profusion, their surfaces always glossy with condensation. The lushness is not tropical; it is cool and temperate, a saturated green that seems to drink the light and hold it close.

The light itself is ordinary—daylight by day, starlight by night, the sun moving in its customary arc. But it falls upon a place so still, so utterly without motion, that it takes on a ceremonial quality. Nothing changes here. The fog does not roll in or lift; it simply is, a permanent soft ceiling that diffuses the light into an even, sourceless glow. The temperature does not rise at midday or fall at dusk. The wind does not gust or die. There are no gradients, no transitions, no cycles. The city exists in a frozen equilibrium, as though time's river has been dammed just upstream, and only the faintest seepage—dampness, breath, the slow blink of a bird—suggests that the world is not a photograph.

Fauna is abundant, and it is this that prevents the silence from becoming oppressive. The creatures here are numerous but quiet, adapted to a world where sound carries too far and too clearly across the open plazas. There are woolly goats with curving horns that navigate the basalt cliffs with absolute silence, their hooves finding purchase on the wet rock without even a scrape. Foxes with pale, silver-tipped fur patrol the wider paths at intervals so regular they might be clockwork, moving between buildings on routes established long before memory. Above, raptors circle in the diffuse light, their cries cut short as if by instinct, reduced to soft whistling calls that barely carry. In the ferns and mosses, insects move—beetles with iridescent shells, pale moths that flutter in the perpetual damp—but they are hushed things, their buzzing dampened by the moisture in the air.

The strangeness of the place is subtle. It reveals itself slowly, in the way the city's layout seems to follow no logic of defense or trade or convenience, but instead maps something else—a constellation, perhaps, or a pattern visible only from very far above. It reveals itself in the fauna's behavior: the regularity of the fox-patrols, the way the goats always face the same direction when they rest, as if oriented by an invisible field. It reveals itself in the vegetation, which grows in spiraling patterns on the broader platforms, concentric circles of grass and herb and moss that seem to echo the city's radial layout at a smaller scale. And it reveals itself most of all in the stillness—the profound, unbreakable equilibrium that holds this place in its grip. Nothing decays here, or nothing decays quickly. The stone walls do not erode. The timber roofs do not rot, despite the damp. The moss grows but does not seem to advance. It is as if the city were caught in the moment after its completion, preserved in a breath that has not yet been exhaled.

Standing in the central plaza—a wide, circular space paved in fitted granite, with a shallow bowl at its center where condensation gathers into a still, perfect mirror—you can look out across the terraces and stairways, the scattered buildings with their mossy roofs, the lush greenery filling every gap, and the angular peaks rising beyond. The silence is not empty; it is full, pregnant with the soft sounds of damp existence—a drip somewhere in the distance, the whisper of a beetle's legs on wet stone, the hollow sound of your own breathing, amplified by the emptiness. The cool air presses gently against your skin. The fog hangs motionless above. And nothing, nothing at all, is changing.
