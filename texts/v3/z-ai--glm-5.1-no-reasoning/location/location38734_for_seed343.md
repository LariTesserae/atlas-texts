To enter the Green Altitude is to step into a cathedral that has no architect, only time.

The land has been erased. Not violently—there is no dramatic ruin here, no jagged evidence of catastrophe. Instead, the mountains that once stood in this vast region were worn down over eons so profound that the stone simply surrendered. What remains is an immense, rolling tableland, an elevated continent sitting so high in the atmosphere that the horizon curves visibly, a primordial plain smoothed by billions of years of rain and root into its final, exhausted state. The elevation is extreme, yet there are no cliffs to climb; you are simply *up*, suspended on a plateau that feels like the roof of the ancient world.

And from that roof, life has erupted in absolute, overwhelming totality. 

The vegetation is not a covering; it is the substrate itself. This is a pure-plant ecosystem, a world where the boundary between geography and biology has dissolved entirely. What appears from a distance to be rolling hills of jade and emerald is, in fact, a single, contiguous mass of hyper-aged flora. There is no bare soil. There is no rock. The ground beneath your feet is a dense, springy weave of root structures, living rhizomes, and packed detritus that goes down miles. Towering above are the ancient mega-angiosperms—trees so wide and deeply rooted that they have become the architecture of the plain. Their horizontal branches interlock for hundreds of miles, forming a weightless, vaulted canopy that serves as the sky. To walk here is to walk inside the living tissue of a botanical god.

Yet, despite the claustrophobic totality of the plant life, the space itself is staggering in its emptiness. The scale is regional, spanning the distance of a small country, but the volume is practically a void. The vaulted arches of the canopy are spaced impossibly far apart, leaving colossal, echoing aisles of warm, luminous air between the trunks. You can walk for days without touching a trunk. The density of the place is an illusion; the green mass forms the boundaries, but the interior is a vast, hollow hall. It is an empty volume enclosed by life, leaving you feeling utterly exposed and minuscule beneath the biological vaulting.

The warmth is a humid, breathless thing, hanging heavy at a steady 1.5 on the scale of heat. The moisture arrives not as sweeping weather, but through the physiology of the space itself. This high altitude traps the condensation of the lower world against the canopy roof. Every few hours, the massive leaf-structures above reach their dew point and weep. A heavy, intermittent rain falls from the ceiling without a cloud in the sky—great, fat drops that plop into the distant undergrowth, creating localized rivers that flow silently through the root-mass for a few hours before evaporating in the warmth. The air smells of damp cellulose, warm chlorophyll, and a faint, sweet tang of ancient decay.

The sound is deafening. It is not the deafening roar of machinery or wind, but the cumulative, thunderous hum of a biosphere at absolute capacity. The fauna here is overwhelming, comprising 2.9 parts of the world's reality. The plain teems with an uncountable biomass of chitin and flesh. Millions of six-legged grazers the size of hounds strip the root-hairs; clouds of gossamer-winged spore-eaters drift in the thermal updrafts, their wings catching the normal, dappled sunlight that filters through the canopy. Prey and predator are locked in a constant, silent, frantic pace. But it is the insects—winged and myriad—that create the sound. Their synchronized pulsing, clicking, and humming resonates through the vast, empty aisles of the forest, bouncing off the living walls until the air itself vibrates. The sound is so dense it has texture; you feel it in your teeth, a continuous, rolling thunder of biological urgency that never, ever ceases.

Amidst this deafening green, the built environment is heartbreakingly faint. A 1.4 on the scale of construction: scattered, partial, almost apologetic. You might walk for a day before you stumble upon one—a small cairn of woven hardwood, a single, leaning watchtower made from flexed saplings hardened into bone-like permanence, or a semi-circle of ritualistically scarred bark. The creators possessed only the simplest tools—primitive hand-axes of sharpened stone, perhaps the first crude smelting of copper—and they built not to dominate the landscape, but merely to survive its deafening presence. These artifacts are not ruins; they are scars. The plant-mass has long since grown over most of them, digesting their intent, leaving only the faintest geometric anomalies in the endless green.

There is little overtly weird about this place—no floating stones, no impossible geometry, no churning magical storms. Its weirdness lies entirely in its extreme normalcy pushed to the absolute brink. It is a world that has settled into its ultimate, ancient state. The processes of erosion have finished their work; the processes of life have consumed the result. The ecosystem is in perpetual, frantic motion within a landscape that is immeasurably still. 

Here, at the end of the world's shaping, you are a ghost walking through the lungs of a completed planet, deafened by the heartbeat of the swarm, wandering the vast, empty spaces between the ribs of the trees.
