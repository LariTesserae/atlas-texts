The world here is a single exhalation, frozen mid-breath.

You stand on the edge of a cliff that should not exist—a sheer, juvenile escarpment of raw basalt and sharp, unweathered granite that drops away into a darkness so complete it feels solid. The stone beneath your feet is fresh, its fractures sharp and clean, as if the mountain was cracked open only centuries ago. And perhaps it was. This place is young. The tectonic violence that thrust these kilometers-high plateaus upward left no time for the softening hand of age; the edges are still keen enough to cut skin.

But the stone is not bare. It is smothered.

Vegetation at density 2.8 is not an overlay; it is the substrate itself. The Gargantua—vast columnar trees that make redwoods look like kindling—rise from the plateau's edges and climb upward into the black, their trunks fifty meters wide, their root systems erupting across the stone like the fingers of buried titans gripping the fresh rock. They are not scattered; they *are* the architecture of space here. Their bark is scaled and cold, slick with the perpetual damp that never quite becomes rain. Between their roots, thick mats of sphagnous moss and dark, gelid lichens cover every surface—not in patches, but in continuous masses meters deep. You sink to your knees in the vegetative substrate. The ground does not exist. There is only plant, layered on plant, layered on plant, all the way down to the new-born stone.

The moisture here is an insult, a cruel joke played by the atmosphere. It is not a river, not a sea, not even a proper rain. It is a presence—a dampness that condenses on the underside of the great leaves, that beads on the lichen-hair, that drips perpetually from the canopy in a stuttering, arrhythmic tattoo. *Drip. Drip-drip. Drip.* Each drop strikes a hollow node on some vast fern below, and the forest rings like a detuned carillon. The condensation is endless because the heat differential is endless: the cold air, just above freezing, meets the slightly warmer breath of the living mass below, and the moisture precipitates out in a slow, permanent weeping. You are inside the lung of something that respires over geological time.

The darkness is profound. Light here is a rumor, a memory of something that happens elsewhere. The canopy overhead—layers upon layers of interlocking frond and branch and epiphytic tangle—is so dense that even at noon, even on the rare occasions when the cloud cover breaks, only the faintest luminescence filters down. It is a darkness with *texture*. You can feel the shapes of the trunks before you see them. Your eyes never adjust because there is nothing to adjust to; instead, the other senses hypertrophy. The smell of the place is overwhelming—cold peat, crushed chlorophyll, the mineral tang of fresh granite, and underneath it all, the rank, mushroomy odor of decomposition happening at scale.

And it is loud. God, it is loud.

Not the loudness of machines or cities, but the loudness of *process*. The dripping alone is a cacophony—millions of drops striking millions of surfaces across the vast, continental spread of the plateau. But beneath that, beneath the percussion of condensation, is the deep, subsonic groan of the Gargantua trees shifting in the cold thermal winds. Their trunks flex by centimeters over hours, and the wood fibers scream in frequencies you feel in your molars. Then there is the fauna.

The fauna is abundant, and it is *noisy*. In the blackness, you hear them before you know their shapes. Things with chitin and things with fur, things that click and things that ululate. Swarm-crickets the size of your fist emerge from the moss-pits in chirring clouds, their combined song a saw-blade whine that oscillates with the temperature. Above them, in the lower canopy, something larger moves—a predator, you assume, from the sudden silence that follows its passage through the insect-dense air. You hear the leathery snap of wing-membrane, the scrape of talons on bark, and then a low, resonant call that drops from audibility into the chest-rumble of the tree-groan. Prey-species answer with alarm-shrieks, high and piercing, and then the chorus resumes. The ecosystem is in constant, grinding motion. Birth, hunt, consume, die, decompose, feed the moss, feed the roots, repeat. The pace is staggering for a place so cold.

The scale of this living machine is difficult to compass. The plateau stretches for hundreds of kilometers in every direction, and it is all this—the same columnar forest, the same moss-sea, the same dripping blackness. Weather forms *inside* it. Thermal columns rise from the metabolic heat of the vegetation and clash with the cold downdrafts from the peak-ridges, spawning localized storms that discharge lightning into the canopy and are gone within minutes. The thunder rolls through the tree-trunks and becomes indistinguishable from their groaning. You could walk for months and not reach an edge. The place generates its own physics of space—distance measured not in kilometers but in the time it takes for a sound to die, in the number of trunk-widths you pass in the black.

And in all of this vastness, emptiness. The density of presence is near-zero. Not because things are absent—the biomass is staggering, the life is everywhere—but because the *space* between meaningful presences is so immense. You are a mote in a cathedral. The nearest Gargantua trunk is a hundred meters away through the moss and the black, and the next after that is three hundred, and the next after that is five. The fauna you hear is distant, their sounds carried by the strange acoustics of the inter-trunk voids. You could reach out and touch lichen, moss, root—but you are alone in a way that is total. The volume of the place swallows significance. There is too much room. Too much dark between the presences.

You find the built structures near the cliff-edge, where the moss thins and the raw stone shows its youth. They are low, rough things—circles of fitted basalt, unmortared, their walls barely waist-high. Wind-breaks. Shelters from the condensation-drip. The people who made them used hand-tools; you can see the percussion marks where stone was struck against stone to shape the blocks. No metal. No fired ceramic. Just rock and the fiber-cord that once lashed the roof-beams, now rotted to stains on the floor. The hearths are cold, filled with the carbonized remains of wood that burned in some distant season. The structures are scattered—three here, two there, always near the edges, never deep in the forest-interior. They feel like observation posts, like the last outposts of people who came to the rim of the void-forest and went no further.

Who were they? The structures are too crude to tell. They left no art, no burial, no tool behind—only the bare statement of *wall* and *hearth*. Perhaps they came to harvest something from the forest's edge—a resin, a fungus, the chitin of the swarm-crickets. Perhaps they came to listen. The place has that quality. It makes you want to listen.

Standing at the cliff-edge, looking out into the darkness where the plateau falls away into the lower altitudes, you can hear the full voice of the forest rising from below. The drip-percussion, the tree-groan, the insect-whine, the predator-call, the prey-shriek—it all blends into a single, harmonic drone, a chord made of life and water and cold and the slow breathing of the world. The young stone beneath your feet hums with it. The moss under your hands is damp and warm where the roots below are metabolizing, and cold where the air touches it. The condensation beads on your face. The dark is absolute.

And somewhere in the black, something large moves between the trunks, and the forest adjusts around it, and the sound changes, and the place becomes, for a moment, something other than what it was.

This is the young world before the light. This is the green throat of the cold. This is the place where plants ate the stone and became the ground and built the dark and filled it with sound.
