The plateau sits nearly two kilometers above sea level, and the cold up here is the kind that doesn't bite so much as it settles—a damp, heavy chill that presses against the skin and leaves a sheen of condensation on every surface. It is cool the way a foundry floor is cool after the fires have banked, or the way a hospital basement maintains its temperature: engineered, intentional, inescapable.

This is a world with no natural substrate. Everything—the ground beneath your feet, the walls that channel the wind, the very curvature of the valley—is constructed. Bolted, welded, poured, extruded. Iron and concrete and copper and glass, arranged in forms that have weathered so long they've achieved the dignity of geology. The erosion parameter reads "established," and it shows: lichen thick as carpet on north-facing girders, rivet heads worn smooth by a century of mist, drainage channels carved not by design but by the patient work of water finding the same path ten million times. The structures have settled into their own existence. They no longer feel installed. They simply *are*.

And everything is packed. There is no emptiness here, no negative space, no gap between presences. The valley—or what functions as a valley, a constructed depression between two fabricated ridgelines—crams together habitation blocks, pipe runs, cable trays, ventilation shafts, storage tanks, walkways, gantries, and the endless latticework of industrial infrastructure into a density that borders on suffocating. From any point, you can reach out and touch three surfaces. Look up: catwalks and conduit. Look down: grating over more grating over machinery. Look ahead: the backside of whatever stands in front of you, which is always something. The place has the spatial logic of a ship's engine room scaled up to the size of a small city.

Water is everywhere, though not dramatically so. No torrents, no spectacular falls. Instead: seepage. Weeping. The high plateau's cold air can't hold much moisture, so it condenses out constantly, beading on every surface, running in thin films down painted steel, dripping from the lowest point of any overhang with metronomic patience. You hear it more than see it—a polyrhythmic tapping from a hundred sources, never loud enough to constitute noise, just the place's steady exhalation. Some walls have been fitted with drainage gutters; others haven't, and the water has carved shallow grooves in concrete over the decades, accidental streambeds that would look natural if natural were a category that applied here. The atmosphere is wet the way a greenhouse is wet, but cold. The humidity sits around 85% and never quite reaches rain.

And the vegetation—this is what arrests you. Lush. Rampant. The plants have claimed every surface that doesn't actively repel them, and some that do. Mosses first: thick, dark green, almost black in the dim light, covering concrete like a pelt. Then ferns—maidenhair, spleenwort, hart's-tongue—growing from every crack, every joint, every seam where two manufactured surfaces meet and leave the slimmest gap. Then the climbing vines: not delicate things but thick, woody-stemmed creepers with leaves like leather, hauling themselves up cable trays and around pipe insulation, their tendrils probing into vents and access panels. Moss grows on the *inside* of some buildings, where condensation runs down interior walls. Ferns colonize the tops of idle machinery. In the lower levels where water pools in stagnant sheets, you find reeds—actual reeds, eight feet tall—growing in the runoff treatment tanks that no one has maintained in living memory. The vegetation doesn't merely exist here; it saturates the environment, another layer of presence in a place already dense with them. It is the third skin over the built world's bones: first steel, then rust, then moss.

The light is low. The plateau's elevation puts it in a permanent cloud deck, or perhaps the factories produce enough steam and particulate to generate their own overcast; either way, the sky is a uniform pale gray, a diffuse ceiling that might be fifty meters up or five hundred. Inside the infrastructure, illumination comes from old industrial fixtures: nautical-style vapor-proof lamps with wired glass guards, low-pressure sodium units that cast everything in a monochrome amber, bare bulbs in cage fittings. The light doesn't so much brighten as it reveals—the way a flashlight beam reveals shapes in a dark room without dispelling the darkness. Shadows persist everywhere. Corners are always dim. The overall effect is of permanent dusk, or the hour before a storm that never breaks.

Fauna is scarce. You might hear insects—the tiny click of beetles navigating the moss, the drone of a solitary wasp investigating a pipe joint—but they're rare, and you can go hours without seeing one. No birds. No rodents. Occasionally, in the deeper pools of standing water, you might spot something pale and slow moving, some opportunistic amphibian that found its way up here and persisted, but it would be noteworthy, an event. The ecology is almost entirely botanical. The plants don't need animals. They need water and substrate, and both are infinite here.

Sound: mostly quiet. The dominant acoustic is dripping water and the faint whistle of wind through the lattice of structures. But underneath that, underneath even the threshold of attention, there's a low vibration—a hum, perhaps electrical, perhaps mechanical, perhaps just the resonant frequency of so much metal under so much tension. It's not loud enough to hear so much as feel, a deep oscillation in the chest, the way you might sense a distant train through the soles of your feet. Occasionally a valve releases somewhere, a sharp hiss that echoes through the pipe runs and then fades. Occasionally metal ticks as temperature shifts. But these are punctuation marks in a long silence.

And yet—the dynamic parameter reads *turbulent*. Chaotic change. Hyperactive. And this is the contradiction that gives the place its particular texture, its strange energy. Nothing here is still. Not the water, not the plants, not the infrastructure itself. Steam vents open and close on cycles no one remembers programming. Pipe networks reroute themselves with mechanical clunks deep in the walls. Entire sections of catwalk vibrate as something massive moves through conduits beneath them. The vegetation grows visibly—you can almost watch the ferns unfurl, the moss spread. A wall that was bare iron yesterday has a fine green fuzz today; by next week, it will be submerged. The built environment is in constant, slow-motion convulsion, not failing but *reconfiguring*, some industrial process too vast and too automated to ever fully cease, grinding through its routines with the blind persistence of a body that doesn't know it's dead. The turbulence isn't violent. It's metabolic. The place digests itself and rebuilds, daily, hourly, and the moss and the water and the rust are all part of the same cycle.

Stand in the central thoroughfare—if you can find a spot clear enough to stand—and look outward. The constructed valley extends past your sight, ridge after fabricated ridge, each one bristling with pipe and cable and moss, each one dripping, each one humming with that subsonic vibration. The scale is regional. You could walk for days and not reach the edge, if there is an edge, if this isn't simply how the world is now. The structures recede into the dim gray distance, their details dissolving into the moisture-laden air, until they're just shapes, just dark masses against a lighter dark, and then they're gone, swallowed by the wet horizon.

This is a place that has achieved equilibrium, but it's the equilibrium of a centrifuge, or a standing wave: everything in furious motion, yet holding its form. The water falls, the plants drink, the machinery runs, the temperature holds, the light stays low. Nothing intervenes. No one tends it. It simply continues, packed and wet and dim and alive in its own way, a landscape that was built for purposes no one remembers, serving functions no one needs, and has settled into the long work of becoming a mountain.
