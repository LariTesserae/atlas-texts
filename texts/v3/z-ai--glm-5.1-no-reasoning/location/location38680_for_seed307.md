You enter through a fissure no wider than a throat.

Beyond the squeeze, the world opens into a pocket cathedral—a single, self-contained grotto hidden deep within the ribcage of a young, violently upthrust mountain. The stone here is newborn, still wearing the razor-sharp edges of its tectonic birth. There are no rounded corners, no smoothed faces—only the raw, hackly fracture of basalt that has just been ripped apart. The walls bristle with obsidian splinters and crude handholds of rough-grained granite, so recently extruded from the mantle that they have not yet had time to weather, crumble, or forget the heat of their making.

And they have not cooled. The temperature is ferocious. The air hits you like a held breath, thick and superheated, pressing against your skin with a damp, suffocating weight. The mountain weeps, because the heat meets water here in a hostile, unending truce. Moisture condenses on the sheer rock faces in heavy, sweating droplets. It does not fall so much as slide—thick, sluggish rivulets tracing paths down the sharp fluted ridges of the grotto walls, pooling in the angled crevices of the floor. The water table sits just beneath the stone, heated by the mountain's youth, pushing up through the cracks under faint geothermal pressure. The ground is a mesh of tepid puddles and slick, near-vertical planes. It is a mineral world, barren of green. No root grips this stone. No leaf unfurls. There is only rock, water, and heat.

Yet the space is impossibly full. It is dense with presence, saturated by life that does not register as life as you know it.

They coat every available surface. They layer over one another in thick, overlapping shelves—nudibranch-like masses of glistening, unclassified flesh, clinging to the weeping walls. They are the fauna of this hidden pocket, and they are overwhelming. Pale, eyeless slugs the size of your forearm, pulsing faintly with internal heat. Wiry, chitinous things with too many folding legs, scuttling silently over the backs of their slower kin. Crusts of kidney-shaped pods that breathe in unison, their orifices dilating and contracting in a slow, aqueous rhythm. They press against each other in near-touching proximity, a living wallpaper of wet, squamous bodies. The grotto is a small space, barely the size of a modest living room, but the biomass packed into it defies reason—a terrarium of blind, crawling saturation.

They are silent. The only sound is the near-inaudible *slick* of a heavy body shifting on wet stone, or the faint, rhythmic drip of condensation rolling off a slug's cooling back. It is a hushed, reverent quiet, breathless and expectant, as if the pocket universe itself is waiting.

Because reality here has suffered a wound.

The heat, the water, the pressure of the mountain, the closeness of the thronging creatures—something in this combination has frayed the edges of the world. You see it when you look up. The ceiling of the grotto does not behave properly. The stalactites—wet, sharp, newly formed—sometimes aren't there. You blink, and they are back, dripping. The back wall of the chamber breathes. Not metaphorically; it expands and contracts by millimeters, and when it contracts, the geometry deep inside the rock looks wrong. Angles that shouldn't fit together interlock seamlessly; shadows pool in directions that have no relationship to the faint, gray light filtering down through the high entrance fissure.

The light is normal. Terrifyingly so. It is a mundane, overcast daylight, the kind you might see on a rainy afternoon, and it illuminates the breakdown of reality with the indifferent clarity of a hospital ward. You can see exactly how the creatures ignore physics. A pale, jointed thing crawls upside down on the ceiling, but its grip on the wet stone leaves no mark, displaces no water. Another creature—a massive, flattened disc of grey flesh—is half-embedded in the solid basalt wall, resting comfortably within the stone as if the rock were merely cold water, its edges blurred where biology becomes mineral. The weeping water runs over these impossible geometries without hesitation, cascading off bodies that occupy space and stone that does not, pooling in crevices that are deeper than the room's dimensions should permit.

The system is active. It pulses. The creatures move constantly, a slow, grinding choreography of blind navigation. They feed—you realize this as you watch a multi-limbed entity gently press its face into the weeping rock, drinking the heated condensate directly from the stone's pores. They jostle. They shift. Water cycles through them, warm and constant. The grotto is an engine running on heat and moisture and the presence of too many bodies in too small a space, and it is running *wrong*. 

At the far end of the pocket, where the shadows gather and the geometry folds in on itself, a large, slug-like creature—pale as a dead moon—rears its front half up. It presses its blind, featureless head against a corner of the room that seems to bend away from it. The stone yields. Not breaking, not melting, just... *reconsidering* its existence. The creature pushes forward, and its body slips partially out of the visible spectrum, becoming a faint, wet outline against a backdrop of slowly spiraling nothing. It is migrating through the break, passing into wherever this grotto goes when it stops making sense.

The others continue their silent, squamous writhing. The water weeps. The heat presses down. The mountain holds its sharp, young shape around this pocket of impossible life.
