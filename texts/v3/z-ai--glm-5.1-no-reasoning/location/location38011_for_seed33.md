The air is too thin to carry a voice, and too cold to hold moisture. At an elevation that should be reserved for the flight of satellites, there exists a city—though that word implies a vitality that this place has long since transcended. It sits upon a plateau of fractured obsidian and lamellated salt, so high that the atmosphere has peeled away into a near-vacuum, leaving a dim, bruised sky that presses down like a lid. The light here is a perpetual dusk, emanating from no sun but from the atmosphere itself—a sullen, rust-colored luminescence that seems to seep upward from the stone. 

This is a landscape of radical absence. There is no water; the geology knows nothing of rivers or rain. The few clouds that exist are hallucinatory, drifting like bruises in the peripheral vision, dissolving when looked at directly. What little life exists here has abandoned the biological paradigm entirely. There are no plants. Instead, the margins of the streets are traced by crystalline filaments—perhaps fungal, perhaps a pre-carbon anomaly—that exhale fine, metallic spores into the thin air. They are the only things that grow, feeding on static and the decay of the city's own geometry. 

Fauna, too, is reduced to the barest margins of existence. Occasionally, something moves in the upper crevices of the empty thoroughfares—a creature that is less an organism and more a localized collapse of the air. They are called breath-stealers by the rare, lost travelers who have seen them, though they do not steal breath so much as they *are* breath: sinuous pockets of vacuum that slither through the alleys, hunting the ambient warmth of the spore-blooms. Their passage is marked only by a sudden, intense chill and a faint, localized deafness, as if the sound around them has been scooped away. 

The city itself is an impossibility of construction. Multi-tiered and labyrinthine, it sprawls across the extreme elevation in dense, organized layers of black glass and quarried salt, yet it was built by no hands and requires no tools to maintain. There is no technology here—no wires, no engines, no purposeful metallurgy. Instead, the infrastructure operates on a logic of sheer necessity forced upon reality. Aqueducts carry dry, echoing wind. Arches support nothing but the heavy dimness. Staircases spiral downward into the bedrock only to emerge seamlessly on the roofs of opposite towers. The city is urban in its density, yet completely empty of inhabitants. It is a settlement without a settled, organized around the principle of its own ongoing existence. 

Erosion has come to an arrangement with the architecture. The structures are weathered into smooth, organic curves, their original angles softened by eons of wind-driven grit, yet they do not crumble. The city is in balance, existing in its own state—a fossil of a timeline that never occurred. The salt-fields beyond the city limits are equally settled, their vast, polygonal basins extending to the horizon, a sea of crystallized silence. 

And yet, despite the stillness, despite the silence that lies thick and muffling as snow, the city is active. It moves at the pace of geology and the speed of a reality failing to adhere to itself. The dynamic processes here are not weather cycles or biological rhythms; they are ontological shifts. A tower on the eastern edge slowly rotates over the course of a century, grinding its base against the obsidian, generating a deep, subsonic hum that vibrates in the marrow of anyone foolish enough to stand near it. The grid of streets subtly rearranges itself, an urban respiration that expands and contracts the city blocks. If you walk the same route twice, you will find the alleyway you passed moments ago has folded shut, the bricks interlocking like a closing iris. 

Weirdness is the fundamental law of this altitude. Reality breaks down under the weight of the empty sky. Shadows detach from their objects and crawl away into the lower levels of the city, pooling in the subterranean vaults where they breed and multiply. Distance loses its meaning—a building a mile away may be reached in ten steps if the atmospheric pressure shifts correctly, while a neighboring door might open onto a drop into an infinite, starless chasm. The dim light occasionally fractures into overlapping spectra, causing the same street to exist in three adjacent moments at once. You can see the ghost-impressions of yourself walking just a few seconds ahead, traversing a city that is slowly, quietly digesting the concept of space.

The only sounds are those of immense, slow processes. The groan of the planet's crust shifting beneath the city's incomprehensible weight. The faint, crystalline tinkle of a shadow freezing solid in the cold. The whisking of the salt-dust against the glass walls. It is a silence so profound it takes on a texture, brushing against the skin like velvet.

Here, at the top of the world, in the cold and the dark, the city breathes its mineral breath. It is a settlement of the void, built from the bones of a broken physics, empty of everything but the slow, relentless churn of a reality attempting to remember how to exist.
