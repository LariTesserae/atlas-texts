You ascend through air that grows thicker with moisture, though it never quite resolves into rain. The plateau sits high—altitude you can feel in the slight labor of breath—but the warmth clings, paradoxical, a tropical fever at mountain height. Then the space opens, and you are there.

It is not large. A cathedral nave, perhaps, or a village green before the first house was built. The boundaries feel implicit: a rim of dark stone, a change in the air's texture, some threshold the eye registers before the mind does.

**The terrain is being made.**

That is the first disorientation. The ground beneath your feet has none of the settled patience of old geology. It heaves, slowly—on a timescale between geological and tidal—and you can feel it if you stand still: a long swell, a breathing. The rock is warm to the touch, dark basalt shot through with veins of something that isn't quite mineral. The veins pulse. Not with fluid, but with *information*—you understand this without knowing how you understand it. The stone is computing. The landscape is a processor mid-calculation, and what it's calculating is *itself.*

Erosion hasn't happened yet because the forms aren't finished. There are ridges that lead nowhere, half-extruded arches, surfaces that terminate in raw seeps of material that hasn't decided whether it will be crystal or membrane. It looks like a quarry where the stone is growing rather than being cut. The rock weeps constantly—water forced from some high interior reservoir, expelled through pore and crack, running down surfaces in sheets so thin they're more like a skin of wetness than a flow. The water is warm. It tastes of salts you can't name, and of something metallic that isn't iron.

**The sound is immense.**

Not mechanical, not organic—some third category. A low harmonic that you hear in your sternum before your ears register it, the sound of the terrain's slow formation, of mass being negotiated at timescales just above your perception. Over that: a higher, irregular throb, like a heart arrhythmia the size of a building. And beneath everything, a white-noise hiss—the water, the endless water, emerging from stone, running, pooling, evaporating in the warmth, condensing again on surfaces cool enough to catch it. The acoustic space feels *crowded*, though the visual space is not.

**Sparse, but not empty.**

Vegetation is scarce—a pale, fibrous lichen on the wetter vertical surfaces, and here and there a growth that might be plant or might be the rock's own extension: a tubular structure, finger-thick, rising from cracks and curling blindly toward the dim light. They flex. Slowly. They might be roots, or they might be feeding tubes, or they might be sensors. There is not enough of them to form an ecosystem; they feel like *pilot species*, the first colonists of a world that hasn't finished being born.

The fauna, though—the fauna is abundant, and this is the second disorientation.

**They are small.** The largest is the size of a rabbit, and most are smaller: finger-length, nail-length, mote-length. And they are *many*. The wet rock swarms with them. You see them best at the edges of the weeping surfaces, where water gathers in trembling menisci: delicate, fractal-legged things that drink without mouths, pressing their geometries flat against the stone. Some move in synchronized clusters, dozens of individuals resolving and dissolving like a school of fish in air. Some are solitary—tiny, armored, trundling along the half-formed ridges with a purpose that suggests they know where they're going, even if the ridges don't yet lead anywhere.

They are not insects. They are not crustaceans. They have too many legs, or no legs at all, propelling themselves by ripple-waves of segments you can barely resolve. Some are luminous—a cold, faint glow, blue-green, brightest when they cluster. In the dimness, their collected light pools in the hollows of the rock, giving the terrain a sick-bioluminescent topography, as if the land is lit from below by something living.

The light overall is dim. A high overcast, or some quality of the air itself—the moisture refuses to scatter light normally. Everything is seen as if through a veil of thin milk. Shadows have no hard edges. Colors desaturate to umbers and blue-grays, except where the fauna glows, and there the color is almost painful in its specificity.

**The structures are what stop you.**

There are three. Maybe four—the fourth might be a natural formation, or it might be a structure so old it has begun to be reabsorbed into the computing stone. They are small, person-height or less, and they are made of a material that is clearly not the basalt: a matte silver-grey, seamless, with no visible joints or fasteners. They look like *nodes* or *terminals*—and the word comes to you unbidden, a vocabulary from whatever part of you recognizes the alien. Each has an aperture, and from each aperture, the same pale light as the fauna, brighter, steadier, directed downward into the rock.

They are plugged in. The structures are plugged into the landscape. They are *writing* to it, or *reading* from it, or both. The fauna cluster around them in thick, moving carpets. The weeping water runs over them without staining them. They are indifferent to the terrain's slow heaving—they stay level, somehow, anchored to something deeper than the stone's current configuration.

One of them hums at a frequency you feel in your teeth.

**This is a place that is becoming.**

You understand this with the certainty of a body registering danger, though it doesn't feel dangerous—it feels *vulnerable*. Everything here is provisional. The rock is trying configurations. The water is finding paths. The fauna are testing niches. The structures are... *seeding*? Overseeing? The word that fits best is *cultivating*, though what is being cultivated is not a crop but a *place*—a process, an ongoing computation that takes the form of topography.

You could stay and watch. You could press your hand to the warm, wet stone and feel the swell of it, the slow inhale and exhale of a landscape learning to be itself. You could listen to the harmonic and let it recalibrate your sense of time until you perceive the speed of continents.

Or you could leave, carrying the taste of those salts on your lips, and spend the rest of your life trying to describe a place where the ground was still making up its mind.
