The plateau goes up forever. This should not be possible—basic physics insists that a landmass of this breadth, billions of tons of basalt and biosmoke, should have long since collapsed under its own weight or been ground down by the friction of the lower atmosphere. But this is a young world, or perhaps a newly-exuded one, still hot and sharp-featured from whatever tectonic spit-up birthed it. The cliffs at its edges are not eroded; they are cleaved, sliced with surgical violence, exposing strata of obsidian and boiling iron that have not yet learned to rust. You stand near the rim of one such drop, and the air is so thin, the scale so continental, that the horizon curves upward, bending away from you as if the planet itself is trying to look away from what happens on this plateau.

There is almost no sound. That is the first wrong thing. By all rights, a place this turbulent should roar. The dynamics are extreme—Solaris-like in their constant, churning revision. The clouds overhead do not drift; they boil and implode, forming and dissolving in minutes, throwing down brief curtains of warm rain that evaporate before reaching the stone. The condensation clings, though. The rock is always damp, beaded with moisture that never quite dries, sweating from some deep hydrothermal source. You hear no wind, only the occasional low-pressure pop of a cloud collapsing, a sound like a distant canvas being struck once. Mostly, silence. A pressure-silence, the kind that lives inside vacuum chambers and deep mines.

Then there is the vegetation, and the silence begins to feel like conspiracy.

The growth is lush—outrageously lush, a riot of chlorophyll and chitin—but nothing here is green. The flora runs to blacks, deep purples, oily iridescents, colors that suggest photosynthesis operating on a spectrum human eyes were not designed to see. The light is failing; it never exceeds a perpetual crepuscular dimness, as though the sun behind those churning clouds is not a star but some industrial furnace, sputtering and coughing. The plants have adapted. They are enormous, layered, near-touching. Wherever you step, fronds press against your shoulders, lianas grasp at your boots, shelf-fungi the size of conference tables jut from the basalt at hip-height. The density is nearly absolute. You move through this place like a needle through felt, parting tissue that closes behind you.

No hand built anything here. The structures that rise from the plateau are not structures; they are geological excrescences, volcanic pipes and tectonic blisters, hollowed by heat and pressure into something that only coincidentally resembles architecture. There are no artifacts. No tools. No marks of intention. And yet—

The technology is everywhere. It is not *placed* here. It is *grown* here, or secreted, or extruded by the landscape itself. Vast engine-forms jut from the stone: cylinder arrays like organ pipes or rocket stacks, venting steam that smells of sulfur and ozone. There are surfaces of machined precision, gear-tooth profilings in the bedrock, perfect involute splines cut into canyon walls by no hand. Somewhere below, the plateau is a factory. It is mechanized, scaled for production, electrified by the constant static discharge of those dying clouds. The vegetation has incorporated it. Lianas wrap around extruded camshafts. Fungal blooms fruit from pipe joints. The heat of the place—the warmth, pervasive, like standing near a running motor—is waste heat, industrial exhaust, the exhalation of processes too large and too alien to interrupt.

The fauna know this. They move through the dense growth with the ease of parts in a well-oiled machine. There are many of them—abundant, teeming, a food web of such complexity that no single species seems to hold dominance. You see them in glimpses: eye-shine in the crepuscular dimness, chitin clicking against stone, sinuous bodies sliding between the pipe arrays and fungal shelves. They are not afraid of you. They are not aware of you. They are processes themselves, components of the factory-ecology, moving along established channels to feed, to breed, to process whatever this landscape processes. Some of them glow faintly, bioluminescence in colors that make your teeth ache. Some of them are large—very large—moving in the deeper shadows with a slow, hydraulic patience. They breathe steam. Their bodies are warm to the touch, if you could touch them, which you would not, because they are part of the system and the system does not stop for inspection.

And here is where reality becomes advisory.

The turbulence is not only weather. The plateau itself is unstable—not physically, not structurally, but ontologically. Things change when you are not looking at them directly. The path behind you reconfigures. The growth you parted has closed, but it has closed differently; the species composition shifts, the arrangement of pipe-joints and fungal blooms has revised itself. You turn a corner and find a cliff that was not there before, or a geyser of warm water erupting from stone that was dry moments ago. The landscape is running some process of creation and destruction at a speed too fast for perception—an anthill-energy, a bazaar of constant transaction between form and formlessness. You catch it at the edges of your vision: a tree becoming a gear becoming a ribcage becoming a waterfall becoming a tree. The transitions are seamless, instant, total. Only when you stare does the world hold still, pinned by observation like an insect under glass. The moment you look away, it resumes its churning.

This is why it is quiet. Sound requires a stable medium. Here, the air itself is being rewritten between one breath and the next. The silence is the silence of a reloading program, a buffer being cleared.

You walk for what feels like hours. The plateau does not diminish. It goes up, and up, and up, the grade so subtle you do not notice you are climbing until your legs ache and the air thins further and the clouds are below you, a carpet of churning grey, lit from beneath by the plateau's waste-heat. You are above the weather now, or beside it, or inside a new weather that operates by different rules. The vegetation thins, then thickens again, but differently—the plants up here are crystalline, their surfaces faceted, catching the dim furnace-light and scattering it in spectra you cannot name. The fauna are different too: smaller, faster, moving in precise formations like machinery in motion, like components on a production line.

You find a crevice in the rock, a natural vent issuing warm damp air that tastes of iron and ozone. You rest. You close your eyes.

When you open them, the crevice is gone. You are standing in a field of something that is not quite grass and not quite cable, a plain of fiber-optic green that stretches to every horizon, and the horizon itself is closer now, or further, or curved in a direction you do not have a word for. The factory rumbles beneath your feet, vast and indifferent, and above you the sky is not a sky but a ceiling of slow-moving cloud that seems to be watching you with the attention of a quality-control inspector.

The plateau has processed you. You are part of it now: another component, another warm body in the dense and churning ecology, moving along established channels, performing a function you will never understand. The silence wraps around you like a second skin. The damp air presses close. Something large and hydraulic moves in the distance, and you find, to your mild surprise, that you are not afraid.

This is what young worlds feel like. This is what it sounds like when reality is still deciding what it wants to be.
