# The Weeping Factory

Below the surface of the world, where geography means nothing because there is no sky to measure it against, someone built a valley. They built it out of iron and intention, and they left the water running.

---

You enter through a ventilation shaft that sweats. Condensation beads along the riveted walls in perfect rows, following the seams where plate meets plate with the obedience of water that has nowhere else to go. The shaft descends at a fourteen-degree angle for three hundred meters, and by the time you reach the first gallery, your clothes are damp and your hands taste like copper and lime.

The valley opens before you—not discovered but revealed, as though a curtain of darkness has simply been pulled away. It is a space carved or excavated or grown from the body of something manufactured, a chamber so large that its far wall is a rumor of darker dark, a place where the ceiling and the horizon conspire to vanish. Industrial lamplights hang from cables thick as your arm, spaced at intervals of perhaps forty meters, and each one hums at a frequency just below hearing, a vibration you feel in your back teeth. The light they give is the color of tea held up to a window—amber, weak, soaked somehow with the moisture it passes through. Visibility extends maybe two hundred meters before the wet air eats it.

This is a young place. That is the first strange thing. The metal surfaces around you have not been here long. You can tell by the edges. Where a catwalk rail has been stamped and welded, the seams are still sharp, still bright with the silver of recent cutting. No patina. No rounding. The bolt heads catch the dim light with the frankness of things that have not yet learned to be old. The valley was made—built, constructed, assembled—and it was made recently, perhaps within living memory, perhaps within the span of a single committed effort. Someone wanted this. Someone drew plans.

But water has already begun to argue with the intention.

---

Every surface weeps. That is not metaphor. The air holds so much moisture that it deposits itself constantly, mechanically, on every available surface. The walls sweat. The ceilings sweat. The support beams, the catwalks, the housing units, the pipe arrays—all of them run with thin sheets and thick droplets of water that has condensed out of the manufactured atmosphere. The water collects and finds the lowest points, as water does, and here the lowest points are the drainage channels cut into the floor at regular intervals, parallel grooves that carry the weeping away to somewhere you cannot see. You can hear it, though. The sound of this place is the sound of water moving through metal at every scale: the tiny persistent *tick tick tick* of droplets falling from pipe to pipe, the broader rush of runoff in the channels, the deeper and slower gurgle of water moving through the main collection pipes beneath the floor grating. It is a place that sounds like a kitchen after dinner, like a greenhouse at midnight, like the inside of a cloud.

And beneath that, always beneath that: the engine sound.

---

The engines live in the walls. You cannot see them. You know they exist because the floor vibrates with their turning, because somewhere behind the iron panels that line the chamber's edge, pistons are moving and gears are meshing and something is being powered that requires this much mechanical commitment. The sound they make is a low continuous thrum, a chord of industrial effort that never alters, never rests. It is not loud enough to drown the water sounds—it layers beneath them, the bassline to the water's melody. Sometimes a valve opens somewhere and you hear the sharp exhalation of steam being vented into a space you cannot identify, a violent *CHHH* that echoes once and is swallowed.

The engines have a purpose. Everything here has a purpose. That is what the youth of the place tells you: nothing has yet been abandoned, nothing has yet been repurposed or left to drift from its original intent. The machines still mean what they were built to mean.

You simply do not know what that is.

---

Vegetation grows in the wet zones. Sparse, specific, alive in the way that things are alive when they have found a niche and committed to it utterly. It is not green, exactly. The light here is too amber for green. The plants are a dark teal, almost black in shadow, and they grow in the drainage channels where the water flow is constant and slow. They look like nothing you have seen on the surface—flat, overlapping leaves in spiraling rosettes, each one no larger than your palm, their surfaces slick with the condensation that feeds them. They do not reach upward. There is no sun to reach for. They reach *sideways*, spreading along the channel floors, overlapping like shingles, and where they have colonized a stretch of drainage, the water sound softens because the plants absorb what the metal cannot.

Fungi grow on the walls where the moisture is thickest: pale, rubbery protrusions that emerge from the seams between metal plates, feeding on something in the sealant perhaps, or on the condensation itself. They are pale as cave fish and they have no scent. When you touch one, it is warm—warmer than the ambient air, which hovers just above freezing. The cold here is the cold of a refrigerator, of a place that was designed to stay cool. The fungi seem to generate their own heat. Perhaps that is their function. Perhaps someone grew them here on purpose.

The ecology is sparse because it has not had time to fill in. The valley is young. The plants and fungi are pioneers, first arrivals, the things that can survive in a cold wet metal box with amber light and the patience of engines. Insects might exist—you see something like a silverfish, metallic and quick, vanish into a seam in the wall—but they are rare, glimpsed, not yet a presence. You hear no birds. You hear no voices. The only animal sound is the scrabble of small things in the ductwork, heard once, not repeated.

---

The valley extends. That is the disorienting thing. You walk for an hour along the main gallery and the far wall does not get closer. The ceiling remains the same height, the lights the same spacing, the drainage channels the same steady flow. The catwalks branch and rejoin. Housing units squat on platforms at intervals—identical, rectangular, with doors that seal against the moisture and windows that show amber-lit interiors furnished with the minimum: a bunk, a basin, a heater, a locker. They are empty. All of them are empty. The bunks are made. The heaters are off. Someone planned for a population that never arrived, or arrived and left, or has not yet come.

The scale is regional. This is not a room. It is a *place*. A municipality of metal and moisture, a town-sized cavity where the streets are catwalks and the parks are drainage channels choked with teal rosettes, and the civic architecture is pipes and valves and the housing of engines you cannot see. You could live here. That is what the place tells you, with its empty bunks and its running water and its constant temperature. Someone intended for people to live here.

The cold and the wet argue with that intention. The damp soaks through any fabric in hours. The chill settles into bone. The light is too dim for comfort, too amber for ease, and the sound—the sound is the thing that would wear you down. The unending water, the unending engines, the occasional violent exhalation of steam. There is no silence here. There has never been silence here. The machines have been running since the valley was built, and the water has been condensing since the atmosphere was established, and the place has been weeping since the moment it was born.

---

There is a strangeness that goes beyond the obvious. You feel it when you stand at the edge of a catwalk and look down into the space below, where the floor grating opens onto deeper channels, where the water goes after it leaves the drainage grooves. The darkness below is not just the absence of light. It is *structured*. There are levels down there, other galleries, other chambers, the valley extending downward as well as outward, and from somewhere far below you hear a different engine sound, slower, more massive, doing work you cannot guess at.

The strangeness is in the purpose. The valley works. It functions. The water is being collected and moved. The air is being conditioned and circulated. The engines are providing power to systems that operate with the fidelity of things that have never malfunctioned. But the product of all this work—what is it *for*? The water is gathered but you never see it used. The power is generated but the housing units are empty. The atmosphere is maintained but no one breathes it. The valley is a machine that runs to run, a process that processes itself, a wound that weeps because weeping is what wounds do.

Or perhaps it is waiting. Perhaps it is a prepared environment, a built nest, a hollowed space ready to receive something that has not yet arrived. The youth of the place suggests this. The sharp edges and bright seams suggest this. The made beds and off heaters suggest this. Someone built a place below the surface where water would always run and the air would always be cold and the engines would never stop, and they built it for a reason that has not yet been fulfilled.

The teal plants in the channels did not get the message. They are colonizing regardless. The pale fungi on the walls did not wait for permission. The water does not care about intention; it condenses and runs and carries on its surface the first thin films of rust at the seams where the sealant is thin. The valley is young but the valley is wet and the water is old and patient, and in the competition between the maker's plan and water's nature, you know which one wins, eventually, in every place that has ever been made.

But not yet. Not here. Not now. The engines are running. The lights are humming. The weeping continues. The valley is still itself, still young, still sharp at the edges, still waiting for whatever it was built to receive. You walk its catwalks and feel the vibration in your feet and taste the copper and lime on your hands and listen to the water moving through metal at every scale, and the place is loud with the sound of a process that has no end in sight.
