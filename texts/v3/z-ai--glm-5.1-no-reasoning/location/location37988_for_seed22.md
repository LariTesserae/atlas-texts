The land is young. Not young in the way of spring or morning, but young in the way of a wound that has just stopped bleeding—a fresh, raw eruption of geology still discovering its shape. The basalt ridges rise in angular, unweathered shoulders, their edges too sharp to have ever known a millennium of rain. And yet there is rain now, or something like it: an intermittent, condensing dampness that doesn't so much fall as materialize, clinging to every surface in transient silver films before the ambient warmth lifts it again into the soupy air. The temperature sits in that close, humid range where skin never quite dries and breath feels like drinking.

The scale is difficult to accept. The mind keeps insisting there must be walls, that the dim grey-green expanse must terminate in a ceiling or a horizon, but it doesn't. The low terrain extends for what might be dozens of kilometers—a wide, shallow bowl of land that wants to be a floodplain but has no river, no sea, no source for the water that beads on its black stone shelves. The elevation barely varies. There are no peaks, only steps and terraces, as if a massive printer had laid down terrain in layers and paused mid-job.

Against all reason, it is lush. Impossibly, chokingly lush. The vegetation has no patience for the logic of water or soil; it roots in hairline fractures, in the condensation channels, in the mineral substrate itself. Broad, dark leaves overlap in fractal profusion, their surfaces slick with the perpetual damp. Vines coil with the tension of wire. Ferns uncurl in geometries that repeat at smaller and smaller scales, each frond a miniature of the whole, and the whole an echo of something the eye can't quite resolve. The growth is dense in its texture but sparse in its distribution—great curtains of green hanging from nothing, isolated columns of moss and creeping stem rising like monoliths from bare stone, with wide expanses of empty space between them. The density of the place is a paradox: thick where life has chosen to stand, and utterly vacant between. You can walk for ten minutes through open air and dimness and encounter nothing but your own footsteps and the humid taste of the atmosphere, then turn a corner and find a vertical wall of living tissue, a column of interlocking biomass three meters across and twenty tall, breathing.

The emptiness is the most disquieting feature. Not absence—there is too much happening for absence—but a hollowing. The space between presences feels engineered. The silence is supposed to live here, but it never arrives, because the sound never stops.

It is deafening. From the moment you enter the bowl, sound presses against you like a hand. Not a single sound but a complex, a living architecture of noise that layers and interpenetrates until the ear surrenders the attempt to parse it. There is a low, constant thrumming—subsonic, felt in the sternum more than heard—that might be the land itself settling, or might be something far deeper. Over it, in overlapping registers, come the animal calls. The fauna are abundant, and they are loud. You hear them before you see them, and often you hear them and never see them at all. Chitterings that sweep from ultrasonic down to a guttural chest-rattle, long trailing howls that Doppler as something moves fast through the green columns, percussive knocks that ring off the basalt in patterns too regular to be accidental. Somewhere beneath it all, a rhythmic pulsing, wet and organic, like a heart the size of a stadium, or the sound of fluid moving through a vessel you are standing inside.

The animals are strange, and their strangeness accumulates. The first you notice are the fliers—not birds, not bats, but something with too many joints in the wing and a body that seems to be mostly mouth. They move in swarms, and the swarms have behaviors: they cluster, disperse, reform, execute turns that suggest a distributed intelligence operating at the speed of thought. Then there are the crawlers, visible only as disturbances in the moss columns, their shapes suggested by the way the vegetation shifts and compresses around them. You catch glimpses—segments, a wet gleam, the architecture of something that opted for radial symmetry early in its evolutionary gamble and never looked back. On the open stone, larger shapes move with an odd, pausing gait, as if they are waiting for instructions. They have too many legs. Or perhaps they have exactly as many legs as they need, and you are simply not the standard by which such things should be measured.

And then there is the city.

It emerges from the landscape the way the vegetation does—suddenly, improbably, in a vertical cluster that contradicts the emptiness around it. It is built, unmistakably built, with the layered logic of construction and the infrastructure-density of a settlement that once housed multitudes. But it is not old, not in the way cities are old. Its edges are too crisp. Its materials have no patina, no weathering, no concession to the damp and warmth that should have dissolved them years ago. The structures are young the way the land is young: freshly arrived, unaged, as if deposited whole.

The engineering on display is beyond modern in the way that a cathedral is beyond a tent—not in degree but in kind. The buildings do not rise in the way buildings are supposed to. They *are*, with an inevitability that suggests they were grown from a specification the laws of physics have only just learned to accommodate. Surfaces transition seamlessly between materials that should not touch—metal into ceramic into something that absorbs light so completely it reads as a hole in vision. There are no visible seams, no fasteners, no concessions to the assembly of parts. The infrastructure is internalized, hidden within walls that occasionally pulse with light or vibration, as if the city were a single organism with a circulatory system and the modesty to keep it private.

At the city's edges, the vegetation has begun to colonize. The green columns lean toward the buildings as if recognizing kin, and the contact points are already producing hybrid forms: structural members that terminate in living leaf, architectural surfaces that have gone soft and photosynthetic. The integration is too fast to be natural. Something is negotiating between the biological and the constructed, finding common ground at a scale too small to observe, and the result is a merging that should take centuries but is happening in the space of this sentence.

The dimness is constant. There is no sun visible, no sky, only a high, luminous overcast that provides enough light to see by but never enough to cast a shadow. The world exists in a permanent twilight that makes distance unreliable and color uncertain. Everything reads in shades of grey and the deep, saturated green of the vegetation, which is so dark it borders on black. Occasionally, the city's internal systems pulse—a slow, blue-white glow that travels through the infrastructure like a signal, like a thought moving through a neural net, and for a moment the geometry of the place is illuminated in sharp relief, and you see how much larger it is than you thought, how much emptier, how many structures recede into the dimness beyond where you've walked, each one potentially inhabited, potentially alive.

The sound is worst near the city, or perhaps the city is the source. The thrumming intensifies when you approach its walls, and the animal calls shift register, becoming more structured, more patterned, as if the fauna are responding to something the city is broadcasting. The wet, rhythmic pulsing is unmistakably louder here, and you begin to understand that it is not coming from beneath you but from *within* the structures themselves, from the infrastructure you cannot see, from the systems that make the walls pulse and the surfaces breathe.

This is a place that is still becoming. The land is fresh and has not yet decided what it will erode into. The vegetation has not yet reached equilibrium with the water that doesn't quite rain. The city has not yet finished arriving, and the boundary between built and grown is still soft, still negotiable, still being written at a scale below perception. The fauna have not yet settled their territories or their relationships; their calls are still proposals, their movements still experiments. The sound is the sound of processes in motion—geological, biological, technological, all of them overlapping and none of them complete.

The dampness on your skin is not just water. It is a medium. It carries information in solution, in suspension, in nanoscale architectures that dissolve on contact with warmth and reassemble in the condensation. You are breathing it. You are part of the process now. The city already knows you are here. The vegetation is already considering how to incorporate you. The fauna are already adjusting their patterns to account for your presence, your warmth, your particular chemistry.

And the sound goes on. It does not pause. It does not care whether you are listening. It is the sound of a world assembling itself, and it will be deafening until it is done.
