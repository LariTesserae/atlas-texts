You climb. The air thins and sharpens, carrying the mineral bite of high altitude, but there is no wind up here—only a slow, cold exhalation that seems to seep from the stone itself. You are ascending a nascent mountain, a place where the crust broke only recently, leaving the bones of the earth raw and jagged. The black basalt is fresh, its edges still possessing the dangerous, un-eroded sharpness of newly fractured glass. And yet, nothing here is bare.

You enter the grove-cathedral. It is not built; it is grown, a single contiguous organism occupying a natural amphitheater perhaps fifty meters across, walled in by the plunging cliffs of the young peak. This is a space defined by walls and ceilings, a room in the mountain, but the architecture is entirely botanical. The substrate is plant. The floor is a dense, springy weave of luminous silver moss, so tightly packed that not a sliver of stone shows beneath. The columns are the trunks of colossal, slow-growing lichens, rising in ribbed spires that press against the cold air. Overhead, a vaulted canopy of interlocking fronds completely occludes the sky, admitting light only through the tiny, translucent gaps between leaves. 

The light that filters down is dim and deeply aqueous, a diffuse teal glow that makes the air feel submerged. It is cold—bitterly, deeply cold, the kind of cold that settles into the marrow and stays. Your breath does not fog; it freezes almost instantly into tiny crystals that drop away, vanishing into the moss. 

Despite the freezing temperatures, water is everywhere. It is not the rushing water of streams, but the patient, inescapable water of a weeping world. The young stone of the mountain is fractured, and from every crack, water is forced upward and outward under pressure, seeping through the porous basalt. Here, it does not pool; it is immediately claimed. The fronds overhead are perpetually dripping, the water condensing out of the cold, saturated air and rolling down the waxy leaves. The thick moss underfoot squelches with a heavy, gelatinous saturation, and the lichen-columns weep slow, frozen tears that harden into crystal crusts before they can fall. The air is so thick with moisture that it feels like breathing liquid, condensing on your skin in a freezing rime.

The density of the place is overwhelming. There is no empty space. Every cubic inch of the amphitheater is occupied by root, tendril, leaf, or moss. The biological mass is so tightly interwoven that moving through it feels like pushing through a crowded street, yet the crowd is silent and entirely floral. This is a city of vegetation—a high-altitude metropolis of stems and capillaries, organized by the ruthless logic of growth. There are streets of sorts, narrow passages where the moss is slightly more trampled, and plazas where the lichen-columns part around a bubbling fissure. There are even structures within structures: shelf-fungi forming stacked dwellings, parasitic vines knitting rooms between the trunks, a layered infrastructure of pure cellulose and chlorophyll. It is urban in its complexity, a favela of flora, yet entirely devoid of technology. No tools shaped this space; only the slow, blind pressing of life against stone.

It is mostly quiet, but the silence is an active, physical thing. It is the sound of water filtering through capillary networks, the low, sub-audible hum of cells dividing in the freezing dark, the soft, rhythmic *tick* of a droplet striking a frozen leaf before shattering. When the wind shifts far above, a low, resonant groan travels through the lichen-columns, vibrating the canopy like the timbers of an old ship.

Fauna is a rarity here, treated almost as an intruder in the plant-city. But occasionally, in the dimmest recesses where the canopy is thickest, you might see the pale, jointed leg of a blind cave-weta, or the gossamer thread of a cold-loving fungus gnat. They move with agonizing slowness, their metabolisms throttled by the cold, ghosts haunting the green metropolis. 

And the city is moving. The dynamism of the place is a slow-motion catastrophe. The young stone is still shifting, and the plant-mass is constantly reacting. You can feel it if you press your hand against the moss—the floor is rising. The roots are prying apart the fresh basalt, turning stone to sand, pulling the mountain down one mineral grain at a time. The canopy above is slowly closing, the fronds reaching across the dim void to intertwine with their neighbors. A weather cycle plays out in miniature: the mist condenses, the water falls, the moss drinks, the stone cracks. It is a settlement in the midst of a slow, grinding siege against the mountain, an engine of biological destruction that hums with the quiet, relentless energy of life conquering cold rock.
