There is no water here. There has never been water here. And yet, you are drowning in life.

You stand on a vast, elevated plateau—a high-altitude caldera so ancient that its original volcanic rim has been ground down by eons into a barely perceptible bowl, hundreds of miles across. The air is brutally hot, a thick, dry atmosphere that presses against your skin like a heated iron. It carries no humidity, only the acrid, metallic tang of machine oil, ozone, and the sour-sweet exhaust of a trillion breathing stomata. 

The landscape is packed. There is no empty space, no gap, no breathable pocket of vacuum. Everything you see, touch, or walk upon is plant. But this is not a forest—this is a substrate. The entire region is a fully constructed, made-environment of woody stems, calibrated vascular bundles, and engineered lignin. The concept of "natural growth" ended billions of years ago. What remains is total architecture. Fungal-reinforced trunks act as load-bearing pillars; massive, overlapping leaves form ceilings and pressure locks; root systems are laid out in perfect geometric grids, functioning as subterranean conveyer belts and processing pipes. It is a factory built from germination, a fully industrialized ecology where chlorophyll is the engine and cellulose is the steel.

And yet, it is dark. Profoundly, suffocatingly dark. 

The canopy above is so dense, so multi-layered and thickly woven with generations of black-leafed photosynthesizers, that it has entirely sealed the sky. No starlight, no sun, no ambient glow penetrates the upper strata. The darkness is absolute, a physical weight pressing against your retinas. Your eyes will never adjust because there is nothing to adjust to. You must navigate by touch, by the radiating heat of the biological engines, and by the faint, occasional flicker of bioluminescent indicators—pale greens and bruised purples blooming and dying in the deep, functioning as chemical traffic lights for the ecosystem's internal logistics.

The noise is mostly quiet, but it is the quiet of a clenched jaw. There is no wind, no rain, no animal calls. Instead, there is the ceaseless, muffled groaning of biomass under immense pressure. You can hear the slow, hydraulic crunch of thick sap being pumped through arterial trunks ten meters wide. You can hear the imperceptible, terrible creak of structural woods expanding in the heat, settling against one another. It is a soundscape of tectonic biology—a low, constant hum of friction and osmotic strain. 

The turbulence is unimaginable, hidden just beneath the stillness. This place is in a state of perpetual, chaotic construction and deconstruction, a Solaris-like churning of industrial plant-matter. Because there are no animals, no herbivores to prune or predators to cull, the system regulates itself through hyperactive demolition. Massive vines constrict and crush entire structural sectors to make room for new growth; pneumatic seed-pods detonate in the darkness like artillery, firing super-heated, toxic spores into neighboring districts to parasitize and dissolve the old architecture. Every surface is a battlefield of chemical warfare and mechanical cannibalism. The plants are the workers, the engineers, and the demolition crews. They assemble and dismantle the world in a blind, algorithmic frenzy—an anthill of photosynthetic automation.

You run your hand along a wall. It is warm, vibrating with internal pressures, and completely dry. The chemistry here is non-aqueous; the sap that bleeds from the scrape is a viscous, amber solvent, a hydrocarbon resin that burns the skin on contact. It is the lifeblood of this place, carrying the industrial lubricants and chemical catalysts that the root-pipes extract from the salt-flats of the plateau's bedrock. The plant-matter drinks the minerals, processes them in chemical vats disguised as melons and gourds, and extrudes the byproduct as new architectural tissue. It is a closed circuit, a perfectly efficient, self-devouring factory running at redline efficiency.

This is the end-state of a world. An ancient, primordial machine made of living wood and chemical sap, sitting high and dry in the absolute dark. It has settled into its final, turbulent form: a perfect, self-sustaining engine of green industry that has long outlived the light it was built to capture.
