The plateau goes on forever. At this altitude—over four kilometers of accumulated construction, compressed geology, and industrial residue—the horizon curves impossibly, bending away into a permanent haze of condensation and exhaust. This is the High City, a continental expanse of layered urbanism that has long since forgotten its bedrock. It is warm here, not with the warmth of sun-baked stone, but with the deep, chronic fever of a million slow-turning engines, of pressurized steam networks, and of biochemical reactions occurring in the vast, saturated substrate. The heat rises from below, meeting the cooler upper atmosphere, and the result is perpetual condensation.

Water is everywhere, yet there are no rivers, no lakes, no oceans. There is only the Weep. Moisture exudes from every vertical surface, sweating through the pores of ferro-concrete and corroded brass. Long-established erosion—the kind that takes millennia—has smoothed the city's once-sharp angles into flowing, almost organic contours. The edges of towers have dissolved into undulating cascades of mineral deposit; the streets have worn into shallow channels where the constant drip-drip of condensing atmosphere gathers and flows. It is a landscape in perfect, patient equilibrium, shaped by the slow, relentless massage of water and time into something that resembles a cavern carved by an underground river, despite being exposed to a gray, normal-lit sky. The light here is diffuse, scattered by the eternal mist, casting no hard shadows but illuminating everything in a flat, damp glow.

There is no plant life. Not a blade of grass, not a trace of chlorophyll. The green here is the sickly patina of oxidized copper, the deep black-green of chemical slime, the pale verdigris of fungal blooms that feed on the city's exhaust. The urban fabric is purely mineral and constructed—iron, stone, glass, and the endless, creeping rust. The infrastructure is dense, near-touching. Buildings lean against one another, buttressed by centuries of added support, their facades overlapping, their interiors hollowed and refilled with ductwork and steam pipes. It is a city built on top of itself a hundred times over, and every surface is slick with the Weep.

And it moves.

The fauna is overwhelming. In the absence of flora, the ecological niches have been filled by something else—something that thrives on the mineral, the fungal, and the mechanical. They are the Crawl: a generic term for the teeming masses of chitinous, many-legged things that blanket every surface. There are the pale, thumb-sized isopods that congregate in the weeping cracks, millions-strong, their shells clicking and rustling as they feed on the lichen-like fungal mats. There are the long, centipede-forms, as thick as a man's arm, that patrol the vertical surfaces with gripping hooks, preying on the isopods in slow, methodical hunts. There are the fliers—not insects exactly, but something with membranous wings and hydraulic mechanisms, that drift through the humid air in dense clouds, their bodies registering as a faint, static crackle against the skin. They fill the air, they coat the walls, they overflow from every gutter and conduit. To stand still is to be climbed upon. To walk is to wade through them, their dry shells whispering against trouser-legs, their bodies crunching underfoot with a sound like fine gravel.

The sound is immense. Not a sudden roar, but a constant, pressurized drone—the hum of the deep engines, the hiss of venting steam, the metallic groan of settling architecture, and beneath it all, the chittering, clicking, rustling tide of the Crawl. It is the sound of a world in slow, inexorable operation, a machine too vast to shut down, running on cycles measured in centuries. Nothing here is rushed. The erosion takes its time. The creatures move with a patient, mechanical instinct. The water finds its way down through the layers over the course of days, not hours.

This is a world of patient processes. The city does not decay so much as it settles, slowly sinking under its own weight into the soft, saturated substrate. The Crawl do not swarm so much as they persist, an inescapable presence in every cubic meter of space. The Weep does not fall so much as it accumulates, bead by bead, on every available surface until gravity claims it. The dynamic is glacial—gradual cycles of rust and condensation, of slow mechanical breathing, of populations rising and falling over generations that span decades.

Even the industrial processes here are ancient and unhurried. The factories—vast, cathedral-like structures of riveted iron and dripping stone—produce nothing recognizable anymore. Their pistons rise and fall in languid rhythm, their flywheels turn with the momentum of centuries, their purpose long since subsumed into the simple imperative of continued operation. They exhale heat and moisture and the faint chemical tang of sulfur and iron, and the Crawl gather around their exhaust vents in thick, writhing carpets, drawn by the warmth and the mineral-rich precipitation.

There are no people. There haven't been for a very long time. The city was built for inhabitants of a different scale and different needs, and they have left behind only the infrastructure and the machines too massive and too slow to stop. In their absence, the Crawl have inherited the spaces between—the apartments, the corridors, the transit tunnels, the plazas—all of them now thick with condensation and the endless, patient movement of chitin. The buildings stand like empty shells, their interiors divided and subdivided by the accumulated deposits of the Weep into smaller and smaller chambers, each one hosting its own micro-population of the Crawl.

From the highest vantage—a platform on a still-standing spire of corroded steel—the view is of an ocean. Not of water, but of roof-lines, stretching to every horizon, their shapes softened by distance and the eternal mist into a single, undulating mass of gray and rust-red. The weather moves across this expanse in slow, vast sheets of heavier condensation—not quite rain, just a thickening of the ever-present damp, a shift in the quality of the Weep. And through it all, the Crawl continue their ancient, measured cycles, and the engines turn, and the water gathers and falls, and the city slowly, imperceptibly, settles deeper into itself.
