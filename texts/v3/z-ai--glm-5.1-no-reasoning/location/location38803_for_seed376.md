# The Singing Loam

## I.

There is a plateau above the treeline that should not be warm enough to grow what grows there.

It sits at a height where the air goes thin and the cold has a cutting quality — not the dramatic cold of blizzards, but the patient, molecular cold of altitude, the kind that works its way into cartilage and stays. Frost forms on everything each night. By midmorning it has usually burned off, though in the shaded gullies it can linger until afternoon, and in the deepest clefts, it never leaves at all. You can find ice in those clefts in the middle of summer. Thin, milky ice, sheeted over the rock like old laminate, with dark shapes moving beneath it — roots, always roots, pressing up against the frozen skin from below, testing it, waiting for the one warm day that will let them through.

The plants should not be here. That is the first thing you understand. Everything else follows from that paradox.

This is a place where the vegetation has solved a problem and not stopped solving it. The growing season should be sixty days, maybe eighty in a warm year. The soil should be poor — scree and gravel, the ground-down remnants of metamorphic basement rock, granite and schist, acidic and thin. The wind should be brutal. And yet the plateau is covered, covered absolutely, in plant matter. Not the stunted, wind-clipped alpine tundra you would expect — the knee-high willows, the cushion plants, the thing-that-survives. This is something else. This is biomass. This is weight. The ground itself is more root than mineral. You walk on it and you are walking on living tissue, on generations of living tissue laid down and folded over and grown through, a deep accumulation that has its own geology, its own strata. Cut a cross-section through the hillside and you would read it like sedimentary rock: here a layer of fibrous root mat from some drought century, here a band of compressed woody material from a period of explosive growth, here a pale vein of fungal mycelium that runs for hundreds of meters in every direction, a white thread in the dark loam.

The plants built this place. The rock underneath is just the foundation they agreed to build on.

## II.

There is no architecture here. No walls, no path, no marker, no post, no fence. Nothing has ever been constructed. And yet the place has structure — an architecture of growth, a form that is not designed but is nonetheless organized, as a forest is organized, as a reef is organized, by the accumulated decisions of ten thousand organisms responding to ten thousand conditions over ten thousand years.

The canopy — if you can call it that — is not high. Three meters, maybe four at the peaks. It is formed not by trees but by a woody shrub that has, over centuries, abandoned the usual constraints of its kind. In the sheltered lowlands it would be a knee-high tangle, a scrappy thing, unremarkable. Up here it has become something else. The cold has slowed its metabolism but not its growth — or rather, it has grown *because* of the cold, has been forced to thicken, to layer, to build density rather than height, to accumulate mass the way a glacier accumulates ice, season after season, adding tissue that never quite dies back, that stays semi-dormant through the winter and then resumes, so that each branch is a record of years, a spiraling, gnarled thing with the texture of driftwood and the tensile strength of cable. These shrubs grow over each other, into each other, through each other. Their branches fuse at contact points — a process called inosculation, common enough in willows, but here it has become the dominant structural strategy. The entire canopy is a single interlocked network. You cannot pull one branch without moving the whole mass. It flexes. It yields. It does not break.

Under this living roof, the light is green and diffuse. Not dark — the canopy is not dense enough to fully exclude the sky, and the shrub's small, thick leaves are spaced to let light through, as though the plant has calibrated its own shading to maintain the conditions it needs below. But the light that passes is filtered, softened, given a color that is not quite the color of any sky. It is the color of light that has been used once already and is being lent to the lower stories.

## III.

Because there are lower stories. Many of them.

Beneath the canopy shrub, a second layer of growth fills the space at chest height — a dense, horizontal tangle of herbaceous plants and low vines, some flowering, some not, all of them growing with a kind of desperate luxuriance that seems wrong in this cold. The flowers are small and pale. Not the showy alpine blooms that use color to attract what few pollinators exist at this altitude — these are modest, almost translucent, the kind of flowers that seem to be pollinating themselves, or not bothering with pollination at all, reproducing vegetatively, by runner and by root, by layering and by fragmentation. They do not need animals. They have largely given up on animals.

Below this is a third layer: mosses and liverworts, cushioning everything, covering every surface, so that the ground is not ground but a deep soft mat of bryophyte that yields underfoot like a sponge. It holds water. That is its function and its genius. The plateau's moisture is intermittent — rain comes in brief, cold showers, maybe twice a week in the growing season, less in winter. Condensation is more reliable. Each night, as the temperature drops, fog rolls in from the valleys below and deposits a fine sheen of water on every surface — every leaf, every stem, every thread of moss. By morning, the whole plateau is dripping. Not rain, exactly. Not dew, exactly. Something between — a constant, low-grade wetness that the moss captures and holds and slowly releases into the root layer below.

The moss is the reservoir. The moss is the aquifer. Without it, none of this would be possible.

And below the moss, the roots — a fourth layer, a fifth, a sixth, going down as deep as the canopy is high, a subterranean mirror of the growth above, but denser, darker, interwoven with fungal networks that are not ancillary but structural, that are in some real sense the nervous system of the whole plateau, transporting nutrients from one sector to another, chemically signaling, mediating relationships between species, deciding who eats and who does not. The mycelium is the thing that makes this place a single organism rather than a collection of organisms. It is the substrate beneath the substrate.

## IV.

And then there is the thing that should not be here either, alongside the plants.

You hear it before you understand it. Or rather — you feel it, a low vibration that comes up through the soles of your boots, through the moss, through the root mat, a deep rhythmic pulse that is not quite sound and not quite motion. A throb. Like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

The plateau is not still. It is not the serene, contemplative place it appears to be from a distance. It is moving. It is always moving.

The root mat — that deep, living accumulation of organic material that has replaced the soil — is subject to something the locals, if there were locals, might call *sloughing*. The plateau is not flat. It has a slope, a gentle but consistent grade toward the east, and the root mat, which can be several meters deep, is not anchored to bedrock. It is anchored to itself. And itself is heavy, and itself is saturated with water, and itself is on a slope, and so, slowly, continuously, the entire mass is creeping downhill.

Not catastrophically. Not as a landslide. But at a rate that is, geologically speaking, quite fast — perhaps several centimeters a year. The whole plateau is sliding east. The leading edge of the slide, where the root mat meets the steeper descent below, is a ragged scarp, a wall of dark, fibrous peat and tangled root that crumbles and sloughs and regrows even as it falls, so that the boundary is alive, is constantly being rebuilt at the top even as it collapses at the bottom. New growth reaches out over the edge. Old growth falls away. The scarp advances and retreats and advances again.

And this movement — this slow, continuous, gravitational reshaping — is what produces the vibration. The root mat does not slide smoothly. It moves in stages, in micro-surges, as sections of it lose adhesion and then catch, lose and catch, the whole mass rippling like a slow wave, like peristalsis, like something ingesting itself. Each micro-surge transmits a low-frequency pulse through the substrate. You feel it in your feet. You feel it in your teeth. If you lie down on the moss and press your ear to the ground, you can hear it — a deep, rhythmic, organic sound, not mechanical, not natural in any way you are used to, something between a groan and a purr, the sound of a living landscape digesting its own past.

## V.

And yet — and this is the thing that stays with you — the place is quiet. The vibration is subsonic, felt more than heard. Above the ground, in the air, in the space between the canopy and the moss, there is almost nothing.

No birds. At this altitude, in this cold, the birds do not come. Occasionally, very occasionally, you might see a raven passing high overhead, a black shape against the grey-white sky, but it does not land. There is nothing here for it. The ecosystem has no animals to speak of. No insects — or almost none. A few species of mite, perhaps, living in the moss, too small to see. A species of springtail that has been found nowhere else, a translucent, blind thing that lives in the deep root layer and feeds on fungal hyphae. But nothing that flies, nothing that crawls visibly, nothing that makes a sound.

No wind, either, most of the time. The canopy shrub is low enough, and dense enough, and the surrounding topography shelters the plateau from the prevailing winds, so that inside the canopy the air is still. Not dead-still — there is movement, a slow circulation, a cold breath that comes and goes as the temperature shifts — but still enough that you can hear individual leaves falling. A leaf detaches from the canopy shrub, spirals down through the green light, and lands on the moss with a sound so small it is almost imaginary. A whisper of contact. That is the loudest thing that happens here.

The silence is not empty. It is full. It is the silence of a place where so many organisms are growing so slowly and so persistently that the aggregate activity is enormous but the individual events are microscopic. The plateau is as busy as a city, as turbulent as a marketplace — roots growing, fungi transmitting, mosses photosynizing, flowers opening, stems layering, the whole mass creeping downhill, the leading edge collapsing and regrowing, the mycelium extending and retreating — but all of it happens at a tempo that human senses cannot register without patience. You have to slow down. You have to stop. You have to stand in one place for long enough that the place reveals its motion, the way a time-lapse reveals the motion of a flower opening.

If you do — if you stand still for an hour, or two — you begin to notice that the green is not uniform. It shifts. There are patterns in the canopy, variations in leaf density and color that move like clouds, or like tides, the plant communities responding to each other, to the moisture, to the temperature, to the chemical signals passing through the mycelium, in a slow, continuous, invisible conversation that has been going on for centuries and will go on for centuries more. The plateau is not one thing. It is a negotiation. It is a parliament of organisms, and the parliament is always in session.

## VI.

And then there is the other thing. The thing that you are not sure about. The thing that might be real or might be the effect of standing too long in cold, thin air, in a place that is too quiet and too alive.

The plants are not doing what plants should do.

Not in any dramatic way. Not in a way you can point to and say: *that, there, that is wrong.* But the more time you spend here, the more you notice small deviations. A branch of the canopy shrub that has grown in a shape that is too regular, too angular, almost geometric. A patch of moss that has formed a ridge, a raised line that runs for several meters in a perfectly straight path before disappearing under the root mat. A cluster of flowers on one stem that are arranged not in the usual spiral but in a pattern that, when you count them, resolves into a Fibonacci sequence so precise it seems deliberate.

You tell yourself it is coincidence. You tell yourself that plants follow mathematical patterns naturally, that phyllotaxis is well-documented, that the Fibonacci sequence appears in sunflowers and pinecones and a hundred other species. This is true. But the precision here is different. It is not the loose, approximate precision of normal biology. It is *exact.* And it is not limited to one species. The same patterns appear in the moss, in the root configurations exposed at the scarp edge, in the branching of the fungal networks that you can see when you peel back a section of the mat. Different organisms, different kingdoms, all producing the same patterns. The same geometries. As though they are all reading from the same script.

And then there are the sounds that are not quite sounds. The ones that come at dusk, when the temperature drops and the condensation begins and the whole plateau starts to drip. You are standing in the green light, in the silence, and you hear — or almost hear, or imagine you hear — something that is not the drip of water and not the creak of branches and not the pulse of the sliding root mat. Something higher. Something that has a quality of *intention* about it. A tone. Not a constant tone, but a shifting one, a sequence of tones that almost resolves into a phrase, a musical phrase, before it dissolves back into the general quiet.

It comes from the plants. You are almost sure of it. From the stems, maybe, or the leaves, vibrating as the condensation changes their weight, as the temperature contracts their fibers. It is a physical phenomenon. It must be. But it does not sound physical. It sounds — and you cannot shake this impression, try as you might — it sounds like language. Not language you understand. Not language directed at you. But language. Patterned sound produced by a living system for a purpose that you cannot identify.

The plateau is talking. Not to you. Not about you. To itself, perhaps. Or to something else entirely. Something that you cannot see, that you have no apparatus to detect, that exists in a medium you do not share.

## VII.

You leave eventually. You have to. The cold gets into you, and the silence gets into you, and the vibration gets into you, and after a while the combination of the three produces a state that is not quite comfortable — a state of heightened attention, of sensory openness, that is exhausting in the way that meditation is exhausting, the way that listening very hard for a very long time is exhausting. You feel as though you have been in the presence of something that was doing a thing you were not meant to see. Not forbidden. Just not meant for you. A process that predates you by centuries and will outlast you by centuries, a process that has nothing to do with you and no use for you and no interest in you, and that will continue, slowly, silently, in the cold and the damp and the green light, whether you are there or not.

The plants do not notice you leaving. The parliament does not pause. The root mat continues its slow slide east. The scarp crumbles and regrows. The mycelium extends its white threads through the dark loam. The tones at dusk rise and fall and dissolve.

You come down off the plateau and the world becomes ordinary again — loud, fast, full of animals and buildings and the sharp, confident sounds of things that know what they are doing. You are glad of it. You are relieved.

But sometimes, now, in the ordinary silence of an ordinary evening, you feel something in your feet. A low pulse. A rhythm. And you stop, and you listen, and for a moment — just a moment — you almost hear it. The phrase. The pattern. The thing that the plateau was saying, and maybe is still saying, and maybe will go on saying, in the green light, in the cold, in the deep, living loam, for a very, very long time.

You don't know what it means. You're not sure you want to.
