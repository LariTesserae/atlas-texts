To stand in the High Kelp is to stand inside the lung of a dying, mechanical god.

The elevation here is extreme—the plateau of the Weltansicht rises above the cloud line, where the air is thin and carries the sharp, coppery chill of a late autumn evening. Yet there is no rock beneath your feet. There is only plant matter. This is a total ecology, a substrate of pure vegetation. The ground is a woven mat of pneumatic rhizomes the size of subway cars, interlocking in a tension that has replaced stone and soil for millennia. To walk is to walk on the skin of something alive and immensely old.

Rising from this breathing floor are the cities. They are not built *from* the plants; they are *grown* as the plants, but they are undeniably urban—a settlement of staggering density and organized infrastructure. Massive load-bearing trunks, a quarter-mile thick, rise like the pillars of ancient basilicas, their bark armored with decades of hardened, blackened sap. Between them, the architecture cascades: tiered structures of compressed lignin, hollowed gourds serving as granaries, and miles of interlocking vine-bridges forming a gridiron streetscape. It is a city of forty million limbs, bearing the geometric logic of a metropolis, yet entirely devoid of mortar or brick. 

The built environment and the industrial are hopelessly entangled here. The vegetation has been bred, or has naturally evolved, to replace the factory. Massive thorn-clusters act as the rivets and hinges of the city's infrastructure. Along the major arterial vines, you can see the industrial engines at work: giant, sap-filled bladders contract with the slow, devastating force of hydraulic presses, stamping out fibers and extruding hardened celluloid panels in a scaled, mechanized production. Electrical arcs jump between modified, copper-rich fronds, powering a crude but effective electrification grid that runs through the city's veins. The smell of ozone mingles with the sickly-sweet scent of fermenting chlorophyll and grease.

It is incredibly loud. The sound is inescapable, a regional din that fills the vast, open sky. It is the rhythmic, deafening thump-whirr-thump-whirr of the hydraulic sap-bladders pumping nutrients up the immense trunks. It is the groaning of wood under tension, the shriek of celluloid being stamped, and the low, subsonic rumble of the plateau's tectonic roots shifting. The noise is a physical pressure, vibrating in the marrow of your bones.

Despite the staggering density of the city, the space itself feels sparse. There is a vast emptiness between the mega-trunks, a low-saturation void where only the thinnest support cables and mist-trap webs span the gaps. Walking the high boulevards, you feel the terrifying openness of the altitude, the sheer drop between the heavily settled nodes. 

Water is a sparse, intermittent blessing. It comes not as lakes or rivers, but as a heavy, intermittent drizzle that the high altitude catches from the passing clouds. Every surface of the city is built to harvest this dampness. Condensation drips from the high canopy down the tiered streets, collected in massive cistern-vines that bulge like goitered throats along the buildings. The rain falls in sudden, brief spells, leaving the air smelling of wet ash and cold iron, before the wind scours the plateau dry again.

In this immense, churning metropolis, fauna is a rarity. There are no birds, no scurrying rodents, no swarms of insects. The plant-city has long since outcompeted animal life, absorbing its functions into its own tissues. When a creature does appear, it is a shocking, almost mythical event—a pale, blind millipede the size of a train car, writhing out of a sap-pipe to be immediately crushed and absorbed by the hydraulic presses, its proteins a momentary spike in the city's diet. 

The city is active, a place of perpetual, grinding motion. Sap flows. Presses stamp. Spores are ejected from the highest spires in massive, green clouds that catch the normal, golden light of the afternoon sun, casting the entire region in a shifting, dappled shadow. There is a biological pace to the industry, a steady, predator-prey rhythm of consumption and growth, but it is entirely inward-facing. The city eats itself to build itself, an eternal motion of mechanized biology.

But as the sun angles lower and the golden light turns to a bruised purple, the reality of the High Kelp begins to fray. The weirdness creeps in at the edges of perception. This landscape is at the end of its life, an ancient, primordial thing settled into its final form, and the weight of its age is tearing the fabric of the world. The deep erosion of time hasn't worn the city down; it has worn reality down *around* the city.

Distance begins to stutter. A vine-bridge a mile away might look as close as your hand, while the massive trunk beside you seems to stretch into an infinite, hollow vanishing point. The rhythmic thumping of the sap-engines sometimes falls out of sync with time—you see the press stamp down, and a full second later, the booming sound hits your ears, yet the vibration in your feet happened five minutes ago. The shadows cast by the structures do not always match the shapes of the plants; sometimes, your shadow raises an arm when you do not. 

On the sparse, open edges of the plateau, looking out over the clouds, the sky occasionally peels. It reveals not stars, but a negative space of knotted roots and industrial piping, as if the world is merely a blister of vegetation growing on the inside of something far larger, and far more mechanical. The city grinds on, deaf to its own impossibility, pumping sap into a sky that is slowly forgetting how to be solid.
