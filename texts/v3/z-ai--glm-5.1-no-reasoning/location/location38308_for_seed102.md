To enter the Throat, you must descend. Not through any grand cavern mouth or dramatic fissure, but through a forgotten, crumbling crack in the bedrock of a sun-scorched plain—a crevice so narrow it forces the human body to turn sideways and breathe shallow. You go down, down, past the reach of surface winds, past the drying kiss of the sun, until the stone around you grows warm, then hot, then alive with heat. You emerge into a pocket of the world hidden from the sky, a subterranean chamber barely thirty meters across, pressed deep beneath the weight of a dead, dry continent.

It is a place of profound, suffocating warmth. The air hangs at a temperature just shy of searing—a thick, wet, mineral heat that coats the lungs and feels like swallowing a warm, dense sponge. There is no water here. None at all. And yet, this place is drenched. 

The walls, ceiling, and floor of the chamber are blanketed in a riot of flora so dense it rivals a rainforest canopy, a staggering paradox in this absolute void of moisture. This vegetation does not drink. It synthesizes. The flora is composed of thick, translucent, waxy stalks and heavy, bulbous leaves in shades of bruised violet, oily indigo, and radioactive yellow-green. They are turgid to the point of bursting, swollen with a non-aqueous solvent—a heavy, slick, lukewarm ammonia-silicate plasma that flows through their veins instead of water. When you brush against them, they do not rustle; they squelch, their fleshy surfaces clinging and peeling apart with a wet, organic suction. Giant fungal-like blooms, the size of clenched fists, hang from the ceiling on drooping vines, glowing with a faint, sickly bioluminescence that provides the chamber’s only light. It is dim down here—a perpetual, shadowed twilight where the edges of the room dissolve into a warm, violet gloom, and the shapes of the plants bleed into one another like watercolors in the heat.

Despite the crush of life on the walls, the center of the chamber is remarkably empty. The floor is a jagged, sharp-featured plain of raw, young basalt, its edges freshly fractured and unweathered, thrust up from some recent tectonic shift. The space feels like a lung, an intimate, pressurized room where the lush, clinging life on the periphery only makes the hollow center feel more vast and breathless. 

In this sparse, open middle, the room’s inhabitants congregate. The fauna here is abundant, yet sparse in its distribution—solitary creatures separated by wide, respectful margins across the sharp stone. They are bizarre, chitinous things that have never seen the sun. Six-legged, low-slung foragers with carapaces of polished obsidian and clusters of pale, unseeing whiskers that sweep the ground. They move with a slow, deliberate grace, pressing their flat, hollow mouths against the fleshy roots that cross the floor, sucking out the warm ammonia-sap with a loud, wet gulping sound. Occasionally, two will meet, circling one another before tapping their hard shells together—a hollow, resonant *clack* that echoes through the chamber, the only percussive instrument in an overwhelming symphony of sound.

Because above all else, the Throat is loud. Deafeningly so. The chamber throbs with a constant, physical vibration that you feel in your teeth and sternum before you process it as sound. It is a deep, subsonic hum that rises and falls in endless, overlapping waves—the collective exhaust of the vegetation. Millions of microscopic pores on the surface of the fleshy leaves are continuously venting excess thermal gas and vapor to regulate their internal pressure. This venting creates a ceaseless, polyphonic hiss, a roaring white noise that builds and rebounds off the low, close ceiling. It is the sound of a billion slow, organic heartbeats, of a world breathing hard in a sealed box.

Amidst this overwhelming soundscape and sparse scatter of foraging beasts, you notice the scattered remains of the builders. They are faint marks, partial remnants of a primitive people who once sought refuge—or perhaps communion—down here. There are no grand structures, only alterations. Near the far wall, a cluster of basalt stalagmites has been laboriously carved with crude hand-tools—flint or bone—into bulbous, eye-like totems, their surfaces smoothed by generations of touch. On the floor, a single building, if it can even be called that: a small, half-collapsed cairn of heat-shattered stone, just large enough for a single person to lie inside, positioned directly over a deep fissure where the chamber’s roaring hum is concentrated into a single, mind-numbing drone. Whoever they were, they worked with their hands, with no metal, no advanced craft, just the slow, obsessive dedication of early minds overwhelmed by the strange.

And then, you realize the most terrifying, hypnotic thing about the Throat. 

It never changes. 

You sit in the dim, violet twilight for an hour, and the heat does not shift. The roaring hiss of the venting flora does not fluctuate by a single decibel. The bioluminescent blooms do not pulse; they emit a steady, unending glow. The obsidian foragers move in the exact same loops, tapping the same roots, clacking their shells at the exact same intervals. The chamber is trapped in a perfect, frozen equilibrium. The plants are not growing; they are not dying. They are simply maintaining. There are no gradients here, no seasons, no decay, no erosion smoothing the young basalt, no water shifting the stones. Time has pooled here, stagnated into an eternal, suffocating present. The Throat breathes, but it does not live. It merely persists, a loud, warm, wet pocket of impossible eternity hidden beneath the dead crust of the earth.
