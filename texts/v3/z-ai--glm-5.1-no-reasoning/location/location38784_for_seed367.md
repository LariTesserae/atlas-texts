# The Hearth-City of Moum

It exists inside a cleft of the world—two hundred armspans deep, carved by some patient ancestor of wind into the flanks of the Great Tepui. A pocket. A wound that healed into something lived-in. From the mesa's top, you would never know it was there: just another stretch of flat, ancient stone, worn smooth by a billion years of rain that no longer comes. But descend the crevice—shoulder-width, then room-width, then the sudden cathedral opening—and you find Moum.

---

There is no water here. This is the first thing a visitor must understand, because every instinct will lie. Moum *looks* wet. It gleams. The air sits thick and humid against the skin, and the walls of the crevice weep with condensation, and the growth that covers every surface has the lush, full look of things that drink deep. But it is not water. What condenses on the stone, what beads on leaf-tips and runs in thin rivulets down the stairways—it is a dense, slightly viscous fluid that the Moum-folk call *breath-stuff*. Mineral-rich vapor, risen from geothermal vents far below, that meets the cooler air of the crevice and precipitates out. It tastes of copper and iron and old salt. It sustains everything.

The plants have learned to drink it directly. They are not quite like surface vegetation—their leaves are broader, flatter, often vertical, designed to catch condensation rather than rain. They grow in profusion. Moss analogs coat the lower walls in thick, emerald-pelt layers. Shelf-fungi jut from the stone in crowded brackets, pale as bone, their surfaces slick with breath-stuff. Vines—thick, dark, rope-like—descend from the crevice's upper reaches, forming curtains, forming walls of their own. The air tastes green. The light—what there is of it—is filtered through a biomass so dense that standing at the crevice's floor and looking up is like looking through murky water toward a distant, gray sky.

---

The light is dim. The crevice opens to the sky above, but it is a narrow slit, and the canopy of vine and epiphyte chokes even that. What reaches the floor of Moum is the light of perpetual dusk. You can see, but only just. Colors desaturate. Depth becomes uncertain. The Moum-folk navigate by touch and sound as much as sight.

And the sound—there is always sound. The crevice holds noise like a bowl holds water. It accumulates. The constant drip of breath-stuff condensing, a thousand tiny strikes on stone and leaf, forms a baseline rhythm. The vents below add a low, subsonic thrum that is felt in the chest more than heard. And over all of it, the creatures.

---

The fauna are overwhelming. This is the second thing a visitor must understand.

The crevice is a sealed system—or near enough. Energy enters as geothermal heat and the thin trickle of organic matter that blows in from above, but mostly it cycles within. The plants convert breath-stuff and dim light into biomass. The arthropods—some the size of a thumbnail, some as large as a man's hand—consume the plants and each other. The mollusks—soft-bodied, shell-less, glistening with the same viscous sheen as everything else—graze the moss-fur and fungi. The vertebrates hunt them all.

They are everywhere. The walls move if you watch them long enough—not stone shifting, but the thousand-legged transit of pale, eyeless centipedal things following chemical trails. The air carries small, fluttering things—not insects, not birds, something between—that navigate by echo, clicking and listening, their calls forming a layered, chattering static that never ceases. The floor, if you stand still, develops a moving texture as beetle-analogs and their larger, more active cousins flow around and over your feet. Nothing bites, at first. They are curious, not predatory toward something they haven't catalogued. But stand still too long and the grazing mollusks will test you—rasping their radulae against exposed skin, not to harm but to taste, to see if you are food.

The Moum-folk have learned to live in the motion. They do not clear the creatures from their homes. They cannot. Instead, they coexist—hunting what they need, building what defenses they must, accepting that their city is also a reef, and reefs have tenants in every crevice.

---

The city. It is a city, though it would not look like one to someone raised in a place of straight lines and open streets. There are perhaps three hundred souls in Moum, and their dwellings cling to the walls of the crevice like the shelf-fungi do—clustered, organic, grown as much as built. The construction is stone and plant-fiber and the dried bodies of the larger fungi, bound with clay that the breath-stuff slowly mineralizes into something harder. Walls are thick. Roofs slope, to guide condensation into collection troughs—though the fluid is not drunk directly. It is fermented, mineralized, mixed with ash and herbs until it becomes the fermented drink the Moum-folk call *deep-voice*, which sustains the body in ways that water never could.

The stairs are carved directly into the crevice walls—shallow, hand-hewn, worn by generations of bare feet into smooth concavities. They connect tier to tier, level to level. At the deepest point, near the vent-shafts where the heat rises and the air is thick with mineral vapor, are the baths—not water, but warm breath-stuff fog, where the Moum-folk sit and breathe deep and let the copper-scent fill their lungs. At the highest point, just below the slit of sky, are the watching-posts, where a few still look outward, though nothing has come through the crevice from the outside in living memory.

The density is extreme. Moum is intimate in the way a throat is intimate—everything close, everything touching, everything in proximity to the pulse of something larger. Homes share walls. Paths overlap. You hear your neighbor's breathing, their arguments, their songs. There are no private spaces, only ones where people agree not to look.

---

It is warm. The geothermal vents below see to that—Moum sits in a perpetual humid warmth, like a greenhouse, like a mouth. The Moum-folk wear little. Plant-fiber wraps, tool-belts, the dried carapaces of larger creatures as armor when hunting. Their skin is slick with breath-stuff condensation. They run hot. They move quickly. Life here moves quickly—the ecosystem cycles fast, generation to generation, birth to death to decomposition and new growth in a constant churning. The moss on a wall today was fertilizer last month. A creature that dies in the morning is scaffold for new growth by evening.

The crevice is ancient beyond reckoning. The tepui itself is ancient beyond reckoning—one of the oldest exposed landforms on the surface, a slab of stone that was old when the mountains were young, and has been wearing down ever since. The crevice was a crack in that stone, opened and widened over eons, reaching its current shape long before anything with eyes evolved to see it. Moum-the-place is older than Moum-the-city by a factor of millions. This is evident in the stone itself—it is smoothed, rounded, every edge and corner worn away by the patient work of time. Even the sharp edges that remain are only sharp in comparison to the greater softness around them.

The city sits in this ancient wound like a growth—new, temporary, a bloom of organization and purpose that will one day pass, as all such blooms do, while the crevice endures.

---

But for now, it teems. A visitor descending the crevice for the first time would be struck, most of all, by the noise and the movement. Nothing is still. The air moves, the creatures move, the people move, the condensed breath-stuff drips and flows. It is loud—which is perhaps the strangest thing about a place so hidden, so enclosed, so small. It should be quiet. It is not. The acoustics of the stone funnel and amplify every sound until the crevice-roar becomes a single, sustained chord: drip and click and thrum and voice and the constant, papery rustle of ten thousand arthropod legs on stone.

And through the middle of it all, the Moum-folk walk their worn stairways, harvest their fungi, hunt their prey, ferment their deep-voice, raise their children, sing their songs—adding their small sounds to the great accumulation, living inside an ancient wound in an ancient stone, in the only pocket of warmth and green for a thousand leagues in any direction.

They do not know they are hidden. They do not know they are small. They know the crevice, its walls, its creatures, its seasons—and that is a world.
