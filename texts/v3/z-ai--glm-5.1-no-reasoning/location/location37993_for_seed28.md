# The Chamber of Ten Thousand Mouths

You find it through a crack. A fissure in basalt, somewhere on a low plateau where the world has been dry for longer than dry has meant anything. The crack is barely wide enough for shoulders. It descends — not far, maybe ten meters — through rock that grows warm as you go, and then opens.

And then there is the sound.

---

It hits like a wall. Not one sound — thousands. A choral roar, a layered and interlocking cacophony that seems to have texture, that seems to have *architecture*. It vibrates in the sternum. It fills the sinuses. It tastes mineral and sweet.

You are standing in a chamber.

It is not large. Perhaps thirty meters across, roughly circular, the ceiling low enough that you could press your palm to it in most places. The rock is basalt on the outside, but inward it transitions — first to a dark sandstone, then to something pale, chalky, almost organic. Veined. The walls are warm. Not hot. Living-room warm. The temperature of skin.

The floor drops away from the entrance in a gentle bowl, and the bowl is *built*. Steps have been carved — not neatly, not with straight edges, but with the confidence of people who understood the stone and worked with hand-tools of a kind you cannot identify. Chert, maybe. Obsidian. Something that could score this soft pale rock and leave grooves that have since been smoothed by generations of feet.

And every surface is covered in green.

---

The vegetation is impossible. This is a sealed chamber. No rain reaches it. No surface water exists — the world above is mineral and void, a salt-flat horizon in every direction, dry as a held breath. And yet the chamber is *lush*.

Moss first — or something like moss. A dense, velvety mat of it covers the floor between the carved steps, springy underfoot, releasing a scent that is difficult to place. Not damp. The air is not humid in the way of a cave with water. It is warm and strangely dry, but charged with something — a gas, perhaps, something faintly sulfuric, something that the vegetation is metabolizing. The moss is dark green, nearly black in the dimmer corners, and where the light hits it, it glows. Not bioluminescence. Something else. The chloroplasts — or whatever serves that function here — are highly efficient, almost reflective, bouncing light back amplified.

Then the shelves.

The walls of the chamber are not smooth. They are *terraced*. Layer after layer of shallow alcoves, each perhaps the size of a loaf of bread, carved into the pale rock in spiraling rows that ascend from floor to ceiling. Hundreds of them. Thousands. And in each alcove, a plant.

They are not all the same species. Far from it. Some are fern-like, fronds arching gracefully outward, deep emerald, their surfaces covered in fine hairs that catch the light. Some are bulbous, succulent things, swollen and pale, with thick waxy leaves that seem to store something — not water, you realize, pressing one. It gives slightly, and the fluid inside is viscous, almost oily. A non-aqueous chemistry. Some solvent that these plants have evolved to use in place of water. Some are tall and thin, reedy, reaching for the ceiling, and these are the ones that are *singing*.

No. Not singing. They are the source.

---

The reedy plants — you count dozens, then lose count — have hollow stems. At intervals along each stem, small pores open, and through these pores, air is forced. Not by wind. There is no wind. The plants themselves are pumping gas through their tissues — drawing the warm mineral atmosphere from the walls, channeling it through root systems that penetrate deep into the rock, and expelling it through these pores at specific frequencies.

Each plant produces a tone. A single, sustained, unwavering tone. But the tones are not random. They are tuned. The plants nearest the floor produce low frequencies — deep, resonant, almost subsonic notes that you feel more than hear. As the plants ascend the terraced walls, their tones climb. The ones near the ceiling are high, piercing, a crystalline ring that threads through the lower register like silver wire.

Together, they produce a chord. A vast, shimmering, continuously sounding chord that fills the chamber at a volume that is genuinely difficult to endure. It is not painful, exactly, but it is *overwhelming*. It presses against the eardrums. It makes the teeth buzz. After a few minutes, the sound seems to separate into layers — you can hear individual voices within it, individual plants, each one holding its note with the patience of something that has been playing since before you were born.

The sound has been here for a very long time.

---

You notice the city.

It takes time to notice, because the vegetation is so thick, but the terraces are not the only built structure. The chamber floor — beneath the moss — is paved. Smooth stones, fitted together without mortar, gaps no wider than a blade. Drainage channels run between the paving stones, shallow and precise, leading to small catchments at the base of the walls. There are no liquids to drain, but the channels are there, maintained, kept clear of debris. Someone built them for a reason that may no longer apply, or for a reason you do not yet understand.

Near the center of the chamber, a structure rises. It is not a building, exactly — more a platform, waist-high, circular, built of the same pale stone as the walls. Its surface is carved with concentric grooves, and standing in the grooves, fitted precisely, are stones of different sizes and shapes. You recognize the pattern. It is a map. Not of the surface — of something else. The chamber itself, perhaps. Or the root system beneath it. Or something conceptual, something you do not have the framework to read.

Around the platform, scattered with the carelessness of long habitation, are tools. Stone tools. Flaked chert in various stages of sharpness. Grinding stones worn smooth by decades of use. Small ceramic vessels — fired, but simply, without glaze or decoration. A few pieces of worked bone, their purpose unclear. No metal. Nothing machined. Everything is hand-made, and everything is old, and everything is *in use* — the grinding stones have fresh powder on them, the chert tools have recent chips, the ceramic vessels hold traces of whatever they last contained.

Someone was here. Recently. Maybe hours ago.

---

The light.

It comes from above. A single opening in the ceiling — not the crack you entered through, but a different one, wider, positioned directly over the central platform. Light pours through it in a solid column, bright and white, and where it strikes the floor, the moss grows thickest, richest, almost luminous. The light is reflected upward by the moss, bounces off the pale stone walls, and fills the chamber with a diffuse, even glow. It is bright enough to read by. Bright enough to see every detail, every carved terrace, every plant, every tool.

The opening in the ceiling is not natural. It has been shaped. Reinforced. There are stones set around its rim, fitted to prevent collapse, and the angle of the shaft above it has been deliberately calculated to track — you suspect — the sun. At certain times of day, the column of light would move across the floor, touching different terraces, different plants. At those moments, the plants in the light would photosynthesize at a higher rate, and their gas output would increase, and their tone would shift — slightly louder, slightly higher.

The chord would change.

This is, you begin to understand, an instrument. The entire chamber. The terraces, the plants, the carved acoustics of the walls, the shaft of light — all of it is a single instrument, and the sound it produces is not static. It shifts with the time of day. It shifts with the seasons. It shifts with the growth of the plants, which are tended, pruned, replanted. The people who built this — or the people who maintain it now, with their chert tools and their ceramic pots — are not just gardeners. They are composers. They are tuning a living organism that has been playing, continuously, for what must be centuries.

---

You listen.

Beneath the roar of the chord — beneath the sustained tones of the reedy plants and the subsonic hum of the moss and the ringing of the ceiling-high notes — there is another sound. It is rhythmic. It pulses. It comes from the walls themselves.

The pale stone is not stone. Or rather, it is stone, but it is also something else. It is a concretion — a mineral deposit laid down over centuries by something living, something that exudes calcium carbonate or something similar, something that builds the walls the way coral builds a reef. The walls are alive, in a slow, geological sense. And they breathe. Not with air — with gas, with the same mineral atmosphere that the plants metabolize. The walls expand and contract, fractionally, on a cycle that is far slower than human breathing. Once every several minutes, perhaps. And each expansion produces a low, resonant pulse that underlies the entire chord.

This is the bass note. The foundation. The one that was here before the plants. Before the terraces. Before the people.

The people built their instrument *around* a sound that was already being made.

---

There is almost nothing alive here that you can see. No insects. No birds. No lizards, no rodents, no signs of any animal life at all. The plants seem to exist in a closed system — they metabolize the mineral gas, they produce oxygen or something analogous, the walls breathe and cycle the gas back. It is an ecosystem of two players: the stone and the green. The people are visitors. Maintainers. Gardeners who come to prune and tune and harvest whatever it is they harvest — you notice that some of the alcoves are empty, the plants recently removed, and you wonder if the plants themselves are the crop. If they are eaten. If they are medicine. If they are used to make something you have not seen.

The tools are simple. The city — this chamber, this single room — is the city. It is settlement-scale for the people who use it, because it is their world. Everything above is salt and void and dry mineral wind. Everything here is green and warm and deafening and alive. They do not need to build more. They have built exactly enough.

---

You stand in the light column. The chord vibrates through you. The moss beneath your feet is soft and warm and faintly pulsing. The walls breathe. The plants hold their notes with a steadiness that is almost devout.

And then — for a moment, perhaps ten seconds — the light shifts. A cloud, above, on the surface. The column dims. And the chord *changes*. The plants in the shadowed terraces quiet, their tones dropping by a semitone, and the ones still in light swell to compensate, and for those ten seconds, the chamber sings a different song. A minor key. A mourning sound. Something that the people who tend this place must know intimately, must anticipate, must perhaps come specifically to hear.

Then the cloud passes. The chord resolves. The roar returns, full and complete and overwhelming.

You understand, now, why the crack in the basalt is so narrow. Why the entrance is hidden. Why the people who come here leave no sign above.

This is not a place you stumble upon. This is a place that reveals itself. And standing in its light, deafened by its music, surrounded by green life in a world of salt and dust, you understand that you have been allowed to find it.

You are not sure you have been invited to stay.

---

You climb back through the fissure. The sound follows you, attenuating as you ascend, becoming a hum, then a whisper, then a memory of a whisper. You emerge into the salt-flat air. The wind is dry. The sky is empty. The world above is silent.

Behind you, in the rock, the chamber continues to sing. It does not need you. It has never needed you. It was singing before your species had a word for song, and it will be singing long after the last hand that tends it has gone to dust.

But someone is coming back. You can see the footprints in the salt — bare feet, small, deliberate — leading toward the fissure. They will descend. They will tend the plants. They will listen to the chord and know, by its texture, whether the day is good.

You step aside.
