The plateau is still being born.

You stand at an altitude that should require breath, but there is no air here to thin—only a warm, dense exhalation of mineral gases and static pressure that presses against the skin like a heated towel. The ground beneath your feet has no flatness to it, no settled plane. This is geology in media res: raw crust shouldering upward from the mantle in real-time, the earth constructing itself with the blind urgency of a thing unfurling from an egg. The stone is so new it hasn't yet learned to be hard. Your boots sink a centimeter into surfaces that are more凝固 than solid—cooling lavalike, but not lava. Something else. A mineral froth, a pumice of elements that have never before combined in this configuration.

The plateau stretches in every direction, and its scale is difficult to parse because nothing here has agreed on what size should be. Ridges thrust upward in the middle distance—no, they are thrusting upward now, even as you watch. You can see the growth happening: the land bulging, tension rippling across hectare-wide domes of raw feldspar and something darker, something with the sheen of cooled glass but the grain of wood. The formations rise at speeds that shouldn't be possible, meters of new elevation arriving in minutes, the surface crackling and groaning as it stretches to accommodate its own arrival. And then, just as quickly, collapse. A spire that wasn't there an hour ago slumps sideways, its base unable to sustain the weight of its own newness, and the sound it makes is a low, warm thrum—not a crash, but a resonance, like a bell made of gravel being struck underwater.

There is no water. The thought becomes a fact you return to, again and again, because the warmth insists otherwise. The temperature is tropical, greenhouse, the kind of heat that on any other world would mean humidity, transpiration, the thick wet breath of growing things. Here, the warmth comes from below—from the furious energetic state of a landscape that hasn't yet decided what it wants to be. You touch a wall of fresh basalt and feel it pulse, not with lava beneath, but with something more fundamental: the thermodynamic anxiety of matter still arranging itself. The stone is fevered. The entire plateau runs hot with the metabolism of becoming.

Darkness here is not an absence but a texture. The sky—if it is a sky—hangs low and lightless, a ceiling of deep rust and charcoal that emits no illumination but somehow contains depth. You can see, but only just. The darkness is warm, like the interior of a closed hand, and it renders everything in tones of umber and dim amber. There is no light source, yet the land refuses to be invisible. It glows faintly at the edges of itself, the newest formations radiating a dull maroon heat-luminescence that fades as they cool. The world is a darkroom where the photograph is still developing.

Nothing lives here. No vegetation softens the razor-raw edges of newborn cliff faces. No animal calls echo across the widening valleys. And yet the plateau is not empty—cannot be empty, because emptiness requires stillness, and stillness requires the completion of something. Here, there is only process. The closest thing to life is the way certain surfaces seem to respond to proximity: walls of vitreous mineral that dim their glow when you approach, then brighten when you withdraw. Not sentience. Not even reaction, exactly. More like... acknowledgment. The land knows something moves across it, and the knowing registers as a shift in luminosity.

You find the structures on the third ridge—or rather, the third ridge finds you, shouldering upward from the plane and revealing them as it rises. They are not buildings in any way that implies architect or intent. They are hollows. Voids left in the rapid geological upthrust, spaces where the matter parted around some obstruction that has since dissolved or been absorbed. But they have the shape of shelter: archways that could be doorways, chambers that could be rooms. Their walls are smooth in the way that things are smooth when they haven't existed long enough to accumulate imperfection. The interiors are warm, warmer than the outside, and faintly resonant—you speak into them and your voice doesn't echo so much as *linger*, held by the stone like water held in a bowl, slowly seeping into the mineral matrix until the sound becomes part of the room's heat.

Someone has been here before. Or something. In one hollow-chamber, a single mark on the wall: a groove, deliberate, made by something harder than the substrate. It could be a tally, a direction, a symbol, or simply the evidence of a hand adjusting its balance against a surface that was still rising. The mark is old enough to have been stretched by the stone's ongoing expansion—the once-crisp line now blurred and elongated, pulled like taffy by the landscape's continued becoming. It is the only artifact you find, and it tells you nothing except that absence preceded your arrival.

The sound of the plateau is the sound of construction. Not the rhythm of building—no hammerfall, no engine-thrum—but the continuous low-frequency murmur of matter assembling itself at scale. It is the sound of mountains being born, compressed into audible range: a hum that varies in pitch as different strata cool at different rates, a crackle as thermal stress fractures spread through formations that are still growing, a deep intestinal gurgle as entire sections of the landscape subside and reform. There are no silences here, only variations in the volume of creation. Sometimes the noise drops to nearly nothing, and in those near-quiet moments you can hear the mineral processes at the micro-level—the tiny pops and clicks of crystal lattices forming, the hiss of gas escaping from stone that hasn't yet decided whether to be porous.

The dynamic is exhausting to witness. You cannot look in any direction without seeing change. Ridges that were knee-high when you arrived are now tower-high, and some have already collapsed and been subsumed into newer ridges rising beside their remains. Valleys between formations deepen and widen as the land stretches, then suddenly fill as adjacent growths lean into each other and fuse. Nothing here is older than a day. Nothing will survive unchanged for another. The plateau is a churn, a continuous demonstration that stability is a privilege of age, and age is something this place has not yet earned.

You walk for hours, or what feel like hours, across terrain that makes the concept of distance meaningless. The landscape you crossed this morning no longer exists—it has been subsumed, overwritten, transformed into the foundation for something that is itself already transforming. Navigation here requires not memory but presence: you must read the land as it is, moment to moment, and accept that the route you took to get here cannot be the route you take to leave. There is a freedom in this, and a vertigo. The ground is always new beneath your feet. You are always the first person to walk here. You are always the last.

At the edge of your exploration, where the plateau begins to slope downward into a region of even more violent formation—you can see the land there still liquid, still molten, not yet pretending to be solid—you find something that might be ecology. Might be the beginning of one. In a crevice between two forming-cliffs, where the stone is youngest and warmest and softest, there is a film. A coating. A layer of something that is not stone, not mineral, not any category you possess. It is faintly iridescent in the dark, faintly moist despite the absence of water, and when you breathe near it, it ripples. Not away from your breath. Toward it.

The film is absorbing something from the warm gas you exhale. Processing it. Becoming marginally thicker.

You watch it for a long time. The plateau continues to construct itself around you—ridges rising and falling, valleys forming and filling, the endless turbulent birth-noise of a world that hasn't yet decided what it will be. And here, in this crack, in this warmth, in this darkness where nothing should live, something is learning to persist.

You leave it there. You do not touch it. Some things that are coming-into-being should be allowed to continue without the interference of a thing that has already finished becoming.

The walk back is through a country that doesn't exist yet.
