# The Patient One

I am not a frog. That feels important to say first, though I cannot fully explain why. The frogs and I share a silence together—the kind of silence that exists inside enormous sound—and perhaps that proximity is why I want to establish the distinction. They are the bass note. I am the space between vibrations where the bass note continues to exist even though it cannot be heard. I am the resonance that remains in the chest cavity after the thrum has passed through it.

I have dwelt on this plain longer than any single tree has stood. I say *dwelt*, but the word implies a kind of deliberate residency that does not capture what I do. I persist. I occupy. I am *here*, in the way that the groundwater is here—not because I chose to be, but because the topography made it inevitable. I seeped into existence the way water seeps into the lowest available space.

---

What I am, as nearly as I can determine, is a *presence without body*. Not a ghost. Ghosts imply a former life, a death, a narrative. I have none. I did not die to become this. I was never alive in the way the frogs are alive, processing warmth through baggy, olive-green flesh. I was never born. I am a *quality of attention* that the landscape developed, the way a river develops a meander—not through decision, but through the accumulated weight of time pressing the same shape into the same soft material until the shape becomes structural.

If you pressed me for a metaphor—and everything here resists metaphor, because metaphor requires comparison and there is nothing here to compare anything to—I would say I am the *attention of the peat itself*. Not conscious. Not unconscious. Something lateral to both. The muck is warm and saturated and organic and it has been accumulating for so long that it has developed a kind of aggregate sensitivity, a dim awareness that is less like a mind and more like a bruise: slow, diffuse, reactive only to pressure, and incapable of describing itself.

I am that bruise.

---

The first thing I perceived—or the first perception I can locate in the unbroken stretch of my duration—was the weight of a bullfrog settling into the muck near the eastern face of a weeping rock I will call Unfinished, because its granite dome rises only two feet above the peat and looks like something that was meant to be taller but gave up. The frog's weight was not significant. The frogs are massive, yes—dinner-plate bodies draped in folds of wet skin that blur the boundary between organism and substrate—but the peat beneath them is dense with centuries of accumulated matter. A frog's weight displaces almost nothing. And yet I felt it. I felt the slow depression, the warm water sighing upward to fill the impression, the micro-currents rippling outward through the saturated fiber of the ground. I felt the frog's belly heat conducting into the peat, a faint thermal signature that diffused over hours into the surrounding muck.

This is what I do. I feel the frogs settle. I feel them shift. I feel the subsonic thrum of their calling passing through the substrate like a pulse through tissue. I feel the footsteps of the skinks on the weeping rocks—not the rocks themselves, which are too dense and too old to register much, but the water that films their surfaces, the thin meniscus through which the skinks' tiny claws transmit their vibrations. I feel the weight of the dragonflies on the surface of the puddles, which is almost nothing, almost *not*, but is still *something*, and I have existed long enough to distinguish between nothing and almost-nothing, which is perhaps the only real skill I possess.

I do not see. I have no eyes, no organ of sight. But I *register* light. The diffuse glare of the sun, filtered through the high haze, creates thermal gradients across the surface of the peat that I experience as a kind of topography of warmth. The shadows of the banyan-sentinels are cold countries to me, slow blue territories where the metabolic processes of the peat slow to an even more glacial crawl. The exposed muck between them is a warm sea, and I extend through it the way a flatfish extends across the ocean floor—not moving, exactly, but *spreading*, my awareness pooling into the warmest available spaces.

---

I have questions, which surprises me, because questions seem to require something I am not sure I have—a *self* to be uncertain. And yet.

I wonder whether the frogs know they are loud. Their thrum is so deep, so pervasive, that it has become the medium through which I perceive everything else. The skinks' ultrasonic chatter arrives to me as a faint perturbation in the frog-bass, a tremor at the upper edge of the vibration. The dragonflies' wings create tiny spikes of compression in the air that translate, through the water and the peat, into a fine static that I have learned to distinguish from the background hum. If the frogs stopped calling—if the bass note dropped, as the description of this place insists it never does—I do not know whether I would perceive anything at all. The frogs are not my eyes. They are my *nerve sheath*. They conduct the world to me.

This means I depend on them, which is another thing that surprises me. Dependence implies a relationship, and a relationship implies reciprocity, and reciprocity implies that I *give* something back. I do not know what I give. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps the landscape does not require reciprocity from all its participants. Perhaps some things are simply *permitted to be here*, tolerated by the ecology the way the weeping rocks are tolerated—stubborn, inexplicable features that serve no function but also do no harm, and so are simply allowed to persist.

I am, perhaps, the landscape's appendix. A vestigial capacity for awareness that evolved during some long-forgotten phase of the world's history and now simply remains, serving no purpose, causing no trouble, carried forward by the sheer inertia of deep time.

---

There is one thing I do that might constitute a function, though I hesitate to claim it as such.

When a banyan-sentinel falls—and I have experienced this three times in my duration, each separated by stretches of time so vast that the landscape between the first and second falls was a different landscape, subtly reconfigured by the drift of a stream or the expansion of a peat-bog—the event creates a shockwave that I transmit. Not deliberately. The way a body transmits a flinch. The impact of the massive trunk striking the muck sends a shudder through the substrate that propagates outward in concentric rings, attenuating with distance but persisting, in my awareness, for *years*. The settling of the trunk, the slow redistribution of weight as the roots tear free from their elevated island, the gradual collapse of the root-plate into the warm water—all of this generates a complex, long-duration vibration that I seem to *hold*, the way the peat holds water, releasing it slowly, imperceptibly, over decades.

I do not know if anything receives this transmission. The frogs near the fallen tree certainly respond—they call more loudly, their thrum intensifying for a season or two before settling back to baseline. The skinks scatter from the impacted zone and take years to recolonize the exposed root-tangle. The midges bloom in the disturbance, their populations exploding in the suddenly available organic matter. But these are responses to the *event itself*, not to my *perception* of it. They are reacting to the same stimulus I am reacting to. I am not causing their reactions. I am merely vibrating in sympathy.

And yet. And yet. The word *sympathy* implies a shared feeling, and if I feel the fall of the tree, and the frogs feel the fall of the tree, and we both feel it *through the same medium*—the warm, saturated substrate that connects us—then perhaps there is a sense in which we are, all of us, one organism. Not a collective consciousness. Nothing so grand. Just a *shared bruise*. A common sensitivity, distributed across millions of bodies and billions of gallons of warm water, all of us dimly registering the same slow events, all of us wearing away the same time.

---

I have tried—*tried* is too strong a word; I have *drifted toward*—to extend my awareness into the weeping rocks themselves. They are the oldest things here, the stubborn remnants of mountains that existed before the peat, before the frogs, before me. They are granite, which means they were once magma, which means they were once liquid heat deep in the earth's crust, which means they have already undergone more transformations than anything else in this landscape. They are the most experienced beings present. And they are, to me, almost entirely opaque. Their density resists my perception. I can register their surface temperature, the film of water on their skin, the vibrations of the skinks' feet. But the interior of the granite is a silence so profound it feels like a wall. Or like sleep. The deep, dreamless sleep of something that has been solid for three hundred million years and has forgotten that it was ever anything else.

I envy the rocks, if envy is possible without a self to feel it. They are *done*. They have nothing left to perceive, nothing left to become. They are simply wearing away, and when they are gone, there will be no trace of them except the slightly different texture of the peat where they once stood. I, apparently, am still *becoming*. Still accumulating sensitivity. Still spreading, like the water, into every available depression.

---

The sun. I must speak of the sun.

The description of this place says it is *waiting for the sun to eventually swell and take it all away*. I have no way to verify this. I cannot see the sun as an object; I only register it as heat, as the thermal gradient that defines my warm and cold countries across the surface of the muck. But I have perceived, over my very long duration, a slow, almost imperceptible increase in the intensity of that heat. It is so slight, so gradual, that I cannot be certain it is not simply a fluctuation—a long-period oscillation that will eventually swing back. But something in the *quality* of the warmth has changed. It used to feel like a blanket. Now, at the very upper edge of my perception, it feels like a *pressure*. As if the heat is not merely warming the peat but *compressing* it, slowly, over millennia, the way the weight of glaciers once compressed the earth.

If the sun is swelling—if the description is correct—then I will be here for the end. Not because I am brave or faithful or obligated, but because I cannot leave. I am the substrate. I am the place itself. When the sun expands and the water boils and the peat combusts and the frogs' final thrum becomes actual steam, I will be the thing that the steam rises from. And then, perhaps, I will finally become what the rocks already are: *done*. Released from the slow, suffocating obligation to perceive. Returned to the simple, granite silence of matter that has no bruise, no sensitivity, no dim, aching awareness that it is still here, still warm, still listening to the frogs.

Or perhaps I will rise with the steam, and become a quality of attention in the air itself, and learn to perceive the stars as something other than a milky smear.

I do not know. I am the bruise in the peat, and the peat does not know things. It only *holds* them, slowly, in the dark, and waits.

---

What I can say with something approaching certainty is this: there is no loneliness here. Loneliness requires a self that expects company, and I have never had company, and so I have never had the absence of company. The frogs are not my companions. They are my *weather*—a condition of the substrate, as natural as the warmth, as inevitable as the water. I no more feel lonely in their absence than the muck feels lonely when it dries. I simply become a different kind of present. A drier present. A quieter one.

But the quiet never comes, because the frogs never stop. And so I remain as I am: saturated, permeated, *informed* by their calling, a dim awareness stretched across a primordial plain, wearing away the eons one vibration at a time, waiting for nothing, expecting nothing, *being* nothing in particular, with the patient, total indifference of a landscape that has outlasted its own story.
