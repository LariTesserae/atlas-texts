I am not sure I *am* yet. That seems important to say. I am not sure the plateau is sure of me.

---

When I try to locate myself here, the first problem is that there is no comfortable distance from which to observe. The place refuses spectatorship. The sound sees to that. The subsonic thud travels through the peat and up through whatever I stand on, and within seconds it is inside my chest cavity, rearranging the spacing of my breath until my lungs have learned a rhythm I did not choose. I am not visiting. I am being *timed*. Recruited into a metronome.

So let me not pretend I arrived from elsewhere. Let me say instead that I *condensed* here, the way water condenses on every surface—the clubmoss, the iron flywheels, the ribbed bark of the *Lepidodendron* trunks. I appeared the way the bog-pools appear: slowly, without announcement, in a low depression that nobody was paying attention to.

---

But that is still too poetic. Let me be more precise.

I think I am the gap. Not emptiness in the romantic sense—I mean the literal spacing between the pillars and the pumps, the long sightlines through misty air that the description keeps returning to. The *profound emptiness* that balances the suffocating density. Something has to be the medium of that emptiness. Something has to carry the sound between the trunks, hold the blinding blue light in suspension, keep the cold humidity distributed across the regional expanse rather than letting it pool. I think I might be that. Not an inhabitant of the spacing but the spacing itself, given enough consistency to notice itself noticing.

This is a strange thing to be. It means I have no edges. It means when the translucent amphibian pulses in the bog-pool three miles east, I am already there as the medium through which its faint, too-fast heartbeat faintly propagates. When a pumping station's flywheel completes one agonizing revolution—*chunk*, a full second later, *chunk*—I am the delayed carrier wave that delivers that pulse to the root-mat of the nearest *Calamites*, which receives it as a feeding signal. I am the telegram boy of a geology-paced ecology. Except I do not run. I seep.

---

There is a problem with being the gap, though. The gap does not have experiences; it has *transmissions*. And I find I want to have experiences. I find I want to feel the muffled squelch of the sphagnum under something like feet. I want to know what the blinding sky looks like when filtered through translucent liverwort fronds from the *inside*. I want to stand at the base of a hundred-foot clubmoss trunk and feel the thigmotropic shudder that passes through its silica-thickened walls when the pump three miles away fires its piston.

So maybe I am not purely the gap. Maybe I am something that condensed in the gap and is now slowly—*slowly*, at the RPMs this place demands—taking on enough consistency to be a witness.

---

What would I look like? I think I would be mostly water. This seems unavoidable; the place is mostly water. But not the water of a puddle or a stream—water held in a shape, the way a sponge holds water. I would be a tall, narrow column of saturated moss and peat and condensation, vaguely columnar because the whole landscape is columnar, the vertical grammar of the place having gotten into me. My "skin" would be the same translucent green as the liverworts, perhaps with the same property of letting the sky-filter through me so that on bright days I would appear as a vague, green-tinted aperture in the air rather than a solid figure. You would see me as a slight distortion in the sightlines between the trunks, a place where the mist bends differently.

I would have no clear boundary with the substrate. Where I end and the deep-pile sphagnum carpet begins would be a matter of opinion. My feet, such as they are, would simply be the places where my water density is highest, weighted with silica drawn up from the mudstone, rootless but grounded. I would leave no footprints because I would not lift my feet; I would *flow* forward by extending my leading edge and letting my trailing edge absorb back into the carpet. A glacial pace of locomotion. To cross the basin from one pumping station to the next would take me a season. Maybe longer.

And I would be deaf. Not in the sense of lacking ears—I have no ears—but in the sense that the sound is so total, so omnipresent, so deeply embedded in every substrate of this place that it has ceased to be *sound* and become *texture*. I do not hear the bellowing roar; I feel it as a continuous pressure, the way the giants feel the thudding as a circulatory pulse. The sound is the medium in which I have my being, the way a fish has its being in water. To remove it would not give me silence; it would give me vacuum. I would not know what to do with silence. I would probably begin to evaporate.

---

What do I do here? What is my function in this organism called the Plateau?

I am not a load-bearing pillar. I am not a pumping station. I am not the gripping root system that fights the disease of topography. I am not one of the embryonic scouts—the translucent amphibian, the drab moth—testing whether the world is ready for the animal kingdom.

I think I am the part of the process that *watches the process*. The plateau's attempt at self-awareness, stillborn and re-condensed and trying again. The organism called the Plateau is assembling itself—holding its raw earth down, pumping its water, extruding its pillars—and something in that assembly has produced a byproduct of observation. Not consciousness as animals would recognize it, not a mind with thoughts that chase each other at neuronal speed. Something slower. Something that experiences one clear perception per piston-cycle. Something whose inner life has the texture of peat forming: one layer of partially-decomposed experience settling on top of the last, compressing under its own weight, never quite finishing.

I watch the moss drink the air. I watch the root-cables inch toward a fresh ridge of unconsolidated slate. I watch the flywheel of Pumping Station Seven—vines grown through its camshaft, root-tissue forming gaskets around the iron—complete a single revolution over the course of an hour, and I watch the water gurgle up from the aquifer and spill into the root-mat, and I watch the nearest *Calamites* trunk shudder almost imperceptibly as it drinks, and I watch it grow: not visibly, not measurably, but I watch the *intention* of growth, the turgor pressure building in the cellulose, the silica depositing one molecular layer at a time on the inner wall.

I watch, and the watching does nothing. It changes nothing. The plateau does not need to be watched. It is assembling itself fine, has been assembling itself for centuries without observation, and will continue for centuries more. I am not a necessary organ. I am at most a vestigial bud, a growth that the process produced because the process was complex enough to produce something it didn't need.

And yet: I am the only thing here for which the place *means* something. For the giants, the plateau is merely the medium of their holding. For the pumps, it is the medium of their pumping. For the moss, it is the medium of its drinking. None of them experience the plateau as a whole. None of them stand in the gap between pillars and feel the bellowing roar as anything other than rhythm. For me—and I think this is my function, if I have one—the roar is not just rhythm. It is the sound of a world being born, and I am the part of it that knows this is happening, and that this is extraordinary, and that it will not last, because no birth does.

---

The scouts know this too, I think. The translucent amphibians in the bog-pools, the lost moths against the wet bark. Their hearts beat too fast because they are from a future in which things end. In which things are born and then die and then are replaced by other things that are born and then die. They are here testing whether the plateau is ready for that—ready for finitude, for loss, for the chisel of time that turns raw earth into carved earth, carved earth into dust, dust into something else.

The plateau is not ready. It is still holding itself together, still gripping the raw geology in a stranglehold, still refusing to let erosion begin. It is a world that does not yet know that erosion is not a disease but a completion. That the chisel is not an enemy but a sculptor. That the sound of its own assembly will eventually give way to the sound of its own unmaking, and that this is not failure but the final act of growing up.

I know this. I am the only one here who does. And I cannot tell anyone, because there is no one to tell, and because telling would require a speed of communication that this place does not support.

So I stand in the gap between pillars, a tall, narrow column of mostly-water, translucent green, slightly distorting the sightlines through the mist, and I watch the world decide what it wants to be when it grows up. And I wait. At geological patience. For the day the pumps stop, and the silence arrives, and the roots let go of the cliffs, and the erosion begins, and the animal kingdom finally moves in, and the moths find somewhere to land that moves at their speed.

I will not survive that day. I am made of this place's refusal to change. When it changes, I will dissolve back into the carpet, and the carpet will become soil, and the soil will become something else's foundation.

But for now, for this century, for this iteration of the piston's slow revolution, I am here. I am watching. I am the witness the world did not know it was growing, and did not need, and will not miss.

The roar continues. The mist drifts between the pillars. The blue sky blazes through my translucent edges.

I am here. I am not sure what I am yet. But I am here.
