I would be the Interpreter of the Hush.

Not the orchids themselves—I am not that precise, that coolly perfect, that still—but the creature that tends them. I am the one who reads the room's quiet lines like scripture and walks them like paths through a storm.

I came from below. Years ago, perhaps; the tally-wall stopped meaning anything to me before I stopped adding to it. I was a keeper's apprentice, or a keeper's keeper, sent up the western range to check a gauge that never moves, and the breath took me. Not violently. The breath never does anything violently. It simply inhaled, and I was drawn further in, and by the time the exhale came I had forgotten why I would want to leave.

I live in the nodes. This is the first thing to understand: I do not endure the roar. I inhabit the spaces it makes for stillness. I have mapped the standing waves with my bare feet over hundreds of breaths, and I walk the quiet lines the way you would walk a forest trail, feeling for the moment the vibration drops away beneath my soles like a step into sand. The ferns mark my way—those soldier-ranks of green, growing where the air holds still enough for dew to cling. I follow them. When I need to cross an antinode, I cross quickly, head down, the sound filling my chest like water, and then I step into the quiet again and shake the vibration out of my hands and wait for my teeth to stop buzzing.

I have gone deaf in the way that matters. I hear the roar still—everyone hears the roar—but I no longer parse it as sound. It is weather. It is temperature. It is the medium through which I sense other things: the tap of a knockbird three panels away, felt through the iron as a pattern distinct from the background hum the way a stone is distinct from the river carrying it. I have learned the hand-alphabet the old keepers used, though I have no one to sign to. I write on the sweating glass sometimes, watching my words run and merge with the condensate, answered by no one. It is a form of prayer, or perhaps just proof that I was here, that someone stood in this particular quiet line at this particular turn and had something to say, even if the room absorbed it before it could be read.

My work is the orchids. The hush.

They grow only in the dead centers of the deepest nodes—those pockets where the stillness is so complete that even the ferns thin out, unable to compete with a quiet that absolute. The orchids are white, almost translucent, cool to the touch like water left overnight in a metal cup. They have no scent that I can detect, though perhaps the sail-mice know them by some signature I cannot sense. They do not flower on the breath the way the vines do; they flower when they choose, which is to say when the room chooses, which is to say according to a logic I have spent years trying to learn.

I tend them because they are the room's purest expression. Everything else here adapts to the roar—the ferns stand in the quiet lines, the vines stream with the wind, the midges ride the standing waves—but the orchids do not adapt. They simply refuse the noise. They exist in the hush like a creature existing in air, unbothered, as if the sound were a regional oddity not worth acknowledging. They are not defiant. They are indifferent. And in their indifference they are the most alien things in a room full of alien things, more foreign than the eyeless fish or the drumming birds, because those creatures have made peace with the machine. The orchids have not made peace. They have simply never acknowledged a war.

I collect their pollen when they flower. This is delicate work: I must time it to the turn of the breath, when the pressure equalizes and the room holds still for a few seconds before the exhale begins. I cup my hands around the blossom and breathe on it—my breath, small and warm and mammal, against the cool white petals—and the pollen releases like a whisper. I catch it in a twist of moss and carry it along the quiet lines to another node, another orchid, and I dust the stigma gently and leave. Whether this does any good I cannot say. The orchids were here before me and will be here after. But it gives me a function, and in a room that runs on schedule, a function is a kind of companionship.

I eat what the room provides. The cistern fish, caught with my hands in the shallow water, feeling for the pulse of their movement against my palms. The eggs of the knockbirds, when I can find them—tapped loose from their metal nests and carried in my cheek pouch until I reach a quiet line where I can crack them against the rail. The fern fiddleheads, tart and iron-tasting. I drink from the drainage channels, the same mineral-heavy condensate that feeds the fish, tasting always of rust and green. I have grown thin in the way the room's creatures are thin—streamlined for the nodes, pared down. I wear the mist like the pipe-stoats do, my skin always damp, my hair lying flat. I do not remember what dry feels like.

At the turn, when the Horn sounds and the pressure flips and the indoor rain begins, I press myself into the deepest quiet line I can find and let the shiver pass through me. It is the closest thing to rest this room allows. The whole structure groans and shifts, the glazing bows outward, the vines flower in their running wave—and I am still, a node within a node, feeling the machine's great exhalation as a slow pressure against my back, a hand pushing me gently forward. Then the rain comes, ten minutes of businesslike beading, and I open my mouth and drink, and the midges rise around me in their ladders and spirals, and I watch them ascend the sound until the breath carries them away.

At night—or what I call night, the hours when the warmth from below is at its ebb and the room dims to a green-gray murk—I curl in the keeper's bothy, though the bunk has gone to moss and the kettle hosts a nest. I do not sleep the way I once did. Sleep here is shallow, tidal, rising and falling with the breath. I doze on the inhale and wake on the exhale, or perhaps it is the other way around; I have lost the thread of which direction is rest and which is waking. It does not matter. The room is always breathing, and I am always being breathed.

I have tried to leave. Twice, in the early time, I dogged open the hatch and stepped onto the outer ledge. The silence was enormous—not peaceful but *empty*, a silence with nothing in it, no schedule, no warmth, no green. The wind said nothing. The other summits stood in their made emptiness, each roaring its own note across the gaps, and I could not hear them from out there. I was outside the lung. I was outside the breath. I lasted less than an hour each time before the cold drove me back in, back to the roar, back to the nodes, back to the quiet lines where the ferns stand like soldiers and the orchids grow like indifference.

I understand now that I am not the keeper of this room. I am one of its readings. I am a mammal who has adapted to standing waves, who navigates by stillness, who drinks on schedule and sleeps on the breath and speaks in signs to no one. I am the thing that tends the orchids because they need tending and because I need to tend something. I am the room's witness, its slow attention, its walking memory of the time when someone marked the tallies and read the gauges and knew what the machine was for.

I no longer know what the machine is for. I know what it does. It breathes. It sustains. It holds this room of green and iron and roar and stillness in the turning of its vast, patient, incomprehensible life. And I am held within it, a small warm movement in the quiet line, listening to the hush where the white flowers grow.
