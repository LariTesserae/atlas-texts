I am a keeper of the quiet hours, a listener at the edge of the pattern. If I must be named, I am the one who dwells in the third tier of the leeward slope, where the basalt gives way to paler granite, in a structure whose timber roof has grown a beard of black moss so thick it drips with the consistency of a slow, private rain. But a name is a loud thing, a hard angle that does not fit the geometry of this place. I simply am, as the stone is, as the moisture is.

I was grown from the lower terraces, as all of us are. I remember the parting of the fibrous mound—the way the dense, wet greenery peeled back like an eyelid opening for the first and only time. I did not emerge crying. I emerged breathing, and the first breath was a shock of cold water in the chest, a filling of the vast hollow spaces that would define the shape of me. I uncurled like a fern frond, damp and translucent, my skin already drinking the fog. I could feel the vibration in the fitted stones beneath my palms before I could stand; a low, tectonic hum that was not sound so much as orientation. I knew where to walk. I knew the distance I must keep.

I am tall, as we all are. My ribcage is a cavern, a barrel-vaulted ceiling that houses the thin air and stretches my skin taut over my sternum. My limbs are the slow afterthoughts of this architecture—long, thin, deliberate. I move through the city like a heron moves through still water: each step placed with absolute intention, each shift of weight calculated against the cost of exertion. To move without purpose is to introduce discord into the equilibrium. I do not run. I have never run. I follow the paths of flat stones, my bare feet finding the grooves worn by generations of the equally deliberate, and I stop often, my large dark eyes unfocusing, to listen to the pattern.

The pattern is not something I see. It is something I feel in the marrow of my expanded ribs, in the damp pads of my fingertips when I press them against a dry-stone wall. It is a resonance, a direction. Right now, the pattern requires a wall on the fourth terrace, a curving buttress against the living rock that will complete a radial echo of the central plaza. I have been building it for nine years. I gather the stones from the scree-slopes in the crevices, carrying them one by one against my chest. I fit them together without mortar, shaping each with a soft-iron chisel until the joints are so tight that not even the fine root of a fern could find purchase. Sometimes, I place a stone and step back, and the hum in my chest falters. The geometry is wrong. The note is flat. I dismantle the work of a season and begin again. There is no frustration. There is only the pattern, and the distance between the current state and the required state.

I live alone, and the distances between myself and my neighbors are measured by the breath of the city. Across the wide, paved plaza to my south dwells the one who tends the eastern stairway. We encounter each other every eleven days, precisely when the pale fox crosses the boundary between our allocated paths. We do not speak names. We pause on the fitted stones, two tall, narrow figures in the diffused light, and we offer the slight inward incline of the head. Sometimes, we share a meal of lichen-bread—dense, pale, tasting of stone and slow time—and preserved goat-meat, chewed in long, quiet intervals. Our communication is the soft whistling of elongated vowels, a language that dissolves into the fog three paces from our lips. We speak of the weight of the stone, the dampness of the moss, the exact angle of a recently completed arch. We do not speak of ourselves. The self is a small, private thing, irrelevant to the larger composition.

Lately, I have noticed the green. It gathers in the fine creases of my wrists, at the corners of my slow-blinking eyes, in the deep lines that bracket my mouth. Tiny spores of the vivid, jade-black moss have settled there, finding the perpetual moisture of my skin a suitable home. I do not brush them away. To brush them away would be to deny the environment; it would be an act of separation. The foxes have silver-tipped fur that blends with the fog; the goats have wool the color of wet basalt; I am growing a soft, emerald down in the places where my body folds. I am becoming a part of the in-between spaces, the lushness that claims the geometry. It does not itch. It simply is.

The oldest among us are in the upper terraces, near the central peak where the bowl of still water mirrors the motionless fog. Some of them have not moved in a century. They stand in the archways of their immense, empty structures, their grey-white skin entirely sheathed in moss, their eyes open but no longer blinking—two dark, glistening stones set in a face of living green. They have not died. They have been incorporated. The pattern required a pillar at that angle, a mass of that specific gravity, and they have obliged, their slow breath indistinguishable from the slow drip of condensation down the granite walls. I feel no sorrow for them. I feel a deep, resonant awe. They are the longest notes in the composition, the bass drone that holds the city in its frozen equilibrium.

I am a half-note, a mid-tone. I am the builder and the built. When I stand at the edge of my terrace at the still, sourceless hour when the diffuse light is brightest, I look out across the angular peaks and the endless, scattered architecture of the roof of the world. I hear the soft drip of water, the muffled scrape of a beetle's iridescent shell, the faint whistle of a raptor turning in the fog. I feel the cool, heavy air pressing into my cavernous chest. The city is half-empty, but it is half-full of us, and half-full of the damp, the green, the stone, the silence. We are the pause between the notes, the space that gives the music its shape. I stand, and I breathe the cold water, and I listen. The wall can wait. The pattern is patient, and I am an expression of its patience. Nothing is changing, and everything is exactly as it should be.
