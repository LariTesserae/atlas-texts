I would be the cartographer of seepage.

Not a keeper of the machine—that role is already filled, held by the soft hands and rooted hem of the one who walks the platforms in their dissolving boots. They maintain the ritual of the industry; I document the digestion. My purpose is not to slow the chamber's consumption of the human, but to witness the exact moment each artifact crosses the threshold from *made* into *grown*.

I live in the higher reaches, where the aerial roots of the banyan descend from the leaf-ceiling like thick, knotted rigging. Down below, the keeper walks their rounds, checking pipes and wiping gauges in their devotion to the old logic. I watch from above, perched on platforms too narrow for standing, where the bamboo has not been polished by footfall but is instead furred with a fine downy moss that drinks the ambient steam. My territory is the lattice where the architecture has not yet fully closed, where the amber light from the filament bulbs is fractured by the gaps in the philodendron vault. 

I do not walk; I climb. My body has forsaken the upright posture of the builders. My arms have lengthened, my shoulders have rotated, and my fingers have grown long and double-jointed, gripping the root-columns with a pressure that relies on friction rather than muscle. My palms and the soles of my feet are covered not in calluses, but in a fine, hooked texture—like the tendrils of the vine-cables—that catches on bark, on moss, on the rough verdigris of the copper pipes. I move through the chamber like a slow spider, finding handholds where the banyan roots fuse, pulling myself into the crevices that the keeper on the floor has forgotten or can no longer reach.

Down there, the keeper tracks the health of the boilers. Up here, I track the conquest. 

I have a system of memory that requires no paper, no ink—both would dissolve in this saturated air within the hour. My memory is architectural. I have tied knots in the living vine-cables, using a technique I developed decades ago: a specific twist of the still-supple green tendril that, as it hardens into steel-like rigidity, locks the knot permanently into the lattice. A knot pulled tight means a pipe bracket is failing; a knot looped slack means a new root-tip has breached the bamboo walkway below. When the vine finally petrifies, the knot becomes a scar, a record pressed into the skeleton of the chamber itself. I have tied thousands of them. The upper canopy of the chamber is a braille history of its own digestion, readable only by the hands of the one who tied it.

I wear nothing that drags. The keeper’s coat is anchoring them, slowly tethering them to the floor-grain; they are becoming a tree. I will not be a tree. I am an epiphyte, an air-plant. I wear only a harness of woven bamboo fiber, tight against my skin, holding a small satchel at my hip. The satchel carries my tools: a sliver of shattered mirror, salvaged from the mechanical press before it calcified, which I use to angle the trembling amber light into dark crevices; a hollowed-out bird's-nest fern stem, which I use as a straw to drink the pure condensation that gathers on the undersides of the highest leaves, avoiding the mineral-heavy seep-water from the floor. 

I do not eat the fungal blooms from the deep moss. My diet is the aerial plankton of the chamber—the spores, the microscopic rotifers, the pollen of unfurling ferns that hang suspended in the humid air. I breathe through my mouth as much as my nose, my palate constantly tasting the green chlorophyll, the iron oxide, the wet cellulose. I filter the air; I am a living sieve. My body is lean, sinewy, and runs hot, a furnace of rapid metabolism that requires the constant cooling of the chamber's wet breath. When I sleep, I curl into the crotch of a major root-column, gripping the bark, sleeping in short, light bursts, waking when the generator's moan shifts pitch or the wind presses the ventilation shafts and the chamber exhales.

The keeper and I do not speak, but we know each other. We are the last two phenomena of the human era, split by our relationship to the encroaching architecture. They are the conservator; I am the undertaker. Sometimes, when they sit at the sorting table, holding the rusted shears in their palm, I descend silently to the lower walkways. I move in the shadows of the pipe-brackets, watching them. I watch the ferns unfurl from their shoulders. I watch the moisture bead on their forearms and drip, drip, drip in time with the leaves. 

I know what they do not: the moss is not just growing on them, it is growing *from* them. The substrate they have become is richer than the wall; the chamber is savoring them. The keeper believes they are the consciousness of the chamber, but they are wrong. They are its stomach. They are the slow, warm center where the last remnants of the human industry are being broken down and incorporated. 

When the keeper finally lies down in their alcove and does not rise—when the moss closes over their chest and the rubber soles of their boots finally split and send their first pale tendrils into the floor-grain—I will be the one to tie the final knots. I will document the exact millimeter where the banyan swallows the last gauge, the moment the bioluminescent fungi finally outshine the dying filament bulbs. I will climb higher, following the receding edge of the architecture, waiting for the gap in the leaf-ceiling to close over my head. 

I will be the last fauna. Not because I survive, but because I am the one mapping the end. When the chamber finally shrinks to its vanishing point—a fist of wood and moss and root, with no empty space left to hold a breath—I will be the knot at its very center, a tight twist of bone and memory, recording the silence.
