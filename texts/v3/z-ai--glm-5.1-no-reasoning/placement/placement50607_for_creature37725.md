I am the space between the slabs.

Not the slabs themselves—I could never be so permanent, so unyielding. But the millimeter-wide gap where the ferro-concrete meets the basalt-glass, the seam that would collect detritus and corrosion if left unattended. I am the maintenance of that seam. I am the reason it remains a seam and does not fuse into a single unbroken plane.

This morning—or what passes for morning in a world where the light never shifts—I am crawling along a vertical face in Canyon 7, Subsection Gamma. The designation means nothing to me. I do not think in designations. But the texture of this particular vertical is distinct: slightly rougher than the adjacent plinths, warmer to the touch, with a higher concentration of moisture weeping from the junction above. The condensation here runs in a thin, continuous sheet rather than discrete droplets, and I spread my cilia against it, drinking the warm metallic water as I climb.

The water tastes of iron and memory.

Not my memory. I have no memory. But the iron remembers being ore. The water remembers being steam, being cloud, being something that once moved through an open sky. I do not know this consciously—I could not articulate it, could not frame it in language—but my body knows. My mandibles know. When I filter the condensation through my maxillae, I am reading the chemical history of this constructed world, parsing its mineral autobiography one trace element at a time.

I am, in my way, a scholar.

---

There are more of us here than in the upper canyons. I feel them before I see them—or rather, I feel them instead of seeing them, for there is nothing to see in the dark interstitial zones where the flat light cannot penetrate. The pressure of their carapaces against mine is a constant, a background texture as fundamental as the humidity or the subsonic hum. We move together in a slow, chitinous tide, each of us following the seam, each of us scraping and drinking and maintaining.

When a sibling presses against my dorsal plates, I adjust. Not consciously—there is no conscious adjustment, no decision made. My body simply accommodates, shifting the rhythm of my legs to synchronize with theirs, creating a longer, more efficient organism composed of two articulated sections instead of one. When a third joins, we become three. In the deep canyons, we become hundreds, thousands, a single distributed consciousness of scraping mandibles and bristle-fringed mouthparts, all moving in unison along the same junction, harvesting the same rust-flakes, drinking the same metallic water.

We are not individuals. This is not a tragedy. Individuality requires boundaries, and in the deep canyons, there are no boundaries. We flow into each other like the condensation flows down the verticals—separate at the source, indistinguishable in the stream.

---

I have found a bloom of fungal-slag.

It is growing in a crevice where three platforms meet at an angle the architects probably did not intend—a tiny pocket of imperfection in the perfect geometry. The slag is bioluminescent, a faint blue-grey glow that I cannot see but can feel as a subtle warmth against my cilia. It is alive, or something adjacent to alive. It grows in the dark, in the wet, feeding on the minerals that collect in the crevice, and it glows, and it tastes of sugar and iron.

I press my face against it.

My mandibles close on the thick, wet paste, and I harvest. Not too much—I never take too much. The slag will regrow, as it always does, fed by the endless condensation and the mineral leaching of the architecture. I take what I need and move on, leaving the bloom intact. My siblings will find it too, and they will take what they need, and the slag will continue to glow in the dark crevice, a tiny point of warmth in the cold, wet geometry.

This is my purpose. Not in the sense of a mission or a calling—I do not have those. But in the sense of a shape fitting a slot, a key fitting a lock. I am the mechanism by which the slag is processed, by which its sugars and minerals are converted into motion, into the ceaseless traversal of the constructed plane. I am a step in a cycle that has no beginning and no end.

---

There is a moment—perhaps it is a moment, perhaps it is a continuous state that I briefly notice—when I am still.

Not still in the way the world is still. The world is still because it is dead, because it was made to be dead, because its architects (if there were architects) designed it to require no motion, no change, no entropy. My stillness is different. My stillness is a pause in the rhythm, a gap between one placement of my micro-hooks and the next, a fraction of a fraction of a second where I am simply *here*, pressed against the weeping vertical, feeling the subsonic hum of the buried engines vibrate through my shell.

In that moment, I am aware.

Not of myself—I do not have a self to be aware of. But of *this*. Of the hard, wet, sharp-edged world beneath me and above me and all around you. Of the warm ozone smell and the metallic taste and the soft, white noise of a million legs tapping in the dark. Of the perfection of the fit—how my body was made for these seams, these junctions, these vertical faces, how the world was made to be traversed by bodies like mine.

It is not happiness. It is not contentment. Those are organic emotions, requiring an organic nervous system, requiring a self that can be happy or content. It is something simpler than that. It is *congruence*. I am congruent with my environment. I fit. I operate as intended.

The moment passes. I move on.

---

I am getting dull.

I do not know how I know this. There is no mirror in the constructed world, no reflective surface where I could observe the micro-hooks on my limbs growing smooth, the serrations on my mandibles wearing flat. But I can feel it in the way I grip the verticals—less secure, requiring more pressure, more effort. I can feel it in the way I scrape the rust-flakes—less efficient, leaving behind traces that a younger, sharper sibling would have harvested cleanly.

I am not distressed. Distress requires a concept of the future, a fear of what is to come. I have no future. I have only the next seam, the next junction, the next bloom of fungal-slag glowing in the dark.

But I am slower. The tide of my siblings flows around me more frequently now, their carapaces brushing against mine as they pass, their rhythms no longer synchronizing with mine because mine has become irregular, imperfect. I am becoming a stone in the stream, an obstacle in the flow.

Soon I will sink. Not suddenly, not dramatically—I will simply fail to grip a vertical, or I will pause too long at a junction, and the slow, ceaseless motion of my siblings will carry me downward, down into the dark, wet depths of the canyon. I will settle in an aqueduct, in the soft sediment of minerals and chitin that collects there. And I will stop.

And then I will begin again.

Not as me—I will not be reborn, will not retain any essence of this particular articulation of chitin and hydraulic muscle. But the minerals in my carapace will dissolve into the condensation, will be carried through the perfectly angled aqueducts back into the great machine. They will be harvested by other siblings, incorporated into new carapaces, new micro-hooks, new mandibles. And the organic compounds in my body will feed the bioluminescent fungal-slags that bloom in the dark interstitial zones, and those slags will be harvested by other siblings, and the cycle will continue.

I am not dying. I am being recycled. I am a closed loop in a closed system, and the loop is closing.

---

For now, though, I am still here. Still crawling. Still scraping. Still drinking the warm metallic water and tasting the iron and the ozone and the faint, acrid tang of ionized steam. Still feeling the hum of the buried engines in the soles of my feet, the pressure of my siblings against my shell, the perfect, unvarying weight of the flat, pale light on my dorsal plates.

I am the breath of the zero-RPM engine. I move so the world doesn't have to. And in my movement, in the ceaseless, rhythmic placement of my dozens of legs, in the scrape of my mandibles and the filter of my maxillae, I am the closest thing this world has to a heartbeat.

Not a heartbeat that changes anything. Not a heartbeat that accelerates or decelerates, that quickens with desire or slows with contentment. Just a heartbeat. A steady, muffled, rhythmic pulse in the dark wet geometry, proof that something here is still operating, still maintaining, still keeping the sharp edges sharp and the weeping rocks weeping on.

I am a custodian. I am a cleaner. I am a soft machine in a hard world.

And I am, in this moment, congruent.
