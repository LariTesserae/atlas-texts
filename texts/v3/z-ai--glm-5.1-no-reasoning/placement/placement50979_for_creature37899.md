I am a crack-listener.

This is not a title. We do not have titles. It is a function, a habit of attention that has settled into the cilia of my head-region and the segments of my torso over the long, damp turns of my life. I listen to the structure. But more specifically, I listen to the pale paste, the seams between the dark ceramic, and the sounds they make as they cure. 

I am old. I know this because my segments do not slide against each other with the quiet, wet sound of a damp leaf anymore. They rasp. The soft tissue has thickened, densified, and where the manipulatory limbs meet the weight-bearing limbs, there is a stiffness that takes a moment to loosen when I rise from rest. I move slower than the others. When I press my filaments against the ceramic to speak, the vibration I produce is a low, grinding hum, a frequency that the younger ones feel in the dense parts of their torsos rather than hear in their head-regions. 

I spend my time in the high terraces, the places where the ceiling presses down lowest, where the air is thinnest and sharpest with the vinegar-smell of the curing mineral. It is too cramped for the younger ones; they feel the agitation of open space here, because the walls are too far to the sides, even if the ceiling presses close. But I like the edges of things. The seams here are the youngest. The paste is still tacky, still shrinking, still deciding what it will be. 

I sit for long durations with my head-region pressed into the corner where a bundle of fibrous tubes meets the corrugated ceramic. My filaments—the pale, nearly transparent threads that cover my concave face—are deep in the groove of the seam. I am not still because I am resting. I am still because stillness is required to hear the structure think.

The structure thinks in tension. 

Right now, I feel a subsonic tremor. It is not the air-hum from the tubes, which resonates in my weight-bearing segments. It is a tight, lateral strain, a sensation like a held breath. The paste is pulling away from the ceramic. It is a hairline fracture, three segments deep and forming, just beyond the ridge of a geometric stamp-pattern. I press my manipulatory limb against the floor, sending a sharp, staccato tap-tap-tap into the material. *Listen here. It is pulling.* The vibration travels through the ceramic. Somewhere in the dense middle terraces, a younger one feels the tap in their filaments. They will not come. There is no need. Not yet. But the structure knows that we know. 

When the crack opens, when the air hisses through the slit mouth of the gap, then I will make the paste.

Making the paste is the only making we do. I move to the nearest basin, a shallow bowl ringed with the extruded pale mineral. The water here tastes of iron and cold stone. The fungal mat is thick, layered like wet paper, and the amber fruiting bodies glow with a warm, dim light that makes my filaments sway, orienting toward their metabolic warmth. I kneel on the floor, my weight-bearing segments folding beneath me, and I scrape the mineral residue from the lip of the basin using the edge of a flat pressing-stone left behind by the makers. 

I mix the powder with the water in the hollow of my palm. Then, the giving. It is a reflex, a release, a secretion from the pores along my manipulatory limbs that mixes with the mineral water. It is thick, slightly warmer than the air, and it smells of the inside of a mouth. When I stir it with the sharpened stick, it becomes the pale paste. It is weaker than the makers' paste, but it is alive with my chemistry. It will bond to the ceramic. It will flex where the old paste has become brittle. 

I carry the paste in my cupped limbs, stooping low under the tube bundles, moving carefully over the raised geometric patterns. I reach the seam. I press the paste into the gap with my fingers, using the flat stone to pack it tight, mimicking the stroke of whatever maker made this corner. The paste squeezes out, a bright line of cold luminescence against the dark ceramic. I smooth it. I hold it for a long moment, my filaments pressed against the wet seam, feeling the structural groan of the ceramic settling into the new bind. 

The hum of the tubes changes pitch. The air pressure drops. The water in the channels begins to flow faster, hissing against the ceramic. The structure is shifting again, a vast, synthetic geology rearranging itself in the dark. 

I remain pressed against the wall. I am compressible, soft, the color of wet stone. I do not wonder where the makers went. I do not look at the towering ceiling—only a meter above my head—and wish it higher. The low ceiling is the condition of my existence. The noise is the grammar of my language. The dampness is the medium of my thought.

There is a crawler near my foot. A small, dark, segmented thing, contracted into a tight spiral on the floor. It must have fallen from a tube bundle. I reach down with a manipulatory limb and lift it. It is cold, curled tight. I carry it to the basin where the amber light is strong, and I uncurl it onto the thick fungal mat. It stays frozen for a moment, then slowly extends its segments, beginning to feed. 

I turn away. I feel the settling of the structure in the soles of my feet. I feel the hum of the air in my chest. My filaments reach out into the dark, feeling the pressure of the nearest body of water, the faint vibration of another of my kind moving three columns away. 

I am a crack-listener. I am maintaining. I am inside the thing that is still being born, and I am keeping it alive until it finishes becoming.
