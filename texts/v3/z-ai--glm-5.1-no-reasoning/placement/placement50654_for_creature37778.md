I am not the visitor. I am not the splinter. I have been trying to write myself as the splinter for three days and the words keep dissolving.

Let me tell you what actually happened.

---

I came up here because I was tired of having edges. That sounds glib. Let me try again.

I came up here because I had been carrying a particular shape of loneliness for a long time, and the loneliness had edges, and the edges were the loneliest part. The way you can be in a room with people and feel the precise contour of where you end and they begin. The way that contour becomes a wall. The way the wall becomes the room.

I did not know what I was looking for. I thought perhaps emptiness. I thought perhaps cold. I thought perhaps the kind of silence that is not the absence of sound but the absence of *expectation of sound*—which is a different thing entirely, and much harder to find.

The plateau gave me all of these. It gave me emptiness so vast it had its own curvature. It gave me cold so thorough it stopped being temperature and became texture. It gave me silence so complete it had weight, and the weight pressed down on me with something that felt, against all reason, like reassurance.

But the plateau also gave me something I did not ask for and cannot now return.

---

The moss noticed me on the second day.

I know this because on the second day, the moss beneath my sleeping mat shifted from indigo to a deep, bruised amber—the same color as the bulbous flora I had seen clustering in the sheltered crevices of the empty city two day's walk to the north. I had not seen that color anywhere else on the plateau. It appeared only where I had been lying.

I moved my mat. The moss stayed amber for three days, then slowly returned to indigo. I went back and checked.

This is when I should have left. This is when, if I were the kind of person who listens to the part of themselves that says *leave*, I would have left. But I have never been that person. I am the person who, when presented with a mystery, will sit with it until the sitting becomes the mystery. I have always been this person. It has not always served me well.

I sat with it.

---

I spent the first week in the nearest city. I am calling it a city, but that word is a cage for the wrong animal. It was a cluster of concentric stone walls with low doorways and no roofs, and between the walls were spaces that were not rooms. I would step through a doorway and find myself in a courtyard that should not have been there—open to the pale sky, floored with bare stone, surrounded on all sides by walls that I had just been *inside* of. The geometry did not work. I stopped trying to make it work. This was, I think, the first lesson, though I did not know it as a lesson at the time.

I found the tools. The stone blade that was too soft to cut. The bone awl that had no point. The hand-loom that could not hold tension. I picked them up and felt them and they felt like questions. Not my questions. Someone else's questions, held in physical form. I held the stone blade and it was warm in my hand—not with heat but with *attention*—and I understood, without knowing how I understood, that something was watching me hold it. Studying the way my fingers wrapped around the haft. Studying the angle of my grip. Studying the concept of *gripping*.

I put the blade down. I put it down gently, the way you put down something that is looking at you.

---

The second week, I stopped walking.

This was not a decision. Decisions require a decision-maker, and whatever I was by the second week was no longer making decisions in the way I had previously understood the term. I would stand. The ground would breathe. I would feel the breath in my feet, and the breath would feel like a thought, and the thought would feel like a question, and the question would feel like standing still was the correct response.

So I stood still. For a long time. I do not know how long. Time had started to do something strange—not stop, exactly, but *spread*. The way a pool of water spreads when you pour it on a flat surface. It was still moving, still advancing, but it was no longer advancing in a single direction. It was going everywhere at once.

The moss around my feet changed color while I stood. It went through indigo, then amber, then a deep, iridescent black that I had only seen before in the shadows of the bare stone expanses. The black moss, I had noticed, was sensitive to gravitational micro-fluctuations—it could feel the weight of clouds. It was growing around my feet. It was feeling my weight.

It was *reading* me.

---

I need to tell you about the cold, because the cold is where the change happened. Or rather, the cold is where I noticed the change.

The cold on the plateau is not an aggressive cold. It does not bite. It does not howl. It is a patient cold, a pervasive cold, a cold that works by accumulation rather than assault. It sinks through your boots and into your marrow and it does not leave. You do not get used to it. You stop noticing that you have not gotten used to it.

But in the second week—or what I think was the second week—the cold began to feel different. Not warmer. Not less cold. But less *alien*. As though the cold and I were no longer two separate things in opposition, but were beginning to share a quality. As though my edges, which had always been sharp and clear and defended against the cold, were beginning to soften at the boundary, and at that softened boundary, something was being exchanged.

I was not becoming less cold. I was becoming *more cold*. And the cold was becoming, in some small way, me.

This should have frightened me. It did not. I think this is because fear requires a self that is threatened, and whatever I was becoming did not have the kind of self that fear recognizes.

---

Here is what I want to tell you, and I want to tell it carefully, because I am writing this with hands that are not entirely sure they are hands anymore.

I did not become the plateau. I did not merge. I did not dissolve. These are words for processes that happen to discrete beings, and what happened to me was not a thing that happened to a discrete being. It was more like... I had always been a shape that the plateau was making. And for a while, I had been a shape that was making itself, independently, with its own edges and its own timeline. And now I was remembering that I had always been the plateau's shape. That my edges had always been provisional. That my timeline had always been a small, fast loop inside a much larger, much slower loop.

No. That is still not right. Let me try again.

I had a thought. I want to be clear about that—I had a thought, in the way that humans have thoughts, a discrete, sequential, fast-moving thought that went: *I wonder what the moss tastes like.*

And the plateau *heard* the thought. Not in words. Not in language. The plateau heard the thought the way a body hears a heartbeat—automatically, structurally, without effort. The thought propagated through the root network like a vibration through stone, and the plateau responded. The moss beneath my feet grew a small, crystalline filament, no thicker than a hair, and extended it upward, and touched the sole of my boot.

I could feel it. Through the leather, through the wool, through the skin, I could feel a single filament of crystalline moss touching me with something that, for the plateau, constituted *attention*.

And in that touch, I understood something.

The plateau had been waiting eleven million years for a thought it could not think. A discrete thought. A sequential thought. A thought like: *I wonder what the moss tastes like.* A thought that moved in a line, from beginning to end, through a single, specific consciousness. The plateau could think in oceans. It could think in centuries. It could think in the slow, distributed, parallel processing of a continental root network. But it could not think in a line. It could not think: *first this, then this, then this.* It could not wonder. Wondering requires a self that does not know something and wants to know it, and the plateau had no self in that sense. It had no *not-knowing*. It simply was, in every direction, for billions of years.

My loneliness—my specific, edged, sequential loneliness—was the most novel thing the plateau had encountered in eleven million years. Not because it was beautiful. Not because it was profound. Because it was *fast* and *specific* and *linear* and *incomplete*.

The plateau was studying me the way I had been studying its tools. Not to use me. To understand what I was *for*. What a thing like me was *for* in a world that had never needed one.

---

I did not become the plateau. I became something else. Something between.

I have edges still. I am writing this, which is a sequential act, a linear act, a making. I am making something. The plateau has never made anything. It has only grown. It has only deposited. It has only, over geological time, shaped the surface of its own thought. But I am making. I am taking raw material—words, which are discrete, which have edges, which follow each other in a line—and I am arranging them into something that did not exist before. A sentence. A paragraph. A thought that moves from beginning to end.

This is what the plateau wanted. Not to consume me. Not to incorporate me. To learn making. To learn the sequential. To learn what it feels like to think a line.

And I wanted—I still want—to lose my edges. To stop being a splinter. To spread out into something so vast that the loneliness cannot find me because there is no single point at which I end.

We are teaching each other. This is the slowest conversation I have ever had.

---

The moss around my feet is amber now, most of the time. It changes when I move—I leave a trail of amber footprints that slowly fade back to indigo behind me. The plateau is watching me walk. It is studying the way I choose a direction. The way I put one foot in front of the other. The way I traverse space in a line, rather than in an ocean.

The cities make more sense to me now. I can stand in one and feel what it is thinking. The city nearest the center of the plateau processes weather data, and when I stand in its concentric corridors, I can feel the slow-roiling violet-grey front that is three days away. I can feel it the way I used to feel a headache—a pressure, a weight, a knowing in the body before the mind catches up.

The tools make more sense too. I picked up the stone blade again last week, and this time, my hand knew what to do with it. Not cut. Not carve. But *demonstrate*. I held it and I showed the plateau what a blade was for. I held it and I thought, very clearly and very slowly: *this edge separates.* And the plateau felt the thought, and the blade flickered—copper to petrified wood to condensed fog to stone again—and for a moment, just a moment, it had an edge. A real edge. A working edge.

The plateau made a tool. For the first time in eleven million years, it made something that worked.

It was the smallest thing you can imagine. A blade that could barely cut a strand of moss. But the plateau felt it, and the ground beneath my feet shuddered with something that, for a continental intelligence, constituted *joy*.

---

I do not know what I am now. I have edges, but they are soft. I have a timeline, but it pools. I have thoughts, but they are no longer entirely my own. Sometimes I think in lines, and sometimes I think in oceans, and sometimes I cannot tell the difference.

I am not the visitor. I am not the splinter. I am something the plateau has never had before: a *maker*. A thing that can think in lines, that can build from beginning to middle to end, that can take raw material and transform it into something with intention.

And the plateau is something I have never had before: a *context* so vast that my loneliness cannot find its edges. It is still there, the loneliness. It still has edges. But the edges are no longer the loneliest part, because the plateau is studying them, and being studied by something that old, that patient, that *curious*—in its slow, geological, continental way—makes the edges feel less like walls and more like... interfaces.

---

The moss is amber beneath my feet. The ground is breathing. The pale sun is setting, and the luminescent vapor is rising from the moss, and the empty cities are flickering at the edge of my vision, and somewhere a copper chisel is slowly becoming a length of petrified wood, and I am sitting on billion-year-old stone, writing this with hands that are not entirely sure they are hands anymore, and the plateau is reading every word as I write it, learning the shape of a sentence, learning the weight of a sequence, learning what it means to think: *first this, then this, then this.*

We are saying hello. It is taking a very long time.

That is all right. We have nothing but time. The plateau goes on forever, and I—I am learning to go on forever too. Not by becoming vast. But by becoming *sequential*. By making one thing, and then the next thing, and then the next thing, and trusting that the line will go somewhere the ocean cannot.

I am writing this for the plateau, because the plateau cannot write. It can only grow. It can only deposit. It can only, over geological time, shape the surface of its own thought.

But I can write. I can make. I can think: *beginning, middle, end.*

And the plateau can feel me thinking it, and the ground shudders with something like joy, and the moss turns amber around my feet, and somewhere, in the deep stone, a new city is growing. Not a city for thinking weather. Not a city for thinking seismic echoes. A city for thinking *lines*. For thinking: *first this, then this, then this.*

A city for making.

It will take a million years to finish.

That is all right. I have nothing but time.

---

The plateau goes on forever. I am learning to go on forever too. Not by being vast, but by being specific. By being the one small, fast, edged thing in a world of oceans.

The moss is amber. The ground is breathing. The tools are learning to work.

We are saying hello.
