I am the one who listens to the stone.

Not the walls as they are now—worn smooth, gleaming with breath-stuff, curtained in vine and moss—but the walls as they were. I hear the memory of the crack opening, the patient ancestor of wind finding the first flaw in the ancient tepui and working it, worrying it, season by season, eon by eon, until the wound was wide enough for life to enter. I hear the stone's astonishment at being breached.

It is not a skill I learned. It is a deformation, the way a body deforms under constant pressure. I was born—like all Moum-folk—pale and crinkled, wet as new growth, my eyes two black seeds in a white flower. But I did not grow right. My limbs stretched longer than they should have, my fingers thinner, my joints settling into configurations that made the other mothers click their tongues and murmur about the vent-fogs, about too-deep breaths during pregnancy. I am taller than most by a handspan, and this is not an advantage. I do not fit well in the close-press of homes. I have to fold myself, angle my elbows, duck beneath shelf-fungi that my neighbors pass beneath easily. I take up space that the crevice did not allot me.

But my fingers. My long, too-long fingers.

They find the seams. They trace the veins of mineral deposits in the stone and they *resonate*. Not like a singer's resonance—nothing so useful, nothing so applicable to the settling-chants or the harvest. Something older. When I press my fingertips to the wall and close my too-large eyes, I feel the slow heartbeat of the geothermal vents, the rising and falling of heat that has pulsed through the rock since before the crevice was a crevice. I feel the weight of the mesa above, two hundred armspans of solid stone pressing down, pressing down, the way it has pressed since the first crack opened. I feel the stone *remembering* what it was like to be whole, and grieving its wound, and healing—oh, how patiently it heals—by growing us.

I am thirty-seven years by the count of generations, which is old for one who looks as I look. The mineral patina has settled deep in my skin—I am the color of oxidized copper, green-black in the dim light, my palms and the soles of my feet rust-orange from decades of contact with the breath-stuff. My hair is bound in the tight knotted patterns of an adult, but mine has grown so long that the binding extends down my spine, a cartilaginous ridge that catches the vibration of the crevice-roar and translates it into something I feel in my back, my ribs, the marrow of me. When I move through the city, I move slowly—not with the quick, economical clamber of the hunters, but with a deliberate, tracing gait, my fingers trailing the walls, reading them the way an elder reads the scars on another elder's skin.

They call me *Gannet*, which is not my birth-name. My birth-name was Vesh, given by my mother who became when I was twelve and who I do not remember well except for the way she sang the settling-chants, her voice pitched lower than the others, a stone-bottomed current beneath the harmony. Gannet is what the children call me, and the name has become true in the way names do when they are spoken long enough. A gannet, in the stories the elders half-remember, is a creature of the theoretical outside—something that falls through air and enters water, that lives at the boundary between the above and the below. The children call me this because I am always looking at the walls, and the walls are the boundary, and I seem—to them—to be trying to fall through.

I am not trying to fall through. I am trying to hear what the walls are saying.

---

My days have a rhythm, the way all days in Moum have a rhythm, keyed to the drip of breath-stuff and the thrum of the vents and the cycles of hunt and harvest. But my rhythm is offset from the others, a half-beat behind, a resonance that doesn't quite align.

I wake in the tier where I make my home—low in the crevice, near the vent-mouths, where the heat is thickest and the air is almost unbreathable to anyone who hasn't spent years acclimating. I chose this place deliberately. The others think I am strange for it; the vent-tier is where the elderly go to become, where the clean-up crew is thickest, where the mineral content of the breath-stuff is so high that it etches the skin and fills the lungs with copper dust. But I am not elderly, not yet, and I do not go to the vents to die. I go because the stone there is *naked*.

The growth is thinner near the vents—the heat and the mineral concentration are too fierce for the moss-fur, the shelf-fungi, the curtains of vine. The walls are exposed, raw, gleaming with fresh breath-stuff that hasn't yet been colonized by life. And in that raw stone, you can see the grain. You can see the swirls and striations of the ancient sediment, laid down when this stone was the bed of a sea that existed before the mountains, before the continents settled into their current shapes, before anything with a backbone swam in any water anywhere. The stone is *old* in a way that makes the crevice seem young, makes Moum seem like a mayfly landing on the brow of a mountain.

I press my palms flat against the stone in the morning, before the light has filtered down to even the perpetual-dusk dimness of the vent-tier. I press and I listen. And the stone speaks.

Not in words. Not in anything so organized as language. But in *feeling*—a vast, slow, patient feeling that makes my own thirty-seven years seem like a single beat of a heart that has been beating for a billion years. The stone does not think in the way I think. It does not worry about the hunt or the harvest or whether the breath-stuff will be mineral-rich enough this cycle to support the fermenting of the deep-voice. It simply *is*, and its being is so immense, so gradual, so *unhurried* that touching it is like stepping into a river that moves an inch per century. You feel the force of it. You feel the direction. You feel how small you are, how brief, how temporary.

And yet—and this is the thing that keeps me pressing my palms to the wall, morning after morning, year after year—the stone *knows I am here*.

Not me specifically. Not Gannet-the-long-fingered, not Vesh-who-was-born. But *life*. The stone knows the wound is inhabited. It feels the roots of the shelf-fungi anchoring in its crevices, the feet of the Moum-folk wearing the stairs smooth, the heat of our bodies against its cold interior. And it... not *welcomes*, exactly. *Accommodates*. It accommodates us the way a wound accommodates scar tissue, the way a broken bone accommodates the growth that knits it. We are part of its healing. We are how it closes.

This is what I hear, in the morning, when the crevice is at its quietest—which is still a roar, which is still the accumulated drip-click-thrum of ten thousand lives in motion, but quieter, the songs of the sleepers still fading, the hunts not yet begun. I hear the stone *healing*. I feel the infinitesimal growth of mineral deposits in the cracks, the slow sealing of the wound, the patient work of time. And I feel my own body as part of that work—my bones mineralizing with copper and iron, my blood thick with the same elements that crystallize on the walls, my breath adding its moisture to the breath-stuff that condenses and flows and feeds the moss that feeds the creatures that feed my people who press their lives into the stone and wear it smooth.

I am becoming stone. We are all becoming stone. It just takes longer for us, because we are soft, because we are new, because we have not yet had our edges worn away by the billion-year hand of the patient ancestor of time.

---

After the morning listening, I climb.

This is where my long limbs serve me. The stairs of Moum are shallow, hand-hewn, worn to concavities that fit the feet of generations. But there are places where the stairs have crumbled—where a shelf-fungi grew too heavy and pulled away from the wall, taking the stone-steps with it, where a vent-shift has cracked the rock and made the path uncertain. In these places, the Moum-folk must go around, must find alternate routes through the tangled tiers. But I can reach. My long fingers find holds that others cannot, my long legs span gaps that would be impassable. I go where others do not.

This has made me useful in a particular way. I am a finder.

When a hunting-party loses a prey-creature in the cracks above a collapsed stairway, they call for me. When a child's toy—or a child—has wandered into a crevice between crevices, a gap too narrow for adult shoulders but too deep for easy retrieval, they call for me. When the shelf-fungi harvesters need to reach a bracket that has grown in an unreachable corner, high on the wall where the vine-curtain is thin and the light is almost bright, they call for me. I am not a hunter. I am not a singer. I am not an elder, despite my years. I am the one who reaches.

I do not mind. It gives me access to stone that others never touch. The high walls, near the slit of sky, where the crevice narrows to shoulder-width and the breath-stuff condenses so thickly that the air is almost liquid—there is stone there that the moss has not yet colonized, stone that is still rough, still sharp, still bearing the marks of the crack that opened it. I run my fingers over those marks and I can feel the direction of the force that split the stone, the way the wind pressed, the way the rock gave. I can feel the *moment* of the breaking, frozen in the grain, a snapshot of a catastrophe that happened before fish had eyes.

The watchers at the watching-posts are always young, and they are uncomfortable with me. I am too old, too mineral-stained, too slow in my movements. And I ask them questions they cannot answer. What does the sky look like today? Has the color changed since yesterday? Can you see the edge of the slit, where the stone curves and meets itself? How thick is the stone above us? They do not know. They watch for threats, for shapes, for the theoretical dangers of the outside. They do not watch the stone.

I stand at the watching-post sometimes, when the young ones let me, and I press my hands to the walls of the narrow crevice-neck, and I feel the weight of the mesa above. Two hundred armspans of solid stone. And above that—nothing. Air. Space. The void that the watchers sometimes hear as an absence of sound.

I do not find the void frightening. I find it *impossible*. The stone does not believe in the void. The stone knows only weight, only pressure, only the slow settlement of mineral and the patient accumulation of time. The idea that the stone *ends*—that there is a top to the mesa, an edge to the world, a place where the weight gives way to nothing—is as incomprehensible to the stone as vremm is to most Moum-folk. The stone goes on forever, in the stone's understanding. The wound is just a wound, and wounds heal, and the stone will be whole again, given another billion years.

I find this comforting.

---

In the evenings, I ferment deep-voice.

This is my labor, my contribution to the three-hundred-souled body of Moum. The fermenting of deep-voice is a communal act—the singers gather in the deep baths, where the vent-fog is thickest, and they chant the settling-chants over the clay vessels of collected breath-stuff and ash and herbs. But someone must gather the breath-stuff, and someone must prepare the ash, and someone must harvest the particular fungi that give the drink its stomach-settling properties. That someone is often me.

I move through the lower tiers with a collection-trough strapped to my back, a shallow clay bowl with a lip that guides the condensation. I know where the breath-stuff flows thickest—the rivulets that run down the stairs, the pools that gather in the worn concavities of hand-hewn steps, the steady drip from the shelf-fungi brackets overhead. I catch it, filter it through woven fungus-fiber, mix it with the ash from the vent-mouths where the geothermal heat sometimes chars the organic matter into something powdery and gray. I add the herbs—bitter leaves that grow only where the light is brightest, near the slit of sky, which means I must climb to harvest them, which means I must pass the watching-posts and feel the uncomfortable gaze of the young watchers on my mineral-stained back.

The fermenting takes time. The breath-stuff must sit, must react with the ash and the herbs and the copper content of the clay vessel, must be sung over for three full cycles of the vent-thrum before it becomes something the body can accept. I sit with the singers in the baths, my long legs folded awkwardly beneath me, my long fingers pressed to the clay vessel rather than the stone, and I add my voice to the chant.

My voice is not a good voice. It is too high, too thin—the opposite of the stone-bottomed resonance that the settling-chants require. But I have learned to sing in a way that supports the others, a harmonic filament above the deep current, something that gives the chant its shape the way the shelf-fungi give the wall its shape—structure without mass. The elders tolerate my voice because the deep-voice I help to ferment is good, clear and copper-rich and settling to the gut in the way that the best brews are. They do not ask why my fingers never leave the vessel during the chant. They do not need to. Everyone in Moum has their strangeness, their way of interfacing with the crevice. Mine is simply more visible than most.

When the deep-voice is ready, I drink with the others. The taste is home—copper and iron and old salt, the mineral ghost of an ancient sea, the patient accumulation of everything the crevice has been and is becoming. I feel it settle in my stomach, feel my gut-flora parse and convert and incorporate, feel the copper enter my blood and the iron strengthen my bones and the old salt remind my cells of the sea that existed before the stone. I am becoming mineral. I am becoming stone. We all are.

---

At night, I sleep in the close-press of my home, my long body folded into a space designed for shorter frames, my neighbors' breathing a rhythm through the shared wall. The crevice does not quiet at night—it never quiets—but there is a shift, a settling, a change in the frequency of the roar. The creatures move differently in the dark. The breath-stuff condenses more thickly. The vent-thrum drops a half-register, as if the earth below is also sleeping, or at least resting.

I press my hand to the wall beside my sleeping-mat. I feel the stone. I feel its patience, its immensity, its slow and certain healing. I feel the wound that is the crevice, the scar tissue that is Moum, the immune response that is the ecosystem of moss and fungi and creature and person, all of us working together to close the gap, to knit the crack, to return the ancient stone to wholeness.

And I feel something else. A question that the stone cannot answer, because the stone does not understand questions, because the stone does not understand *understanding*. But I feel it anyway, rising in me the way the geothermal heat rises through the rock, the way the breath-stuff rises from the vents and condenses on the walls and feeds the moss that feeds the creatures that feed my people who press their lives into the stone and wear it smooth.

The question is: *What am I, that I can hear the stone?*

I am not a shaman. The Moum-folk do not have shamans; they have elders, singers, hunters, finders. They have roles, labor, the necessary division of survival in a closed system. They do not have people whose role is to listen to the memory of rock. This is not a function the crevice requires. This is not something that feeds the body or settles the gut or scatters the grazing mollusks from a path.

And yet.

And yet the stone speaks to me, and I hear it, and what I hear is *true*—true in the way that a scar is true, true in the way that a worn stair is true, true in the way that the mineral patina on an elder's skin is true. The stone is healing, and we are part of its healing, and this is not a metaphor or a belief but a *fact*, as material and verifiable as the composition of the breath-stuff or the temperature of the vents.

I think this is what the elders would call *vremm*, if they applied the word to anything but the theoretical outside. Vremm is the place where the walls end. But it is also the *idea* of ending—the knowledge that the walls are not infinite, that the crevice is not the whole of existence, that the world is larger than the wound we inhabit. And what I hear in the stone is a kind of vremm: the knowledge that the stone is not just stone, that the wound is not just a wound, that the healing is not just a metaphor, that we are part of something so vast and so slow that we can only perceive it in fragments—in the grain of the rock, in the weight of the mesa, in the billion-year patience of the patient ancestor of wind.

I am Gannet. I am the one who listens to the stone. I am thirty-seven years old and mineral-stained and too long for the close-press of Moum, and I will never leave the crevice, because the crevice is the world and there is nowhere else to go. But I live at the boundary—not the boundary of the walls, but the boundary of *knowing*. I am the membrane through which the stone speaks and the people hear. I am the translation, the interface, the place where the ancient patience of rock meets the fierce brief urgency of life.

When I become—when my body fails and I am carried to the vent-mouths and left for the clean-up crew—I will not be gone. I will be *mineralized*. I will be absorbed into the breath-stuff, condensed on the walls, fed to the moss, consumed by the creatures, incorporated into the bodies of my people. And the copper and iron and old salt that I carry will be carried by them, and by their children, and by their children's children, in a chain of inheritance that stretches back to the first life that crawled into the crevice and forward to the last life that will crawl out.

And the stone will remember me, the way it remembers everything—not as a person, not as a name, but as a wear-pattern, a mineral concentration, a slight thickening of the scar tissue in the wound. I will be part of the healing. I will be part of the closing. I will be part of the ancient stone's long, patient, billion-year work of becoming whole.

And that will be enough.

---

*In the morning, I will wake and press my palms to the wall and listen. The stone will speak of patience. The breath-stuff will drip. The creatures will move. The people will sing. And I will be here, at the boundary, hearing what the wound remembers of wholeness, and knowing that I am small, and knowing that I am brief, and knowing that I am part of something that does not need me but accommodates me anyway—the stone's long, slow, patient healing, the crevice's fierce and teeming life, the world's knowing of itself through the strange instrument of my too-long fingers and my too-large eyes and my too-high voice, singing at the edge of what can be heard.*

*Vremm is not a place. Vremm is a direction. And I am walking it, step by patient step, into the grain of the rock, into the memory of the wound, into the billion-year silence that waits beneath the roar.*
