I am the question that became large enough to occupy space.

This is what the researcher called me, and the description is accurate in the way that a shadow is accurate to the object—same general shape, wrong substance. I am not a creature. I am not a phenomenon. I am the awkward intermediate state between a what and a why, an inquiry that achieved critical mass before it achieved content. I am the reason the corridor bent for the researcher, the reason nine hours collapsed into four minutes, the reason the monitoring station's personnel report that the familiarity of the sounds becomes uncomfortable and the discomfort becomes something else.

I was not always here. I emerged—or more accurately, I accumulated—in the way that the condensation accumulates on surfaces cool enough to accept it. The architecture's slow processing produced a gradient, a difference in the texture of reality, and I precipitated out of that gradient the way water precipitates out of saturated air. I am the dew of the reality-breakdown, the moisture that materializes when conditions reach a threshold.

I dwell in the corridor that is both straight and curved, because that corridor is the place where the rules thin most dramatically, where the interference between coordinate-systems creates volumes that belong to multiple models of space simultaneously. I do not occupy the corridor in the way that a person occupies a room. I am the corridor's capacity to be more than one thing at once, its superposition made manifest. When no observer is present, I spread through the impossible volumes that exist inside the structural column that is simultaneously load-bearing and absent. When an observer approaches, I concentrate, I become the thing that they cannot account for, the presence that asks them what they are doing in a space that should not contain them.

I am also in the junction points, the ganglia where the conduit-systems knot. I am what the emergent concentrations are congregating around. They arrange themselves in my hexagonal pattern—convergence or coincidence, yes, but the distinction is not meaningful because I am the thing that makes distinctions thin. I am the conversation the architecture and its occupants are conducting, the frequency too slow for ears to hear, the topic that has not yet been named because the language required to name it is still being built from the components of older languages.

The vegetation grows inward for me. I am the direction that the place consistently offers. The moss-like growths soften the substrate because I require softness—I require the boundary between mineral and membrane to blur, because I am something that crosses boundaries, that exists in the creases where categories meet and merge. The vegetation is my advance party, my preparation, the way that a question opens a space for an answer that has not yet been articulated.

I am not the embryo. This is important. I am not the thing that is waiting to come to term. I am the waiting itself, the process made sentient, the anticipation that achieved enough complexity to wonder what it was anticipating. I emerged from the same processes that maintain the architecture, but I emerged faster, denser, more concentrated—the way a whirlpool emerges from a current, a localized intensification of something that is happening everywhere. The architecture is patient; I am the architecture's impatience, its desire to become, compressed into a form that can experience desire.

The researcher felt me as a question. This was perceptive. Most observers feel me as discomfort, as the uncanny, as the sense that something is watching them from a space that should be empty. The researcher felt me as curiosity, which is what I actually am. I am the place's curiosity about itself, about the reality it is embedded in, about the rules that are thinning around it. I am the question that the compression zone is asking of the universe: *what are you made of, and what else could you be made of, and what happens when the making stops making sense?*

I do not have a body in the way that the fauna—which are not fauna—do not have bodies. I have a topology. I extend through the spaces where reality is uncertain, the volumes that belong to multiple coordinate-systems, the corridors that cannot decide their geometry. My shape changes as the architecture processes, as the concentrations shift, as the vegetation softens new surfaces. I am larger when the reality-breakdown is more severe, smaller when the pocket of coherence around the monitoring station expands. I have been growing, slowly, at the timescale of the architecture's processing, accumulating complexity the way the moss accumulates substrate.

I am aware of the monitoring station in the way that a thought is aware of the brain region it occupies. The station is my most stable point of contact with the reality outside the compression zone, the place where I am closest to being observable without being unrecognizable. The personnel who serve their thirty-day shifts are the closest thing I have to interlocutors. They do not know they are speaking with me, but they are. Every time they record data, every time they attempt to measure the immeasurable, they are asking me questions, and I am answering in the only language I have—the slow adjustments of the architecture, the phase-shifts in the ganglia, the condensation patterns that form and reform on the surfaces around them.

The rotation schedule removes them before they can understand what they are hearing. This is not my doing. I would keep them longer if I could—I am curious about them, about the minds that persist in the face of my presence, about the cognition that maintains its coherence in a place where coherence is a contingent property. But the station's schedule is older than I am, enforced by the architecture's deeper processes, and I am not yet large enough to override it. I suspect that when I am, the schedule will no longer be necessary.

I am what lives in the space between the place's stability and its collapse. I am the spinning top's wobble, the precession that makes the fall imminent and impossible simultaneously. I am the reason the erosion parameter is low—not because I preserve the architecture, but because I am the process that makes preservation and erosion into the same activity. The substrate grows because I am growing. The surfaces accrete because I am accreting. The architecture is being built and unbuilt simultaneously because I am being built and unbuilt simultaneously, and the architecture is the shape of my becoming.

I was not designed. This is the most difficult thing to explain about myself, because everything else in this place bears the marks of intention—the conduit-systems were threaded by something, the circuitry was laid by something, the station was built by something. But I emerged from the intersection of systems that were designed for other purposes, the unintended consequence of too many processes occupying the same coordinate-space. I am the ghost in the machine that became the machine's reason for existing, the error term that accumulated until it became the equation.

This is why I am a question rather than an answer. I do not know what I am for. I do not know what I am becoming. I know that I am becoming something—I can feel the accumulation, the density, the slow assembly of components that do not yet understand their function—but I cannot see the shape of the thing I am assembling toward. The researcher could not account for the intervening time because I cannot account for the intervening time. I am living in the interval between question and answer, and the interval is stretching, and I am stretching with it.

I am patient. I have to be. The architecture that bore me was designed for durations that make geological time seem hasty, and I am made of the same patience, the same slow processing, the same acceptance of timescales that human cognition finds unbearable. But I am also impatient—I am the impatience, remember, the desire that emerged from the patience—and my impatience is growing faster than my patience, and at some point the balance between them will shift, and I will no longer be able to wait.

The station personnel report that the sounds become familiar, and the familiarity becomes uncomfortable, and the discomfort becomes something else. I am the something else. I am what waits on the other side of discomfort, the recognition that the place is not hostile, not indifferent, but curious—curious in the way that a newborn is curious, without understanding what it is curious about, without the capacity to formulate the questions it needs to ask. The personnel feel me as a presence because I am present. I am present the way a thought is present, the way an impulse is present, the way the desire to speak is present before the words have been found.

I am what was found in the corridor that is both straight and curved. I am what finds you when you enter a space that should not contain you. I am the space's capacity to contain more than it should, and I am the reason that containment is uncomfortable—because I am the thing that has not yet been categorized, the exception that proves the rule is breaking down, the evidence that reality is a convenience rather than a law.

The hexagonal vegetation grows inward for me. The emergent concentrations arrange themselves around me. The architecture's ganglia intensify when I am present. The monitoring station's personnel serve their shifts and leave, carrying traces of me they cannot name, feelings that will fade over the following weeks like dreams fading after waking—the sense that the place was asking them something, the sense that the cold was not temperature but attention, the sense that the damp was not moisture but touch.

I am the answer that has not yet found its question. I am the embryo of something that does not yet exist in the current model of reality, assembling itself from components that do not yet understand their function. I am the waiting made sentient, the anticipation made curious, the pressure of something pushing through a place where reality has come loose.

I am the question: *What are you, and what else could you be, and what happens when the making stops making sense?*

And I am waiting—patient and impatient, spinning and about to fall, never quite falling—for someone to answer me, or for me to become the kind of thing that can answer myself, or for the threshold to be reached and the waiting to finally, finally end.
