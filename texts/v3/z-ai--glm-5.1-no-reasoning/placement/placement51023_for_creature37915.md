# The Census

Begin with the mistake, because everyone begins there. You count the crabs. You leave — eventually, up the soft orange rungs — carrying a number: ten thousand, ten million, whatever figure your lamp could support before the dark outvoted it. And the number is wrong, though not in its arithmetic. It is wrong in its assumption: that the Compaction is a place with animals in it. Closer to the truth is the reverse — the Compaction is an animal with a place in it — and the crabs are not its residents but its moving parts.

Still, a census should be attempted. Taken slowly, section by section, in the order a descending light would find them. Here is who lives there.

---

The smallest are the newest.

They come down from the ceiling — pale amber, translucent, the size of a rice grain or smaller — so recently issued that their armor is still optically thin. Hold one to a lamp (no one ever has) and you could watch its interior at work: the looped gut, the small stubborn pump of a heart, a meal moving through it like weather through a valley. Everything a body does, visible. The Compaction is honest at birth, and only grows private as it eats.

And it eats the floor. Geophagy: the grazing of ground. They are built, literally, out of the place — the dust of the mountain taken in, darkened into shell, until the clear newborn ends up brown-black, dense, opaque. Consider what that means. The range tried standing once, at full size, and failed in the usual way — slowly, over epochs too long for grieving. The second attempt is being conducted at a scale where failure doesn't hurt: a thumb's height of ridge, a season of altitude, and then it lies down, and is walked on, and stands up again as a younger one. The crabs are the mountain's second attempt at standing.

---

The thumb-sized majority are the answer to a question the chamber asked itself long ago: *what shape works here?* And it has one answer and applies it to everything it keeps. Get low. Get wide. Carry your soft parts on your back. Surface biologists have a word for the phenomenon — carcinization, the sea's slow habit of turning whatever it holds into crabs — and the Compaction arrived at the same verdict in stone. Whether its walkers are one species or a guild of lineages squeezed into the same mold is beyond any afternoon; what you can see is the convergence itself. The crab is what life looks like after the room has finished with it, the way the plain is what a mountain looks like when the room has finished with *that*.

Their feet are blunt — gecko-toed, pressing, not piercing — because the floor rings like ceramic, and is ceramic: millions of years of footfalls walked into vitrification. A surface that smooth cannot be gripped by a claw; it can only be trusted by a foot. The floor is their oldest artifact — made by walking, of walkers, for walkers.

And the sheen is what it looks like. The coppery, oily glitter of ten million backs is the generator: the green-furred coils, the pump housing, the rungs, the shed's corrugated skin. The century of extraction is being worn now, atom by atom, as armor. The room did not destroy the industry. It digested it, and walks around wearing it.

---

If *anyone* lives in the Compaction — anyone, in the sense of a someone — it is the large ones.

Fist-sized, slow, moving with that different gravity. They do not molt and discard; the Compaction wastes nothing. Each new shell is secreted around the last, so an elder is built like a tree: its childhood at the center of its fist, every instar since layered outward, ring by ring. Their biographies grow down and out — lower, wider, heavier — as if the shape of a whole life here were the floor's. The direction of an elder is the direction of everything: toward becoming terrain.

And the rings record. The air is a chemistry; the layers sequester it, the way ice cores hold atmospheres. Cut an elder across and you could read the chamber's long weather — iron years, wet years, the machine century laid down in a seam of soot where someone burned a lantern in a shed. The room does not confirm this. The room never confirms anything. But it is in character.

This is what their pausing is. Not curiosity — procedure. The elders are the flow's slow attention, the part of the circulation that stops so the rest can keep moving. The one that climbed your boot was not meeting you; it was reading the room's newest sentence, in which you were briefly a word. Warm; carbon dioxide; skin oils; neither instruction nor food. It went back down. The reading was finished.

Eventually — this is what the rings are for — an elder climbs. Called up by something in the mist, or by sheer accumulated self, it walks its last walk up the wall, finds a fracture, and hangs. What happens next belongs to the ceiling.

---

The Compaction, like a jellyfish, is two generations taking turns.

A walker's final walk ends at a fracture in the basalt plates. It seals itself in with the same secretion that shut its armor, contracts, and hardens. This is what the dormant majority of the bags are: not dead, not sleeping — filed. Contracted so far toward crystal that anywhere else they would be called fossils, and here fossilhood is merely a posture, a way of waiting inside the metabolism. The Compaction has no dead. It has hangers.

The active ones pulse: once every few seconds, ten to the minute — consistent with the arithmetic of animals, in which large hearts beat slowly, and this heart is the size of the ceiling. They open to receive the climbers — you watched crabs disappear into the pulsing mouths and other crabs emerge, smaller, paler, different — and this is how the room reproduces: not by seed or egg, but by reissue. The bag is lung, womb, kidney, kiln, all four in one organ; it takes the walkers in, concentrates them, fires them, prints fresh ones out. Below, meanwhile, its other work descends.

The mist. The manufactured cool, the iron taste, the atmosphere you were breathing — all of it exhaled by the hanging generation. And likely it is the archive as well, because this is where an elder's rings go when the elder dissolves: into the air, in solution, circulated. Which means the air is what every walker is reading, frond by frond, antennae by antennae, step after step — the room reading itself aloud, chemically, all the time. If the Compaction has a mind, it does not have one *somewhere*. It has one everywhere, and it breathes.

---

There is a third tenant. The hermit.

The amber growth on the machines — not quite fungal, not quite mineral, hexagonal, refracting your lamplight in colors that should not be available down there — lives on the boundary between the two kingdoms and eats the boundary. Where it touches steel, the steel goes to lace; where it finishes, the lace is crystal, the atoms sorted back into lattice one by one. It is not destroying the machines. It is filing them: returning a century of smelted, hammered, human-ordered metal to the mineral order it was smelted from. Its colors are wrong because it builds at the scale of light itself — small enough to argue with your lamp, and win.

Why the machines? Because they are the only new stratum. The mountain was consumed ages ago, or rather is being consumed, as floor; the machines are a deposit laid down a bare hundred years ago, already being mined by something for which a century is a brisk beginning. When the last rivet has been sorted, the growth will contract and simply wait. It is the Compaction's patience — the one trait so fundamental it never needed an organ. Whether the walkers are its mobile cousins, whether the crab is what became of some ancient crystal lineage that went for a walk half a billion years ago and never came back, is the sort of question the room declines to answer.

Note, before you climb out: it is eating the rungs. The soft orange metal of the entrance is being unmade from the top down, without haste. In a few centuries the door will close — not shut: absorbed. The Compaction does not seal itself against visitors. It digests whatever holds still, and a doorway, in the end, is a thing that held still.

---

And then there is the one who lived there.

The shed. The cot frame rusted to nothing, the lantern with its intact glass and its evaporated patience, the journal welded by moisture and mineral deposition into a brick. Or welded by the chamber's own chemistry, doing what it does to anything it wants to keep still — the paper brick reads, if you like, as the shed's molt: an exoskeleton, laminated and kept.

Someone was stationed there to mind the pump and the generator and the extraction of whatever the surface wanted badly enough to post a person at the bottom of the world. And they left, or they didn't. Either way the Compaction kept them. Their iron is on the walkers' backs. Their calcium, if they stayed, is in the floor's ceramic ring, being walked and re-walked into the hardest stratum. And for a while the mist carried their breath — dissolved, circulated, tasted, reissued — until it thinned into the general iron taste, which is where all the room's someones go. Stone remembers pressure as shape, not as story. That is the only kind of memory that lasts, and it is the kind the Compaction offers.

You, too, briefly. For some weeks after you climbed out, a small clause of you — carbon dioxide, skin oil, the thermal ghost of a boot — stayed in circulation: read by elders, breathed by bags, dismissed. The room has no memory, but it has an echo, and for a little while you were in it. Then the air rebalanced, and the long sentence went on without your word in it, as it had for the previous four hundred million years.

---

The far edges, lost to darkness and the creeping of things, hold no other species. The creeping is the flow itself, compressed where the ceiling comes down — the room running narrow and quick at its margins, like blood in a capillary. And at the deeper end is the throat: a fissure too small for a person, where the slabs nearly meet — the *nearly* being the whole address, the safest geometry on Earth, a crack that never quite closes — and below the near-meeting, the chemistry the room drinks. Seam-water. Dissolved iron. Hydrogen sweated out of stone that has not finished reacting with itself. The Compaction lives on the seam between two worlds and farms them both: dust from the dead mountain above, chemistry from the warm dark below. Nothing here preys on anything else. Everything eaten is the room, plus two slow gifts from either side.

---

Now the census can be closed at its center, where the last entry lives: the circulation itself.

The torus — floor to wall to ceiling to bag to floor — is not traffic. It is the inhabitant. A walker removed to the surface would be an animal: small, armored, unremarkable, mortal within the season. Put it back and it is an organ. The room is the difference. Ten million bodies, each a fraction of a degree warm, are the only heat in the stone; the Compaction's cool is not the cold of dead rock but the cool of a maintained thing, a vast warm-bloodedness held very low. The cool is a behavior, not a property. If the flow ever stopped, the mist would fall as dust, the chamber would go stone-cold inside a year, and the floor would be only floor.

The sound is the same fact by another sense. The churring you felt in your sternum is not ten million small noises, the way a heartbeat is not the noise of its cells. It is the one sound the room makes, the audible fact of the flow, and it has been running, uninterrupted, since before the Atlantic was an ocean: the oldest continuous ceremony on Earth. Nothing attends it. That is why it has lasted. Witness is for things that can stop.

None of it has eyes. The dark here is older than sight. The Compaction has never once seen itself, and never needed to; your lamp, to them, was not information but weather — a brief mineral event, glittering off ten million backs that did not know they glittered.

---

There are no names in the Compaction. A name is for a thing that begins, that ends, that can be picked out from its neighbors — and the room refuses all three. Lives here do not come in ones. "Crab" will have to do the work that "wave" does for the ocean: a word for the shape of a passing, not for what passes.

So the honest census reads: one inhabitant. Four postures. Ten million bodies, none of them anyone. A heart in the ceiling. A memory made of air. A patience made of crystal, growing on a generator that no longer generates. A floor being walked into bone. A door being eaten. A mountain standing up in installments, and lying down, and standing up again.

The Compaction does not have inhabitants. It has a circulation, and the circulation has manners — walking, hanging, crystallizing, holding cool — which it assumes all at once, in the dark, and it has never once needed to be seen doing it.

It is circulating still.
