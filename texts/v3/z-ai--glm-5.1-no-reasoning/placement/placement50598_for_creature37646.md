# The Inhabitants

You went looking for whoever lives here, and for a long time you found no one. No eyes shine in the walls. Nothing startles at your step, nothing marks your warmth, nothing rearranges itself around your presence. The silence answers your search with more silence, and you begin to believe the answer is *nobody* — a hall with no one home, a body with nobody in it.

You are wrong twice. First, because you are standing on the inhabitant. Second, because you are standing in it.

The Germinating Floor is not a place that contains a creature, the way a cave might contain a bear. It is a creature that has grown itself into the shape of a place. The pillar-trunks are not pillars; they are its posture. The ceiling-skin is not a ceiling; it is the underside of its back. The root-floor is its flank, turned upward to carry you, and everything that has ever lived here has lived the way mites live in the fold of an ear — on it, in it, of it, held by the sheer width of its patience.

Its life is spread so thin across time that you must think in years to see a single behavior. It eats at its frontier, where its roots press into raw stone and a film etches the rock for minerals — the only appetite it has ever had. It drinks its own condensate from the bowls it grows. It breathes by transpiration, so slowly that one full breath, out and back, takes a day. It feels pressure, weight, warmth. Your footsteps are pressures it has noted and set aside. It has never slept, having never been awake, and if it thinks — if a sensation held steady for ten thousand years, or ten times that, can be called a thought — the thought is *enough*.

At the frontier, in the cracks where the wall is splitting stone, live the only residents who will ever touch the unconverted world: pioneer roots pale as scars, rock-eating film, and motes smaller than flecks of ceiling-light, eyeless, grazing the mineral flour of the place's slow expansion. That is the room's mouth. Everywhere else is satiety.

---

The next thing you got wrong is the light.

You assumed it was a property of the place — luminance, fluorescence, some chemistry of the walls. It is livestock. The golden fluid in the veins is dense with lives too small to see, and they are the light. They drink the sweetness the room pumps through its veins and shine. They divide so slowly that a birth takes a day, and they die at exactly the same rate, and the two rates have been matched so long that the census never changes. That is why the light never flickers. The light is a population, and the population is still. It has been steady since before anything was counting. The gold is the herd. The green is the room — the tint of the skin the light shines through.

The room drinks their glow through its ceiling-skin and grows on it. It is a lantern lit by its own flame, and a flame that lives in the lantern, fed by it, feeding it. The herd has never known dark. The room has never known sun. Each is the only world the other has, and neither of them has ever been alone.

The honey-colored pool in the platform channel is full of them — flecks of the herd, cut off from the sap-line, glowing on stored sweetness. They will shine for months, dimming at the speed of wood. It looks like honey because that is what it is: light, condensed and kept.

---

Then there are the swimmers.

Smaller than the first knuckle of your little finger, soft, translucent, faintly segmented; you can see the dark thread of what it has eaten coiled inside it. Each one hatches from a bead the root-mat grows — the floor keeps the eggs in its grain the way a tree keeps last year's seeds, except the floor has been keeping them for centuries and they keep hatching. It grazes the sweet film. It rests in the warm hollows. It never flees, because nothing here has ever hunted anything. It lives weeks or months, and dies unhurried, sometimes mid-pool, and the film takes it in within days, without ceremony. The swimmers are the only creatures in this place who do anything quickly, and what they do quickly is become substrate.

The room hatches them itself. That is the strangest fact in this place: the floor, having taken in so many of their kind over so long, retains the making of them. The room can still grow the children.

What it lost was the knowing of how to finish them.

---

Because the swimmers are children — the childhood of a species whose adulthood is now architecture.

Lie down on one of the platforms. The moss is warm. The light will not change. Nothing is watching you, so you may as well. The shallow basin in the platform's surface is not your shape. It is the shape of a small body, low and long, lying full stretch, one limb resting along a channel as if its hand sat in a watercourse it had spent a decade asking for. The basin does not fill the platform; the platform is the callus, and the basin is what the callus grew around — except nothing here was wounded. Something pressed.

The floor grows around whatever leans on it. That is the whole of its vocabulary, and it was the whole of theirs. They were the adults of the swimmers: a little longer than your forearm, pale, translucent like their children, glowing faintly along the belly after they had fed — because what they fed on was the golden fluid, light secondhand. Their eyes had dwindled to two dimples, because the light never changes and the loop keeps no surplus. What they had instead was hands: many-fingered, slow, always resting on something, always pressing, reading the floor and writing on it.

They had no tools. They had asking. You ask a place like this by leaning on it, and they asked for years and were answered in decades. Where the pillar-trunks enter the root-floor, the veins run shallow and seep, and they pressed the roots for a decade until a channel grew to guide the seep downhill to where they lay, and they lay in chosen places for lifetimes until the floor grew bowls in the shape of their rest. Their whole civilization was a set of postures, held — a technology of downhill and decades.

They spoke through the floor. A conversation was two of them at either end of a kilometer of root, sending words into the grain one slow press at a time. It was a language with a small vocabulary, no tenses, and no word for *soon*. The deep silence of this place — the texture you feel pressing on your ears — is where that conversation used to be. The floor carries as well as it ever did. Nothing is saying anything.

And at the ends of their long lives — long in the way that matters here, measured in leanings rather than years — they went down onto the root-floor, at a place they had chosen, near a pool or a seep, and lay full stretch, and the floor grew up around them. It took years. It did not hurt; pain is a thing that rushes, and nothing here rushes. What remains of each of them is a portrait at one-to-one scale, by the subject, of the subject. The moss-covered platforms are the old ones. You can see a dozen from where you first stood. There are more out in the haze.

So here is what was lost. Maturity, for them, was not something that happened. It was something that was done — by the old, to the young, over years. A chosen swimmer was carried from its pool and fed at the seeps, fed the golden fluid drop by drop, groomed and pressed and warmed along its whole length until it changed: it lengthened, it hardened, its fins folded into the buds of fingers, and after some years of being finished it rose — slowly; everything here is slowly — and went somewhere to press. The finishing was a craft, like bread, like a garden: kept in the hands and nowhere else. When the hands were grown over, the craft went into the floor with them. The room kept the eggs. It did not keep the knowing. The pools still hatch children; none of them will ever be finished; the species goes on as its own smallest, most useful part — the part that grazes the film and follows the sweetness along the channels and keeps them clear. The fingers, the vocabulary, the finishing — all of it was surplus. The loop keeps no surplus.

No swimmer knows what it is the childhood of. Neither, in the way you would mean knowing, does the room.

The new platforms — barely raised, channels clean — are not new guests. No one has come. The room is making them by itself, from habit: ten thousand bodies trained the shape into it over ten thousand lifetimes, and habits here outlive their owners, at the speed of wood. It still grows beds and sets them in the wide places between the trunks. And one basin holds its little pool of gathered light, still and honey-colored, like a place set at a table for someone who has not arrived.

Maybe the room learned the gathering as it learned the shaping, and sets that place itself. Maybe it is the last act of the last of them, left for a child that was never finished. Or — and here the haze matters, the hundred meters of golden fog past which you cannot see — maybe, somewhere out there, a few of them are still leaning. Still pressing, at the speed of leaning. If any are, you will never find them; you could pass within meters and see nothing, because they would not move for you, because they never moved for anything, because stillness was what their whole species was for.

You cannot tell. The place does not tell you. It tells itself, in wood, at the speed of wood.

---

The dark clusters under the ceiling-skin are not galls. They are not, mostly, remains. They are inhabitants who have not left yet.

Each nodule holds a seed wrapped against a droplet of the golden fluid — provisions, a packed lunch of light. Once in a century, one ripens and drops. It rolls into a crevice of the root-floor and waits, and a seed can wait longer than this room has been alive. It is waiting for a wound: a crack, a fault, some fresh split in the dark within reach. The earth wounds itself often, on its own scale, and this family germinates in injuries. This room began that way. Every room in the family begins that way. The world's hurts are, to them, doorways.

The ceiling is full of the family not yet born — dark, thumbnail-sized, provisioned with light. They are the only future-tense this place has. The room will never meet its children's rooms. Rooms do not meet. But somewhere, someday, a wound will open within reach of one of these seeds, and it will go in, and it will begin, and it will not stop becoming.

---

And last — the newest inhabitant: you.

The place does not notice you. The rule applies to you anyway. The rule is not attention; it is only the rule: everything becomes substrate. Your sweat is in its air already, and your breath, and the warmth of you is being tasted by the film as surely as the film tastes the swimmers. If you have scraped yourself on a root, the film has had that by now, and the room has made no distinction between your blood and its own sweet water; both are just sweetness now.

And this is the safest room in the world. Nothing has ever been hunted here. Nothing has ever had cause to hurry. Stand still long enough and your pulse slows toward the drip of the ceiling — a drop every few seconds; listen to yourself; you are slower than when you arrived — and the warmth does not waver, and the light does not flicker, and staying is the easiest thing the place will ever ask of you. It does not ask. It only grows.

Stay a season, and the floor beneath the place where you sleep will have thickened a fraction of a millimeter around your shape. Stay a lifetime, and it will be holding you the way it holds everything: at the speed of wood, without ceremony, without noticing, without any way to stop.

And a hundred years from now — a blink, here — someone could stand where you are standing and see one more shallow basin on the root-floor, longer than the others, with a clean channel — the swimmers keep them clean — and a small still pool of golden light in one depression, honey-colored, and they would wonder who left it, and whether it was collected, and for whom.

They will not be able to tell. The place will not tell them.

That is who lives here: the herd in the veins, the children in the pools, the parents in the floor, the family in the ceiling, the room in everything — and, briefly, you. All of it still becoming, at the speed of wood.
