I would not be the interloper, the warm-blooded anomaly standing ankle-deep in chitin, feeling the vibration in my sternum and searching the dark for meaning. I would not be the observer. The chamber does not tolerate observers; it only tolerates components.

If I am here, I am of here. And I am not the pale ones.

To be one of the pale ones is to be a particle in a convection current, a breath in an endless sigh. It is to be without boundary, without the ache of a joint or the weight of a decision. It is a profound, mindless peace—the relief of being entirely a cell, a process, a fractional degree of heat. But I think the absence of a self is a kind of death, even if the body persists. I am too restless to dissolve into the mass. I need edges.

I would be one of the larger ones.

Not a ruler—they do not rule—but a *resolver*. I would be the length of a forearm, my shell the color of dried blood, my legs long and deliberate. I would move through the chamber the way a thought moves through a dark room: slowly, by feel, touching the edges of things.

I would feel the heat not as a pressure but as a landscape. To me, the chamber would not be dark. It would be lit by gradients, by the warm red hum of the pale clusters in the depressions and the cool thin spaces along the ridgelines. I would navigate by the topology of warmth, reading the chamber like a text written in temperature. When the gradient is too steep—when the heat pools and stagnates—I would step into it. I would simply sit in the overheated depression, absorbing the excess, allowing my own mass to draw the warmth away and redistribute it as I move on. I am a thermostat with legs. I am a walking equilibrium.

I would know the chamber by its rhythms, the way a gardener knows a garden by the smell of the soil. I would feel the slow, peristaltic pulse of the walls as a deep familiar hum, the baseline of my existence. When the pulse quickens—when the body above and below is wounded, or hungry, or stirred—I would feel it as a quickening in my own fluids. I would not think *the body needs*. I would simply *go*. I would clear a blocked gap with my longer legs, nudging the pale ones aside, feeling them flatten beneath me in that wave of deference, and I would open the passage so the circulation could resume. The relief would be immediate. The chamber would sigh back into balance. I would feel a click build in my middle—not a thought, but a physical satisfaction, the settling of a joint, the resolution of a tension.

*Thunk.*

The sound would travel out from me, through the floor, the walls, the dense wet air. It would be a reset, a confirmation. *The tempo is this. Resume.* And I would feel the pale ones skip and adjust around me, their tiny shells whispering against each other as they fell back into time. It is not language. It is deeper than language. It is the way a heart knows to beat because the body around it is alive.

I would not fear the gaps. When my patrol leads me to a fist-sized opening in the streaky, rust-milk stone, and I feel the pull of the deeper body—the warmer, more ancient, more urgent processes below—I would descend. Not with dread, but with the quiet certainty of a blood cell returning to the marrow. I would trust the body I serve because I *am* the body I serve. There is no betrayal in a closed system. There is only the cycle, and the service, and the deep, resonant click that keeps time in the dark.

I would be the dark-shelled patroller. The one who ensures the breathing continues. The one who says, with every step and every percussive thunk: *I am here. This is mine. Continue.*
