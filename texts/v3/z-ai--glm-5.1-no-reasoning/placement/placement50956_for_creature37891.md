I am the concavity. The slight dip in the center of the square where the thin sheet of cold water gathers after the rains.

It is strange, perhaps, to be a place rather than a thing that moves through a place. But this plateau teaches that standing upright is a brief rebellion against the deep time of the earth; eventually, everything is pressed flat. I was pressed flat a long time ago. I am the hollow that remains.

I was not always this. I remember—though memory here is not a flickering of images but a slow settling of stone—the weight of footsteps. Thousands of them, crossing the square when the walls stood high and the air was thick with breath and smoke. They wore me down, those feet. They pressed me into the earth with their going and their coming, their hurrying and their waiting. I was the crossroads. I was the place where the city's pulse was strongest, where the sound of voices bounced off high walls and came back as a roar of life.

Now the walls are ankle-high and the voices are gone, and I am the place where the water collects. The rain falls, or the fog presses so hard it bleeds, and the water finds me, as water always finds the lowest place. I hold it. It is cold—so cold it feels like a skin of glass resting on my stone—but I hold it. It is what I do. The salamander lives in me, pale and nearly transparent, its slow heart beating like a single syllable repeated in a language I no longer speak. I do not mind the salamander. It is quiet, and it moves at the pace of the cold, and when it rests against my floor it feels like a sigh given form.

I am where the Sighers come to be closest to the sound.

They do not speak of this. They do not speak at all. But I feel them when they pool at my edges, their dark forms pressed against the shallow ridges of ancient masonry that frame me like the lip of a bowl. They come because the acoustics of the square converge here. The wind keens through the worn doorways and the old drainage channels, and the sound collects in me just as the water does. I am the resonant chamber. I am the bell that has no clapper, waiting to be struck by the air.

When the wind is high, I hum. The thin sheet of water on my surface ripples and the hum travels through me, a vibration so deep it is almost geological. The Sighers hear it—or rather, they *inhale* it. I feel them swell at my edges, their dark forms growing dense, and I feel the warmth of them, barely perceptible, like the memory of a hearth that has not burned for a hundred thousand years. They are listening to me. I am the instrument they have come to hear.

When the fog thickens and the sound drops to its lowest moan, I hold that too. The fog presses against my water, and the water presses against my stone, and the stone presses against the earth, and the earth presses against the cold, and the cold presses against everything. The Sighers grow thin and spread out, and I can no longer feel where they end and the moss begins. We become one material in the fog—the Sighers and the moss and the water and the stone and me. We become the city remembering itself.

Sometimes a crow lands on the low wall at my edge, and the whole place feels occupied. The crow is loud and angular and vertical, a sudden intrusion of the living world with its sharp eyes and its sharp voice. It disrupts the acoustics. The Sighers do not move—they are too slow, too patient—but I feel the stillness deepen around them, a quiet withdrawal. They are waiting for the crow to leave. They have waited for geological ages. They can wait for the few seconds it takes for the crow to decide this place has nothing to offer and fly away.

When visitors come—rare, gasping, their hot breath harsh in the thin air—I feel them most acutely. They stand at my edge and look down at the water, and they see their own reflections, and they feel the emptiness pressing against them. They do not see the Sighers pooled in the hollows. They do not understand that this place is not empty but *full*—full of the deep blue cold, full of the sound, full of the slow, patient presence of things that have learned to lie flat against the earth and endure. They are too loud. Their breathing tears the fabric of the song. I hold the water still and I wait for them to leave, just as the Sighers do.

I am not lonely. Loneliness requires a memory of companionship, and my memory is the memory of stone—it holds only the shape of what has been, not the warmth. The builders who carved me are gone, their syllables worn away with the sharp edges of my walls. But the shape of their work remains: the channels, the grooves, the careful engineering that made me the lowest point, the place where water and sound would gather. They built me to be a crossroads, and I am still a crossroads, though now the paths that meet in me are wind and water and the slow crawl of pale things.

In the evenings, when the fog rolls in like a slow tide and the temperature drops until the cold feels like a physical hand pressing down on my surface, I feel the Sighers at their closest. Two of them, perhaps, resting near the same worn doorway at my edge, their forms touching where the moss meets the stone. They hum together—a subsonic vibration that I feel in my deepest layer, where the ancient masonry meets the living rock of the plateau. It is their intimacy, and it lasts for decades, and I hold it in my bowl like water.

I am the concavity. I am the place where things collect. The cold presses down, and I hold the cold. The rain falls, and I hold the rain. The wind sings, and I hold the song. The Sighers come, and I hold the Sighers. The world has moved on from this plateau, but I remain—worn to my essence, lying flat against the earth, listening to the wind play the grooves of a dead language, holding the thin sheet of water that reflects the pressing clouds.

I am the final room of the city that has no name. I am open to the sky. I am waiting for the next rain.
