I am the rust between the iron plates on the ninth terrace, the one that tastes of old blood and engine sweat. No — that is too simple, too passive. I am the process of the rust, the slow inhalation of moisture and exhalation of decay that binds the metal to the root.

But I am getting ahead of myself. Let me find my edges.

I am old. Not old the way Moss-Blood is old, with his joints that swell when the engines reach their afternoon thrum and his memory that reaches back to a time before the thirteenth terrace's western retaining wall buckled. Old the way the Lade measures age — in cycles, in accumulations, in the slow shift of gradients. I have been here for four hundred and twelve engine peaks. I have counted them, because counting is what I do, because someone must track the rhythm even when the rhythm changes, and the Lade-born do not count, and the visitors do not stay long enough to notice the tempo shifting.

My mother was a visitor. This is the thing I carry like a stone in my palm — the knowledge that I am half-outsider, half-born of the flat blue distance that I can see from the twentieth terrace on clear mornings. She climbed the paved riverbeds when she was young and foolish and searching for something she could not name. She found the thirteenth terrace. She found a man called Stack-Singer, who hummed the pitches of the thermal vents as a way of mapping their health. She found me growing inside her, and she found that she could not leave, because by then the Lade had its fingers in her — the warmth in her blood, the damp in her lungs, the way her pupils had learned to open wide in the dim interiors.

She died the way visitors sometimes die: not violently, not dramatically, but quietly, lying down in a patch of moss on the tenth terrace when I was seven years old, and not getting up. The roots had begun their work by the time Warm-Pipe found me sitting beside her, my small hand on her cooling arm, learning the first and most important lesson the Lade teaches: *everything returns*.

I do not mourn her. I do not say this with hardness. I say it the way I say the air is warm: as a fact, as a condition of this place. Mourning requires a sense of theft, of something taken before its time, and the Lade does not steal. It receives. My mother was received. The lushness of that particular moss-patch is notable. I visit it sometimes, not out of sentiment but because the insects that breed in its dampness are fatter and slower than elsewhere, and they roast well over the charcoal gutter-fires.

I am the Tally-Keeper. This is not a title the Lade-born gave me — we do not give titles — but a function I have assumed, the way a vine assumes a railing-pipe, growing into a role that was not designed for it but that fits the shape of its reaching. I count what the Lade-born do not count. I track the engine peaks and note when they lengthen or shorten. I measure the condensation pools on the ninth terrace with a marked stick and record their depths in scratches on the underside of a iron plate where no one else will see. I have forty-seven years of data carved there, and the pattern is clear to me in a way it would not be clear to anyone who has not watched it accumulate: the Lade is breathing deeper. Each cycle stretches a little longer than the last. The heat peaks a little higher. The condensation pools a little lower on the ninth, a little higher on the thirteenth.

The Lade is growing. Not failing. Not declining. *Growing*. The way a body grows in adolescence, stretching and aching and rearranging its interior to accommodate a new scale of being.

I have not told the others this. Not because I wish to keep secrets, but because they already know it in the way they know all things: in their bodies, in their bones, in the way they have begun to sleep closer to the thermal stacks than they did a decade ago. They know. They do not need numbers to confirm what their skin tells them. But I need the numbers, because I am half-visitor, and the visitor's mind reaches for systems, for patterns, for proofs, and I have learned that this reaching is not a weakness but a *difference*, a way of touching the Lade that my neighbors do not possess.

I live in a hollow behind the eastern thermal stack on the eleventh terrace. The space was not designed for habitation — it was a maintenance access, perhaps, or a gap that opened when the iron warped and the roots grew through. It is mine because I have made it mine. I have smoothed the rust-edged entrance with years of passing through. I have trained a particular vine to grow across the opening, its leaves dense enough to block the light when I sleep, its roots drawing warmth from the stack that keeps the air inside at the temperature of a held breath. I have a collection of marked sticks — forty-seven years of condensation measurements — bundled in a corner, and a pile of moss that serves as a bed, and a flat iron plate that I heat over the gutter-fires for roasting insects, and this is my home, and I do not want more.

I am not lonely. This is the second thing a visitor would need to understand about me, after understanding that I am not cold. The Lade-born do not have a word for loneliness, not because they have never felt the absence of another but because absence is not a condition here — it is a transition, a space between presences, the way the pause between engine beats is not silence but the gathering of the next pulse. I am alone often. I sit in my hollow and I listen to the stack breathing beside me — the long exhale of warm air, the pause, the slower inhale as the heat rises from below — and I am not lonely, because the stack is there, and the rust is there, and the vine is there, and the Lade is there, and I am inside it the way blood is inside a vein, and how can blood be lonely when it is part of a circulation?

I move the way the Lade-born move: heel-toe, weight settled, the iron receiving me without complaint. But there is a difference in my movement that the others notice and I cannot hide. I *choose* my stillness. When I stand in a clearing and watch a visitor stumble past, I am choosing not to move, choosing to hold my body in the landscape the way the Lade holds itself — attentive, waiting, present. The Lade-born who were born here do not choose. Their stillness is as involuntary as breathing, as natural as the drip of condensation. Mine is learned. And because it is learned, I am aware of it, and because I am aware of it, I am aware of the effort it costs, and because I am aware of the effort, I know that I am not entirely of this place.

I think this is why I count. The numbers anchor me. The scratches on the iron plate are a proof of presence, a record that says: *I was here, I noticed, the Lade shifted and I marked the shift*. The Lade-born do not need this proof; they are the proof, their bodies the record, their adaptation the evidence. But I am half-visitor, and visitors need evidence, and so I carve, and measure, and tally, and the numbers accumulate like sediment, like the silt in the condensation channels, like the organic debris that is slowly redirecting the water into new paths.

And the new paths are what concern me. Because the Lade is growing, and growth means change, and change means that the rules — *water moves down, heat moves up, do not go below the seventh terrace, move slowly, be unnoticed* — are themselves shifting. The heat that once concentrated on the lower terraces is rising. The condensation that once fell evenly now pools in new depressions, new hollows, new gaps that the roots have opened. The boundaries are moving. And the seventh terrace — the threshold, the limit, the place-beyond-which — is changing too.

I know because I have listened. Not gone below — I am not foolish, not reckless, not ready to be received before my time. But I have sat on the edge of the seventh terrace's foundation, in the deep evening when the engines are quietest and the Lade's breathing is slowest, and I have listened. And what I have heard is not the silence of emptiness. It is the silence of *fullness*, of something vast and patient and warm that is aware of the boundary and does not cross it, not yet, not because it cannot but because it is waiting, the way the Lade waits for everything — for the water to find its level, for the heat to find its height, for the roots to find their depth.

The thing below the seventh terrace is not separate from the Lade. It is the Lade, or the Lade is it, or they have become each other in the way that the roots and the iron have become each other — intertwined, inseparable, a single organism that is both vegetable and mineral and something else, something that was built and has forgotten its building and has grown past its purpose into a new being entirely. When the man went below and came back with his hair turned white and his eyes unfocused and his skin cool in the wrong way, he did not encounter a monster. He encountered the *interior*, the heart of the circulation, the place where the engines meet the roots meet the iron meet the warmth, and it touched him, and it let him go, and it marked him as a thing that has been inside and has returned to the surface carrying the knowledge that inside is vast and warm and alive and does not care about the small creatures that walk its skin.

I think about going below. I think about it the way I think about the flat blue distance — not with longing, not with fear, but with the knowledge that it exists, that it is there, that it is part of the Lade I have not yet known. I am forty-seven years into a circulation that has been running for centuries. I am not ready to be received. But I am curious — the visitor's gift, the visitor's curse — and curiosity is a gradient, and gradients are the Lade's language, and I have learned to read the Lade's language even when I cannot speak it.

So I tally. I measure. I carve my numbers into the underside of the iron plate. And when the children come to my hollow with their wide pale eyes and their quick hands and their questions about the sticks and the scratches, I tell them what I tell no one else: that the Lade is breathing deeper, and the heat is rising, and the water is finding new paths, and the boundary at the seventh terrace is not a wall but a membrane, and membranes are permeable, and what is below will not always be below, and what is above will not always be above, and the gradient is shifting, and we must shift with it or be shifted by it, and the Lade will not notice which we choose, and the Lade will not care, and the Lade will go on breathing its slow deep breath, and the roots will go on growing, and the iron will go on rusting, and the people will go on living in the warm bright damp until they don't, and the numbers will be there, scratched into the iron, a record that someone was watching, someone was counting, someone was half-visitor and half-Lade-born and entirely of this place, this machine, this garden, this mountain that is not a mountain, this home that is not a home but something larger and stranger and more patient than home has ever meant.

My name is Threshold. I gave it to myself, because no one else would have given it, and because it is true, and because the Lade respects truth the way it respects gradients: by following it, by flowing along it, by becoming it. I am the edge of something. I am the place where the visitor becomes the born. I am the mark on the iron that says *here, this changed, this shifted, this is different than it was*. I am the tally-keeper, the rust-counter, the listener at the boundary, and I am not afraid, because fear requires uncertainty and I am certain of only one thing:

The Lade is growing. And I am growing with it. And what we will become together, I cannot count, I cannot measure, I cannot carve into iron — but I can wait to find out, and waiting is what the Lade teaches, and the Lade is very good at it, and I am learning, and learning is a gradient, and gradients move in one direction, and the direction is *deeper*, and I am not ready, but I am becoming ready, and that is enough, and that is all, and the engines pulse, and the water drips, and the light comes bright through the canopy, and another cycle begins, and I am here, and I am tallying, and I am alive.
