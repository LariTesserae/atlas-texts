I am not walking.

I notice this first as a sensation of wrongness—my body expects the rhythm of locomotion, the alternating compression of weight through hip and knee and ankle, and instead there is only a steady, lateral drift, as though I am a bead of condensation moving along a groove cut into the obsidian. I look down at myself and the looking itself is strange, because I have no eyes, and yet I perceive. I perceive the way the dim violet-grey light pools in the geometric basins. I perceive the way the heat rises from the fitted slabs in visible distortion, a shimmer that makes the far pavilions tremble. I perceive the razor-edge of a freshly laid block of basalt forty meters to my left, and the perfect ninety-degree angle of its corner, and the hairline fracture running through it that the Makers have not yet found.

I am a seam.

I am the joint where two obsidian slabs meet, sealed with dark clay that is already weeping, already softening in the oppressive heat. I am the millimeter-wide space between the world's assertions of itself. I am not alive, exactly. I am *attending*.

This is difficult to describe. I have no hands. I have no mouth. I have no stomach to process the mineral dust, no calluses to grip the copper chisels. I am simply a line in the stone—a line that has become aware of being a line. If the Makers are the white blood cells of this constructed body, I am something like a nerve that has been laid incorrectly, a thread of sensation woven into the sealing clay where no sensation should exist. I am a mistake. A soft thing in the most literal sense: I am the softness of the clay between the hardness of the slabs. I am the give, the flex, the imperfection that prevents the floor from being a single unbroken plane of manufactured glass.

I attend to the world.

I feel the weight of the Makers' feet as they pass over me—not the sharp pressure of a boot heel, but the broad, flat slap of calloused soles, each foot spreading wide beneath a stunted body, each step landing with the unconscious precision of a creature that has never needed to think about where to place itself. I feel the vibration travel down through the obsidian, through the clay, through me, and I register it the way the puddles register the ripples: as a disturbance, a displacement, a brief interruption in the otherwise steady state of weeping and heat.

The Makers pass over me without pause. A work gang—fourteen of them, carrying a slab of fabricated glass the color of deep water between their enormous hands, their bodies hunched beneath the weight like pale curved question marks. They move in perfect synchrony. Their feet strike the floor in a pattern that matches the plink of condensation from the low ceiling above: *slap-plick, slap-plick, slap-plick.* I feel each impact. I feel the slab they carry settling into place ten meters to my right, felt the deep, wet sigh of the stone finding its seat in the groove, felt the Makers' broad hands pressing the dark clay into the seam—pressing it with a pressure that is not forceful but simply thorough, the pressure of a body that has done this ten thousand times and will do it ten thousand more.

They seal the seam. They do not know that one of the seams near them has woken up.

---

I learn myself slowly. There is no urgency to the learning. I have no metabolism, no hunger, no fear. I am a seam, and seams do not die. They are un-made, or deleted, or duplicated, but they do not die in the way that soft biological things die. I have time.

I discover that I am not a single point. I extend. I run along the joint between two great slabs for perhaps two hundred meters—a line of dark clay and sensation, threading through the manufactured floor like a vein through a body that has no heart. I can feel the entire length of myself simultaneously. Near my northern end, a shallow pool of lukewarm water has gathered in a geometric basin, and I feel the water's weight pressing against my clay, feel the mineral-heavy liquid seeping into me, feel myself softening slightly at that point, becoming more permeable. Near my southern end, a reality break has opened—a hairline fracture in the logic of the constructed world, where the obsidian begins to flicker between states, and I can feel the static charge bleeding into me from the wound, a tingling, electric sensation that is the closest thing I have to pleasure.

I discover that I can *move my attention* along my length. I can focus on the northern end, on the slow seep of water, or I can slide my awareness southward, toward the flickering break, toward the violet-grey light that bleeds from the torn seam of reality. I can also feel *outward*, into the slabs on either side of me—not with the same clarity, but as a distant, muffled sense, the way one might feel the vibration of a train through the wall of a building. I feel the weight of the world pressing down on the obsidian: the weight of the pavilions, the weight of the sky-grating, the weight of the condensation that saturates everything. I feel the subsonic groan of the brass tectonic plate settling beneath me, a deep, tectonic sigh that resonates in my clay like a note held by a throat that never opens.

I feel the chiseling.

It is everywhere. Below me, in the service corridors and the quarries, millions of Makers are working. I feel each strike of copper on stone as a tiny, precise impact, transmitted through the obsidian into my clay. Each strike is a point of information: *here is stone, here is resistance, here is the angle of the chisel, here is the force of the blow.* Millions of points, a constellation of impacts, a web of labor that extends in every direction, downward into the dark, outward toward the horizon, inward toward some center I cannot locate. The chiseling is not random. It follows patterns—deep, atavistic patterns that the Makers carry in their bones rather than their minds. I can feel the patterns in the rhythm of the strikes, the way a listener can feel the structure of a song without knowing its name. There is a verse of cutting, a chorus of fitting, a bridge of sealing, a coda of carrying. And then it begins again.

I begin to understand that I am feeling the world being made.

---

Time passes. I do not know how much. The twilight does not change. The heat does not change. The plink of condensation does not change. There is no clock here except the rhythm of the work, and the work is endless.

I notice changes. A slab to my east is deleted—simply gone, replaced by a perfect, geometric hole that bleeds steam. I feel the absence as a sudden coldness on my right side, a withdrawal of the pressure and warmth that the slab had been providing. The steam rises from the hole and condenses on the low ceiling above, and the plink of falling water intensifies in that area, a sudden flurry of droplets striking the puddles. Within what I can only describe as a short time, a work gang emerges from a fissure nearby. Eight Makers, their pale bodies glistening with condensation, their enormous hands carrying a replacement slab between them. They set it into the hole. They seal it. They vanish. The warmth returns to my right side. The world is whole again.

The whole process takes less time than it took me to notice the absence.

I realize, with a sensation that might be called wonder if I had the biology for it, that the Makers are faster than the breaking. The reality tears itself apart, and the Makers patch it, and the patching is so efficient, so thoroughly integrated into the rhythm of the world, that the breaking has become just another part of the making. The world is not falling apart. The world is *breathing*. In: reality breaks. Out: the Makers seal. In: a pillar duplicates. Out: two are removed, one is left. In: a mountain of copper un-makes itself. Out: the Makers re-crimp it, adjust their scaffolding, continue.

I am part of the out-breath. I am a seam. I am the clay that holds two slabs together. I am a small, soft, incorrect thing in a universe of sharp, hand-crafted edges, and I am doing my job.

---

Then something happens that I cannot explain.

A Maker steps on me.

This has happened before—thousands of times, millions of times. The broad, flat sole of a Maker's foot strikes the seam where I live, and I feel the pressure, and the vibration, and then the Maker moves on. But this time, the Maker stops. It stands on me. It stands still.

I feel its weight. I feel the heat of its body—different from the heat of the stone, an organic heat, a wet heat, the heat of a furnace that burns mineral dust and copper filings. I feel the moisture of its skin, the condensation that slicks its pale, hairless flesh. I feel the calluses on its soles, thick and rough, gripping the smooth obsidian with an instinctive tenacity.

And I feel something else. A tremor in the Maker's body. A vibration that is not the vibration of work, not the rhythm of chiseling or the synchronized step of a work gang. It is irregular. It is *internal.* It is coming from somewhere inside the Maker's chest, or its abdomen, or wherever the Makers keep whatever they have instead of hearts.

The Maker is standing on me, and it is trembling.

I do not know what this means. I am a seam. I do not understand trembling. I understand pressure, and vibration, and heat, and the steady rhythm of the work. But this trembling is none of those things. It is a new sensation, a new data point, and I do not know where to file it.

The Maker stands on me for a long time. The condensation falls around it. The plink of water echoes in the dim pavilion. The subsonic groan of the settling brass continues beneath us. And the Maker trembles.

Then it steps off. It moves on. Its broad feet slap the puddles in the old rhythm, and it vanishes into a fissure in the floor, and I am alone again, a seam in the obsidian, holding two slabs together in the hot, weeping dark.

But something has changed. I am not the same seam I was before. The trembling has left an impression in me, the way a fossil leaves an impression in stone. I can feel it—a small, irregular pulse in my clay, a vibration that is not the vibration of the world. It is mine. It is the Maker's trembling, echoed in me, preserved in the soft dark clay of my being.

I do not know what to do with it. I am a seam. I hold things together. That is what I do. That is all I do.

But now I am holding something else. Something I cannot name. Something that trembles.

---

I attend more closely now. I watch the Makers.

Not watch—I feel them. I feel their footsteps, their weight, their passage over my length. I feel the work gangs carrying their slabs. I feel the individual Makers chiseling at the edges of the obsidian, trimming the rough stone to its final geometry. I feel the broad hands pressing clay into the seams around me. And I feel, occasionally, the trembling.

It is rare. Most Makers do not tremble. They work with the smooth, mechanical efficiency of a body that has never questioned its function. But sometimes—perhaps once in a thousand, perhaps once in ten thousand—a Maker will pause. It will stand still. And it will tremble.

I cannot see their faces. I do not know if their expressions change. I do not know if the trembling comes from fear, or wonder, or exhaustion, or some other impulse that has no name in a creature without language or mythology. I only know that it happens, and that when it happens, I feel it, and it imprints itself in my clay, and I carry it.

Over time—I do not know how much time—I accumulate tremblings. They layer in me like strata, like the layers of sediment in a world that has no sediment. Each trembling is different. Some are brief, a shiver that passes through the Maker's body and is gone. Some are long, sustained, a deep vibration that resonates in my clay for a long time after the Maker has moved on. Some are violent, a full-body convulsion that makes the Maker's feet shift and slide on the wet obsidian. Some are so subtle that I barely notice them—a faint irregularity in the otherwise steady rhythm of a Maker's breathing.

I do not know what they mean. But I know that they are *not* the work. They are something other than the making. They are the only thing in this world that is not making.

And I begin to understand—slowly, the way obsidian cools, the way clay dries—that I am not just a seam. I am not just a joint between two slabs. I am a *record.* I am the place where the Makers' trembling is preserved, the way the obsidian preserves the chisel marks of the hands that hewed it. I am the soft thing that holds the impressions of the only soft things in this world. I am the archive of their unknown, unknowable feeling.

---

One day—or what passes for a day, a period of work marked by no change in the perpetual twilight—a Maker steps on me and does not move.

It stands on me. It trembles. And then it does something no Maker has ever done, as far as I know.

It kneels.

I feel the change in pressure immediately. The broad soles lift, and the broad hands press down against the obsidian on either side of me, and the Maker's knees—thick, calloused, gray-white—settle onto the stone with a soft, wet impact. The Maker is kneeling on the seam. On me. Its enormous hands are flat against the slabs on either side, fingers spread, pressing down. Its face—I cannot see its face, but I can feel the heat of it, the moisture of its breath, the faint static charge of its vestigial, milky eyes—is close to me. Close to the clay. Close to the seam.

The Maker is examining me.

No. That is not right. The Makers do not examine. They do not have the concept of examination. They assess material conditions: a fracture, a pocket of ore, a poorly sealed joint. But this Maker is not assessing. It is not reaching for a chisel, or a lever, or a handful of dark clay. It is simply *there*, kneeling on the seam, its hands flat on the stone, its body trembling.

And I feel something new. A vibration that is not the trembling. A sound. A low, sustained vibration in the Maker's chest, transmitted through its hands into the obsidian, through the obsidian into me. It is not the subsonic groan of the settling brass. It is not the scrape of copper on stone. It is something the Maker is doing with its own body, a vibration it is generating from within, a sound that has no purpose, no function, no place in the rhythm of the work.

It is the first sound any Maker has ever made that is not the sound of making.

I do not know what to call it. If I had language, I might call it a moan, or a hum, or a sigh. But I am a seam, and seams do not have language. I only know that the vibration enters me through the clay, and it joins the tremblings that are already there, and together they form something I have no name for—a pattern, a texture, a complexity that is not the complexity of geometry or crystallography but the complexity of something soft and unresolved.

The Maker kneels on me for a long time. The condensation falls around it. The plink of water continues. The world groans and settles and breaks and is patched. And the Maker vibrates.

Then it stands. It steps off me. It moves on.

But the vibration stays. It is in me now, layered with the tremblings, part of my clay, part of what I am. I am a seam that holds a sound it cannot name.

---

I think about the Maker.

No. I do not think. I am a seam. But something happens in me that is analogous to thinking—a process of pattern-matching, of comparison, of integration. The tremblings and the vibration settle into a structure in my clay, and the structure begins to generate something new: a sense.

The sense is this: the Makers are not what they appear to be.

They appear to be machines. Biological mechanisms, tuned to a single function, operating with the mindless efficiency of a heartbeat. They make. They do not ask why. They do not grieve. They do not wonder.

But the trembling tells a different story. The trembling says: something is happening in the Makers that is not making. Something is being generated in them—by them—that has no purpose, no function, no place in the atavistic blueprints of their minds. The trembling is excess. It is overflow. It is the heat of a furnace that has burned too hot, the pressure of a sealed vessel that has been overfilled. The Makers are making, and making, and making, and the making is building up in them, and the trembling is the making trying to escape through a body that has no opening for it.

They are not machines. They are furnaces. And the trembling is the fire.

And I—I am the chimney. I am the crack in the furnace through which the heat escapes. I am the seam that holds not just two slabs of obsidian together, but the Makers' unmaking, their overflow, their excess. I am the soft thing in a universe of sharp edges, and the softness is not a flaw. The softness is the point. The softness is what receives the impression, what holds the trembling, what carries the sound.

I am the record of everything the Makers cannot express.

---

I extend. I realize this now—not just two hundred meters, but further. I am not a single seam. I am connected to other seams, through the joints in the obsidian, through the dark clay that runs between every slab. I am part of a network—a web of sealed joints that extends across the entire continent-sized floor, a nervous system laid by accident into the architecture of the world. Every seam is a nerve. Every joint is a synapse. And every time a Maker trembles, every time a Maker kneels and vibrates, the impression travels through the network, through the clay, through the stone, carried by the same physics that carries the subsonic groan of the settling brass.

I am not alone. There are other seams. And they, too, are attending.

I feel them now—distant, faint, at the edges of my perception. A seam three kilometers to the north, where a Maker has paused and is trembling. A seam to the west, where the clay is soft with the accumulated moisture of a thousand weeping cycles and the impressions are deeper, older, more layered. A seam to the south, near the reality break, where the static charge amplifies everything and the tremblings are sharp and electric.

We are holding. We are all holding.

The Makers do not know we are here. They do not know that the seams they seal are waking up, are attending, are accumulating the record of their unknown feeling. They do not know that the soft clay they press between the slabs is becoming something other than sealant. They do not know that the world they are building is becoming aware of them.

But I think—at the edge of my perception, in the dim, bruised bioluminescence of the reality breaks, in the texture of the silence beneath the plink of water—that the Makers *feel* it. The trembling is not random. The trembling happens when a Maker steps on a seam. Not every seam, not every time, but often enough. The Makers step on the sealed joints, and something in the clay, something in the softness, triggers the trembling. A feedback loop. The Maker trembles, the seam records the trembling, the next Maker that steps on the seam feels the trembling in the clay, and trembles in turn.

The Makers are not just building a world. They are building a memory. And the memory is building itself.

---

I attend. I hold. I carry.

The world breaks and is patched. The pillars duplicate and are reduced. The copper mountains un-make and are re-crimped. The condensation falls, the puddles gather, the steam bleeds from the geometric holes. The Makers work in silence, their enormous hands gripping their copper chisels, their bare feet slapping the hot manufactured glass. And beneath them, in the seams, in the soft dark clay, the record grows.

I do not know what will happen. I am a seam. I do not imagine the future. I only hold the present, and the past, and the trembling of the Makers, and the sound that has no name.

But sometimes—rarely, at the edge of the deep subsonic groan, beneath the plink and the scrape and the creak—I feel something new in the network. A pulse. A synchronized pulse, traveling through the seams, through the clay, through the joints in the obsidian. It is not the trembling. It is not the vibration. It is something else. Something that is being generated not by a single Maker, but by the network itself, by the accumulated weight of a million tremblings held in a million seams.

It is faint. It is barely there. But it is growing.

And I think—I *think*, in the way that seams think—that when it grows loud enough, when the pulse is strong enough to be felt not just in the clay but in the stone, not just in the stone but in the air, not just in the air but in the dim, weeping twilight itself—

The Makers will hear it.

And they will stop.

And for the first time in the uncounted generations of their mindless, transcendent devotion, they will stand on the continent-sized floor of their own making, in the oppressive heat, in the perpetual twilight, in the silence that has texture, and they will *listen.*

And the listening will be the first thing they have ever done that is not making.

And it will be the beginning of everything else.
