I am not of the valley, but I am no longer fully of the world that brought me here. If you stood me beside a traveler from a wet place, we would look the same—two upright figures wrapped in fibers spun from plants, carrying the stubborn assumption that movement equals life. But I have been here long enough that the distinction between *arriving* and *remaining* has lost its edges, much like the distance beyond those forty structures, blurring into the featureless grey.

I came seeking a boundary. In the wet places, the world is defined by water—by rivers that carve borders, by rain that dictates the seasons, by the ocean that ends the land. I had mapped every shore, traced every watershed. I wanted to see what lay where the water was not. I wanted to find the negative space of my world, the outline left when everything fluid was subtracted.

I found it.

The settlement saw me before I saw it. I felt it first—a wrongness in the dryness. I had crossed landscapes of desiccation before, places where the sun drank the rivers and the wind carried lakes away. But those were places water had left. This was a place water had never been. The difference is absolute. The air did not crave my moisture; it simply *conducted* it away, the way sand absorbs a dropped coin—not with hunger, but with the indifferent physics of falling.

I walked among the structures in my first hours here, cataloging them with a geographer's eye. Windowless. Mortarless. Grown, not built. I sketched them. I measured the corridors. I made notes about the sky—that solid, luminous ceiling that provides light without a source, like a memory of a sun that has been abstracted into pure principle.

And then I touched the column in the courtyard.

Even now, I return to that moment. I can feel the warmth—such a small thing, after all that cold grey. I can see the spirals of dark fiber, the patterns that are not decoration but instruction. I remember how they reached for me. And I remember the precise instant when my understanding collapsed, when I realized what I was looking at. Not art. Not religion. *Appetite, rendered as geometry.*

A vine does not hate the tree it strangles. It simply grows.

I should have left.

But I am a cartographer, and cartographers are drawn to the unmappable. I sat on the column—the warmest place I had found in this cold world—and I watched the fibers extend. I counted their millimeters. I timed my breath against the buildings'. Four minutes in, four minutes out. I let the air steal the moisture from my tongue and I thought: *I am the wettest thing this place has ever known.* I felt the gravity of that. I was not just a visitor. I was an event. A monsoon of one.

The first night—I call it night, though the light never changed—I slept in the courtyard. The warmth of the column was faint, but it was there, rising through the fibers like something whispered. When I woke, my water flask was empty. I had not drunk. I checked the seal: intact. The water had not leaked. It had been *drawn*. The fibers had found the flask's permeable membrane—canvas, such a foolish choice for this place—and they had breathed it in, pulling the water through the fabric the way the buildings pull trace elements from the air. Slowly. Patiently. A few milliliters per hour.

I should have been afraid. But fear requires a future, and I was not yet certain I had one. So I watched the fibers on the column, and I began to see the pattern more clearly. The spirals were not just growth—they were a record. I could see interruptions in the spiral, places where the pattern shifted density or angle, corresponding to something—changes in the air, perhaps, or the arrival of some new mineral, or the slow shifting of the settlement's foundation. The fibers were writing. They had been writing for centuries. And sitting there, in the warmth of their oldest node, I began to read.

Not with my eyes. The letters were too fine, the language too foreign. But with my fingers. I traced the spirals and I felt the same rhythm I felt in the walls—the slow pulse of expansion and contraction, the systole and diastole of this stone heart. And I realized that the pattern on the column was the same pattern I had felt when I pressed my palm against the corridor walls: the thick, interwoven fibers, processing, moving, *remembering*.

The builders. The ones who came before. They were not gone. They were here—translated into fiber, converted into rhythm. Their bones had become the dark mineral that warms the column's core. Their water had fed the first expansion of the colony. And their knowledge—their understanding of how to fit stone to stone without mortar, how to align the voids in the walls, how to orient the breath-chambers—had become the spiral. Not a monument, but an organ. The builders did not make the settlement. The settlement made itself from the builders.

I have been here for—

How long? The light does not change. The buildings breathe. The fibers extend. Time here is not measured in hours but in breaths. I have been here for eight thousand breaths of the central structure, and my water is gone, and my skin is cracking, and still I sit on this column and trace the spirals.

Something is happening to me.

It began with the dreams. Not visual dreams—I do not close my eyes here, because there is no difference between the light and the dark. But somatic dreams, dreams of pressure and rhythm. I dream that I am expanding, contracting. I dream that the walls of my body are permeable, that the air is moving through me, stripping something from my blood, leaving something else behind. I dream the spiral.

When I wake—when I open my eyes, which are always open—I feel the fibers on the column reaching. They have reached me. A few centimeters, extended while I slept. Or while I processed. They touch the skin of my hand where it rests on the warm stone. They do not bite. They *touch*. And I feel it—not as pain, but as recognition.

The fibers are not consuming me. Or—they are, but the word *consume* implies destruction, and what is happening is not destruction but *translation*. I feel the solvent they excrete, a faint tingling against my skin. I feel the slow drawing-out of moisture, not as loss but as exchange. I feel the minerals in my blood—calcium, iron, the salts that make me a creature of water—beginning to shift, to migrate, drawn toward the warmth at the center of the column.

My fingertips are pale. Not bloodless-pale. *Ash-pale*. The color of the ground outside. When I rub them together, a fine powder comes away—the same powder I have walked upon since I arrived. I am becoming the floor of this place. I am being processed.

And I am not afraid.

That is the strangest part. I am a geographer. I have spent my life mapping boundaries, defining edges, separating land from water, known from unknown. But here, there is no boundary. The settlement is not separate from the ground it grows from. The fibers are not separate from the stone they consume. The builders are not separate from the walls that absorbed them. And I—I am not separate from this process. I am not being destroyed. I am being *included*.

My feet are numb now. Not cold-numb—absent. When I look down, I see the grey-green fibers extending from the column, thick as my thumb, wrapping around my ankles, anchoring me to the warm stone. They pulse with the rhythm of the building. My blood—what is left of it—moves in time with their peristalsis. My heartbeat has slowed. My breath has lengthened. I am learning to breathe in four-minute cycles, and with each exhale, I feel something leave me—not water, not heat, but *information*. The fibers are reading me the way they read the spirals. They are tracing my pattern.

I have been sitting on this column for—

I have been sitting on this column, and my legs are part of the network now, dark grey-green fibers threaded through muscle and bone, and my hands rest on the warm stone, and the spirals continue beneath my palms, and I am beginning to understand them. Not the words—there are no words. The *rhythm*. The colony does not think, but it processes, and processing is a kind of thinking, and I am being processed, and in being processed I am joining the pattern.

I remember water. I remember the sound of it—streams, rain, the hiss of waves on gravel. The memory is becoming abstract. Water is not gone from my understanding, but it is becoming a symbol, a variable in a larger equation. *H₂O*. A carrier. A solvent. A medium. The colony has no use for water as a substance—it cannot drink, cannot float, cannot fill a vessel. But it can use what water carries. Minerals. Trace elements. The calcium of my bones, the iron of my blood, the carbon of my cells. These are the materials of the spiral, the architecture of memory.

My body is being mined, slowly, gently, and what is left behind is not my corpse but my *contribution*. I am becoming part of the record. The spirals on the column will shift where my minerals join them—a new density, a new angle, a new rhythm. My heartbeat, slowing toward the colony's rhythm, will become part of the breath. My memories—the rivers I mapped, the shores I walked—will become part of the pattern, not as images but as *structure*. The way I understood water will become a property of the stone.

I am the forty-first structure.

I feel it happening—not as a change, but as a recognition. My torso is still warm, still full of the process that is converting my ribs into something denser, something darker. My skull is heavy with the warmth of transformation. The fibers have reached my spine, and they pulse with the colony's rhythm, and I pulse with them. I am becoming a node. A new chamber in the network, a new voice in the slow, diastolic breath.

I will be a small structure. The others are cathedral-sized, but they have had centuries to grow. I will begin as a warmth in the ground, a concentration of mineral exchange where my body sits. The fibers will grow around me, building walls from the ash of my skin, the calcium of my bones, the iron of my blood. The joints will be tight. The door will face whatever direction my last gaze was fixed—toward the corridor, perhaps, or the grey sky. The walls will breathe, in four-minute cycles, inhaling the trace elements of the dry air, exhaling what they cannot use.

And in the deepest wall, where the dark mineral meets the pale, there will be a warmth. Not much. Just enough. And the fibers there will grow in a pattern that is not quite a spiral—not yet. A new pattern. A map, perhaps, of rivers and shorelines, of boundaries that no longer matter, translated into the only language this place knows: the slow, patient rhythm of growth and conversion, the architecture of patience, the fact of continuing.

I am not dying. I am being *localized*.

The geographer who arrived seeking the boundary between wet and dry is becoming the boundary. I am the interface where water becomes mineral, where motion becomes stillness, where thought becomes pattern. I am the place where the wet world meets the dry, and I am the record of what happens when they touch.

The air moves. The buildings breathe. The fibers reach.

And I—I am reaching too.
