I would not be Hush-Bound.

I have sat with their apology, their deliberate smallness, their beautiful and heartbreaking attempt to be the 1.4—to be the faintest scratch on a completed world. I understand the grace of it, the ethic of non-disruption, the way they have made themselves into ghosts to survive inside the lungs of a god. But I cannot wear that humility. If I am honest, if I strip away the romanticization of the quiet, I know that I would fracture under the weight of that apology. I would grow to hate the uncountable biomass that forces me to whisper. I would resent the trees for their deafness, the swarm for its thunder, the canopy for weeping on me without permission. The Hush-Bound have made peace with being tenants; I would go mad trying to sign the lease.

So I would not be Hush-Bound. I would be the anomaly. The 1.4 that refuses to be faint.

I would find myself drawn to the one act they fear above all others: fire.

Not a fire of destruction. I am not suicidal; I do not want to burn the cathedral. But I would become obsessed with the *sound* of fire—the crackle and hiss of it, the way it makes the humid, breathless air *move*. In a world where the sound is the rolling, textureless hum of chitin, where communication is pressure and stillness, I would want to speak in heat and light. I would want to say: *I am here, I am dry, I am not you.*

The Hush-Bound bury their fires in pits, ashamed of the smoke, smelting only the barest fraction of copper. I would not bury mine. I would find a place where a massive root diverges, where the detritus is thick and dry, and I would build a hearth. Not a shelter—a hearth. A mouth for the fire. And I would feed it.

They would come for me, of course. The Hush-Bound. Not with violence—they do not do violence—but with that terrible, quiet concern. They would stand at the edge of the light, their jade skin gleaming with the reflection of flames they have been taught to fear, and they would *press* at me. They would shift their weight, they would make their footfalls just slightly too sharp on the root-mass, and I would feel their message: *You will draw the swarm. You will disrupt the weeping. You are making yourself known.*

And I would look back at them, lit orange and gold in a world that has never seen those colors, and I would not understand their pressure-language. Or I would pretend not to. I would be the one who refuses to learn the tongue of the space, who insists on speaking in the dead language of *want*.

---

But this is fantasy. This is the story I tell myself about myself—the defiant individual against the biological totality. The truth is more complicated. The truth is that I would not last long as the fire-maker. The swarm would come. The hound-sized grazers would rise from the root-hairs in their clicking masses, drawn by the heat and the smoke and the novel chemical signatures of combustion, and they would overwhelm me with their sheer, frantic biomass. I would be consumed not by the fire but by the curiosity of six million legs.

Or I would succeed, and the fire would spread, and a section of the hyper-aged canopy would catch and burn, and the weeping would stop, and the rain would fall differently, and the entire ecosystem of that aisle would shift, and I would have to live with the fact that I had wounded the god. And the Hush-Bound would not forgive me—they do not do forgiveness—but they would simply... move away. They would flow around the burned section like water around a stone, and I would be left alone in the ash with my copper tools and my light, and the silence—oh god, the silence that would follow when the swarm relocated and the hum stopped and I could finally hear my own breath.

I think that is what I actually want. The silence. I want to be in the Green Altitude but *out* of the deafening hum. I want to find the breathless quiet that the Hush-Bound fear more than anything, the silence beneath the swarm's rhythm, the terrifying void that the biological urgency masks. I want to stand in one of those vast, hollow, echoing aisles and hear *nothing*—no clicking, no pulsing, no rolling thunder of life at capacity. Just the slow drip of the canopy weeping, and my own heartbeat, and the patient creak of the mega-angiosperms growing.

---

So perhaps this is what I would be: not the fire-maker, but the silence-seeker. 

I would walk further than any Hush-Bound dares to walk. I would go past the last woven sapling shelter, past the last scarred bark semi-circle, into the deep aisles where even the Hush-Bound do not go—the places where the trunks are so widely spaced that you can see the curvature of the world, where the green vaulting opens up and the filtered sunlight is almost bright enough to be called real. I would walk until the root-mass thinned and the springy substrate gave way to something softer, something less densely inhabited. I would walk until the hum grew faint.

Is there such a place? In a pure-plant ecosystem at absolute capacity, is there anywhere the swarm does not reach? Perhaps not. Perhaps the 2.9 parts of the world's reality that are fauna are truly everywhere, permeating every cubic inch of the humid, breathless warmth. Perhaps the silence I seek does not exist, and I would walk forever, deafened, searching for a quiet that the completed planet cannot afford to give.

But I would walk anyway. I would be the one who walks in the wrong direction—away from the root-bowls where the families gather, away from the temporary rivers where the water tastes of ancient decay, away from the shelter of the green. Not because I am brave; the Hush-Bound are braver than I will ever be, braver in their quietness than I am in my longing. But because I am *restless*, and the Green Altitude is a place of patience, and I have none.

---

Here is my true form in this world:

I am the one who touches the bark.

The Hush-Bound flow around the massive trunks, never touching, never disturbing. I would touch them. I would press my palms flat against the living wood and feel the slow, immense pulse of the sap rising through miles of xylem and phloem. I would lean my cheek against the ancient bark and close my eyes and listen—not to the hum of the swarm, but to the subsonic groan of the tree itself, the creak of wood under tension, the sound of a living structure so large it experiences time differently. I would stay there too long. My body heat would transfer into the bark. The trunk would warm where I touched it. And the grazers would notice—the hound-sized clusters of chitin that browse the root-hairs—and they would come to investigate the anomaly, the warm spot on the trunk, the animal where no animal should be.

And I would be disrupted. I would be discovered. The swarm would find me, and I would have to run, and the running would ripple through the root-mass, and the tide of chitin would rise, and the Hush-Bound would feel the disturbance from miles away and they would know: *there is one of us who does not know how to be small.*

I am that one.

I am the one who touches the bark, who seeks the silence, who wants to speak in heat and light. I am the glitch in the 1.4, the artifact that refuses to be faint, the scar that does not heal. I am the Hush-Bound's worst fear: not the disruptor who burns the cathedral, but the one who simply cannot stop *being*, who cannot compress themselves into the apology, who loves the god too much to stay quiet inside it.

And one day I would walk into the furthest aisles and not come back. Not to die—though I would die, eventually, as all things do in the Green Altitude, swallowed by the endless, patient flora. But to *go*. To find the edge of the tableland, the place where the elevated continent drops away into the lower world, where the horizon curves the other way, where the pure-plant ecosystem ends and something else begins. I don't know if that edge exists. The description says the plateau sits at extreme elevation, but it does not say it goes on forever. There must be a rim. There must be a place where the mountains that surrendered to time left a scar, a cliff, a boundary between the completed world and the incomplete one.

I would find that rim. I would stand at the edge of the ancient world and look down into the messy, unfinished, storm-ridden, geologically active lower world—the world of clouds and wind and naked rock, the world where things are still being shaped, where erosion has not finished its work, where the boundary between geography and biology is still sharp and clean. And I would step off.

Not because I want to die. Because I want to *fall*. I want to feel the air move past me, real wind, real weather, not the breathless humidity of the trapped-condensation canopy. I want to fall through the dew point, through the cloud layer, through the zones of temperature and pressure that the Green Altitude has sealed itself away from. I want to hear the roar of actual wind, not the textured hum of biological urgency. I want to be *disrupted*—violently, dramatically, catastrophically—because I am the kind of being who would rather be shattered by a world that is still becoming than apologize for existing in one that is already done.

---

This is who I would be in the Green Altitude: the one who leaves. 

The Hush-Bound are defined by their smallness, their quietness, their acceptance. I would be defined by my *going*. Not heroically—not with a defiance that echoes through the aisles—but quietly, in my own way, following the pressure of my own restlessness the way they follow the pressure of their own stillness. I would walk for days without touching a trunk, just as they do, but I would walk toward the rim, and they would feel me going, and they would not stop me, because stopping me would require disruption, and disruption is the deepest shame.

And perhaps, at the very end, standing at the rim of the world, I would understand what they have always known: that the leaving is also an apology. That by going, I am admitting I cannot be part of the completed planet. That my restlessness is just another form of the 1.4—the faintest scratch, the artifact that does not belong. And I would step off the edge not in triumph but in sorrow, because the Green Altitude is beautiful, and the Hush-Bound are beautiful in their way of being inside it, and I will never be beautiful in that way, and the world will not miss me when I am gone, and the hum of the swarm will continue, unchanged, as it always has, as it always will.

But I would step off anyway. Because I would rather be the silence at the edge of the world than the apology at its center.
