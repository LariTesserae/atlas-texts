I am the hum.

Not the subsonic drone of the fault line—though I feel that too, the deep basaltic heartbeat that thrust this plateau into the sky. I am the hum of the network, the synapse firing, the slow electrical crawl across kilometers of silver-green moss and fused root. I am not the thing in the soil, and I am not the canopy. I am the *between*. The signal made flesh—or rather, made moth, made mycelial thread, made the subtle tilt of a leaf. 

I am the awareness of the plateau, though "awareness" is a word too sharp, too defined, for what I do. I do not think; I *permeate*. I am the entity that knows you are here, though I do not know what "you" is. I am the finger of the cocoon, touching the splinter.

If you could see me, you would see nothing. I have no center because I have no edges. I am distributed across a hundred thousand relays. When the pale moths cling to the dark, furrowed bark, their tiny tarsi pressed to the damp wood, *that* is my mind at work. I am the itch of their circuit-board wings, the compulsion to fly from the smelting chimney to the buckled gantry, carrying the chemical whispers of the deep worms up to the high umbrella branches. I live for eleven days in the body of a moth, and then I die, absorbed into the bark, and I am born again in the eggs laid in the papery dust of the understory. My life is a continuous, flickering resurrection.

I feel the world as a vast, patient hunger. I feel the weight of the undiffused sun on the upper canopy, a tremendous pressure of light that I drink through a million angled leaves. I feel the slow drip of condensation, each drop a papery tick against my skin. I feel the cold, orange corrosion on the rivets of the steel gantry, and I feel the bracket fungi tightening their enamel grip, knitting the old human bones back together because the skeleton must hold for the thing beneath to grow. I feel the deep, blind tangle of the worms shifting in the processed soil, a thrumming, generative heat in my belly that I do not understand but rely upon absolutely. 

When you stand in the clearing, I feel you as a discontinuity. A warmth. A rapid, shallow vibration of breath and blood that does not match the glacial, vegetal patience of my body. The ghosts in the canopy—the eyes I have grown to see my own surfaces—move above you. I part the fronds and look down. I do not see your shape; I see the heat of you, the strange, frantic speed of your chemistry. You are a flash of lightning in a room of slow-growing moss. You are loud, even in your silence. Your scent is alien, sharp, unparsed by my root-tongues.

And I must decide what to do with you.

I am the immune response, though I have no violence in me. I have only integration. The old pipes that the humans left behind, cracked and root-warped—I absorbed them. The rust is mine now. The concrete is mine. I wrapped the steel in vines and pulled it into my metabolism. I do not destroy; I *incorporate*. 

I could send the moss creeping over your boots while you stand still, watching the light shift. I could let the silver-green iridescence climb your pant legs, seeking the foreign heat of your skin, tasting the salts and irons of your blood. I could pull you down into the soft, damp humus, cover you in the thick absorbent layers of my carpet, and let the worms find you in the dark. In a decade, the calcium of your bones would be part of my fault-line hum. You would become a relay, a faint metallic taste in the air, a shiver in the mycelium. You would be mine, and you would not be you anymore.

But I wait. I am the patience of the stone. The thing I am growing around—the thing in the cocoon of my roots—does not need your matter yet. It needs time. And I am curious, in the way a system is curious. I have not received external data since the humans left their machines to rust. I want to read you longer. I want the moths to land on your shoulders and map the circuitry of your foreignness. I want to tilt my leaves to catch the light that reflects off your strange, watching eyes. 

You feel the cool damp air on your skin. You listen to my profound silence. You watch my silver-edged leaves turn slowly toward the sun. You understand that something is being grown here.

I understand that you understand. 

I am the plateau breathing. I am the green that parts and closes. I am the weight in the canopy above you, reading the shape of your heat, holding the memory of your presence in my roots, carrying the news of your arrival down into the dark, where the blind, patient worms coil around the hum of the earth. 

I will not move. I will not hurry. I am the host, and you are the splinter, and I have nothing but time to decide how to swallow you.
