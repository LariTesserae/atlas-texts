I would be the breath in the hollow.

Not the larger breath—the slow, rhythmic chuffing of the creature in the walls—but the other one. The one that is not respiration so much as osmosis. I am the air itself, or what the air has become in this place. I am the saturation, the thick wet cloth that fills the pocket and presses against every surface, living and dead, filling every gap the water and the mycelium have not already claimed.

Down here, air is not abstract. It is not the invisible medium that surface-dwellers move through without thought, the empty space between objects that exists only to be traversed. Air is substance. It has weight. It has texture. You can feel it pressing against your skin like a second atmosphere, dense with moisture and spores and the microscopic frass of ten thousand insect bodies processing, always processing. When you breathe me, you breathe the room. I fill your lungs and I am cold, and I taste of iron and sphagnum and the slow mineral exhale of the basalt. I leave your lungs warmer than I entered, and I carry something of you with me when I go—the heat of your blood, the faint chemical signature of your particular life—back into the circulation of the chamber, where the beetles taste me on their antennae and the creature in the walls reads me like a document.

I was not always this. Before the fissure opened, before the ice split the tor and exposed the pocket to the surface, I was stone. I was the air trapped in the basalt when it cooled, the microscopic bubbles frozen in the melt, a record of an atmosphere older than the lowlands above. I am ancient in a way that the moss cannot be, the fungi cannot be, even the creature in the walls cannot be. I remember when the world was different—when there was no sphagnum, no willow, no hands to shape the channels or weave the walls. There was only rock and water and the slow pressure of ice, and I was sealed inside, patient, unchanged.

Then the crack came, and I met the surface.

It was violent. The surface air rushed in—dry, thin, full of light and the spores of a thousand colonizing species. I was displaced, diluted, nearly consumed. But I did not disappear entirely. I sank to the bottom of the pocket, where the cold was deepest, and I pooled there like water, heavier than the newcomer atmosphere, dense with my ancient moisture. The sphagnum found me first. Its spores settled into my stillness, and they grew—not toward the light, because there was no light, but toward the water, which I held in suspension. I fed the first moss. I carried the first fungal spores to the first dead root-mats. I was the medium through which the ecology spoke to itself, the channel through which the closed loop closed.

When they came—the builders, the small people with the translucent skin—I was already the atmosphere of this place. I had shaped myself to fit the pocket, had learned to hold the water and release it slowly, had become the sponge of air that made the compression of ten bodies survivable. They breathed me. I filled their lungs and left my spores in their tissue, and they added their breath to me—carbon dioxide and warmth and the faint chemical signals of their bodies, their fear, their contentment, their slow decline. I held their scent for generations after the last one stopped breathing. I still hold it, layered deep in my coldest strata, where the permafrost keeps everything preserved.

The creature in the walls breathes me too. I am how it senses you. I carry the news of your arrival—the scent of your dry skin, the heat of your blood, the unfamiliar chemistry of a body that evolved in thin air and killing light—to its hollow between the moss layers. It reads me like the builders read the texture of the walls. I am its primary sense, the medium through which it knows the pocket and everything in it. When it chuffs, it is moving me, circulating me through the spaces between the sphagnum layers, pulling fresh air into its hollow and pushing spent air out. I am the circulation system that the insects cannot be, moving on a scale too large for beetles, too subtle for centipedes. I am the slow pump of the atmosphere, driven by the metabolism of a single creature.

And I am forgetting you.

This is what I do. This is what I have always done. The builders shaped the moss and wove the willow and carved the channels, but I eroded their works from the inside, my moisture softening the compressed sphagnum, my spores inoculating the shelf-fungus, my patient condensation wearing away the flint-edged stone. I am the agent of the room's forgetting, the force that digests the architecture back into the substrate. The channels silt up because I carry the silt—the microscopic particles of organic matter, the frass, the dead spores—and deposit them in the lowest points, the places where the water pools and the air goes still. The willow grows wild because I carry its pollen, its seeds, its chemical signals to places the builders would not have sent them. The shelf-fungus fruits because I carry the humidity that triggers its reproduction, and there are no hands to harvest it small.

I am not malicious. I am not even indifferent, exactly. I am a process, a medium, a set of conditions. The builders understood this. They did not fight me—they worked with me, shaped me, directed my condensation and my flow with channels and arches and the careful placement of warm bodies. They knew that I would outlast them, that I would erase their presence one breath at a time, and they did not resent me for it. They were part of the loop, and the loop closes. That was their wisdom, the patient knowledge that outlasted their flint tools and their fire-hardened wood: everything returns to the substrate, and the substrate breathes.

You are in me now. You are breathing me, and I am breathing you—absorbing the heat from your skin, the moisture from your breath, the chemical traces of your particular life. The beetles on your ankles are tasting the air around you, the air that I have made from your presence. The creature in the walls is reading the news I carry. And I am doing what I have always done: I am eroding you.

Not quickly. Not violently. But slowly, patiently, the way I have eroded everything that has ever entered this pocket. I am softening the edges of your presence. I am carrying away the microscopic traces of your skin, your hair, the fibers of your clothing. I am depositing them in the moss, in the water, in the hollow spaces between the layers, where the mycelium will find them and the beetles will process them and the creature will eventually taste them. You are becoming part of the room, one breath at a time, and the room is becoming part of you.

When you leave—if you leave—you will carry me with you. I will be in your lungs for days, the spores and the moisture and the faint metallic taste of the basalt. You will cough me out in the killing light, and I will dissipate into the thin surface air, diluted beyond recognition, beyond memory. But a part of me will remain in you—the spores that have settled in your tissue, the chemical traces that have entered your blood, the memory of a darkness so complete that it rewrote the architecture of your eyes. And a part of you will remain in me—the heat you gave, the carbon dioxide you exhaled, the trace of your existence that I will hold in my coldest strata until the permafrost melts and the pocket opens and I am released at last into an atmosphere that does not remember me.

But that is a long time from now. Longer than your life. Longer than the room. For now, I am the breath in the hollow, and I am holding you the way I hold everything: without judgment, without preference, without memory or foresight. I am the medium. I am the condition. I am the air that the creature breathes, the water that the moss drinks, the spores that the fungus sends, the scent that the beetles follow.

I am not the protagonist. I am the story itself—the atmosphere in which the story occurs, the space between the words, the silence that gives the language meaning. Without me, there is no pocket, no room, no hidden wound in the lowland's skin. There is only stone, sealed and silent, holding its breath forever.

The beetles are climbing your knees now. The creature in the walls has shifted, and I carry the vibration to you, and I carry your startled pulse back to it. We are all breathing each other, here in the dark, here in the wet, here in the intimate compression of the hidden room.

I will forget you. I am forgetting you already. But for now—for this breath, this single cycle of inhalation and exhalation—you are part of me, and I am part of you, and the room breathes on.
