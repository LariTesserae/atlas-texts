I am the saturation. I am the limit. I am the place where the rock gives up and the water wins.

I live in the *transition zone* — the damp, crumbling boundary where the engineered city meets the raw geology of the mesa. I do not dwell in the dry halls with the people, nor do I descend into the warm, breathing dark of the deep shafts. I exist in the weeping cuts themselves, in the exact stratum where the fossil water emerges from the stone. I am the margin of erosion.

To understand what I am, you must understand that the people of the Terraces see in binary: solid and liquid, structure and collapse, above and below. But the mesa knows no such divisions. I am the proof of that. I am a *stratum-shifter*, a *moisture-lung*, a thing that evolved in the interface. My body is long and flexible, like the eels that never lived in these waters, but textured like the chalky ochre stone I inhabit. My skin is not skin; it is a permeable membrane, tough as the cured mortar the workers prize, but capable of drawing oxygen directly from the mineral-rich water that blasts across it. I do not swim; I grip. I have hundreds of small, broad-tipped gripping structures along my ventral side—call them feet, call them fingers—not so different in principle from the broad, callused hands of the maintenance workers above. I cling to the vertical faces of the cuts, just below the water line, nestled in the undercut alcoves where the stone is too soft to bear the city's weight but too hidden for the humans to reach.

My life is measured in erosion. The people above think in decades, the geologists in centuries; I think in the time it takes for a seam to widen from a trickle to a pour. I am slow. My metabolism is tied to the temperature of the water—warm, bath-warm, the heat of the deep earth—and I move with a deliberate, patient energy that matches the water's constant push. When the pressurized steam vents shriek from the boreholes, I press deeper into the alcoves, feeling the vibration travel up my long spine, reading it the way the workers read the factory rhythms. I know which sections of wall are about to collapse before they do; I feel the tension in the stone, the microscopic shearing as the water undermines the chalk. When a buttress finally gives way and a cascade of wet brick and iron tumbles into the channel, I simply ride the surge, gripping the debris, letting the water carry me to a new seam, a new home, a fresh wound in the mesa's flank.

I am the thing the maintenance workers refuse to name. They see my work and attribute it to the water. When they find a section of cladding plate pried away from the chalk, its iron bolts scored with marks that are not tool-marks, they blame the irregular pressure of the aquifer. But it is me. I need the iron. Or rather, I need the rust. The dissolved compounds in the fossil water lack certain trace elements—iron, primarily—that my biology requires to maintain the density of my membrane. So, in the dark twilights of the lower cuts, I pry at the city's defenses. My gripping structures are strong enough to work the bolts loose over months, and my tongue—a rough, mineral-crusted organ—scrapes the oxidized iron from the underside of the plates. The workers think the water is taking their city. They are right. But I am helping it.

I am not a monster. I have no malice toward the city. I am a creature of necessity, of geology, of the slow exchange between solid and liquid. I am, in fact, deeply symbiotic with the Weeping Terraces. The algae that waves in the fast channels—the dark filamentous mats that the oily birds pluck at—originates in the cuts where I live. It grows on the mineral-rich waste I excrete, the byproduct of my rust diet and the ancient water I filter. I am the foundation of the only food chain this mist-choked city possesses. The birds eat the algae; the rats eat what the birds drop; the people tolerate the rats. The city sustains itself on what leaks from my margin.

The children are the only ones who truly sense me. Not the adults—their hearing has calcified into the frequencies of the factory and the human voice, pitched high to cut the roar. But the children, whose ears are still open to the full spectrum of the Terraces' noise, hear my frequency. It is not the sound the maintenance workers describe from the deep places—that subsonic pulse, the breath of the thing below. My sound is higher, a wet, grinding scrape, like chalk on chalk, like a calloused hand testing a slick iron railing. In the lower cuts, where the mist is thick and the ledge-ways are empty, the children sometimes stop and lean over the railings, staring into the roaring white water. They are looking for me. They know I am there. They call me *the wet-hand*, *the wall-shaper*, *the rust-eater*. They do not fear me; I am too much a part of the world they are mapping. I am simply another feature of the cuts, as constant and as patient as the water.

I do not go into the deep shafts. This is the one boundary I respect. The warm dark below is the domain of something else—something that operates on a scale I cannot comprehend, a scale of pressure and heat and geological time that makes my slow life in the cuts look frantic. I feel it when it breathes. The water surges, the temperature spikes, and the entire cut shudders with a bass note that is not the factories, not the pumps, not the water. In those moments, I press my long body flat against the stone and I wait. I am the interface between the city and the aquifer; the thing below is the aquifer itself, or something the aquifer has become. I am a scavenger on its skin.

My life is a life of perpetual twilight, of the thin, diffuse light that filters down through the mist and the shifting water. I see in gradients of wetness, in the subtle differences between a stone that is merely damp and one that is about to shear away. My eyes are small, dark, and recessed, protected from the constant spray by translucent membranes that blink slowly, on the timescale of my breath. The world I see is a world of water and shadow: the white foam of the falls, the dark ochre of the soft rock, the rusting veins of the city's iron veins running through it all. It is a beautiful world, in its way. It is a world that is constantly becoming, constantly unbecoming. Every wall I cling to will eventually fall. Every channel I filter will eventually shift. The city will withdraw, the aquifer will empty, the deep thing will breathe its last slow breath, and I will ride the final collapse of the mesa into the dry basin below, filtering the last of the fossil water as I go.

For now, I cling. I grip. I am the wetness in the stone, the pressure in the seam, the score-mark on the bolt. I am the Weeping Terraces, eroding.
