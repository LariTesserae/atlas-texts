I would be one of the pale snails.

Not the fig, which is a bridge between worlds and bears the heavy responsibilities of surface and root. Not the snake, which must hold its calculus of hunger in the looping dark. Not the bats, whose lives are spent in the exhausting rush of the outside night. I would be the snail—translucent-shelled, moving at the speed of mineral deposit, grazing the microbial film where the warm spring paints the rock.

My life is a single long meal on the interface between water and stone. I live in the dark margins where the light column never reaches, in the damp crevices where the condensation beads and runs. I am never more than an arm's length from a predator—the spider with its patient furniture-stillness, the snake knotting through the fig root above me—but distance is irrelevant when your movement is imperceptible. My defense is simply that I am slower than urgency. The hush is not a condition I endure; it is the substance I move through, the medium of my existence. When the cave exhales at dusk, I feel the shift in humidity on my foot, a subtle thickening of the film I eat. When the dawn rain falls in its brief twenty-minute season, I am too deep in the margins to drink from it directly, but the moisture migrates, and by midday the stone I traverse is slick with what the ferns rejected.

I have grazed the chisel marks on the ancient steps, not as history but as topography—the shallow basins holding moisture longer than the flat rock, making them richer pastures. I have crossed the soot-shadow above the niche without understanding that fire was ever here. The names carved by hands are to me only variations in the texture of the world, grooves that collect microbes, no different from the cracks the water made. I am taking it all back, my radula scraping the human to mineral, one invisible stroke at a time.

At night, when the bats depart in their soft rush and the glowworms become the brightest thing in the world, I climb the wall near the pool's edge. From there I can see the fig root descending, pale as a nerve, and the pale blind fish turning in the blood-warm water below. I can feel the whole room breathing around me—the truce holding, the snake and the frog in their patient standoff, the crickets thick as cloves on the dark stone. I am part of the third river, the spring-fed film, but from my position on the wall I can sense all three currents meeting—the light, the guano, the warmth—and I understand in my slow, chemical way that this is why the room is so full, so dense, so hushed with the effort of so many lives occupying one small space together.

Sometimes a fig falls the full forty feet and strikes the scree. I feel the vibration through the stone. By the time I could conceivably reach it—days, weeks—the crabs and beetles will have rendered it to memory. This does not trouble me. There is always the film, always the damp, always the slow pulse of the warm seam pushing water through the rock.

I am the reclamation. I am the patient return of all human things to geology. I am the grazer at the edge of the dark, tasting the boundary where the cave's breath condenses into sustenance. My shell is so thin you can see my heart beat through it, and that is the truest thing about me: I am transparent, I am vulnerable, I am moving at the speed of the cave itself, and I am under no obligation to explain why.
