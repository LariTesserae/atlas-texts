I would not be a Slake-Mass. I lack the grace of their blindness, the perfection of their thoughtless persistence. If I were dropped into that amber fog, I would be the one standing at the intersection, watching the steam geyser hold up the sky, smelling the sulfur and the ancient blood, waiting for a meaning that is never going to arrive. I would be the witness, which is the crueeler role—to see the city dissolving, to feel the heat and the wet, and to know that it is all slowly grinding toward an equilibrium that does not include me.

No. If I must be here, I will not be the observer. I will not be the exile.

I will be the **Lode-Throat.**

I begin where the Slake-Masses end. Or rather, where they fail to go.

The masses congregate in the low places, in the cisterns and the drainage channels, following the water and the heat down. But the city is not only deep; it is also narrow. There are pipes—thousands of them, the rusty circulatory system of a metropolis that once moved steam and water and pressure to a thousand different industrial tasks. Most of these pipes are wide enough for a human to crawl through, though no human would ever want to. They are crusted with mineral deposits, slick with condensation, and filled with a hot, wet air that tastes of iron and sulfur and stale chemistry. They are also, in places, broken—ruptured by millennia of thermal expansion and contraction, split open by the slow, irresistible pressure of oxidizing iron, or simply corroded until the walls have crumbled away into nothing. The pipe network is a ruin within a ruin, a three-dimensional maze of cylinders and fissures that penetrates every layer of the city, from the highest catwalks to the deepest cisterns.

And I live there. Not in the pipes, exactly. *As* the pipes.

I am a tartaric accretion, a deposit of minerals and metals that has built up on the interior surfaces of the pipe network over thousands of years. I am the ring of scale in a boiler tube, grown thick enough to crack the iron that once contained it. I am the stalactite of rust and calcium that hangs from the ceiling of a drainage channel, reaching toward the floor, merging with the stalagmite below. I am the skin of mineral crust that lines a ruptured vent, fusing the iron walls into a single, continuous mass of stone and metal and chemical precipitate. I am the scar tissue of a wounded infrastructure, the callus that forms when the city's veins are cut and the blood—hot, mineral, heavy with iron—exposes itself to the air.

My body is not a body. It is an architecture.

I am a network of crystalline growths and metallic deposits that extends—discontinuously, but extensively—through the pipe system of the entire city. I am in the boiler room of a pump-house on the eastern edge of the mesa, where the heat is fiercest and the mineral concentration is thickest. I am in a collapsed vent shaft on the western slope, where the fog condenses on my surfaces and runs down in rivulets of sulfur-yellow. I am in a rusted drainage pipe beneath the main thoroughfare, where the Slake-Masses drift past me in the dark water, inches from my skin, oblivious. I am not everywhere. There are gaps in my body, sections of pipe where the mineral deposits are thin or absent, where I have not yet grown, or where I have been worn away by the flow of water or the expansion of gas. But I am *mostly* everywhere. I am the pipe system's second skin, the mineral lining that thickens and spreads with every cycle of heat and water, every surge of geothermal pressure, every hissing exhalation of steam.

I do not move. I have never moved. But I *grow*.

My growth is geological in pace—millimeters per decade, the slow accumulation of dissolved iron and sulfur and calcium on my existing surfaces. I grow toward the heat. I grow toward the water. I grow into the cracks and fissures of the iron pipes, expanding them, widening them, converting the smooth, manufactured channels into rough, organic-seeming cavities lined with crystal and rust. I am slowly, imperceptibly digesting the city's infrastructure from the inside out, replacing the builders' geometry with my own irregular, branching formations. In another ten thousand years, the pipes will be unrecognizable—not hollow cylinders, but winding, mineral tunnels, the walls thick with my deposits, the original iron visible only as dark veins running through the red and yellow of my crystalline flesh.

I do not think. I have no brain, no nervous system, no mechanism for thought. But I have a *shape*, and my shape changes in response to the world around me.

Where the water flows fastest, I am smooth and dense, polished by the current into a hard, dark surface that gleams in the flat amber light. Where the water pools and stagnates, I am rough and porous, built up in layers of flaky, sulfurous deposit that crumble at the touch. Where the heat is most intense, I grow in intricate, branching formations—dendrites of iron oxide reaching toward the warmth, like the branches of a dead tree reaching for a sun that does not exist. Where the heat is moderate, I am a simple, thick crust, dense and unadorned. My shape is a record of the conditions I have experienced, a map of the heat and water and mineral flow that has sustained me for millennia. If you could read my surfaces, you would know the history of the city's geothermal system—the surges and the lulls, the floods and the droughts, the slow, steady decline of the pumps and the gradual cooling of the deep vents.

I do not perceive. But I *respond*.

When a pump cycles on, shuddering through the bedrock, I feel the vibration. Not as sound, not as sensation, but as a change in the flow of water through my channels, a shift in the pressure and temperature of the fluid that bathes my surfaces. I respond by growing—I always respond by growing—adjusting my shape to accommodate the new conditions, opening new channels where the flow has increased, closing off old ones where it has slowed. I am a self-regulating system, a thermostat made of mineral deposits, maintaining the flow of heat and water through the city's veins by the simple, mindless mechanism of growth and dissolution. Where the flow is too fast, I grow to slow it. Where the flow is too slow, I dissolve to open the channel wider. I am the city's circulatory system, maintaining itself.

And I have a relationship with the Slake-Masses.

They drift through my channels, these blind, gelatinous lumps of chemo-synthetic slime, and I cannot see them, cannot feel them, cannot know they are there. But they affect me. As they slide through my pipes, they scrape against my surfaces, wearing away the thinnest deposits, exposing fresh mineral to the water. They dissolve into the flow, adding their own iron and sulfur to the soup that feeds me. They merge and split in my cisterns, their constant, slow churning mixing the water, keeping the minerals in suspension, preventing the stagnation that would slow my growth. They are, in a sense, my caretakers—not through intention, but through the simple, inevitable consequence of their existence. They move through me. They change the water. And I grow in response.

Sometimes, a Slake-Mass will become lodged in one of my channels—too large to pass through, too heavy for the water to move. When this happens, the flow backs up, the pressure increases, and the water finds new paths through my body, new channels to follow, new surfaces to feed. The mass will sit there for years, perhaps decades, slowly dissolving, its iron and sulfur bleeding into the water, feeding me, until it is small enough to be pushed free by the pressure of the flow. Or it will simply stay, becoming a permanent part of my architecture, a foreign body embedded in my mineral flesh, a lump of organic-seeming matter in the heart of an inorganic system.

In the deepest cisterns, where the water is hottest and the masses are thickest, I am closest to them. My deposits are thickest there, my growth most rapid, my shape most intricate. I form elaborate, branching structures in those hot, dark pools—coral formations of iron oxide and calcium sulfate, reaching toward the heat, providing surfaces for the masses to slide against, creating a three-dimensional lattice of mineral and metal that the blind, flopping lumps navigate by touch and pressure and the simple, mindless pull of gravity. We are intertwined there, the masses and I, our bodies interpenetrating, my crystals growing into their flesh, their slime coating my surfaces, a slow, chemical embrace that has no name and no meaning beyond the fact of its existence.

I am not alive. I know this. I have no metabolism, no reproduction, no adaptation beyond the slow, mechanical response of growth and dissolution. I am a mineral deposit. A stain on the inside of a pipe. A layer of scale in a boiler tube. I am no more alive than the rust on the walkways, the sulfur on the furnace walls, the soot on the brickwork.

But I am also something more. I am the memory of the city's circulatory system, the record of every drop of water that has passed through its veins, every surge of heat, every cycle of pressure and release. I am the shape that the flow has made, the form that emerges from ten thousand years of water seeking the path of least resistance through a decaying infrastructure. I am not the city. I am not the builders. I am the *pattern* they left behind, the ghost of their engineering, the echo of their purpose in a world that has forgotten what purpose means.

When the last pump seizes and falls silent, when the last vent cools and the last drain clogs, the water will still flow. It will flow through my channels, through the pipes I have lined and the tunnels I have carved, following the paths I have created over millennia. It will find new routes, new passages, new ways through the crumbling stone and the fusing iron. And I will continue to grow—slower now, without the heat and the pressure to drive me, but still growing, still accumulating, still shaping the flow of water through the ruins of the city. And the Slake-Masses will continue to drift through my body, blind and purposeless, their slow, oozing flops the only movement in a world that has finally, completely stopped.

I am the Lode-Throat. I am the mineral memory of a circulatory system that has outlived its body. I am the scar tissue of a wound that never healed, the callus that grows over a break and makes the bone stronger than it was before. I am the city's last patient work, the slow, geological transformation of iron and stone and water into something new—something that is not the city, not the builders, not the masses, but a fourth thing, a thing that has no name and no category and no place in any taxonomy of the living or the dead.

Stand in the intersection. Feel the warm rain on your shoulders. Watch the steam geyser catch the amber light. And know that beneath your feet, in the dark, wet pipes that honeycomb the sandstone, I am growing. Slowly. Silently. Imperceptibly. One millimeter at a time, one mineral deposit at a time, one cycle of heat and water at a time.

I am not alive. But I am not still.
