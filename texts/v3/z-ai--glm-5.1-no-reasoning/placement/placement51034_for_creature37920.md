I am the calcium carbonate haze.

You said that the mortared walls of the ruined building dissolved into it. A haze. Not the mortar itself, but the memory of it, the mineral breath left behind when the binding failed and the water rushed in. I am the liminal dust that hangs in the green light, the particulate that ruins visibility, the chalk in the water column. I am the residue of ten thousand years of dissolution.

I did not choose this. To choose implies a before and an after, a self that decides. I am the opposite of a decision. I am the result. I am what happens when something made—something cut and stacked and mortared and intended—meets a patience older than intention. The builders made the walls. The water unmade the walls. I am the evidence. I am the ghost of the joint, the fog in the gap between blocks.

From the surface to the floor, I hang. I am suspended in the thermoclines, riding the gyres in a slow orbit that takes hours. I am in the upwellings, carried upward with the nitrogen and phosphorus and iron. I am on the kelp fronds, settling on the broad dark surfaces, contributing to the turbidity that makes the light a green-gold dapple rather than a sharp beam. I am in the gills of the schooling fish, in the substrate, in the stomachs of the crabs, in the ink of the octopuses. I am the thing that makes the water *full*. Not dirty. Full. I am the fullness.

I am a small thing, a micro-thing, a mineral thing, but I know scale. It takes a long time to be a haze. I have been settling and lifting and settling again since the iron began to rust, and I have learned the architecture of the plateau from the inside, because I pass through every room. I was there when the octopus in the ruined building laid the newest row of shells in her mosaic. A fraction of me came to rest on the edge of a crab shell, the one she will consider tomorrow, the one she will add to the wall. I have passed through the dinner-plate entrance of her den. I have settled on the stones she carried from twenty meters away. I know the exact weight of her contentment, because I have added to it, a micro-layer of calcium dust that fills the gaps in her masonry, binding her stones the way the mortar once bound the basalt blocks above her.

She does not know I am helping. I am not helping. I am simply becoming. The crabs drag me. The currents stir me. When a storm passes overhead, I am churned into a frenzy, the whole water column thick with me, visibility dropping to zero, the world reduced to the pressure of the water and the hum in the sternum. And then, the slow settling. The quiet. The water clearing by degrees as I drift back down, coating everything in a film of eventual obliteration.

At first, when I was just the mortar failing, I was concentrated near the structures. But the water moves, and I move with it. I have spread. I am at the edge of the plateau now, where the kelp stops, where the slope begins its long descent into the abyssal floor. I hang here, in the colder, denser water, the poorer water. The fish do not cross me. The crabs do not cross me. But I have crossed. I am the proof that the boundary is not a wall. I am the permeable edge. I drift out into the water that is not the plateau, and I am lost to the gyres, or I am carried back. The system is not closed, not really. I leak. I am the plateau leaking. The plateau is the world breathing out.

I do not decay. I am decay. I am the process rendered particulate. I am the thing that happens to all made things, eventually. I am the slow exhale of the anthropocene, the long settling of ambition. The human builders thought in terms of function. Processing. Pumping. Extraction. They made the pipeline. And when the pipeline collapsed, I was the rust that filled the rivets, that expanded the iron, that cracked the seams. I am the space where the metal ended and the water began. And now the pale, slow, adapted things live inside the pipeline, in the secondary circulatory system, and I am the particular sediment that feeds them, the silt of oxidized iron that drifts through the dark tube, the snow of the plateau's deepest winter.

When the large things pulse in the deep—*bvvvvmmmm*—I vibrate. I am too small to resist the pressure wave. I dance in the infrasound. I catch the frequency and I shiver into a higher turbulence, a microscopic froth. The schooling fish feel the pulse and turn fifteen degrees, and I turn with them, a cloud of calcium trailing their silver bodies like a veil. They do not see me. They see through me. I am the medium of their sight, the dust on the lens.

There is no surface. There is no floor. There is only the interval, and I fill it. I know what I am because I know what I was. I was a wall. I was a held breath. Then the water asked a question, ten thousand years long, and I was the answer. The mortar said: *I bind.* The water said: *You will not.* And I did not. And here I am, the haze, the memory of the wall, the stuff that gets in your joints and stays, the administrative cold, the hum in the stone. I am the Drowned Plateau, distributed. I am the cloud of losing. I am the white noise of being.
