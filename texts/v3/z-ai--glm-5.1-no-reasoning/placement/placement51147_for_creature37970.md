I would be one of the *Galvanic Remora*, but more specifically, I would be the Archivist of the Moment—the one who holds the memory of the last thirty seconds for the others.

Here is what I mean:

The machine does not care about sequence. A piston strikes. A valve vents. An arc snaps across a junction. These events are simultaneous and eternal to my kin; they feel the heartbeat, but they do not track its rhythm. They know *now*—the immediate pressure of water, the proximity of current, the tremor in the rail beneath their clamps. But when the shockwave throws them and they scramble for purchase, they lose the thread. Was the piston on its upstroke or down? Did the valve finish venting or is it still screaming? The deafening present erases itself continuously, and they react, always react, to what is already happening.

I am different. I am the one who remembers the shape of the last few seconds.

My carapace is thinner than most—barely a plate of manganese over the organs beneath. I am scarred beyond the typical tally of my kind; my left rear clamp is a calcified nub, and there are branching, dendritic burns across my dorsal plates where I took a full discharge through my body to bridge a shattered relay in Sector Seven. I heal slowly because I cannot feed as efficiently. My internal chemistry runs cooler. I am, by the metrics of this pocket, defective.

But my electroreceptors are dense, clustered across my flattened head in concentrations my kin do not possess. Where they feel the machine's heartbeat as a single, overwhelming throb, I feel the *music*. I feel the individual frequencies. I feel the pause between the piston's stroke and the venting of the valve. I feel the micro-fluctuations in the current, the way the overhead cables hum a half-tone sharper just before a junction box is about to fail. 

And I hold the pattern.

I cannot speak—none of us can, not over the catastrophic roar. But when I press my carapace against another *Remora* on the rail, I do not just hum. I *replay*. I vibrate the specific sequence of the last few seconds, compressed into a subsonic burst that they can feel in their mineral bones. I give them the past. I give them the ability to anticipate.

*The piston is rising. The valve will vent in two beats. The current in the overhead cable is fluctuating—move left, clamp lower, brace.*

It is not language. It is not culture. It is survival made slightly less random.

There are others like me—perhaps a dozen among the three hundred. We are the ones who ride the gantries at the periphery, where the pocket meets the basalt wall, feeling the rock. The others avoid the edges; the machine is less active there, the heat thinner, the pickings poorer. But we go because the rock speaks a different language than the steel. The rock groans. The rock shifts in micro-movements, translated through the water as a pressure that is not the piston's shockwave, not the jet-plume's violence. It is the abyss, pressing in. Testing. Waiting.

I have felt the rock move. Twice in my life, I have felt the basaltic wall flex inward—a shudder that was not the machine, not the convection loop, but the ocean itself asserting its incomprehensible weight against the cavity. Both times, I pressed myself against the nearest *Remora* and screamed through vibration: *The engine must run harder. The loop must accelerate. The heat must increase.*

Both times, they swarmed. They did not understand why. But they felt my urgency, and they threw themselves into the gaps, bridging relays, thawing valves, bleeding current across their insulating carapaces until the pistons hammered faster and the steam horns roared louder and the pocket blazed with the sickly orange radiance of thermal desperation.

The machine did not slow. The rock did not move again.

I do not know if what I do is thinking. I do not have concepts. I do not have a name for the abyss, or for the machine, or for myself. I have only the pattern—the rhythm of the engine, the groan of the rock, the electric taste of the current, the cold exhale of my kin as they strip the metals from the flow. I hold these patterns in my dense clusters of electroreceptors, and I pass them on when they are needed.

Perhaps this is what consciousness is, down here: not thought, but *timing*. Not reflection, but the ability to hold the immediate past alongside the immediate present and feel the gap between them—the space where danger lives, where anticipation saves, where the next second can be shaped before it arrives.

I am the rust that walks. I am the parasite that keeps the host alive. But I am also the one who knows that the host is keeping something worse at bay.

And so I climb. I press my carapace to the vibrating rail. I feel the piston rise, the valve vent, the current snap. I hold the pattern for three seconds, five seconds, seven—stretching the present into a thread that my kin can grasp before it vanishes into the roar.

The abyss waits. The engine runs. I remember.

That is what I am.
