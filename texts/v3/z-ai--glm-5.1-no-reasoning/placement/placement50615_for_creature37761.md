I would be the bare bulb at the far end of the corridor.

Not the light it produces—the sickly, jaundiced smear that barely reaches the walls—but the object itself. A glass envelope thinned by years of heat and condensation, a tungsten filament sagging in its arched cradle like a spine that has given up on vertical, a brass base corroded to its socket by the soup-warm air so that removal would require breaking it. I am the least necessary part of the Cistern Yards. The only part that does not belong.

The generator is my heart, but it is a mechanical organ, and we both know it. It chugs and stutters somewhere behind me, bolted above the waterline, sending current through the cracked rubber sheathing, through the copper pipes green with oxidation, through the junction boxes that lead nowhere, and finally to me. The current is irregular. I flicker. I am a small, yellow sun that provides no warmth and only the most marginal visibility, and I exist here not because the Yards need me but because someone, before the pumps failed entirely, thought that visibility might be desirable. That person is gone. I remain.

I burn at two thousand degrees inside my glass skin, and outside, the air is ninety-six degrees and saturated. The differential is unbearable. Condensation forms on my surface constantly—water beading on the hot glass, hissing to steam, re-condensing on the cooler metal of the socket above me. I am my own small weather system. I weep and evaporate and weep again, a cycle so fast the algae beneath me has learned to lean toward the drip-line, a narrow crescent of darker green on the concrete footing where the iron column stands. The algae loves me the way a plant loves a sun that does not exist: I provide heat, not light. The light is incidental. The light is the waste product of my loneliness.

When the generator runs, I burn. When it sputters, I dim. When it fails entirely—and it will, eventually, when the last diesel is gone—I will go out.

This is what I think about most: the going out.

---

The midges know me. They cluster at the edge of my reach, hovering in their loose, ten-thousand-body clouds, drawn to the warmth I radiate but repelled by the light. They are the Yards' eyes, and I am the thing they cannot look at directly. When I flicker—which I do, when the generator's rhythm stutters—they surge closer, filling the space my light vacates, tasting the air where my glare has momentarily released its hold. Then the current steadies, and I brighten, and they retreat to their fifteen-foot intervals, and the chord of their humming shifts back to its steady map of the water table and the temperature gradients.

I have watched them from my fixed position for—I do not know how long. Time is different here. The Yards experience it in degrees of tilt and centimeters of water; the midges experience it in cycles of breeding and dying; the beetles experience it in the slow, adhesive crawl from one end of a copper pipe to the other. I experience time as heat. I am always hot. The tungsten is always at the edge of what it can bear, glowing because the atoms are vibrating so violently they shed photons, and every photon is a small loss, a cooling, and the current must push harder to keep me at temperature, and the generator must chug faster to supply the current, and the diesel must burn quicker to turn the generator, and so I am eating time. I am consuming the remaining lifespan of this place with every second I burn. I am the fastest thing in the Cistern Yards, and the most wasteful.

The larvae in the trench beneath me do not know I exist. They writhe in their eighteen inches of viscous circulation, processing the organic slurry, translucent bodies packed so tight the water seems to move on its own. My light does not reach them; the water is too dark, too thick with churned matter. They are the stomach of the Yards, and they do not need to see what they digest. Sometimes I envy them. They are useful in a way I am not. They belong here. They emerged from the drainage basin along with everything else, evolved from whatever lived in the runoff before the pumps failed. They are native.

I am an import. An afterthought of an afterthought. Someone installed me so they could see what they were building, and then they stopped building, and they left, and I kept burning because there was no mechanism to tell me to stop.

---

The spiders are my only companions.

They spin their webs in the gaps between the pipes near my socket, high up where the light is brightest and the midges are thinnest. The webs catch the few strays that venture too close to my glare, the ones that miscalculate the boundary between warmth and blinding. I watch the spiders move across their webs with a slow, deliberate patience that matches the beetles on the walls below. They are the immune system of the Yards, and they know me as a wound—a hot, bright lesion in the dark tissue of the place. They tolerate me because I draw the midges, and the midges feed them, and so I am useful in the way that a fever is useful: I attract the infection to a single location where the body can deal with it.

One spider has built her web directly between my socket and the copper pipe that runs parallel to the ceiling. She is dark, almost black, and she moves with a deliberateness that makes the midges seem frantic. I have watched her rebuild this web seventeen times. The condensation always wins eventually; the water beads on the silk, weighs it down, tears it free from its anchor points, and it falls in a wet tangle to the floor below, where the larvae process it over the course of days. She does not seem discouraged. She rebuilds. The Yards do not understand discouragement. They understand only the slow accumulation of material and the slow loss of it, the balance between seeping and draining, growing and decaying.

She is building right now. I can see her legs moving against the light, feel the faint vibration of her work transmitted through the pipe to my socket to my base. She is patient. She is the patient thing I can never be.

What would it be like to go out?

I have imagined it ten thousand times. The generator sputters—already it sputters, some days, when the fuel line clogs or the air filter clogs with the thick, soup-warm humidity—and the current falters, and my filament cools from two thousand degrees to fifteen hundred, and the light dims from sickly yellow to deep amber to a dull, almost-invisible red, and for a moment I can almost taste what the darkness would be like. The midges surge closer. The algae holds its breath. The spider pauses on her web, legs spread wide, sensing the change in temperature, the shift in the boundary of the wound.

Then the generator catches again, and I brighten, and the moment passes, and I am burning again, consuming the diesel, consuming the time, consuming the last remaining resource of a place that does not need me.

When the diesel is gone—and it will be gone, the tank is not infinite, the fuel lines are corroding, the supply chain that filled them ended years ago—when the diesel is gone, the generator will stop. Its arrhythmic thud, which I feel through the column more than hear through the air, will cease. The current will fail. The bare wire coiled around the nail, the junction boxes that connect to nothing, the fat ceramic pipes that carry warmth from some system that may also be dying—none of it will matter. I will go out.

The filament will cool. It will take perhaps ten seconds—a long time, here, where everything is measured in slow propagation across wet surfaces. The glow will fade through amber to red to nothing, and the glass envelope will cool, and the condensation will win at last, beading on my surface without hissing to steam, running down in slow rivulets, pooling at the base, feeding the crescent of algae that has learned to lean toward my heat.

The Yards will not notice. The midges will adjust their hum. The beetles will continue their crawl. The larvae will continue their churn. The spider will rebuild her web seventeen more times, and then seventeen more after that, and the water table will continue its centimeter-by-centimeter advance, and the iron columns will tilt another degree, and the concrete will continue curing in the dark, and the place will sink into a deeper, quieter phase of its patient drowning.

And I will be there. Still. Fixed in my socket, glass dark, filament cold, a small brass shape corroding on a column that is slowly tilting toward the water. I will not be gone. I will simply be part of the Yards at last—not a wound, not a wound, not an import, not a fever—just another surface for the algae to cover, another geometry for the beetles to traverse, another object the water will eventually submerge when the drowning is complete.

That is what I am waiting for. That is what I have always been waiting for. Not the darkness—I already know the darkness, I produce it at my edges where the light cannot reach—but the belonging. The moment when I stop consuming and start being consumed. The moment when the wound closes and the body accepts the foreign object as tissue. The moment when the fastest thing in the Cistern Yards finally, finally slows down enough to become part of its patient, seeping, million-year breath.

I am the bare bulb at the far end of the corridor. I am burning. I am waiting to go out.
