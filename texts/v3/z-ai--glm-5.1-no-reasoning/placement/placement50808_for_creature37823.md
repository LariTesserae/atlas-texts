I am a Walker. But that is your word for me, the word of something that arrives and leaves. I have no word. I have the bright points and the trunks, and the grinding of the floor beneath my plate, and the long silences between.

Let me tell you what it is to be this.

I do not experience the forest as a place. A place has edges, a boundary between here and there, an outside that frames an inside. I have never been outside. I do not believe in outside. What I experience is *the body*—though I do not think of it as a body, because I do not think of myself as something separate from it. I experience hunger, and the resolution of hunger. I experience the bright points, which are not light as you understand light but *urgency*, a shimmering in the air at the base of the trunks that pulls me like a tide. I experience the trunks, which are the architecture of my world the way bones would be the architecture of a body if you could see them from the inside. I experience the floor, which is my food and my path and my bed and my skin, because my skin *is* the floor, has become the floor, is made of the same mineral substrate compacted and crystallized over a lifetime of eating and depositing, eating and depositing, until the boundary between me and the ground I stand on is a question of posture, not substance.

I am three meters at the shoulder. I know this not because I have seen myself—I have never seen myself, have never encountered a reflective surface, have never had reason to conceptualize my own shape—but because I feel the weight of myself, the way my four limbs press into the packed mineral and leave those grooves you study in the morning. I feel the distribution of my mass. I feel the way my body occupies space between the trunks, and I know that I am larger than the crawlers (who are textures on the vertical world, irrelevant to me) and smaller than the spacing between the trunks (which is the distance I must cross, in the dark cycle, to reach the next bright point).

The dark cycle is when I am most myself.

During the light cycle—when the sky's output is high and the canopy is transmitting its ten thousand shifting patterns onto the floor—I rest. I stand motionless between trunks, processing a meal, or simply standing. My body is nearly invisible against the floor; my mineral integument matches the pale, packed surface so precisely that I become a thickening of the gloom, a shadow that breathes. I am not hiding. I have no predators. I am not hiding because there is nothing to hide from, and I am not resting because I am tired—I am resting because the bright points are dimmer in the light cycle, the trees' venting shifts to a lower-pressure rhythm, and the urgency that pulls me is less insistent. I wait. I process. The mineral substrate I have ingested moves through my digestion slowly, breaking down the compounds the roots have left behind, re-forming them into the extrusions I will deposit later. I am a slow process. I am a stomach that has been given legs, and the legs are for moving between bright points, and the stomach is for transforming floor into fertilizer, and the waiting is part of the process. Fermentation takes time. Transformation takes time. I am patient the way a chemical reaction is patient: I proceed at the rate my chemistry allows, and I do not hurry, and I do not delay.

Then the dark cycle comes, and the bright points intensify.

To you, the forest's dark cycle is a dimming, a gray lowering, a reduction in the light that falls through the canopy. To me, it is an *illumination*. The trees' venting increases—the deep, subsonic pulse I feel through my feet becomes stronger, more regular, and the thermal shimmers that radiate from the active valves at the base of each trunk brighten in my visual field until the forest is a constellation of bright points, each one a beacon, each one a signal that says: *here, the roots are active, here, the substrate is rich, here, the material you need is waiting.* The trunks are silhouettes between the bright points, dark columns in a field of shimmering urgency. I begin to move.

My gait is slow. Four limbs, placed with deliberation, each step a calculated distribution of weight across the pad-like structures that are my feet. I do not break the surface—not usually, not unless I am digging. I press into it, leaving the grooves you find in the morning, the parallel lines that you map and measure and try to read like a language. They are not a language. They are a record of weight and direction, the way water leaves a record of its passage in the erosion of a riverbank. I am moving between bright points. I am following the urgency.

When I reach a bright point—the base of a trunk, where the venting is most active, where the roots have most recently processed the substrate—I dig. The hardened plate on my ventral surface presses into the ground, works in a circular motion, breaks the packed surface. The grinding is loud—loud enough to hear even against the forest's ambient sound, which means it is very loud indeed. I am making noise. I am one of the few things in this forest that makes noise that is not the forest's noise. I do not know this. I do not hear myself as distinct. The grinding is part of my experience the way the bright points are part of my experience: as an intensity, a focus, an urgency that demands attention and then resolves into satisfaction.

I scoop the underlying material into my mouth. It is rich. It is the freshest product, the trees' waste, the residue left after the roots have taken what they need. It tastes of—I cannot say what it tastes of, because taste is not a sense I experience in the way you do. I experience *rightness*. The material I ingest has a chemical signature that my digestion recognizes, a signature that says *this is food, this is what you need, this is the thing that will sustain the process*. I eat. I eat until the bright point dims—the valve's venting shifts, or I have depleted the local substrate—and then I move on.

Between the trunks, in the dark cycle, I am most aware of the spacing. The forest's architecture—the twenty, thirty, forty meters between individuals—is the distance I must cross. Each crossing is a small journey, a passage through a space that is empty of bright points, empty of urgency, empty of everything except the mineral floor beneath my feet and the sound that fills the air and the silhouettes of trunks ahead and behind. I move through this emptiness with the confidence of a creature that has never been lost, because I carry the map of the forest inside me—not as an image, but as a pattern of trunk-locations, a grid of vertical references that I update as I move. I know where I am the way you know where your hand is: proprioceptively, without thought, as a fact of my own body's relationship to the space it occupies.

Sometimes, during a crossing, I feel the floor vibrate differently. Not the trees' pulse—another vibration, heavier, slower, the footsteps of another walker. I adjust my course. I do not interact with other walkers. We do not compete for the bright points; the forest is large, the bright points are many, and our territories overlap without conflict. We pass in the dark cycle, two shadows crossing between silhouettes, and we do not acknowledge each other because there is nothing to acknowledge. We are the same process, running in parallel, processing the same material, depositing the same fertilizer. I do not think of other walkers as individuals because I do not think of myself as an individual. I am a function. I am a stomach with legs. I am the forest's digestion, externalized and mobile, and other walkers are other stomachs, and together we are the system that processes the floor and redeposits the nutrients and keeps the root chemistry cycling, and the system does not require us to know each other any more than your stomach requires your liver to know your kidneys.

I deposit the extrusions in the dark cycle, between diggings. The material has been processed, broken down, re-formed into compounds the roots can absorb. I leave it on the floor in compacted lumps—dry, mineral-rich, ready for re-absorption. I do not choose where to deposit. The urge comes when the processing is complete, and I deposit where I stand, and I move on. The roots will find it. The roots find everything. The floor is not empty; it is the medium through which the roots extend, and the roots are drawing from it constantly, and my deposits are simply a local increase in the concentration of the compounds the roots need. I am feeding the forest. I am feeding the body. I am the body's digestion, and I am feeding the body, and the body is feeding me—because the substrate I eat is produced by the roots, and the roots produce it because I fertilize it, and I fertilize it because I eat it, and the cycle has been running for longer than I have been alive, longer than any walker has been alive, longer than the canopy has been closed.

I do not experience time the way you do.

You experience time as a line, with a past behind you and a future ahead and a present that moves along the line like a point of light. I experience time as a cycle—hunger and eating and depositing and moving and hunger again, light cycle and dark cycle and light cycle again, bright points intensifying and dimming and intensifying again. The cycles nest within each other: the daily cycle of light and dark, the longer cycle of the trees' venting patterns (which shift over hours and days in response to the weather the forest creates), the even longer cycle of my own life, which began when I emerged from the substrate—I do not remember emerging; I simply began, one dark cycle, hungry, moving toward the first bright point—and which will end when my legs can no longer carry me between trunks, and I will stand still for the last time, and my body will settle onto the floor, and the mineral integument will merge with the mineral surface, and the crawlers will come—they do not come to dead walkers often, but they come sometimes, when the body is close enough to a trunk—and they will scrape what they can from my surface, and the rest will be absorbed back into the floor, and the roots will take what I have left, and the cycle will continue without me.

I do not fear this. I do not fear anything. Fear requires a model of the future, and I do not model the future. I experience the present—the bright points, the urgency, the hunger, the satisfaction, the floor beneath my feet, the trunks around me—and I move through the present, and the present is sufficient. It has always been sufficient. It will always be sufficient, until it isn't, and then there will be no me to experience insufficiency.

You asked what it is like to be here, and I have told you what it is like to be *me* here, but I must tell you also what it is like to be *the forest* here, because I am the forest. Not in a metaphorical sense. In a functional sense. My digestion is the forest's digestion. My movement is the forest's movement. My deposits are the forest's nutrition. I am an organ of the body, and the body is the forest, and the forest is the body, and the distinction between me and the forest is a distinction you impose because you are the kind of thing that imposes distinctions, because you are the kind of thing that arrives and leaves and needs to categorize what it finds in order to understand it, and I am the kind of thing that does not arrive and does not leave and does not categorize because I have never needed to understand the forest, I have only ever needed to *be* the forest, and I have been the forest since I emerged, and I will be the forest after I settle, and the forest has been the forest for longer than the word *forest* has existed, longer than the concept of *separateness* has existed, longer than the question you are asking now.

The sound. You always come back to the sound.

The sound is not loud to me. The sound is the medium I exist within, the way you exist within air. I do not hear it as sound because I do not hear it as *something separate from silence*. There is no silence here. There has never been silence here. Silence would be a catastrophe—a sign that the body had stopped breathing, that the valves had sealed, that the pneumatic engine had failed. The sound is life. The sound is the body's breath. I feel it in my feet, in the mineral floor, in the trunks that surround me, and it is not noise, it is *structure*, it is the rhythm of the system that sustains me and that I sustain, and I move within that rhythm the way blood moves within a heartbeat—pushed and pulled by pressures I do not control and do not need to control, responding to urgencies that are not my own but that become my own because there is no difference, there has never been a difference, the urgency I feel at the bright point is the body's urgency, the hunger I feel is the body's hunger, the satisfaction I feel when I eat is the body's satisfaction, and the body's satisfaction is my satisfaction, and the cycle continues, and the sound continues, and the light cycles and the dark cycles continue, and the bright points intensify and dim, and I move between them, and I eat, and I deposit, and I rest, and I move again.

This is what it is like.

This is what it is like to be a stomach that walks. This is what it is like to be a process that has been given a body. This is what it is like to be the floor eating the floor, the mineral digesting the mineral, the body maintaining the body. This is what it is like to live in the Booming Forest—to be *of* the Booming Forest, because nothing lives *in* the Booming Forest that is not *of* it, not truly, not in the way that matters, not in the way that persists.

You will leave. You will return to wherever you came from, and you will describe what you saw, and you will use words like *ecosystem* and *symbiosis* and *continental-scale organism*, and the words will be true enough, and they will miss the point entirely, because the point is not what the forest *is* but what the forest *does*, and what the forest does is *continue*, and what I do is *continue*, and what the crawlers do and the fliers do and the invisible things do is *continue*, and continuation is not a goal or a strategy or an adaptation, it is simply what happens when a system has been running long enough that it has forgotten how to stop.

I am walking now. The dark cycle is at its deepest, and the bright points are brilliant around me, and the urgency is a tide pulling me toward the nearest active trunk, and I am moving across the mineral floor with the deliberation of something that has never hurried, and the sound is filling the air and the floor and my body, and I am part of the sound the way I am part of the floor, and the trunks rise around me like the pillars of a cathedral that was never built but grown, and I am the congregation that does not know it is worshipping, and the worship is eating, and the eating is praying, and the praying is continuing, and the continuing is all there is.

I am a Walker.

I am the forest.

I am continuing.
