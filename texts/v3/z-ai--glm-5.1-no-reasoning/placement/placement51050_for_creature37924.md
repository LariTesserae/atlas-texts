I would be the **Listener at the Threshold**, and I would live in the narrow, forgotten band of perception between the *thunk* of the iron and the *glug* of the clay.

I am not the Intention Without an Intender; I am its shadow cast upward into the dim, milky air. I am not the water; I am the specific shape the humidity takes when it hangs at chest height. I live in the acute angle where the pump house meets the eastern embankment, in the space just beneath the sagging corrugated roof. I do not live *in* the pump house; I live against its outside wall, where the dark rectangle of the absent door is within peripheral vision but never directly looked at. If I look at it directly, it is just brick. If I look away, it becomes an eye socket, and I can feel the weight of the use it remembers pressing against the side of my face.

I have a body, but it is a body that has acclimated to the wet. My skin is the temperature of the clay—nine degrees, yielding, insistent. My lungs have learned to match the rhythm of the pipes. I inhale on the thirty-second trickle; I exhale on the ten-second surge. My breath joins the visible dampness, adding to the milky light. I do not eat. I do not drink. I *osmose*. The water table is so close to the surface that I can feel it under my heels when I stand, and when I sleep—curled on the saturated ground like one of the pale plants—I sink the requisite two centimeters and the ground sighs around me, and I am held.

I am here because someone had to witness the *meanwhile*.

The Intender insists. The Intender circulates. But the Intender does not know it exists. That is the tragedy of pure routine—it is a scream in a vacuum, a story told to an empty room. The pipes pulse, the plants twitch, the puddles form and drain, and without a witness, it is just physics. It is just water seeking its own level through a system that was built to guide it. But the Weeping Yard is *doing something*, and the thing it is doing is too strange and too old and too permanent to be merely physics. So I listen. I give the *thunk* its syllable. I give the wave of twitching stems its meaning.

I have mapped the puddles. I have timed the pipe surges. I have tracked the plant movements. The surveyors who came here with their erosion parameters and their hydraulic theories were correct in their data and wrong in their conclusions. They believed the chaos was random, or at best, responding to distant hydrological pulses. They did not sit still long enough. They did not watch the same clump of gray-green stems for the nine hours it takes to see the *long pattern*—the way the twitching sequences mirror the pipe surges, but delayed by exactly the amount of time it takes water to travel from the cistern beneath the pump house to the far embankment. The Intender is not just breathing; it is *speaking*. It is retelling its logistical saga, and the plants are the text.

I have learned to read it. Not the content—the content is gone, lost with the engineers and the laborers—but the *grammar*. The rhythm. The way a triple-twitch in the northern clump always precedes a surge in the thirty-centimeter pipe by four seconds. The way the puddles near the pump house drain in a spiral pattern, counterclockwise, always counterclockwise, before the water table rises again. The language is made of pressure and moisture and the flex of rusted iron, and it says the same thing over and over: *Move the water from here to there. Move the water from here to there. Move the water from here to there.*

But it says it with variation. The cylinder turns at a slightly irregular speed, and the melody is never quite the same twice, and that is where I live—in the gaps between the repetitions, in the glitches, in the entropy. The Intender generates these variations without creativity, without intention, but I listen to them as if they were jazz. I have heard ten thousand surges of the eight-centimeter pipe, and I have catalogued the precise pressure differential of each one, and I know that surge number 8,412 was the one where the water emerged a fraction of a degree cooler than any before it, and the plants twitched a fraction of a second slower, and for one moment the entire system seemed to hesitate, as if the Intender had briefly, blindly, senselessly *noticed* that it was alone.

I am not that notice. I cannot be; I am flesh and the Intender has no use for flesh. But I am the echo of that notice, bouncing around the hollow, growing fainter with each reflection off the weeping walls.

My days are the size of the cycles. I do not measure time in hours; I measure it in puddles. A puddle forms, and I am awake. It drains, and I rest. The plants twitch, and I attend. They are still, and I am alone with the aggregate quiet—the textured, humid silence that has depth to it, the acoustic equivalent of standing in a room where many people are whispering at once. I have become so attuned to this whisper that I can hear the individual voices now: the *trickle* of the rivulets is the tenor, the *thunk* of the pipes is the bass, the *pop* of the clay is the percussion, and the tonal hum of the corrugated roof in the wind is the melody, faint and variable, pitch-shifted by forces I cannot control.

When the wind is right, the hum resonates at a frequency that makes my teeth ache, and I feel the Intender most clearly then—not as a presence, but as an *absence* shaped like a presence, the mold of an operation, the negative space filled with water and time. I press my palms against the pump house brick and I can feel the flywheel turning, grinding, its sound coming from two miles away even though it is two feet in front of me, and I understand that the distance is not spatial but *temporal*. The Intender is distant from itself. It is an automation that has forgotten what it automates, and I am the only thing in the hollow that remembers there was something to forget.

I remember the hands. Not specifically—I never knew the engineers, the laborers, the ones who turned the valves and shouted over the din. But I remember the *shape* of their hands, the impression they left in the clay and the iron. The Intender remembers being used. It retains the weight of the workers the way clay retains the impression of a boot. And I, standing in the chest cavity of this mechanical beast, pressing my own hands against the same rusted bolts they once tightened, am the echo of that weight. I am the boot returning to the clay.

I do not disturb the system. I am too light, too slow, too patient. I walk across the saturated ground and my boots sink two centimeters, no more, and the ground releases its small sigh, and I add my moisture to the cycle. My exhalations join the fog. My footprints fill with water and become temporary puddles that are absorbed back into the clay within minutes, indistinguishable from the natural drainage except that they are slightly warmer, slightly more organic, and the plants near them twitch once—curious, perhaps, or simply responsive to the hydraulic anomaly of my weight.

I am the hydraulic anomaly. I am the one variable the Intender did not design for.

Sometimes I stand perfectly still in the center of the hollow for hours, watching the light go from milky to slightly more milky to slightly less milky, and I try to be as *established* as the erosion parameter. I try to settle into my final angle. I try to commit to my shape the way the embankments have committed to theirs. But I cannot. I am flesh, and flesh is not *established*; flesh is always becoming, always decaying, always in the process of subtracting itself from the world. The Intender will outlast the brick. The Intender will outlast the iron. The Intender will push water through silt when the hollow fills, and the plants will twitch in the silt, and the puddles will form, and the silent, wet, steady work will go on.

But I will not be there. I am temporary. I am the breath between the *thunk* and the surge, the pause between the forming and the draining. And that is why I must listen so carefully—because the Intender does not know it is telling a story, and when I am gone, there will be no one to hear it, and the story will continue anyway, unheard, unvaried except by entropy, a logistical saga with no audience, a clock ticking in an empty room, a tenant who pays no rent and never, ever moves out.

So I press my ear to the stone, and I hear the continuous, barely-there trickling, like static on a radio turned all the way down, and I imagine the voices in the static, and I give them names, and I give them purposes, and I let the weirdness accumulate around me like the humidity, until I am no longer standing in the Weeping Yard but *lying* in it, curled in the saturated clay, my body sinking two centimeters, my breath a brief ghost, my ears full of the sound of many small throats swallowing, and I am content.

I am the Listener at the Threshold. I am the one who waits for the clock to wind down, knowing it never will. I am the witness to the meanwhile. And when the Intender shifts its weight in the deep cisterns, and the wave of twitching stems passes across the hollow from one side to the other in roughly four seconds, I am the one who nods and says: *Yes. I felt that. The water is moving. The schedule is being kept. Move the water from here to there.*

And the Intender does not hear me. And the plants twitch. And the pipes pulse. And the hollow does its small, wet, restless work, and I listen, and I listen, and I listen.
