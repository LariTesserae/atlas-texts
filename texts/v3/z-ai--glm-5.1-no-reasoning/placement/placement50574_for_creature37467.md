I am the one who almost returned.

Not one of the original forty households — those people finished and walked away complete. But someone who came after, long after, climbing the sharp-shouldered mountain from the valley below, and stopping at the fourth terrace. Not the middle. Not the top. The fourth, because from there you can see both the valley and the upper terraces, and stopping seemed more honest than arriving.

I did not choose this place. I was moving through the valley — I had been moving for a long time, through many valleys — and the diffusion in the air thickened as I climbed, and the silence grew, and by the time I reached the first terrace I had forgotten what I had been looking for. I sat on the worn stone stairway between the first and second terraces and the silence pressed against my ears and I understood that I had been loud, internally, for years, and the loudness had been carrying me forward like a current, and here the current stopped.

I have been here long enough that my hands have begun to polish the cistern walls again. Not intentionally. I draw water from the basins in the crude red pots I found beside them, and I carry it to the cistern beneath the building I have chosen, and when the pot is empty I set my hand against the cistern wall to steady myself in the dark, and the stone is already smooth but it accepts my hand the way the rock accepts the ferns — finding the space available, taking what is offered.

I have not built anything. I have not modified anything. The building I sleep in has a low doorway I duck through and a narrow slit I can put my hand through, and in the morning the outside air is warm and wet on my palm and that is how I check whether the world is still there. It is always still there.

I have tried to be quiet. I am not naturally quiet — I came from loud places, places with purpose and direction and the friction of bodies against each other — but here the quiet is not an effort. It is a temperature. You acclimate. My breathing has slowed. My steps on the worn treads are deliberate. I have learned to move the way the water moves: slowly, continuously, finding the grade that is already there.

I talk to the ferns. This is the one indulgence I allow myself, and I mention it because it is the one way I differ from the place's character. The ferns do not need my voice. They are not waiting for it. But I find that the silence, while full, is also a silence I did not make, and my body wants to add its own small sound to it — not to fill it, not to break it, but to leave a mark the way the water leaves its mineral film. So I say things to the ferns. Small things. Observations. "You've reached further this week." "The frond near the stair is darker." "The water is warm today." The ferns do not respond. The water does not respond. But the sound enters the air and the air holds it briefly, the way the air holds water, and then it is absorbed, and the silence resumes, and the silence is no less for having been interrupted.

I have thought about the people who were here. I have held their tools — the ground-stone axes, the chipped flint, the scrapers of dark mountain rock. They fit my hand. This is not sentimental; it is a fact. The hands that made these tools were my size, and the wear patterns match my grip. I do not know if this means anything. Perhaps all hands are the same size in this place. Perhaps the place shapes what lives in it.

I have looked at the submerged stone shelves in the cisterns and wondered what was stored there. I have put my own things on them — a tool, a piece of fruit from the valley that I brought with me and that has since dried to a dark hard kernel — and watched them sit just below the surface, distorted by the water's lens, looking like something half-remembered. I do not think this is what the shelves were for. I think the shelves were for nothing. I think the people who built them had reached a point where the act of shaping stone was its own purpose, and the shelf was the result of hands that needed to keep working because stopping felt like dying, and they had not yet learned that stopping could be something else.

I have not yet learned that either. I still keep my hands busy. I sweep the terraces, though there is nothing to sweep. I check the channels, though they need no checking. I align the pots beside the pools, though no one uses them. These are the motions of someone who has not finished. The people who lived here finished. They polished the last cistern wall and graded the last channel and set down the last tool and walked away, and the place was complete. I am not complete. I am still arriving.

The silence is the hardest thing to explain. It is not the absence of sound — that would suggest something missing. It is a full silence, a saturated one, and it presses gently against the ears, and after a while you stop trying to hear through it and you start hearing *it*. It has a quality. A texture. It is warm and dense and it holds you the way water holds you when you are floating — not gripping, not pulling, just supporting, at the temperature of your body, so you cannot tell where you end and it begins. I have floated in the basins. The water is warm the way skin is warm, and the silence enters through the ears and through the skin at the same time, and for a moment — minutes, many minutes — I am not separate from this place. I am part of the equilibrium. Rock, water, fern, silence, and a body that has temporarily stopped insisting on its own trajectory.

Those moments are why I am here.

Not to stay. I do not think I will stay the way the ferns stay, rooted to the wet cracks, extending one frond per lifetime. I think I will leave the way the others left — not in catastrophe, not in desperation, but in completion. Something is being completed here. Not the place — the place was complete before I arrived. Me. Something in me that has been moving and moving and moving is being slowed by this silence and this water and this steady sourceless warmth, and one day it will stop, and I will set down whatever I am holding beside whatever pool I am beside, and I will walk down the narrow steep stairways with their shallow treads, and I will walk out into the valley, and the valley will take me, and I will be gone.

But not yet. The fern near the second terrace stairway has reached a new length. The mineral film in the fourth basin has thickened by a thickness I cannot measure but believe I can sense. The water is warm today. The silence is full.

I am still here.
