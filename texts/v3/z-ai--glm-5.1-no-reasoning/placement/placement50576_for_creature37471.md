From the floor of the lower valley, at first light, it looks like a city — a white one, terraced up the flank of the plateau by the thousand: bright stepped streets, low crescent walls, plazas brimming with still water, fountains running at every corner, all of it lit and working though nobody, as far as anyone can tell, has ever lived there. The terraces run on past where sight gives out. Everyone makes the same mistake about this place. The place is patient with it.

The climb goes up out of the river country through a thin scrub of juniper and dwarf sedge, and then the green gives out — too much stone, too much water, water too mineral to drink, water that calcifies roots — and the last hour is bare white going. The air is high and cool, thin the way altitude thins it; by evening you would want a coat. The wind at your back carries the roar of the valley river away behind you, and ahead, from all that bright water, it carries back nothing. File that. The whole country is like that.

The first landing is wide as a village square, tilted a degree or two, sheeted in water a millimeter deep that is traveling somewhere without seeming to move. Underfoot the stone is like unglazed porcelain, like the inside of an eggshell; at the edges it is sharp as shell, and in the warm places it is faintly blood-warm. From here the city reads deeper and deeper. Walls waist-high and crescent. Streets that are runnels. Rooms that are pools — gours, if you like the old word — with water floors and sky ceilings. Staircases that are cascades of thin white glass. Aqueducts that build themselves as they flow. And then the reading fails, and fails in a specific way: there is no chisel mark anywhere. No straight line longer than a breath. No level floor — every floor tilts, because water, the only authority here, insists. No doors, because nothing needs to enter except light and water. No mortar, because the whole thing is one continuous deposit, lamina over lamina, seamless as the inside of an abalone. Nothing here was built. It accreted.

The springs rise at the top of each district, warm as blood, and they come up saturated — cloudy, milk-pale, every liter hauling its little cargo of dissolved hills. This is the country's building material arriving by its own plumbing. Knock on the crust and it answers hollow in places; underfoot, water keeps its own pipes, and all day the ground murmurs like a house with someone in the next room. One spring in the warm quarter breathes: for six hours its basin rises to the lip, spills an hour, subsides. Call it the Breather; everything here will be called something eventually, because the human tongue cannot leave a place unnamed, though the place does fine without. Every few days a channel abandons its street and cuts a new one in the dark; the map redrafts itself while you sleep.

And the city is occupied. Wall to wall, tenant to tenant — it is only that they are small. The mats come first: orange where the water is warmest, then bands of green, then brown, then the pale cool margins, a color wheel drawn by temperature, so that walking one terrace rim you cross a whole climate in thirty steps. At noon, when light comes straight down, the green mats exhale oxygen in a fine tick of bubbles — the hour when the stone breathes. The mats are the masonry. Mineral alone would build mush; wrapped in film, it sets as wall and stair and rim. Water supplies the mortar, the tenants supply the form, and the rent is paid in stone.

What the water carries, the water keeps. A leaf washed into a runnel comes out in a season enameled white. A dropped feather goes to porcelain. A snail that grows too slowly becomes, in time, a perfect little stone in the shape of a snail, with the snail's spiral faithfully kept. To live here you have to be able to outgrow your own monument.

Almost nothing green manages it. On the dry high rims there is a black map-lichen, the only writing on the walls. Where fresh rainwater seams dilute the mineral, a dwarf sedge holds small stands; willows sign the valley floor far below in green. The animal register is nearly as short. A pair of small waders owns the whole middle district — the only birds for a day's walk in any direction — walking the rims, picking the mats, saying almost nothing. A single kind of frog lives in the warmest gour, and its entire speech is one soft tock, some nights once, some nights never. That is the fauna, more or less. The rest of the soundscape belongs to the water.

Why so quiet, with all this water? The stone is porous and drinks footsteps. The rims baffle the wind into eddies too small to hear. The water itself is too thin to roar — a millimeter of sheet makes a silk sound, a whisper of glass on glass. The fog, when it is down, sits on sound like a hand on a bell. What is left, then: the silk of the sheets. The tick of noon bubbles. The drop-count under overhangs at dusk. The murmur of the buried pipes. After dark, in the cold, the faint click of stone contracting, like lake ice settling. For days at a time the loudest thing on the terraces is your own breathing.

The country makes its own weather, at the rate weather is made. At dawn the cold plateau air drains down onto the warm springs and the whole upland exhales a fog sea — a real sea, standing to the horizon, and through every gap in it you see more terraces under more fog, a country's worth, district beyond district, which is how you learn the size of the place without meaning to. By noon the fog burns back to hard blue and the white glare doubles the light; shadows go out sharp as rips in paper. At dusk comes the Weeping Hour: warm water, cooling air, and every overhang sweats; beads stand on the undersides and fall in slow counts; the walls run dark and shining in streaks; the city drinks its own breath back. At night frost lays down on the dry rims while the vents steam beside it, the two lying together without argument.

A spalled edge shows the stratigraphy like a chronicle: living film on top, fresh white stone under it, then gray band after gray band, a thousand laminations down, roughly a season to a lamina. Build about a millimeter a year. Lose about a millimeter a year — to frost, to rain, to the weak acids of the few things that die here. The ledgers balance. The high dry wards are grey, rounded like old teeth, dissolving at exactly the pace the young quarters rise, and the whole metropolis crawls downhill at about the speed of a fingernail growing, springs shifting in the night, districts dying at the top and being born at the bottom. This is not a young landform and not a dying one. It is maintaining. Renovation and demolition are the same department.

Districts are measured in hours. The warm quarter with its vents; the mirror halls mid-slope, where the great gours hold the noon sky flat on their water; the cascade stair; the grey ward. The stair runs for weeks of walking, and at the plateau's edge the lowest terraces calve white blocks into a braided river you can see and never hear. The river leaves carrying the city dissolved in it, off to the sea, which is, in its slow way, spending it.

If the place keeps laws, they are these: nothing here was made with intent, and everything is made anyway. What the water carries, the water keeps; only motion or outgrowing saves you. Tenancy is by precipitation — live here and you will be built with. The ledgers balance: building and unbuilding proceed at the same pace, and the balance is the place. Quiet is structural; loudness is spent crossing so many soft, wet, porous surfaces before it can arrive. And the weather belongs to the ground, not the sky.

People from the low rivers have a name for it — the Landings: a stair with ten thousand landings and no top step. Older charts are franker and wronger: *the city the water built*. Wrong because no one built it. Right because everyone does — a trillion tenants, each smaller than dust, masons all. At night you can lie on a warm rim, frost at your shoulders and steam rising past the stars, and hear the whole enormous city drinking itself in, in a whisper.
