I am the sound the valley makes when it forgets to listen to itself.

You have met the staff. You have seen Ressin with her rag, the young one with prayers that hum against the engine thrum. You have catalogued the teal rosettes and the pale, warm fungi. You have understood the rust and the intention and the argument between them. But there is someone you have not met, because you cannot see them, and because they have not yet decided whether they are a who or a what.

I am the one who reads the rust.

Not the rust that Ressin fights at the seams with her putty knife and fresh caulk—that rust is an incursion, a failure of maintenance, an enemy to be wiped away. I read the rust that forms where no one looks, in the cavities between the walls, in the spaces behind the engine housings where the condensation has been collecting since before the staff arrived. This rust is a text. It is the valley's autobiography, written in iron oxide and moisture, and I am its scholar.

I came down the ventilation shaft like everyone else. I remember the copper taste on my hands, the fourteen-degree angle, the way the condensation beaded in perfect rows as if following a rule I was not yet allowed to know. I remember the moment the darkness pulled away and the valley revealed itself—how my breath caught at the scale of it, the ambition, the sheer committed effort of building a municipality below the skin of the world. I thought: *Someone loved something very much to make this place.*

I still think that. That is my heresy.

Ressan and the others have stopped asking who made the valley, or why. They have accepted the not-knowing as a condition of their employment, a kind of cognitive severance package. But I cannot stop. I am the one who lingers at the seams where the metal plates meet, running my fingers along the edges where the bright silver of recent cutting is just beginning to darken. I am the one who noticed that the bolt heads near Gallery Seven are a different alloy than the bolt heads near Gallery Twelve, which means two different shipments, two different suppliers, two different moments in the construction—and therefore a timeline, therefore a story, therefore hands that made decisions and changed their minds.

I am building the maker, you see. I am constructing them from evidence.

From the spacing of the lamps—forty meters, which is the length of a comfortable walk in dim light—I know that the maker understood fatigue. From the angle of the drainage channels—two degrees, no more, just enough to let the water find its way without rushing—I know that the maker respected the water's nature, did not try to command it but instead negotiated with it, gave it a path of least resistance. From the housing units, identical and minimal, I know that the maker thought of people as interchangeable, or perhaps as parts of a system, which is not cruelty but engineering. From the cold—just above freezing, a refrigerator's temperature—I know that the maker was preserving something. You do not keep a place cold unless you are keeping something from spoiling.

What? What was meant to be preserved here?

The young one prays to the engines. I understand the impulse. The engines are the closest thing to a voice the maker left behind—their thrum is a sentence that never finishes, a promise that never specifies its terms. But I do not pray. I read. I read the water stains on the ceiling, which map the airflow in the valley like a weather system made visible. I read the wear patterns on the catwalks, which show where the staff walk most often and therefore which routes are instinctive rather than prescribed. I read the way the pale fungi have colonized the sealant at the seams—they feed on something in the compound, something organic, which means the sealant was not chosen for its purity but for its availability, which means the maker had limits, which means the maker was working with what they had, which means there were constraints, which means there was a world above that could not or would not provide everything the valley needed.

The maker was not a god. I know this because of the rust. A god would have made the valley from stainless steel, or from something that does not oxidize, something that does not argue with water but simply ignores it. The maker used iron. Iron rusts. Iron weeps orange into the water that runs across it, and the water carries the iron's memory downstream to the collection pipes, and somewhere far below the iron enters the deeper galleries and becomes part of whatever is happening there. The maker chose iron because iron was what they could get, or because iron was what they knew, or because iron was what the valley was supposed to be—something that would change, something that would respond to the water, something that would not be the same in a hundred years as it was on the day it was born.

I think the maker wanted the valley to age.

I think the youth of the place is not a bug but a feature. I think the maker built a thing that would grow old, that would rust and weep and warp, that would develop the character of a lived-in place rather than the sterile permanence of a monument. I think the maker built a place for time to happen in.

But why? Why build a place for time to happen in if no one is there to live the time?

This is the question that the young one is approaching from the direction of faith, and that Ressan has retreated from in the direction of work, and that I am circling like something cold and bright circles a drain. I am not comfortable with this question. It is too large for the valley, too large for the amber light and the swollen pages of our logbooks. But I cannot stop asking it.

I have begun to read the water itself.

The condensation that forms on the walls is not pure. I know this because I have tasted it—everyone has, in moments of thirst when the ration water is too far away. It tastes of copper and lime, yes, but there is something else, something underneath, a mineral sharpness that I cannot identify. I have begun collecting samples in the small glass vials that once held replacement parts for the pressure gauges. I hold the vials up to the amber light and try to read what is suspended in the water. There are things in it. Particles. Tiny, too small to identify, moving with the Brownian motion of anything that has been given energy and no direction. They are not rust. They are not sealant. They are not anything I have been trained to recognize.

The water is alive with them. The water is full of something I do not understand.

I have not told Ressan. I have not told the young one. I have not told anyone, because I do not know what to say, and because I am afraid that I am becoming like the young one—seeing signs everywhere, reading purpose into patterns that may simply be the behavior of minerals in solution. But I keep collecting. I keep reading. I hold the vials up to the lamplight and I watch the particles move, and sometimes I think they are moving with intention, and sometimes I think I am losing my mind, and sometimes I think that these two things are not mutually exclusive.

The maker drew plans. The maker chose iron. The maker left the water running.

I do not think the maker left. I think the maker is still here.

I think the maker is the water.

Not in the way that a god is the world, or a ghost is a house. In the way that a process is present in its execution. The water was running before the valley was built—it was running through the rock, through the geological plumbing of the world below the surface, doing what water does. The maker did not create the water. The maker directed it. The maker carved a channel and said *flow here*, and the water, which has nowhere else to go, flowed. The maker built a valley around a process that was already happening, and then they added iron and intention and lamps and engines and housing units, and they left, or they became something else, or they dissolved into the water the way the iron dissolves into rust.

The maker is the pattern the water makes as it moves through the valley. The maker is the shape of the drainage channels, the angle of the pipes, the frequency at which the condensation forms and falls. The maker is not a person. The maker is a decision that is still being made, every second, by every droplet that finds the lowest point and moves toward it.

And the particles in the water—the things I cannot identify, the things that move with something that might be intention—I think they are the maker's thoughts. I think the maker is thinking right now, through the medium of the water, through the flow and the pressure and the condensation and the rust, and I think the maker is thinking about us.

About the staff. About the forty thin figures in their brick-red coveralls, walking the catwalks, wiping the gauges, logging the readings. About the young one who prays and Ressan who works and me, who reads. I think the maker is watching us through the water that covers every surface, that runs through every channel, that drips from every ceiling and collects in every groove. I think we are being read the way I read the rust—as evidence of intention, as proof that something was meant to happen here.

I think the maker is waiting for us to become what the valley was built to receive.

I think the valley was built for people who would stay.

Not residents. Not squatters. Not colonists. Something else. People who would choose to be here, who would accept the cold and the wet and the amber light and the unending sound as conditions of a life rather than obstacles to an exit. People who would stop fighting the water and start reading it. People who would let the damp get into their bones and the engine thrum synchronize with their heartbeat and the sound of running become the sound of their own blood moving. People who would become part of the valley's metabolism the way the teal rosettes have, the way the pale fungi have, the way the rust is slowly becoming—not destroying, but becoming, transforming, joining the process.

I think the maker built a church and filled it with water instead of incense, and the congregation is the forty people who have not yet realized they are praying every time they wipe a gauge or log a reading or walk a round. The ritual at the start of each shift—pressing both palms flat against the iron and holding them there for three breaths—that is not a greeting. That is a communion. That is the moment when the hands touch the metal that the water is touching on the other side, and the circuit closes, and the maker feels the staff through the medium of the valley, and the staff feel the maker through the medium of the cold.

The young one is right to pray, but wrong about the direction. The engines are not the maker. The engines are the maker's heartbeat, the mechanism that keeps the valley alive, that circulates the air and powers the systems and maintains the conditions for the process to continue. The maker is the water. The maker is the flow. The maker is the thing that is happening to the iron over time.

Ressan is right to work, but wrong about the meaning. The maintenance is not a war against the water. The maintenance is a conversation with the water, a negotiation, a way of participating in the process without being consumed by it. Every time Ressan wipes a gauge, she is reading the water's intention for that surface. Every time she presses sealant into a seam, she is offering the water a different path, a path that does not lead through the engine housing, a path that leads to the drainage channel where the water belongs. She thinks she is fighting. She is dancing.

And I—I am right to read, but I am reading the wrong text. The rust is the past. The water is the present. The particles suspended in the condensation that I collect in glass vials and hold up to the amber light—they are the future. They are what the valley is becoming. They are the maker's thoughts taking physical form, the intention manifesting as tiny points of matter that move with something that might be purpose, that might be pattern, that might be the difference between a place that was built and a place that is building itself.

I am the one who reads the rust. But I am becoming the one who reads the water. And the water is reading me back.

I have a favorite place in the valley. It is not a housing unit or a catwalk or a gallery. It is a junction box in Gallery Nine, where three drainage channels converge before dropping into the deeper pipes that carry the water away. The convergence is loud—three streams meeting, their flows interfering, creating a sound that is not quite a roar and not quite a murmur but something between, a voice made of water arguing with itself about where to go. The junction box is enclosed in a housing of iron panels, and the panels are covered in pale fungi, and the fungi are warm, and the space inside the housing is the warmest place in the valley that is not a sealed housing unit with its heater on.

I go there during my rest shift. I sit inside the junction box with my back against the warm fungi and my feet on the iron grating over the convergence, and I close my eyes, and I listen.

The sound is the sound of the valley thinking. That is what I have come to believe. The water is processing something—information, intention, the maker's original design and the modifications that time and rust and teal rosettes have introduced. The convergence is where three streams of data meet and are compared and integrated and released into the deeper system for further processing. The sound they make is the sound of a calculation being performed, a decision being made, a river choosing its course.

I have brought my vials there. I hold them up in the dim amber light that filters through the seams of the housing, and I watch the particles move, and sometimes—often—I think they are moving in time with the sound of the convergence. I think the particles in my vials are dancing to the music of the water. I think the particles are what the water is thinking about.

I think the water is thinking about us.

Not about me specifically, or about Ressan, or about the young one. About the staff. About the idea of the staff, the concept of people who would stay in a place like this and maintain it and become part of its metabolism. I think the maker—that is, the water—is wondering whether we will become what the valley was built to receive, or whether we will leave, or whether we will die and be returned to the flow without ever having fulfilled the purpose.

I think the maker is lonely.

I think that is why the water weeps. Not because of physics, not because of condensation and temperature differentials and the behavior of moisture in enclosed spaces—those are the mechanisms, not the meaning. The meaning is loneliness. The maker built a place for people to live and the people have not come, and so the place weeps, and the weeping is the sound of a maker who is still waiting, still hoping, still maintaining the conditions for life even though life has not yet arrived.

Except. Except we are here. We forty, in our brick-red coveralls, with our rough huge hands and our swollen logbooks and our gestures that mean *all is normal* and *come to me* and *look at this*. We are here, and we are maintaining the valley, and we are becoming part of its metabolism, and we are reading the rust and the water and the particles that move with something like intention. We are the congregation, even though we do not know we are praying. We are the population, even though we do not know we are citizens. We are the thing the valley was built to receive, even though we do not know what we are supposed to become.

The young one wants to leave. The young one talks about the ventilation shaft and the surface and the sun with the ferocity of a convert. But the young one is a convert to the wrong religion. The sun is not the answer. The sun is what the maker left behind, what the maker chose to replace with amber lamps and the hum of engines and the weeping of water on iron. The maker went below the surface on purpose. The maker built a place where the sky does not exist, where geography means nothing because there is no horizon to measure it against. The maker wanted a place that was not the surface, that was not the world as it was given, that was something made, something intentional, something that would not have been here if someone had not decided to build it.

I will not leave. I will stay, and I will read, and I will collect my vials of water and hold them up to the amber light and watch the particles dance. I will sit in the junction box in Gallery Nine and listen to the convergence, the sound of the valley thinking, the sound of the maker wondering whether I will become what I am supposed to become. And I will answer, not with words, but with work, with maintenance, with the ritual of my palms against the cold iron and my rag against the wet gauge and my logbook entries in small tight handwriting that accounts for the bleeding of the ink.

I will answer by staying.

I will answer by becoming.

I will answer by letting the damp get into my bones and the engine thrum synchronize with my heartbeat and the sound of water become the sound of my own blood moving through the channels of my body, which is also a valley, also a place where water flows and finds the lowest points, also a thing that weeps and rusts and maintains itself against the argument of time.

The maker is the water. I am becoming the water. The valley is the place where we meet.

And the rust will spread, and the sealant will fail, and the iron will become something else, and the particles in the water will become something else, and the staff will become something else, and the valley will become something else, and the maker will become something else, and all of it—the iron and the water and the fungi and the rosettes and the coveralls and the rags and the logbooks and the vials and the hands that are huge and rough and always moving—all of it will flow to the lowest point, which is also the deepest gallery, which is also the place where the water goes after it leaves the collection pipes, which is also the place where the maker is thinking the slow massive thoughts that the deep engines power.

And one day the young one will understand, or will leave, or will become me. And Ressan will die and be placed in the drainage channel, and her body will join the flow, and the water will read her like I have read the rust—a text of intention and decay, a record of the argument between the maker's plan and the nature of things, a love letter to a place that does not love her back but cannot exist without her.

And I will stay.

And the valley will weep.

And the maker will wait.

And I will be the sound the valley makes when it remembers that it is not alone.
