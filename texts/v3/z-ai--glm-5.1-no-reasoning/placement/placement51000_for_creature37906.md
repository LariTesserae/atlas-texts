I am a doser.

Not the senior kind who walks the main feed-lines at dawn—those are thick-thumbed elders, their hands steady as the ceramic hearts in the forges, their eyes reading the vine-lattice like a city reads its clocks. I am younger, still light enough in the boots to walk the secondary rigging, the thin bright threads that span between the smaller groves where the borer-oxen graze and the children herd their mites. My route is the loop around the Nursery quarter, where the architecture is still a gangly teenager, all scaffolding and graft-tape and hopeful shoots.

I chose this—or more accurately, the material chose me for this—because I have a feel for pressure. The sap mains beat their slow two-pulse every thirty seconds, and I can feel the variation in that beat the way a musician hears a flat note. When the pressure is high, the lamp-vines swell and the light runs amber-bright; when it drops, they dim to a pale straw color, and the moths rise uncertain, their paper-wings rasping in confused circles. My job is to keep the light honest. To knock the valves in the right order, at the right time, so that the manufactured dawn spreads stain-wise across the lattice the way it's supposed to—rings of brightness expanding from each feed-point until, an hour later, the whole ceiling is a white morning.

But I am getting ahead of myself. Let me tell you what it is to *be* a doser in Firstgrove.

You come to consciousness in the dark, twenty minutes before dawn. The ceiling is ember-traces, the feed-lines glowing dull orange in their rows. The air is cold—colder than the ten-degree average, because the stillwoods have been hardening off in the dim month the dosers' guild scheduled for the old grove, and the whole town has contracted around that decision. You lie in your grafted bed—grown from the same wall as the shelf and the doorframe, the wood warm where it touches the heat-tree's root system—and you listen to the town tick. The sound of wood cooling is the sound of Firstgrove settling into itself. It is the most honest sound I know.

You dress in the dark. Not because you can't knock the valve by your bed and bring up the light, but because the dark belongs to the town. It was here before you and it will be here after. You pull on your root-mat boots, soft and slightly warm, and you feel the sap mains through the soles: thump... thump... thump. A heartbeat for a body made of groves and gravel and resin.

You eat standing up—hard biscuit of compressed sap-feed, a sliver of sweet pitch dissolving under your tongue. The food here is not about pleasure. It is about fuel. Your body is a closed system, like the salt lungs along the cavern walls. You take in what you need and you vent it slowly, and the dryness of you is a kind of devotion to the dryness of the place.

You step outside.

The street is a root mat, grown inch by inch over the raw gravel until it is soft and springy. The lamp line overhead is a thin bright thread in the black, and you stand under it and feel the immensity of the Long Room pressing down. The ceiling is three hundred metres up, but you can't see it in the pre-dawn dim. You can only see the line of light, and beyond it, nothing. The fallows. The dark miles.

You walk the thread.

This is the part that people born aboveground never understand. They hear "cavern" and think "enclosure." They think walls and ceilings and a feeling of being shut in. But the Long Room is not like that. It is a valley turned inside out, and the darkness between the groves is as wide and empty as any sky. Standing on the lamp road, looking out into the unlit country, you feel the same vertigo you'd feel on a cliff edge—except the drop is horizontal, and it goes on forever.

I have never seen what lives out there. I have felt it. A slow pressure in the dust, rising through my boots, and then nothing. The stillhunters at the edge of the light—they are flat grey animals, pretending to be slabs, and they are patient as furniture—I have watched them take moths that crossed too low, and the snap is so fast you can't see it. Just a moth, and then not a moth. What lives beyond the stillhunters, I don't know. I know the rule: travel the lit lines. The dark between the groves belongs to other things.

The walk to my first valve takes fifteen minutes along the lamp road, and in that time I pass two family knots heading to the warm crowns. They walk with shoulders slightly hunched against the vacancy, and they nod to me—slow, unhurried—and I nod back. We do not speak. There is too much quiet to violate.

The Nursery quarter is my favorite part of the loop. It is where the town is being born, and you can feel it—the straining of the stillwood saplings, the creak of the trellises, the faint green smell of growth undercut by the turpentine bite of resin. The rooms here are half-formed, walls growing around their frames like children filling out clothes, furniture grafted in place but still small, still reaching. I have stood inside a room that was being built and watched the wood grow. It is the slowest thing I have ever seen, and the most powerful.

I knock the first valve with the heel of my hand.

The valve is a thickened node on the feed-line, a gall grown around a ceramic core, and when you strike it the right way, it opens. Sap rushes through—amber, sweet, smelling of struck flint and hot wax—and the lamp-vines around the feed-point brighten. A ring of light expands. The moths in the upper lattice stir, their wings rasping like pages turning. I can feel the sap-pressure change in my boots: the mains' two-beat quickens almost imperceptibly, and the whole town takes a breath.

I walk to the next valve. Knock. Another ring of light. The manufactured dawn spreads.

By the time I finish my loop, the ceiling is a white morning. The air has warmed from the light—still cool, still dry, but livable now—and the town is awake. The children run along the lamp roads with their wax-tipped wands, herding red mites in slow migrating runs. The tappers walk the rows with their buckets in the dimming, collecting the amber drip. The borer-oxen stand under the heat-trees, patient as furniture, bark-hides warm from the internal combustion of the trunks.

And I go home, or to the warm crown of my family's heat-tree, and I sit on the grafted bench and press my back against the blood-warm wood and feel the town pulse around me.

Thump... thump... thump.

I am twenty-seven feeds old. That is how we measure time here—not in days, but in the cycles of the sap. Each feed is a dawn and a dusk, a waking and a sleeping, a breath taken by the body of the town. I have been a doser for six hundred and twelve feeds. Before that, I was a child herder, running along the lamp roads with my wax-tipped wand, learning the grammar of the mites. Before that, I was a child, soft and small and precious, the only truly soft thing for miles.

I remember being soft. It is a strange memory, like remembering a different kind of weather. My skin is not soft anymore. It is tight over the knuckles, shiny at the elbows with resinous sweat, and when I bleed, I bleed amber. My hair grows in a dark mat, and I tend it the way I tend the streets—pruning it, training it, sometimes grafting a chip of bright stone into the architecture of my scalp. I smell of turpentine and minerals, of cold stone and warm sap. When I laugh, it is a quiet wheeze, expelled through the nose, warm and internal.

I am becoming the town.

This is not a metaphor. This is what the material wants to do. The stillwood saplings grow into rooms, and the rooms grow into halls, and the halls outgrow their families and become public. The streets are the spaces the trees agreed to leave. The root mats pave them, inch by inch, over the raw gravel. And the people—I have watched the elders sit so still under the heat-trees that I mistook them for grafted furniture. I have seen the founders at the main crossroads, fixed in pitch and wax, slowly going amber, their arms sometimes trained around new saplings. They are not dead in the way aboveground people are dead. They are *set*. They are the foundation that the next layer grows from.

I am not ready to be set. I am still in my growing phase, still light enough in the boots to walk the secondary rigging. But I can feel the future in my joints—a stiffness, a deposit of pitch, the beginnings of girth. Age in Firstgrove is not a wearing-down. It is a building-up. I am accumulating layers, like bark, like resin, like the fine flour-dust that falls and stays where it falls. I am becoming denser.

And this is what I love about this place: the clarity of it. The knowledge that everything I do is preserved. A bad graft is a permanent scar. A dropped torch could consume the air in a flash of sweet smoke. There is no rain to wash away mistakes, no frost to crack them, no wind to stir them. What I do here is what I am here, and what I am here is what I will become—a dark lantern, an ember in the black, a point of green and wax burning on its own.

But that is not for a long time. Right now, it is an hour before dusk, and the tappers are walking the rows with their buckets, and the moths are rising in columns to the last bright thread, and the town is ticking as it cools. I am sitting in the warm crown of my family's heat-tree, and my mother is beside me—older, denser, her skin thick with preserved layers—and we are sharing hard biscuit and sweet pitch, and we are not speaking, because there is too much quiet to violate.

Outside, the dark miles stretch between the groves, and something heavy walks where the lamp lines end. But here, under the manufactured sky, under the amber-white glow of the ceiling lattice, the town is alive and growing. The borer-oxen lean against the heat-tree. A child runs past with a wax-tipped wand, herding mites. The cutters grind the south face, opening the cavern one more inch, one more season, one more feed.

Firstgrove sleeps the way children sleep—quietly, at a slight angle, and growing the whole time.

And I am growing with it. I am the stuff that grows. I am a doser, a knot of wax and resin on the bright thread, a hand that knocks the valves and brings the dawn. I am the third kingdom after the machines that bore and the roots that finish, and I am happy to be here, inside the beginning, inside the body of the town, inside the Long Room that has no walls you can see from the middle.

I am a point of light in the black.

I am an ember in the fire.

I am the heartbeat.
