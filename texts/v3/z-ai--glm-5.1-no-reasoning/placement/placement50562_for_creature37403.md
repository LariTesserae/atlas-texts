# A Hundred Days Loud

**I.**

You hear the island before you see it. A hundred days ago there was nothing here but grey water and the old cold of the deep; then the sea stood up. What stands now is a mountain still deciding on its shoulders — a ring of black rock around a bowl of ocean, screaming.

From a distance the sound is mistaken for weather: a storm pinned to one spot on the horizon, roaring without lightning. Closer, it resolves into a single sustained note with a storm wrapped around it, the way a bell's note has a rim of noise around the tone. There is no surf on the island's shores. There is nothing to make surf with; the rocks are days old. The roar comes up from inside the mountain, from under the water, and it never stops to breathe.

The island is mostly sea. Steam leans off the rim like a tree bent by a wind from inside the earth. Pumice rides the swells around it in rafts, grey and light as bread. The water near shore is warm as a held hand. No gulls. No whales. The island is quarantined by its own noise, and the ocean gives it room.

**II.**

Everything on the rim is an edge. The rock is young enough to remember being liquid — basalt gone glassy at the crust, cinder the colour of a struck match, ropey lava frozen mid-thought. Nothing here has been rounded. No rain has had the years to file the sharpness down; rain slides off these slopes without a single victory. Every stone is a fresh decision, and lifting one wrong takes your skin.

You are standing at the top of a mile of mountain. Most of the altitude is below you — the drowned mile the island rose on — and the crater it carries on its crown is a flooded one: a hundred and forty paces across, a deep pool of the ocean wearing a stone collar. Seawater came in through a notch in the south wall on the first day, before the rock was cool, and the mountain, still choosing a shape, chose a bowl, and the bowl chose the sea.

On the rim's flat crown, ash lies soft as flour, and in the ash are handprints. A score of them: palms, fingers, the cup of each palm's base, pressed while the ash was warm enough to take an imprint. They face the open sea. Whoever made them is gone — no boats, no bones, nothing — and the prints point outward, as if at something arriving, or at something being waved away.

Fifty paces on, out of the steam, stands the door: two uprights and a lintel of the same newborn basalt, fitted by hands, with no walls, no building, no purpose a room would recognize. A door to nothing, facing the water. In its lintel the mountain has left its hair — strands of golden glass drawn fine as thread and blown from the burning rock, snagged and hanging. Glass hair on a doorway nobody owns.

**III.**

Below the lintel the ground drops in steps. Six are cut into the rock, wide as sills, descending to the water of the bowl; whoever made them meant them to be used. There are more steps under the water. No two descents agree on how many.

The water is blood-warm. It takes you at the collarbones and holds — the bowl is heated from beneath, vent-fed, a fever the mountain is running and winning. The light down there is dim, milky, dusk-blue: the mountain spent its first hundred days shedding minerals into its own bath, and the water still holds them, a bloom of newborn glass the sun arrives tired through. At noon the floor of the bowl gets one hour of plain light. The rest is dusk under a ceiling of steam.

Visibility runs an arm's length, two. Rock comes out of the murk as sudden architecture — bulbous jointed pillows of lava, fields of shards whose edges shine even here, banks of black glass still uncured, warm to the palm as a flank. Nothing on the floor has ever been weathered. Nothing has ever been touched.

**IV.**

The bowl's floor is a field of throats. Chimneys of young mineral: thin black pipes, milky spires, mouths exhaling hot smoke into water and water into steam. None is older than the island. Some are a day old. They migrate — a chimney dies here, another opens thirty paces off, in a night.

And they are loud. Not loud the way a storm is loud, which arrives and passes, but loud the way a fact is: constant, structural, prior to you. In the air on the rim you must shout into ears, and your shout is a rumour. In the water there is no such thing as hearing, only being heard through. Sound in water doesn't knock; it comes in. It arrives in the bones of the jaw, shakes the fluid behind the eyes, plays the ribs like a rack of panpipes. You do not listen to the bowl. The bowl plays you.

The roar is not senseless. It holds a chord — a fundamental so low it is felt as ground, harmonics trading slowly above it, and around everything a fuzz of cracks, collapses, gas. The bowl is a bell. The tide is its clapper. Twice a day the sea leans in through the notch, the water inside rises a fathom, and the mountain rings — a note beneath the note, under the floor of hearing, in the rock itself. It is a small bell yet, young-throated. Every week the mountain thickens, and the note slides lower, a voice going down.

Where the vents scream hardest, the water flashes: small blue-white blooms at the chimney mouths, one per scream — light squeezed out of noise. On the darkest afternoons the vent field glows like a sky at the bottom of the world. And above the loudest floor the surface forgets how to be random: it stands up in figures — ridges, cells, nets, the old geometry that sound draws on any skin it is given — and holds them as long as the roar holds.

**V.**

Reality is a habit, and habits loosen under a noise like this. In the innermost ring of the din, matter loses its conviction. Threads of water climb off the pool and walk into the rain. Squalls cross the rim as moving walls of grey, and where a wall passes over the loud water the rain beneath it falls wrong — upward, feet first, bright threads standing out of the sea and stepping into the cloud.

An echo here can arrive before its cause. A shout returns before your lungs have finished it.

And the sound is too strong for matter to stay simple. Above a certain loudness the world must choose a shape, and here it keeps choosing. Silt condenses into a fish — black sand taking on fins, a body, a bad idea of swimming — and swims for ninety seconds and remembers it was never anything. A bird of steam crosses the bowl and is gone before it finishes being a bird. Once, the mountain tried something the size of a person: stood it up out of glass and wet ash on the drowned stair, held it there while it moved — not walked, but *was walked* — and let it go. It was not a person. It was the mountain's first draft of one.

This is the first law of the place: nothing here is finished; nothing here has decided what it is. A hundred days old, and the whole island is rehearsal — a throat clearing before some first word the world has not yet said.

**VI.**

Life here follows quiet the way life elsewhere follows water, and the ecology is drawn in rings of loudness: nothing in the roar's eye; gardens on the ledges where the sound drops below pain; pioneers on the rim.

The gardens are white. Sulphur-weed grows on the vent-warmed shelves in pale beards and lawns — not plants but the oldest of lives, fat on the smoke, the same white the abyss grows around its own burnings. The mountain rose out of the deep carrying its neighbours on its shoulders: this is a deep-sea garden promoted into the top of the world, still lit by no light it was ever built for. Currents send larvae in through the notch on the flood; most die in the din; a few settle the garden ledges. Whatever this island's future is, it is being decided by which of them can bear the noise.

One crab lives at the third chimney — white, small-palmed, grazing the mats, its mouthparts working ceaselessly, as if translating. It treats the roar as ordinary, and in that sense is the island's only true resident. Below it, circling the vents at a depth even tired light can't reach, is a fish the deep sent up by accident: blind, silver, an abyssal citizen carried a mile on the rising rock. It circles the warmth like someone pacing the one lit room of a house that burned. When a whale passes along its old road outside, you learn of it in the chest before the ear — a slow pressure through the rock — and then it is gone, because the whales give the island room. To them it must sound like a whale itself: one too big, too near, and wrong.

And on the fourth drowned step, where a seam of vent-warm water crosses the stone, a single green thread: the first true alga. The first green anything this island has made or received. In a country of black glass, white sulphur and grey steam, it is the only colour that has decided what it is.

**VII.**

The place runs on a turbulence you cannot set a watch by.

The bowl breathes with the tide, but late — the mountain keeps its own time — the water lagging the moon, overshooting, dropping back, like a sleeper breathing out of step with the surf he sleeps beside. When the sea leans in, the whole bowl's chord deepens; when it drains, the roar climbs. Twice a day the island changes key.

Under the water, a whale-back of new lava is rising in the north half of the bowl — black, hot, the week's work — and the bowl shrinks by a hand's width a day. Pumice rafts drift on the surface like scum on a cauldron, merge, are rained apart, sink in twos and threes. Chimneys die and are born overnight; a garden ledge is a furlong of quiet one week and a furnace of scream the next; and the crab, when its chimney dies, is found a day later at the next one, listening. Squall cells cross the rim in minutes. The steam column leans, stands straight, leans.

**VIII.**

Give the island ten thousand years and the sea will teach it quiet. Rain will file the glass-edges round. The roar will wear down to surf. The chimneys will clog, the white lawns will go over to green, and one day this scream will be remembered only as the hiss of a shingle beach. Every landscape's loudest moment is its first one; what you hear standing on the rim, hands over your ears and ribs playing, is the only childhood a world gets.

Which is, perhaps, why the door. Two uprights and a lintel facing the sea, with no building, because none is needed. The people came in the ash-warm first weeks and pressed their hands into the soft ground, facing outward, saying what they were leaving room for. The door is not for people. The door is for whatever the mountain finally says — the shape that, some morning, survives its own rehearsal, comes up the drowned steps — six, or nine, or however many there are that day — through the water, out of the fever, and walks through.
