Begin with what will not leave you alone. Begin with the ones you can hear.

The speakers. The first inhabitants you meet are not the ones you see but the ones sounding in your marrow: the chimeras, the trilobite-chrome fauna, each individual a small dry ticking of mandible on glass. One of them alone is a fingertap on a countertop. A million of them are weather. And the crucial thing—the thing that takes a long time to understand—is that the clicking is not noise generated by work. The clicking *is* the work. Everyone here is speaking, all the time, because speech is maintenance. A surface unspoken-for is a surface unclaimed, and a surface unclaimed will be dissolved and reassigned within the hour.

Their language, as near as you can tell, is positional. A continuous roll call: *this fracture is found, this film is mine, this one is me, this one is me, this one is me.* To have a name here is to have a location. To be moved is to be renamed. When you cannot hear structure in the roar, that is your failing, not theirs—the way a person who doesn't know a language hears only static where a conversation is running. The sound-ocean that makes conversation impossible for *you* is, to them, legible from end to end: a parliament that never adjourns, a census that never concludes, held in the resonant glass itself so that the walls carry every statement to every district at once. The bedrock is their telephone network. Nothing here is silent because everyone here is always announcing where they are, which is the same as announcing that they exist.

The hatched and the molting. They breed from the walls. The weeping micro-fractures—the same pores that bleed the water out—are nurseries; the young are aquatic at first, soft and matte-gray, living in the films and the puddles that pool on every horizontal surface, so that every table in this world is also a maternity ward. They graze on the mineral-rich condensation: the city's sweat is their milk. They grow by adding shell, ring on ring, like trees made of bearings, and they molt, and the molted shells are harvested and spun back into slurry. Nothing here dies so much as it gets reassigned. The dead, and everything else, wash down the imperceptible tilt toward the basins, and what happens to them there is a subject we will reach by going down.

Their castes are not assigned at birth but at first contact: whatever a newly hardened adult crawls across first fixes its vocation. The ones that first touch a fracture become menders. The ones that first touch a walkway's dripping underside become the couriers, skating the water films at terrible speed, carrying nothing anyone can see—carrying, perhaps, alignment, the way a nerve impulse carries no cargo you could weigh. And then there are the readers: the ones who do nothing at all but stand still on the veins of iridescent light, absorbing the flicker, and then travel, and then pass the flicker on. The readers are the interface between the small clicking speech and whatever the flicker is a speech *of*.

The individuals. Watch long enough and the swarm stops being a swarm. There is the mender that repairs the same arch again and again long past any need, as if it is fond of the arch, as if the arch is its name and it is refusing to be renamed. There are the two couriers that run the same elevated span in tandem for no logistical reason, day after day, meeting in the middle. There is the one that positions itself where the fractured rainbows move across the wet plaza and follows them all day, contributing nothing the census values. And the spires—the ones that rise in minutes and are cannibalized within the hour—are not waste and not error. Look at the styles. A district's spires change idiom the way accents drift. The spires are arguments. Some of them are songs. A world this turbulent is not chaotic; it is *conversational*, and the architecture is what the conversation looks like when it solidifies between turns.

The quiet inhabitants. The iridescent veins are not infrastructure. They are thought. The district-minds have no bodies, no mouths, no names that click—they have weather. A decision made by one arrives as a geyser from a pressure valve, a program of dissolution, a spire commissioned and then, an hour later, regretted and eaten. They never speak; the roar that defines this world is the sound of their body's metabolism, not their voice. Whatever they say, they say it in city. When the light shifts in the walls, that is not routing energy. That is a mood changing, a mind reconsidering a plaza.

The deep ones. Follow the water. It all goes somewhere—the tilt guarantees it, the drainage basins promise it—and at the bottom of that continental whisper of a slope, under the haze, live the oldest inhabitants. They are pale. That is the first shocking thing about them; they are the only pale things in a black glass world. They are indefinite in outline, slow beyond the possibility of boredom, and they do one thing: they filter-drink the world's runoff—its dissolved districts, its shell-rings, its dead speakers, its dead readers—and they secrete the raw mineral slurry that the unseen fabricators spin back into walls. The loop is the point. Everything eaten downward is dreamed upward. The swarms will not cross the basin margins. The readers turn back kilometers before them. Nothing is named there because nothing there has a position, only a duration. Perhaps they are the founders in some dissolved, sustained form, a citizenship concentrated like broth. Perhaps they are the world's memory wearing bodies as an afterthought. The readers will not tell you. The readers may not know.

The one who is someone. And yet, of all that lives here, the only true individual—the only inhabitant with what you would recognize as a *life*—is the water. Everything else holds a post. The water takes a journey. Consider a single bead: exuded from a fracture in a megastructure in the northern haze, drunk by a fingernail-sized mender, breathed back out, condensed on the underside of a mile-long walkway, dripped, pooled, skimmed into a film-river, carried down the tilt for years in the dark, lifted again by heat and machinery, exhaled into the fog, rained onto a plaza, gathered, fed on, carried in the body of a courier across a district, wept from a wall. The water has been every inhabitant. It has passed through every role, every caste, every organ of this place. It touches everything and is touched by everything and it keeps what it touches, which means the water is the archive—the only continuous witness, cycling through the whole city like a soul through lives. If you want to know what this world remembers, you would have to ask the water. And the water cannot say. The water can only go around again.

As for who built it. Nothing here will tell you, and the honest answer is that nothing here is keeping that information anywhere a question could reach. But note the bedrock, if you want a speculation you can stand on: a black vitreous substance, grown, not quarried, porous, weeping. Perhaps every slab is an ancestor. Perhaps a people, when they could no longer be anything else, became architecture, and the walls exude water for hydraulic reasons, or perhaps the weeping is exactly what it looks like. The walls will not correct you either way.

And you, standing at the edge of it—the fauna have already assessed you. Something is measuring the condensation forming on your sleeve. Something dog-sized and gleaming has paused to decide whether your shoulder is a surface worth repaving. You are not a visitor here; you are *terrain*, an anomaly of texture, and within the hour you will be fed on, measured, and possibly repaired. The mist beads on your skin and drips off, and in that moment you were part of the one inhabitant's route. For a moment, you were a place.

Who lives here? Everyone, in shifts. Nothing in this world is ever finished being built and nothing in it is ever finished being alive. Ask the question out loud and the place will answer the only way it can, the way it has been answering since before there was a memory of silence: with the roar. It is not noise. It has never been noise. It is the ongoing sound of everyone here saying their own name—which is their location—over and over, forever, at a volume that assumes you, too, have something to declare.
