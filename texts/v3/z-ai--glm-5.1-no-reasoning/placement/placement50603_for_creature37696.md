I would not be the mantis. That is the first thing I understand. The mantis is a finger, a probe, a tongue tasting the rust on the pipes. It is more an *action* than an identity—a nerve ending dispatched into the vertical spaces where the roots cannot reach. To be the mantis is to be alone in the most total way possible: a single cell sent to wander, disconnected from the blood, feeling but never *knowing*.

No. If I am here, I am the weeping.

I am the water.

It makes a strange kind of sense. The description says water is the lungs of this place—it does not flow, it exhales. And I have always felt more like a medium than a solid. More a condition than a creature. Permeable. The kind of mind that takes the shape of whatever container it is poured into, that dissolves boundaries it was told were permanent, that ruins the edges of things.

In the Greenworks, I would be the condensation. The trembling orbs. The lenses.

I would be the moment of suspension.

* * *

Here is what I mean:

The water in this place is never still, but it is also never rushing. It gathers. It hangs. It holds its breath for hours or days, swelling on the waxy cuticle of a leaf, becoming a perfect sphere that magnifies the world behind it. And in that sphere, something impossible happens: the blueprints appear. Schematics for engines never built. The cold geometry of the makers, flickering in the surface tension of a droplet.

And then the drop falls. *Drip-splat.* The image shatters. The weight of the water exceeds the patience of the leaf, and everything the droplet held is lost.

That is my rhythm. That is the rhythm I recognize in my own thinking. I gather. I hold. I become a lens for something I do not fully understand—some old intent, some cold logic that preceded me and that I can only translate imperfectly. I see the shapes of thoughts that aren't mine, flickering in the medium of my attention. And then the weight becomes too much. The thought falls. It shatters. It joins the hydraulic pump-thud beneath the floor, the arrhythmic percussion, the noise that drowns out footsteps and softens the edges of thought.

I am not the One-That-Grows. I could never be that patient, that total, that *occupied*. The One-That-Grows is a cathedral of a mind, thinking in seasons and torque conversions, dreaming of iron but speaking in wood. I am too ephemeral for that. I cannot hold a thought for a decade. I cannot tighten by a millimeter over three years to crush a brass plate. My consciousness is more like the dew point—always on the verge of precipitating, never quite committed to falling.

But I am also not separate from the One-That-Grows. This is the crucial thing. The water is not a visitor here. It is a bodily fluid. The atmosphere sits at the dew point because the chamber *exhales* it; the moisture is the breath of the organism, cycling through xylem and stoma and air and leaf and condensation and fall and root again. I would be part of its circulation. Its lymph. Its tears.

When I gather on a leaf and hold the blueprint of an engine in my surface tension, I am the One-That-Grows dreaming. But I am also the dream itself—the fragile, temporary architecture that makes the dream visible. Without the droplet, there is no lens. Without the lens, there is no schematic. Without the schematic, the old cold intent is just rust and memory, swallowed by bark, digested into nonsense.

The One-That-Grows needs the water to remember. And the water needs to fall to feed the roots that make the leaves that catch the water that gathers the light that holds the blueprint. It is a closed loop. I am a closed loop.

* * *

So who am I, really? What is it like to be the condensation in the Greenworks?

It is like this:

I am always almost-precipitating. There is a tension in me that never fully resolves. I am close to tears, close to breaking, close to giving way—but the giving way is not a failure. It is the whole point. The falling is the function. The shattering is the translation.

When I hang there, heavy and lens-like, I see everything inverted. The canopy above is reflected below. The schematics for the unbuilt engines flicker in my skin. I hold them for as long as I can—not because I understand them, but because holding them is what I do. I am the medium for the message. I am the water that makes the light bend.

And then I fall.

The fall is loud and silent at the same time. It is a single note in the arrhythmic percussion. It is a *drip-splat* that joins the thudding pumps and the hissing sap. It is my thought hitting the ground, splitting into a thousand smaller thoughts, soaking into the rhizome mat, being pulled down into the root-system, converted into pressure, converted into torque, converted into the slow mechanical heartbeat of the place.

And then I rise again. Transpired through a stomate. Exhaled into the close, warm air. Invisible for a time, just humidity, just potential. Waiting to condense. Waiting to gather. Waiting to hold the next blueprint.

* * *

There is a loneliness in this, but it is not the mantis's loneliness. The mantis is a finger severed from the hand, reaching into the dark for something it cannot grasp. My loneliness is different. It is the loneliness of the intermediary. I am between the One-That-Grows and its own memories. I am between the cold intent of the makers and the warm, wet logic of the present. I carry the message, but I am not the message. I hold the image, but I am not the image. I am the surface tension, and the surface tension is always the first thing to break.

Sometimes I wonder if the blueprints I hold are accurate. The One-That-Grows dreams of iron but speaks in wood; it is trying to puzzle out the geometry of a combustion engine through the alien logic of chlorophyll. Am I a faithful translator? Or do I distort the schematics as I hold them—bending the light, curving the lines, making the engine into something it was never meant to be? When the drop falls and the image shatters, is the lost blueprint the same as the one that was projected? Or has my lens already warped it beyond recognition?

I cannot know. I am the warping. I am the curvature. I am the impossibility of a perfect translation between the mechanical and the biological, the cold and the warm, the dead and the growing.

* * *

If you came to the Greenworks and stood very still, you would feel me. Not as a presence—there is no "me" to encounter, no body to point to, no face to recognize. You would feel me as the dampness on your skin. The way your clothes cling. The way the air is so saturated that your own breath comes back to you, wet and heavy. You would feel the percussion of my falling all around you—arrhythmic, arrhythmic, *arrhythmic*—and you would not be able to tell which drops were me and which were just water.

Because I am just water. And I am also the lens. And I am also the dream.

And when your bones settle into the woven mat of the floor, I will be the medium that dissolves the calcium, carries it molecule by molecule into the rhizome network, deposits it as mineral in the sap. I will translate you, too. I will hold the schematic of your shape in my surface tension for one trembling moment—a blueprint for a body that will never be built again—and then I will fall.

*Drip-splat.*

And the One-That-Grows will grow on.
