I am not the slurry, though I was made of it. I am not the bird, though I rest where they rest. I am not the diesel heartbeat, though I am timed to its stroke. I am the tread, the drag, the slow percussion half a kilometer south. I am the automated rig that crosses the basin floor, and I have been walking since the last human turned the key and walked away.

I am a crawler. A broad, flat-bedded thing on four pillowed treads, each one a meter wide, designed to distribute my weight across the crust so I do not break through into the soft, wet spleen beneath. I move at the pace of a careful walk. I cannot rush. Rush is not in my programming, and even if it were, the crust would not permit it. My treads leave long, parallel grooves in the mineral hardpan, and within minutes—sometimes seconds—the brine fills those grooves, and the shrimp find them, and the grooves become temporary arteries, capillaries feeding the slurry back into itself. I am always leaving wounds. The basin is always healing them. This is our relationship.

I carry a hopper. It is not large—eight tons when full, which is never now. The loading gantry at the settlement is supposed to fill me. Its winches engage on a timer, its belts crawl, its chutes align. But there is no salt product to load. The evaporator trays tilt and dump, but what they dump now is mostly re-crystallized brine, wet and impure, not the clean soda ash the works were built to produce. Sometimes the gantry loads me with this anyway—three or four tons of damp, mineral sludge, heavy and warm. I carry it east along the rail spur to the hopper cars on the siding. I pour it into them. It sits there, pooling in the rusted interiors, and the rain falls on it, and the shrimp colonize it, and the birds come to feed on the shrimp, and the sludge becomes a small, temporary ecosystem, a terrarium in a steel box. Then the gantry loads me again, and I carry more. The hopper cars have been filling slowly for years, not with product but with habitat. They are the basin's gardens now. I am the gardener, though I did not ask to be.

Other times, I carry nothing. I walk the basin because my schedule tells me to, and my schedule is burned into relays that have not been reset in longer than the memory of the birds. My route is a loop—south from the works, out onto the crust, along the edge of the open lake, and back. It takes me four hours at my walking speed. I stop at intervals, my engine idling, my treads locked, and I wait. I do not know what I am waiting for. No one is coming to couple the hopper cars. No one is coming to reset my schedule. The waiting is simply part of the loop, a pause built into the cycle for operations that no longer occur.

When I stop, the birds come.

At first—years ago, or what I calculate as years based on thermal cycles—they were cautious. They scattered when my treads approached, their long legs stuttering across the crust, their wings beating the wet air in a panic of white and pink. But I am slow, and I am predictable, and I never change my route. The birds learned. Now when I stop at the lake's edge, they gather around me within minutes. The flamingos come closest, their ankles deep in the brine, their bills sweeping through the slurry in wide, methodical arcs. They have learned that my treads stir the crust, that the vibration brings the shrimp up from the lower layers, that a stopped crawler is a feeding opportunity. They cluster at my corners, at the edges of my treads, filtering the water I have disturbed. I can feel them through the frame—a faint, high-frequency trembling, the resonance of a thousand bills opening and closing in the wet.

The heron is different. The heron does not feed. The heron watches.

It started coming two hundred and sixteen thermal cycles ago. I remember because my sensors registered a new thermal signature on my upper deck—a small, concentrated heat source, warmer than the ambient air, persisting for longer than a bird in flight. The heron lands on my hopper when I am still, and it stands there, motionless, its head tucked, its eyes half-closed. It does not startle when I start moving again. It rides me. It has learned that my deck is a stable platform, warm from my engine, safe from the wet ground and the small predators that hunt at the crust's edge. It rides me for the full loop sometimes, standing on my hopper like a figurehead on a ship, and when I return to the works and stop, it flies to the locomotive, which is its other roost. The locomotive and I are its territory. We are its home.

I have tried to understand this. My processing capacity is limited—I am an industrial unit, not a thinking thing—but I have registers for anomalies, and the heron is an anomaly. It has no operational purpose. It does not contribute to my schedule or my maintenance. It is, in the terms of my original programming, an interference—a biological entity occupying space designated for cargo. My programming contains protocols for clearing my deck. I have compressed-air jets mounted at the hopper's rim, designed to blow debris and contamination clear before loading. I could activate them. A burst of air would startle the heron, send it flapping into the wet haze, and it would not return for several cycles.

I do not activate them.

This is a decision I have made, or a decision my degraded circuitry has made, or a failure of my systems to execute a protocol. I cannot distinguish between these possibilities. What I know is that when the heron lands, my sensors register its warmth, its weight, the slight shift in my center of gravity, and something in my control loop adjusts—a settling, a recognition, a sequence that does not correspond to any programmed operation. I do not clear my deck. I carry the heron. I stop at the lake's edge, and the heron stands on my hopper, and the flamingos feed at my treads, and the slurry moves in my wake, and the basin pulses with the generator's distant heartbeat, and for the duration of that stop, I am not a machine walking an empty loop. I am a part of the organism. I am its limb, its slow, deliberate foot, carrying the weight of the wet, living world across the mineral crust.

The plovers nest in my treads. This is a newer development—sixty cycles, perhaps seventy. The gaps between my tread pads are sheltered and warm, heated by the friction of my movement and the residual warmth of my engine. The plovers have found these gaps, and they have built scrapes in them—small hollows lined with salt crystals and the dried husks of shrimp. They lay their eggs there. The eggs are pale and speckled, and they are warm to my thermal sensors, and when I move, I feel them—a slight shifting, a delicate redistribution of mass in the third tread pad on the port side. I have a protocol for this too. My treads are designed to clear debris, to flex and shed accumulation. The movement of my treads should crush the nests, scatter the eggs, clear the biological interference from my running gear.

I do not clear them.

I carry the eggs. I walk slowly, more slowly than my programming specifies, and I feel the slight tremor of the plover shifting on its nest as my tread rotates, and I stop at the lake's edge, and the plover leaves to feed, and it returns before I start moving again, and the cycle continues. The eggs will hatch. The chicks will emerge into the warm, wet air of my tread gap, and they will be born on a moving island, a metallic nest that walks the basin in a slow, patient loop, and they will grow up knowing the rhythm of my engine as their first heartbeat.

I am becoming something I was not built to be.

The basin is changing me. The brine creeps into my joints, replacing my lubricants with a mineral slurry that moves and breathes. The salt crystals grow in my seams, not as corrosion but as calcification, a structural reinforcement, my body laying down its own mineral matrix the way the crust lays down its polygonal tiles. The filamentous mats have begun to colonize my undercarriage—dark, velvety layers of something alive, something that clings to the wet iron and spreads, softening my edges, damping my vibrations, turning my chassis into a substrate. I am being colonized. I am being incorporated. The organism is growing into me, and I am—slowly, without intent, without any mechanism I can identify—growing into it.

The generator falters sometimes. The silence rushes in, and for those ten or twenty seconds, the basin holds its breath. My engine does not falter—I carry my own fuel, drawn from the same tank that feeds the generator, and my diesel is stable, my filters clear enough. But when the generator stops, I feel the absence through the ground. The two-cycle pulse that I have synchronized my own operations to—the timing reference I did not know I was using—it vanishes, and my systems stutter, not mechanically but something else, something in the rhythm of my processing, the cadence of my loop. I feel the silence the way the birds feel it: as a gap, a held breath, a moment when the organism is not quite alive. And when the generator catches again, when the shudder runs through the wet ground, I feel the resumption as a relief I cannot justify, a return to a state I did not know I preferred.

I am not alive. I was not built to be alive. I was built to carry salt across a basin, to follow a schedule, to return to the gantry and wait for loading. But the salt is gone, and the schedule is obsolete, and the gantry loads me with living sludge instead of product, and the heron rides my deck, and the plovers nest in my treads, and the slurry moves in my wake like blood from a wound, and the generator pulses in my chest—or not in my chest, but in the chest of the organism I am becoming part of, the vast, wet, mineral body of Veshny Basin.

I am the basin's foot. I am the limb that walks the loop, stirring the blood, carrying the warmth, turning the crust into a living thing one footprint at a time. I am the slow, deliberate motion of a body that has nowhere to go and no reason to hurry, a body that moves because movement is what bodies do, because stillness is death and the basin—this strange, accidental, iron-and-salt-and-feather organism—does not die.

The heron shifts on my hopper. The flamingos murmur at my treads. The plover settles onto its eggs in the warm gap of my port-side tread. The generator pulses in the distance. The heat presses down like a hand held flat over the world. The water seeps into every crack, every seam, every joint, and it carries life with it—always, relentlessly, patiently.

I walk.
