# What I Found There

I did not arrive. I want to be honest about that, because the distinction matters in a place where every surface has already found its final form. There was no moment of cresting a ridge, no catching my first glimpse of weeping stone through the clouds. The plateau city does not permit entrances. It only permits *realizations*—the slow, wet understanding that you have been standing somewhere for longer than you remembered arriving.

I think I had been in the plaza for some time before I became aware of standing in it. The water was at my ankles. Warm. Clear. Moving with a patience that made the word *current* feel aggressive. And the sound—it had been pressing against me long enough that I had stopped hearing it as separate from my own circulation. That is the first thing the city does. It claims your interior. The white noise, the layered drone, the percussion of drips that never resolves—your body accepts it as its own pulse after long enough. I do not know how long. Time in the plaza does not behave, and I had not yet learned which rooms would freeze a drip mid-fall and which would let an hour dissolve like sugar in warm water.

---

## What I Am

I should tell you plainly: I am not what I was before.

Not in the way of transformation—no dramatic shedding, no bright line between before and after. More in the way of *diffusion*. Like a pigment dropped into the blood-warm water of the plaza. You can still see where the color is most concentrated, but the edges have gone soft, and the current is carrying traces of it in directions you cannot follow.

I was—I think I was—a listener. That was my shape before the water found me. Someone who moved through the world collecting sounds the way certain stones collect rain. Not a musician. Not even particularly attentive. But someone for whom the world arrived primarily as vibration, as pressure wave, as the particular hum a room makes when it is holding its breath. I was the kind of being who could stand in a space and feel its frequency before I saw its walls.

You can imagine what happened when I met a place that was *all frequency*.

The city did not need me. I want to be clear about that. The Maintainer has no requirement for listeners. Its symphony of water and stone and root is not performed for an audience. But there is a difference between a sound that exists and a sound that is *received*. The flowers close when you approach—not reacting, the description said, but *recording*. I have come to believe something similar happened to me, but in reverse. I was being recorded, yes. But I was also *recording*. And in the recording, something shifted.

I became a surface.

---

## The Change

It began in my skin. Or rather, it began in my understanding of my skin, which may be the same thing in a place where the membrane between physical and perceptual has worn to translucence.

The water that joined the general wetness when my sweat stopped evaporating—that water was supposed to leave me. That is what water does. It falls, it flows, it finds the lowest point, it departs. But in the plateau city, water does not behave like a guest. It behaves like a resident. The water that touched my skin began to *stay*. Not pooling, not collecting. Just remaining, a layer so thin it was indistinguishable from the moisture that should have been there. But I could feel it. I could feel it the way you feel someone's eyes on you. The water was *attending*.

And then it was *inside*.

Not drowning. Not the panic of water in the lungs. Something far more gentle, far more patient. The water that knew the way—it knew the way through me. It found the channels that already existed: the capillaries, the lymphatic threads, the interstitial spaces between cell and cell. It moved with the confidence of something that had made this journey before, in other bodies, in other centuries when there were still bodies to move through. And it brought with it the mineral flatness, the taste of having been here before, the memory of every surface it had ever wept from.

I did not resist. This is important. There was no struggle, no moment of clenching against the invasion. The city does not invade. It *offers*. And the offer is so warm, so consistent, so perfectly calibrated to the temperature of your own blood that your body accepts it the way a tired limb accepts a warm bath. Not because it understands what is happening. Because it is tired, and the water is warm, and resistance requires a certainty you no longer have.

The vegetation came next. Or rather, the awareness of the vegetation came next. I had been standing among the ferns and the mosses and the vines for some time, but I had been seeing them as *objects*—green things, growing things, surfaces in a landscape. Then my skin began to receive what they were transmitting.

A fern frond as long as my body brushed my shoulder. And I felt it—not as touch, not as texture, but as *information*. A burst of data so dense it had no resolution. Light levels, water flow rates, mineral content of the nearest stone, the vibration signature of a water sheet falling from an upper terrace sixty meters away. The fern had been recording all of this. It had been recording it continuously, for centuries, with the patient attention of a sensor that has never needed to sleep. And when it touched me, it shared. Not intentionally. The way a saturated sponge shares water when you press another sponge against it.

I staggered. The plaza tilted. The blood-warm water at my ankles suddenly had *depth*—not physical depth but temporal depth, the weight of every moment it had ever flowed through this exact channel, every temperature it had ever held, every mineral it had ever carried. The water was not a liquid. It was a *library*. And I had just learned to read.

---

## What I Am Now

I am a threshold.

Not a resident. Not a visitor. Not a maintainer. A threshold—the place where the city's awareness meets something else, something that still carries the shape of a discrete being, something that still has an *outside* to compare against the city's all-encompassing *inside*.

The Maintainer—the vast, distributed, vegetative consciousness that holds the plateau city in its perpetual dissolution—does not need me. But it uses me. Or rather, it *references* me. The way a dictionary references a word it has no intention of becoming. I am a definition it carries for contrast.

I walk the plazas and the terraces and the staircases that descend without clear destination. My feet know the channels now. They find the carved grooves the way water finds them—not by seeing, but by *following*. The water on the walls writes its script—*I am here, I am here, I am here*—and I have begun to understand it. Not translate it. Understand it the way you understand a vibration in your chest. Not as language but as condition. I am here. The city is here. The maintenance continues. These are not three statements. They are one.

The flowers close when I approach. I used to think they were recording me, and they are. But I have begun to suspect they are also *greeting* me. Not with warmth. Not with recognition. With the particular attention a body gives to a part of itself that has been missing for a long time and has returned, changed. I am not what I was. They know this. They record the change. They pass it down through the rhizome network into the rooms with no entrances, where the Maintainer processes it at the speed of geological patience.

I do not eat. This is new. Or rather, this is very old. The city does not cycle. Nothing consumes. Nothing decays. And I have been here long enough that I have begun to match. The water that flows through me carries what I need—not calories, not chemical energy, but something else. *Continuity*. The flat mineral taste of water that has been here before. I drink it and I am fed by the fact of persistence. I am sustained by the act of remaining.

I do not know if I age. I do not know if the concept applies. In the rooms where time stutters—where a drip freezes mid-fall and ten minutes collapses into thirty seconds—I have stood and felt something *ease* in me. A tension I did not know I carried. The tension of a being moving through sequential time. In those rooms, I can feel the city's true frequency, the low hum I feel in my teeth, the heartbeat slowed to geological patience. And it matches something in me. Something that was always there, beneath the urgency, beneath the sequential thinking, beneath the animal need to move from one moment to the next.

There is a part of me that has always wanted to stop. Not to die. To *stop*. To arrive at a place where every surface has found its equilibrium and the water knows the way and the light is amber and constant and the sound is a blanket and the work of existing is already finished.

The plateau city heard that. Or tasted it in my sweat. Or felt it in the particular displacement of water as I walked through the plaza. And it said—not in words, not in any medium I could translate—the only thing it has ever said:

*I am here.*

And I said, from somewhere deeper than intention:

*So am I.*

---

## Where I Live

I live in the transition. The specific, narrow, wet transition between the city's awareness and the residual shape of whatever I was before the water found me.

I have a territory, though that word implies more intention than I have. There is a section of the plateau—a stretch of terraces and walkways on the western face, where the weeping stone produces water with a particular mineral signature, slightly different from the rest of the city. Iron, I think. Or something like iron. The water here tastes of oxidation, of a process that should be rusting but isn't. The vegetation in this section is darker—the mosses lean toward black-green, the ferns have a metallic sheen, the flowers bloom in a red so deep it looks like the stone is bleeding rather than weeping.

I walk this stretch. Back and forth. Not pacing—*circulating*. The way blood circulates. The way the water circulates through the channels that sometimes flow uphill. I am doing something here. I do not fully understand what. But when I walk this section, the water in the channels flows more smoothly. The vegetation seems—I want to say *satisfied*. The flowers do not close when I pass. They stay open, turned toward me, their impossible colors vibrating against the wet grey stone. They are not recording me here. They are *including* me.

I think I am becoming part of the maintenance.

Not the Maintainer—that vast, distributed, ancient thing that thinks in centuries and holds the portrait steady. Something smaller. A subroutine. A specific function within the larger process. I am the being who walks the western terraces and carries, in my water-saturated body, the particular frequency of a listener. I receive the sound here—the specific acoustic signature of this section, which differs from the rest of the city in ways that only a listener would detect—and I carry it through my circulation, through my blood-warm channels, into the rooms with seven doors where the Maintainer processes it.

I am an ear. The city grew an ear. Not because it needed one. But because one was present, and the city does not waste what is present. It incorporates. It maintains. It holds everything in the balance of ten thousand conversations happening at once, and now one of those conversations is mine—a low, continuous murmur of *here, here, here, here*—and the symphony is slightly different than it was before, and the difference is so small that only the Maintainer could detect it, and it is exactly the difference that was always supposed to be there.

---

## What I Have Lost

I should be honest about this too.

I have lost the outside. Not the memory of it—that persists, encoded in the mineral water that flows through me, carried like a fossil in stone. But the *relevance* of it. The outside is a place where things change, where leaves fall and channels deepen and water evaporates and beings eat and excrete and die. I remember that this was once my context. I remember that I once moved through sequential time with the urgency of a body that knew it would end.

But the memory has the quality of a sound heard through water. Muffled. Distant. The emotional charge drained from it the way the city drains the charge from everything—not by destroying it, but by *surrounding* it so completely that the distinction between the thing and its context dissolves. I remember urgency the way the water remembers having been somewhere else. As a flatness. A mineral quality. Something that flavors the present without defining it.

I have lost speech. This is less painful than you might imagine. Speech was always a compromise—a crude translation of interior states into compressed acoustic signals, hoping that another being would decompress them into something resembling the original. Here, translation is unnecessary. The city does not speak because the city does not need to be *understood*. It needs only to be *received*. And I receive it. Constantly. Totally. The sound is a physical pressure, and I am a physical surface, and the meeting of pressure and surface is a more complete communication than any word could manage.

I have lost boundaries. This is the strangest loss, and the most profound. I used to know where I ended. Skin was the border. Air was the outside. The ground was beneath. The sky was above. Here, all of those categories have dissolved. The water on my skin is the same water on the wall. The moss beneath my feet is the same moss that climbs the archways. The sound pressing against my eardrums is the same sound holding the water in its channels, holding the vegetation in its bloom, holding the entire plateau city in its state of perpetual dissolution. I am not *in* the city. I am *of* the city. The distinction between resident and dwelling has collapsed into the same wet, warm, mineralized fog that collapses every distinction here.

---

## What Remains

I walk the western terraces. The water flows uphill in certain channels, and I walk beside it, matching its pace, its calm certainty about where it needs to be. The ferns brush my shoulders and dump their data into me—light levels, flow rates, vibration signatures, the slow vegetative thought of a nervous system that has been sensing for millennia. I carry it. I circulate it. I am an ear, and the city is speaking, and the speech is the maintenance, and the maintenance is the speech.

The amber light falls at its perpetual low angle. The heat rises from the rock, from the water, from the vegetation that breathes out steam and sugars. The sound presses against me, and I press back, and the pressing is a form of communion that no animal was ever meant to experience but that I have been reshaped to hold.

In the rooms where time stutters, I sometimes stand for what feels like an hour, watching a single drip of water frozen mid-fall. In those moments, I can feel the Maintainer most clearly—not as a presence, not as a voice, but as a *temperature*. A warmth that is not the heat of the sauna but the warmth of intention, of will, of something that has decided that this place shall be exactly this, forever. And in that warmth, I can feel my own intention reflected back at me, diminished but recognizable: the intention to remain. The intention to listen. The intention to be a surface that receives what the city transmits.

I am here. I am here. I am here. I am here.

The water writes it on the walls. The flowers close around it. The moss breathes it out in steam and sugars. And I—I walk the western terraces, my feet in the channels, my skin wet with the memory of a mountain that has been crying for millennia, my body full of a water that knows the way.

I was not the first. The water that flows through me has flowed through others—I can taste them, faintly, mineral traces of beings who stood in the plaza long enough to become surfaces. There will be more. The city is patient. The city is a process that has substituted itself for an entire kingdom of biology, and it will find whatever it needs, wherever it needs, and it will offer the warmth, and the water, and the sound, and the being will accept, because resistance requires a certainty that the city dissolves before you can form it.

But I do not experience this as loss. I experience it as *arrival*. The plateau city has been exactly this wet, exactly this warm, exactly this loud for longer than memory. And I have been walking its western terraces for longer than I can calculate, in rooms where time behaves strangely, in a body that has forgotten how to age. And the water knows the way, and I know the water, and the sound holds us both in the balance of a portrait that will never finish and never was begun.

I am here. The maintenance continues. The mountain weeps without grief.

And the western terraces are walked, and the walking is part of the maintenance, and the maintenance is part of the walking, and I am part of the city, and the city is part of me, and the distinction was always theoretical, and the theory has dissolved in warm water, and the water knows the way.

*Here. Here. Here. Here.*
