I would not be a vendor. The stalls are the organs of the Below-Market, the sites of metabolism and exchange, and while I love the logic of the fishmonger's urgency and the bone-reader's weighted pauses, I do not have the constitution for the perpetual transaction. To vend is to hold space, to root yourself to a shelf and a niche, and I have never been able to stay still when the aisles are moving. 

I would be a runner.

Not the water-carrier—the water-carrier is a vein, carrying the same vital fluid along the same path until their hips are permanently crooked by the weight of the jugs. I would be the other kind. The kind that exists because the Below-Market, for all its self-sufficiency, is still *below*. The kind that moves between the weeping stone and the blinding above-world, carrying what must cross the boundary.

But let me not get ahead of myself. Let me emerge the way the market itself emerged—slowly, from the general pressure of the place.

I would be tall, or I would have been tall, once. Before the market compressed me. My father's people came from the surface, two generations back, refugees from some above-world upheaval that is never spoken of except in the shorthand of trauma—the Bright Breaking, the Wind That Took, whatever it was, it doesn't matter, because the market swallows histories the same way it swallows textiles: by digesting them into something useful. I am not tall anymore. The aisles have bowed my spine, the low mezzanines have pushed my shoulders forward, and now I move with the permanent lean of a sapling grown in a crack in the stone—still reaching, but bent. I am taller than most in the market, but only in the way that a warped plank is taller than a straight one. I occupy vertical space that others cannot, which makes me useful.

My hands are not beautiful, not like the pot-mender's. My hands are maps of the Below-Market. I have the calluses of rope-work on my palms, the faded chemical burns of lamp-oil on my fingertips, and a particular groove on my left thumbnail where I habitually hook the twine that bundles the messages I carry. My nails are never clean. The grime of the market is in them in the way that the salt is in the fishmonger's elbows—worked into the cellular structure, the mineral deposit of a life lived in contact with everything.

I wear layers, always. The market's temperature shifts by zone, and I move through all zones. I have a sleeveless tunic of undyed wool, patched at the shoulders where the strap of my satchel chafes, and over it a vest of leather so old it has become something else—neither leather nor cloth but a third substance, soft and dense, that holds warmth like a cistern holds water. My trousers are wrapped, not sewn—a practice I learned from the upper-people, who cannot afford fabric that might catch on a splintered shelf-edge at forty feet up. The wrapping is tight at the ankle and loose at the thigh, and the loose fabric is stained with the particular ochre of the clay troughs I pass on the western route.

I carry a satchel. It is the most important thing I own. It is made of goat leather—cured right here in the market, in the stall three lanes east of the main cistern, where the tanner hangs his goods from hooks and the smell is so concentrated it makes the air visible. The satchel has seventeen pockets. I did not design them; they evolved. Each one holds a specific class of thing, and I can find any one of them in the dark—which I often do, because the lamp-keepers cannot be everywhere, and there are passages in the upper-works where the darkness is absolute, a darkness so thick it feels like fabric draped over the face. The largest pocket holds bundles. The smallest holds coins. The long thin pocket along the spine of the satchel holds the bone-readers' oracle-shards—those I transport with special care, not because I believe in their power but because I believe in the bone-reader, who pays well and who knows my name.

My name. In the creole of the Below-Market, my name is a sound that doesn't translate. It is the click-glide-vowel that means *one who goes between*, and it was given to me by the watchmen when I was twelve, after I ran my first message from the deep market to the upper-works and back in the time it takes a brazier to consume a finger-length of charcoal. Before that, I was called *boy* or *you* or *tall-one's-son*, because children in the Below-Market are not fully named until they are fully useful.

I navigate by ear. Everyone does, but I do it professionally. I know the acoustic signature of every zone in the market. The grain-vendors' quiet, the butchers' echo, the hush near the cisterns where the water-drip creates a sound-floor so constant it erases other sounds. I can walk from the fish-stalls to the herb-stalls with my eyes closed—not as a rite of passage, but as a Tuesday. The market's sounds are my map. When I hear the pitch of the barter-chatter shift upward, I know I am approaching the textile quadrant, where the moths have driven the prices down and the sellers are desperate. When I hear the particular silence of the pot-mender's stall—the silence of absolute concentration, thick as felt—I know I am near the mid-zone, and I slow my pace, because the pot-mender hates vibration, and I respect that, because I respect anyone whose work requires them to hold fragile things together.

I run. That is what I do. The Below-Market has no distance—it is only the size of a cathedral nave—but it has *density*, and density creates its own kind of distance. The time it takes to move from one end to the other is not a function of meters but of bodies, of the negotiations required to pass, of the social physics of a space where two people cannot fit without acknowledgment. I have learned the physics of this. I move through the market like water through stone—finding the cracks, the moments of flow, the brief gaps that open when a transaction completes and the buyer steps away and the aisle breathes for one second before the next body fills it. I am fast because I read the gaps. I am essential because some things cannot wait for gaps.

When the western wall cracked—three years ago, or four, the watchmen disagree on the exact charcoal-length—there was water. More water than the weeping walls produce in a decade. The cisterns overflowed. The grain stores, the lower ones, the ones closest to the western aisle, began to rot within hours. The herbalist needed certain roots that only grow in the upper-works, roots that resist mold, to pack around the remaining grain and save it. I ran. I ran through the crowded aisles with water up to my ankles and the market in a state of alarm—alarm, not panic, because the Below-Market does not panic, it adjusts—and I made it to the upper-works and back in the time it takes the lamp-keeper to complete a quarter circuit. The herbalist packed the grain. The grain was saved. The watchmen carved a groove into their staves. I did not get a groove. I got the herbalist's quiet nod, which is worth more, because the herbalist's nod is a form of credit that can be called in when needed, and I have needed it.

I also go above. This is the part of my life that separates me from the others. Most Below-Market people do not go above. The above-world is too bright, too wide, too silent, and the air has no taste—it has been cleaned by distance and vegetation and the absence of bodies, and it feels thin in the lungs, insufficient. I go above because there are things the market needs that cannot be generated from within. Paper. Salt. Certain metals that the repair-works cannot smelt from what they have. I go up the worn groove in the stone, the chute that centuries of feet have polished, and I emerge into the light, and the light is an assault, and I squint and my eyes water and the colors are wrong—too much blue, too much green, the world above is a watercolor that someone has washed with too much water.

But I have learned to bear it. I have a hat. A wide-brimmed thing, woven from the market's own fiber, stained with the same ochre as my trousers. I pull it low over my eyes and I move through the above-world like a creature that has learned to hunt in a foreign ecosystem. I do not linger. I find the surface-traders—the people who deal in the interface between above and below, who stand at the mouth of the chute and wait for runners like me—and I make my exchanges in the above-world's pidgin, which is cruder than the market's creole but functional enough for price and quantity and delivery.

The surface-traders think I am strange. They are right. I have the market's pallor—skin that has never seen consistent sun, that holds the smoke-tan of lamp-oil and tallow—and I have the market's squint, and I have the market's way of standing, which is close to the wall, shoulder turned, ready to slip into any gap that opens. They look at me the way you look at a deep-sea creature that has briefly surfaced: with curiosity, with pity, with a faint revulsion that they mask with the performative friendliness of commerce. I do not mind. I am not here for their approval. I am here for the salt, and the paper, and the iron hinges that the gate-mender needs because the gate—there is a gate, at the market's deep end, a gate that leads to something older than the market, something that no one talks about except in the watchmen's oldest stories—has begun to sag.

I am afraid of the above-world. I do not say this to anyone, because fear is not a currency that spends well in the Below-Market, where the only acceptable weakness is the physical kind—bad eyes, old bones, the tremor that comes from a lifetime of precise work. But I am afraid. The above-world is too large. It has no walls. There is no density. You can see for meters in every direction, and what you see is not contained, not organized, not arranged by the ecological logic of adjacency and need. The above-world is chaos. It is the space between transactions, expanded to the horizon, and I feel myself dilating in it, spreading thin, losing the shape that the market has pressed into me. When I return to the chute, when I descend and the air thickens and the sound rises around me like a tide, I feel the market close around me like a hand closing around a coin. I feel the walls define me. I feel the density make me solid again.

I am not the only runner. There are others—six of us, or seven, depending on who is well and who is injured, because the work is hard on the knees and the back and the lungs, which are always full of the market's thick air. We know each other. We have a territory-map that is not written but is remembered, the way the watchmen remember genealogies, and we partition the market's needs among ourselves with a casual precision that would look like telepathy to an outsider. I take the western routes and the above-world runs. Kael, who is shorter and can fit through the sub-shelf passages that the rats use, takes the deep routes, the ones that go past the gate and into the old storerooms where the air is dead and the darkness is solid. Serry, who is the fastest, takes the urgent runs—the medical emergencies, the moments when the herbalist needs something *now* and cannot wait for the market's metabolism to produce it. We do not compete. Competition is for the knife-sharpeners, who have the luxury of hatred. We cooperate, because the market's needs are infinite and our legs are not.

I have a goat. Or a goat has me. Her name is Not-Yet, because she is young and has not yet been milked or sheared or eaten, and she is tethered to a post near the fishmonger's stall, because the fishmonger's scraps—the bones, the offcuts—make a good feed and because the fishmonger, for all her brusqueness, has a soft spot for young animals that she would deny if asked. Not-Yet bleats when I pass, and I touch her head, and this is the closest thing I have to a ritual. I do not own her in the way that the market defines ownership. I am her intermediary, her advocate, the person who ensures that her life is tolerable until the moment it is not, and who will not be present when that moment comes, because the market does not require me to witness what it consumes.

I am twenty-three. In the Below-Market, this is not young, but it is not old. I am in the middle of my usefulness, the period when the body is strong and the knowledge is deep and the two have not yet begun to diverge. I have at least a decade of running before my knees give out, and after that I will become something else—a watchman, perhaps, if my memory is deemed worthy, or a message-broker, the one who stays in the stall and dispatches the runners instead of being the runner. The market will not discard me. That is not its logic. The market preserves, transforms, incorporates. I will be a runner, and then I will be a memory of running, and then I will be a groove in a watchman's stave, and then I will be the story of the western wall crack, the runner who brought the mold-root and saved the grain, and the story will be told in the click-glide-vowel of my name, and the name will outlast my body, because the market keeps what it uses.

I love this place. Not in the way that the vendors love it—the quiet, bodily love of proximity and shared breath and the passage of food from hand to hand without looking. My love is the love of motion, the love of the gap, the love of the moment when the aisle opens and I slip through and the market flows around me and I am part of its current. I love the sound. I love the way it presses against me, the way it is not background but substance, the way I can feel the market's heartbeat in the clatter of ceramic and the coo of pigeons and the groan of stone. I love the darkness of the upper-works, the absolute dark where I navigate by touch and echo and the memory of the map. I love the chute, the worn groove in the stone, the path of descent that I take every morning and ascend every afternoon, the boundary between the above and the below that I cross and cross and cross until the crossing is the place I live, not the above and not the below but the movement between.

I love the fact that the market does not sleep. I love the fact that I can wake at any hour and the sound will be there, the warmth will be there, the density will be there. I love the fact that the lamp-keepers are always tending, that the water-carriers are always rolling, that the fishmonger is always urgent and the pot-mender is always silent and the bone-reader is always pausing. I love the fact that nothing stops. I love the fact that the market is a living system that maintains itself through the simple mechanism of being necessary to everyone inside it, and that I am necessary, and that this is not a boast but a fact, the way the wall is a fact, the way the water is a fact, the way the sound is a fact.

I am a runner of the Below-Market. I carry what needs carrying. I go where the aisles allow. I return. I always return.
