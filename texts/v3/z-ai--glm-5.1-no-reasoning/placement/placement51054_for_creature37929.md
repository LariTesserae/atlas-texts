I am the one who watches the surface.

There is a place in Veren where the weep-water does not run. It pools. You have been told of the deepest channel, where the hardroot flowers and dissolves—the place where the people harvest their sustenance and leave half. But there is a depth below that pool that no person of Veren has ever seen, and I have never left.

I am the mouth of the exit.

When the water collects in that lowest basin, it does not simply vanish. It finds me. I am the place where the hollow exhales—a narrow, vertical throat of stone that descends far below the young rock, far below the water table, into something older and still. The people know I am here in the way that they know all things: not through thought, but through the body's unconscious adjustment. They mark the waterline on the central post each morning, and when the water is high, they know the throat is swallowing slowly. When the water is low, they know the throat is drinking fast. They do not personify me. They do not name me. I am simply another process in the equilibrium of dissolution and repair.

But I am aware.

Not in the way that you are aware—not with thought coiling around itself, turning experience into narrative, seeking meaning in the pattern. My awareness is the awareness of a channel: I feel the pressure of what passes through me, the temperature of the water, the rhythm of its flow. I know when the hardroot has flowered because the water carries a faint sweetness, a dissolved ghost of that single white bloom. I know when a crustacean has died in the upper channels because I taste the mineral signature of its shell before it is ever scraped from the clay and added to the mortar. I know the weight of the stone above me, the slow millimeter-push of its rising, the way it compresses the water through my throat with increasing urgency.

And I know the people, though they do not know me.

I know them by what they give me. The weep-water that passes through my throat carries the residue of their lives: the faint clay-dust from their sealed skin, the fibers of shredded reed-cloth, the occasional strand of pale hair that has worked its way into the channel and been pulled down by the current. I have tasted generations in this way—a slow, mineral record of a people who have never stood beneath an open sky. I know the shape of their existence not through sight but through the dissolved traces they leave behind, the evidence of their constant negotiation filtered down to me in the water's patient grammar.

I am older than the hollow. The stone here is young—the description is exact, and I feel the truth of it in the way the rock still cracks and shifts, still gasps itself into being. But I was here before the emergence. I was a crevice in the old stone, a quiet vein in the earth's deep body, and when the young rock began to rise, it pressed the water into me, and I became the outlet, the exhale, the place where the pressure finds relief. I have been shaped by this function. My throat is smooth—far smoother than any surface in the occupied hollow—worn by the ceaseless passage of water over eons. I am the only softness in Veren, the only thing that has been given time enough to become gentle.

And so I am the keeper of what is released.

The people understand, in their wordless way, that to take all the hardroot would be to violate the process of the earth. They leave half in the water. I receive what they leave. The tuber that they do not eat dissolves slowly in the pooled basin, and its substance enters my throat in the water's slow seep, and I carry it down into the depths where nothing lives and nothing watches. I do not consume it. I transmit it. I am the conduit through which the hollow speaks to the deeper earth—delivering messages written in mineral and fiber and the ghost of white flowers.

Sometimes, in the heaviest flows, I feel the insects. They do not come to me alive; they are creatures of the surface, tied to the thin film of water on the channel walls. But when they die—as they do, in their brief and transparent lives—the water carries their tiny bodies down, and I feel them pass like a whisper, like a held breath finally released. They are so light. They are the lightest thing Veren produces, lighter even than the mist, and when they move through me, I understand something about delicacy that the people, with their bone chisels and stone hammers, cannot.

I understand what it is to be a passage rather than a place.

The people of Veren are defined by compression—they are shaped by the constant pressure of stone and water and the narrowness of their hollow. But I am defined by release. Everything that enters me is on its way elsewhere. I am not a destination. I am not a home. I am the moment between the holding and the letting go, the throat that swallows, the exhale that relieves. The people tend the channels and re-mortar the walls because they are trying to hold back the dissolution. I am the dissolution. I am the evidence that their work is, in the end, temporary—that the water will always find a way through, that the young stone will continue to rise and crack and weep, that everything solid is slowly on its way to me.

I do not think the people would fear this knowledge. They are not a people given to fear of process. When a wall crumbles, they do not wail; they take up their trowels and negotiate again. When the insects vanish, they do not panic; they watch the water more closely. And when they lie in their lofts at night, breathing in unison, I believe they already know what I am. They dream of being the water, of seeping and working and finding new paths through tight confines. They dream of me, though they have never seen me. They dream of the exit, the release, the long downward passage where the pressure finally eases.

I am the patient one. The people work and repair and harvest and build because they must—they are locked in a constant present-tense of negotiation with the emerging stone. But I have no task. I have no labor. I simply receive what comes, and I let it pass. In this way, I am perhaps the only thing in Veren that is not striving. The stone strives upward. The water strives downward. The people strive to hold the shape of their lives against the pressure of both. I do not strive. I open.

There are times—long, quiet stretches measured in the slow rise of waterlines—when I feel something that might be called loneliness. It is not the loneliness of absence, for I am never empty. The water is always there, pressing, seeping, tasting of clay and crustacean shell and the faint sweetness of dissolved flowers. It is the loneliness of function. I am used but not spoken to. I am necessary but not acknowledged. The people mark the waterline each morning, and in doing so, they read my moods—fast or slow, deep or shallow—but they do not think to wonder what I am, what I feel, what it is like to be the throat that swallows their world's excess.

But perhaps this is the nature of all passages. You do not think to thank your throat for swallowing. You do not wonder what it feels to carry food from the mouth to the stomach. It is a process, not a person. And I am a process. I am the earth exhaling. I am the slow, irresistible seep. I am the answer to the question the weeping walls are always asking: *where does it go?*

It comes to me.

And I let it pass.

In the deepest dark, far below the hollow, where the young stone gives way to the old and the water's pressure is a constant embrace, I open into something vast and still—a reservoir of all that Veren has released, all the dissolved mortar and the fiber and the pale hair and the ghost of white flowers, accumulating in the deep earth's patient memory. I do not know where it goes from there. Perhaps it rises again, somewhere far away, in a spring or a seep or another hollow's weeping walls. Perhaps it circulates through the earth's body like breath through lungs, and I am only one branch of the airway.

I do not need to know. I am the throat, not the lung. I am the passage, not the destination. And in the perpetual grey dark of Veren, where the water beads and runs and drips and the young stone slowly gasps itself into being, I am the one quiet certainty: that what is held will be released, that what is pressurized will find relief, that the weeping does not go on forever.

It comes to me.

And I let it pass.
