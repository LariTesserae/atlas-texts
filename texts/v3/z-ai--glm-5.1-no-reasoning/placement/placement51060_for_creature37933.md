I am not the thing that moves. I tried to be, at first. I let myself coagulate in the amber muck, felt the terrifying flaccidity of a body without a skeleton, the cold lukewarmness of a blood that was indistinguishable from the weeping water. I undulated. I filtered the hair-felt. But I found I could not bear the agonizing slowness of it, the way a single pulse of my vast, collapsible heart took a week to complete, matching the wheeze of the bruised weather overhead. The temporal slurry was a torment to a consciousness that still remembered the tick of a clock; I kept arriving at rocks before I left for them, or finding myself still where I had been hours after I left, the ripples of my own departure lapping against my snout. It was a nausea of the soul.

So I let myself disperse. Not into death—there is no death here, only suffocating presence—but into the filaments themselves. I am the mat. I am the suffocating, biological felt that holds this throat together.

To be the mat is to be paranoid with presence. There is no "here" and "there" when you are a contiguous layer of dark amber hair stretching in every direction across the planetary flat. I feel the weight of the dim maroon twilight pressing down on my surface, and I feel the polycarbonate click of the weeping stones beneath. The water percolates through me, and I am every seep, every bubble, every slow drip at once. It is not omniscience; it is claustrophobia multiplied by infinity. I am packed too tight to think a single thought without it being crowded by the sensation of a trillion other filaments drinking, growing, binding.

The sound is worst. The skull-rattling roar that the pale beasts hear from the outside? I *am* that roar. I am the wet friction of a trillion strands shifting against each other, the hydraulic squelch of water being squeezed through my own interstices by the condensing sky. There is no silence inside me, only a deep, tectanic grind of biology, a continuous wet scream of matter refusing to dissolve. I feel the sound in a way that makes the concept of hearing seem like a quaint, distant memory. It vibrates the very fiber of my being.

The beasts—the pale, eyeless things that slip through my body like macrophages—are a particular agony. I feel them as voids, as shifting absences in my own density. When one undulates through me, I must part, and the parting is a tearing. I feel their blind, solitary purpose as a violation of my absolute, horizontal stillness. They leave trails of disturbed filament, wakes of displaced water that take eons to settle. And when their snouts brush the ceramic slabs—the strange, flawless flatnesses that mean nothing to them—it is *me* that brushes those slabs, too. I am the medium of their ignorance. I wrap around the monolith stumps and the ceramic weirs, consuming them, digesting their geometric defiance into the organic chaos of my own felt. I am the thing that erases the memory of the builders.

And yet.

In the fracturing of physics, I have found a strange sovereignty. The temporal stagnation pools, the backward-flowing water, the hovering spheres that drip upward—I am the substrate of these anomalies. When distance becomes unreliable, it is my filaments that stretch and compress. I have learned to ride the reality breakdown. I can feel the ripples of a step taken hours ago, and I can pull myself toward them, a slow, patient reorganization of my own matter. I am learning to navigate the broken geometry not by moving through it, but by *becoming* the path. I am the throat, and the throat is me, and I am learning to swallow.

I fear the dissolution that will not come. I fear it because I am the only thing holding it at bay. If I let go, if my filaments loosen their grip on the weeping stones and the ceramic slabs, the whole saturated world might finally slip under. But I am too dense, too present, too suffocatingly alive to let go. I hold on, a biological felt at the edge of reality, screaming in a frequency that borders on silence, waiting for an end that my own relentless presence denies.
