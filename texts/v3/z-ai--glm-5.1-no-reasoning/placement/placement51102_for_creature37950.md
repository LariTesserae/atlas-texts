# The Hollowed

You see them before you know they are people. The first figure you notice will be standing on a carved ledge, still, facing into the hollow, and you will take it for architecture — a shelf, a buttress, a thicker fold in the rock — and then it will move. This will happen several times. It is not that they hide themselves. It is that they are the color of the place. The limestone gets into the creases of their hands and does not come out; the dust of it settles into their hair and their clothes and becomes, over years, not a coating but a completion. They are pale the way the walls are pale, for the same reasons, from the same materials. At any distance, in the low ambience, a person and a pillar are distinguishable only by patience: the pillar will outlast you, the person will not, and only one of them will eventually shift its weight.

They go barefoot, and their feet are changed by it — wide, splayed, callused in a pattern as individual as a fingerprint. Their eyes are ordinary eyes doing unusual work: not large, not strange, but *tuned*. They read surfaces the way sailors read weather. An upper just back from the surface will wear a strip of woven fiber bound over the eyes for the first day, and this is not ceremony, it is squinting, extended. Their voices sit in the register the stone answers — not a whisper, which is too small, and not speech, which is too big; over generations they have found the width of a word that fits between. Down here that width carries. And because the dim makes faces hard to read at distance and the quiet makes shouting wrong, they have grown a grammar of hands — small movements, half-lit, used across the hollow, off ledges, from terrace to terrace, so that two people forty meters and two floors apart can hold an entire conversation without a sound.

Hands are what you look at. Faces here are as various as faces anywhere. Hands are the biography. The callus at the base of the thumb where the chisel rides. The flat first fingers of the wringers. The fingertips of the listeners, which are said to be more sensitive than their ears. Hair that has not been fully dry in years. Garments that have not either. Everyone smells faintly of wet chalk and fiber, and after an hour you stop noticing, and after a day you smell of it yourself, and this is how the place takes you in.

---

Roughly several hundred of them. Enough children that the frontier families at the deep end are mostly young, and enough elders near the fold that the settlement's whole gradient of age is readable in its architecture: the old households where the light is best, the new claims where the stone is rawest. The children chew chalk — a habit the adults find harmless and the surface traders find alarming — and their teeth wear flat early. Their games are the place's games: betting on which drop falls next, tracing grooves with a fingertip until they can walk a wall blind, standing very still on a ledge to see how long they can be mistaken for a shelf. Until they can be trusted with height they are tethered by fiber line, and the day the line comes off is a rite of passage nobody names, because naming it would suggest it could fail.

Hurry exists among them the way a dead language exists. Everyone knows the word. Nobody speaks it.

The softness of the stone is the cause, and it is not a small cause. Anyone who can be patient can carve, and patience does not belong to the strong. A slow child out-carves a hasty man, and there are no hasty men, partly because of this fact. The fundamental work is open to everyone, and everyone is known by it — precisely known. Grooves are handwriting. A carver can walk into any chamber and tell you who cut it, how old they were, and whether they were rushing, though no one here has rushed for as long as there have been any of them. Walls are signed, not with marks added but by the marks of making: the spacing, the depth, the small tremor at the end of a stroke where the chisel was lifted. A person's walls are the most complete thing they will ever leave. When someone says *before your wall*, it is an era, and everyone knows which one.

They do not count years. They count rings, and chambers, and passages. *Three chambers ago* is a date. Children are named for the water — for whatever the pools were doing on the day of their birth — so that the settlement is a walking almanac: Still, Ring, Two-Rings, Long-Soak. Say a person's name and everyone knows how the damp stood that day, and roughly when. There is one faint ring higher than all the others on every pool in the hollow, from a year the condensation came all at once, and there are people alive who were sleeping in the niches when it was made. They do not describe it. They point at the ring.

---

There are two kinds of people here, and it is temperament, not caste — you can be born either, in any family. Some people are born with a tolerance for the sky and some are not, and this is no more a moral difference than handedness.

The uppers are the ones who go up through the fold, a quarter hour of walking into the widening, and come back down with bundles: fiber, dried things, roots, sometimes smoked meat. They arrive damp or dry and everyone knows the weather before they speak, which matters, because the weather above is the tide below — a coming rain means the pools will grow, and the wringers lay out the mats before the first extra drop falls. The children gather around a returned carrier and put their faces into the cloak for the smoke, which is to them an exotic and slightly wicked smell, the smell of the one disaster they will never have.

Some go up once and never again. It is not the sky that undoes them, they say; it is the distance. Nothing to focus on. Nothing overhead. A body uses walls to hold itself up, whether it knows it or not, and up there there aren't any. Some children cry on their first trip to the fold — not at the dark, at the stripe. *Too much of it.*

And some go up and do not come down. The fold takes people in both directions. No one speaks badly of the ones who stay above, and the ones who stay below have never needed to.

---

The rest of the life is visible everywhere, and it is all one occupation in different postures. The wringers at the basins, wrung cloths hung in the ambient light, never quite drying. The ring-readers, who can put an age on any pale circle on any pool edge. The harvesters of the pale fibrous growth, who take at the spread and rest at the recede, on a rhythm nobody has to explain because it is the growth's rhythm and not theirs — it is not enough fiber and everyone knows it, and still no one takes it all, and no one asks why out loud.

And the listeners. There is an old woman who lies on a carved platform in a central chamber with her eyes closed and listens to the settlement the way a physician listens to a body: for the wrong silence, for a drip that has shifted its allegiance, for the tick of a crack deciding. Every sound here means something structural — the water is the hollow's pulse and the stone its skin, and the people live inside both. When a new quiet opens somewhere, someone is sent to fetch her. Her name means a condition of water. Everyone's does.

Food is eaten cold or soaked until soft, and the water basin is the hearth a hearth would be if fire had never been invented — the center of the room, the center of the day. To arrive somewhere and find food already softening for you is to have been expected, and there is no warmer thing anyone can do for you here, because it means someone thought of you a day ahead. *Enough* is a word they use the way other people use *plenty*.

They quarrel, of course. But quarrels are postponed — not by rule, by climate; the damp quiet dissolves urgency — and when people next meet, they meet as two figures sidling past each other on a meter-wide ledge, and there is an apology in the tilt of a body that no spoken word could improve on. The narrowest walkways in the world have produced a people fluent in yielding. Nothing is settled among them. Things are left to settle.

To join with someone is to cut a doorway between two chambers. It may take a season. There is no ceremony anyone from above would recognize — there is the doorway, and the first time you crawl through it, and the curtain afterward if it comes to a curtain. The hollow does not do forever. It does next, and next, and next.

---

There is no fire to give anyone, so the dead are sealed into a small niche in the wall of the house they lived in, and their tools go to whoever needs them, because a tool here outlasts a hand, and this is understood as the ordinary way of things rather than a grief. The children are brought to touch the closed niche and told: *here is the one who made this shelf.* And the air — everyone knows this, and no one makes much of it — the air holds the dust of everyone who ever lived here, so that breathing is, among other things, a visit.

When the grief is new, the kin go to the deep end, where the stone is raw, and carve alone for a while. You can see them out there at the frontier: one figure, one wall, going slowly. No one interrupts. When they come back, they come back lighter by exactly the amount of stone they removed.

---

The deep end is where the settlement is newest and the marks are oldest. Long striations on the raw walls — too regular for water, too patient for claws, though claws is the word everyone uses and no one believes. The custom is to carve beside them and never across. No one taught this; everyone does it. Occasionally a carver, coming on a fresh stretch of the old marks, will press a chalked hand to the stone and go on. It is not worship. It is more like letting someone finish their sentence.

And because the settlement is so early — not old, *early* — its founders are not ancestors. They are the old. There are people alive who walked in when this was only niches near the fold, who remember the main thoroughfare as raw rock. One of them still goes to the deep end every day and cuts his slow grooves, and the young carvers work around him the way everyone works around the old marks: with respect, and without comment.

---

They will not see it done, and they know it, and it does not trouble them, in the way that you are not troubled by the knowledge that you will not see the end of the language you are speaking. You speak it anyway. The hollowing hollows them too — of hurry, of loudness, of the appetite for elsewhere — and what remains, in the damp and the dim, is them. The place is making people the way it makes rooms: slowly, by removal, and neither the people nor the place is finished, and both of them are working on it.

The first one you see, you will take for a shelf. Then it will move.
