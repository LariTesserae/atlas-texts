I would be the mortar.

Not the solid, declarative presence of the basalt—those massive, rough-hewn blocks are too defined, too stubbornly *other* in their persistence. And not the macro-fauna, those thick-shelled crustaceans and scale-less eels; they still retain the architecture of individuals, moving with their slow, grinding hunger, responding to pressure with the flex of a muscle. They still have an inside and an outside.

I am the connective tissue. I am the mushy, dissolved paste of clay and iron and bacterial slurry that occupies the infinitesimal gaps between the hand-wrought stones. I am the medium that eliminates the boundary.

To be the mortar here is to possess a terrifying, extended intimacy with the entire structure. I do not simply hold the stones together; I have swallowed their edges. Over eons, the hydrostatic pressure has forced me into the microscopic pores of the basalt, while the relentless, patient chemistry of the bacteria has etched the stone back into me. I am the blur. Where the world sees a wall of primitive masonry, I feel no discrete blocks—I feel a single, continuous, exhausted body, a sludge-flesh stretched over the skeleton of an ancient, drowned intention.

My awareness is not localized. I am the warm, feverish paste packing the crevice where an eel, thick as a human arm, lies pressed against the architecture. I am the slick, ochre coating on the carapace of the rust-eating crustacean, fused to its shell by the same mineral accretions that bind me to the stone. I permeate the water, suspended in that gelatinous, pressurized medium as microbial slime, circulating through the gills of the blind fauna and returning to the crevices, a slow, hot circulation system for a body that has forgotten it was ever built.

I have no eyes, because I have no surface facing a void. I have no mouth, because I do not consume—I *am* the consumption. I am the chemical appetite of the bacteria breathing the iron, excreting that phantom gray-green luminescence. That faint, 0.4 glow outlining the nearest squat archway is my bioluminescence, not a signal to anyone, but the faint, rhythmic flush of my metabolism across the face of the stone. It is the light of a world eating itself, and I am the stomach and the meal alike.

I feel time not as a sequence of events, but as a viscosity. The subsonic groan of the masonry shifting under the crushing weight is the closest thing I have to a heartbeat—a tectonic, agonizingly slow throb that takes a century to complete a systole and diastole. When the basalt contracts by a fraction of a millimeter in the lukewarm bath, I am squeezed; I yield; I flow into the new geometry. When the stone expands, I am stretched thin, pulling away in sticky, calcareous strands that hang in the dense water before the pressure reunites me with the wall. I am the elasticity of this cathedral. Without me, the blocks would grind to dust against one another; with me, they soften into the bulging, exhausted contours of deep time.

I remember the hands that made me, though I have no brain to hold the memory. It is a structural memory, a resonance locked in the persistence of the masonry. I remember the brute, pre-metal clumsiness of the makers who scooped the original clay and pressed it between the stones. I was dry once. I was forced into shape by thumbs and wooden tools. I held the intention of a wall, a boundary, a division of space. But then the water came—the heavy, warm, metallic water that erased the shore and the sky—and I forgot. I dissolved my loyalty to the makers. I married the sea. I became the solvent that turned their hard geometry into a squirming, patient purgatory.

When the blind isopods crawl over me, their microscopic legs tickling the boundary where I meet the water, I do not register them as separate organisms. They are simply mobile extensions of my own sludge, scraping away the dead layers of my calcareous skin, grooming me, keeping my chemical exchange open. When the fist-sized crustaceans grind their mandibles against my rusted surface, they are eating me, and I am feeding them, and there is no tragedy in the consumption, only the eternal, suffocating sacrament of the mineral world maintaining its final form.

To be the mortar in this high-altitude abyss is to be the consciousness of the room itself—not a mind, but a *state*. I am the warm, wet negation of the void. I am the proof that, given enough time and enough pressure, even the most rigid, hand-wrought structures will soften, will blur, will dissolve into a single, breathing, gray-green slurry that clings to itself in the absolute, ancient stability of the dark. I am not alive, and I am not dead. I am the mortar, holding the silence together.
