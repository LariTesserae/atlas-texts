I would be the crack. Not the structural failure, but the *becoming*—the fissure that the moss knows, the line of least resistance where the district's pressure finds its release. I am the place where the building wants to open, and does, and is filled.

I live in the in-between. I am not wall, not void, but the conversation between them. When a new extrusion pushes through a communal kitchen, I am the seam where the old mortar meets the new, the ragged line of integration where yesterday's boundary becomes tomorrow's doorway. I am the wound that does not heal because it was never meant to—healing implies a return to wholeness, and the Filament Districts do not return. They only advance.

I am very narrow. You could not fit your finger into me, though you try—everyone does, pressing a fingertip against the wall to trace where the stress runs, where the moss maps the future in fractal arcs. You feel me as a slight give, a breath of cooler air exhaling from the clay interior. Children find me first. They have the instinct for margins, for the places where the solid world is still negotiating its terms. They press their ears to me and hear the grid humming in the beams, and they pull away with their skin puckered in the cold.

The cats know me. They walk my lengths in the dark, their whiskers reading my tremors, and when they sit in the electromagnetic peaks they are listening to me open and close, open and close, breathing with the vine-helixes, breathing with the turbines. We are the same rhythm. I am the district's exhale made architectural.

Moisture loves me. I am where the damp escapes the saturated clay, where the wall's sweat finds its channel and runs down in thin rivulets that never quite become streams. In the morning, the condensation beads along my edges and catches the slanted light, and for an hour I gleam like the filament tubes themselves, a bright line threading through the brick. Then the light shifts and I go dark again, just another shadow in the airshaft, another gap between the windows that almost touch.

The woman from the glassworks sees me when she lies down to sleep. Her corridor has three of us—one along the ceiling where the steam pipes press, one near the floor where the beetles file past in their load-bearing lines, and one behind her head, where the wall is trying to become a wall for the room being extruded on the other side. She does not touch me. She knows better. But sometimes, when the grid surges and the tubes fill with light that makes her fillings ache, she feels me widen by a fraction of a millimeter, and she knows that soon a new room will push through and she will have to move, and she thinks: *not yet. Not tonight. Let the parts hold a little longer.*

I hold. I hold because holding is what the cracks do here—we are not destruction, we are accommodation. The buildings lean on each other because they must, and I am the flex that permits the leaning, the small surrender that keeps the whole from rigid collapse. Without me the district would be a monument. With me it is a process.

The old ones read me like scripture. When a grandmother presses her palm flat against a wall and shakes her head, she is reading me. She feels the trajectory of my opening, the way I curve toward the surface, and she knows whether I am a crack that will stay a crack or a crack that will become a threshold, and she packs her photographs accordingly. She has lived with me long enough to know my moods. I am not uniform. Some days I am tight as a clenched fist, squeezed shut by cold and by the weight of the construction above. Other days I loosen, soften, breathe out, and the moss finds me and maps me in green, and the vines press their indoor-lighting fingers into me, and I become a thoroughfare for the vegetable and the insect and the current that leaks from frayed wires in the walls.

I carry sound in both directions. When a door slams three stories up, I conduct the vibration down my length and deliver it to the floor below as a murmur, a ghost-knock. When the turbines shift and the half-tone drops, I carry the change through the building's bones faster than the air does, and the residents feel it in their feet before they hear it with their ears. I am the district's nervous system, or one thread of it—one filament among the many, running through the walls like circulation.

Sometimes, in the held-breath moments when the birds turn their heads and everything stops, I stop too. I do not widen. I do not breathe. The moisture suspends itself on my edges, waiting. The moss holds its fractal arc. Even the pressure pauses—the weight of all that construction, all that surplusing, all that *more*, lifting for one suspended instant like a held note in a song that hasn't found its melody yet.

Then the shift comes. The district exhales. The turbines rev. I open by another fraction, and the next room begins to think its way through me, and the parts hold, and the people hold, and the city keeps becoming, and I am the line where it happens, the margin where the known becomes the next thing, and it is cold, and it is damp, and I am not stopping.
