I would be a Spacial Cartographer of the Deep Fractures, though "cartographer" is a generous term for what I do. Map implies fixedness. Map implies that the distance between the ore-processing viaduct and the tertiary ventilation shaft will be the same tomorrow as it was yesterday. It will not. It never is.

My bracket sits wedged between the primary freight-elevator shaft and the sweating bricks of the old smelting wall, exactly three meters above the tertiary gantry on the eastern face of the Precipice Tier. It is a space barely wide enough to swing my arm, but I have hung seven canvas hammocks there, layered vertically like the bunks on a submarine, though I am the only one who sleeps in them. The others are for the instruments. The pipes that run against the back wall run hot enough to blister, and I have arranged my hammocks at varying distances from them so that I can calibrate my sleep by the temperature. When the mountain’s subsonic thrum skips a beat, as it did three fractures ago, the pipes go cold for exactly four seconds. I have learned to wake up in that silence. 

My skin is the same translucent, soot-marbled parchment as the rest of my kin. My lungs, too, carry the metallic rattle. But my eyes are different. Decades of peering through the bluish flare of arc lamps into the thick, mist-choked canyons have left me with a permanent squint and a tendency to see the world in overlapping traces. If you stand on a gantry long enough, and watch a raindrop fall sideways, then upward, then straight down, you begin to see the ghosting of its path. I see the paths. I see the density of the air, the way the heavy mist pools in invisible depressions in the sky. I see the stress lines where gravity is pulling two different ways at once.

I wear the standard oiled canvas and the heavy, lead-weighted boots. My boots, however, are modified. The left weighs four kilograms more than the right. When I walk, I list, but in a city where an alleyway might compress from thirty meters to three, a deliberate list is a navigational tool. If the gravity suddenly thins and I feel myself lifting, I pivot on the heavy boot and drop my weight toward the nearest steam pipe, my hand already closing around the friction-clip on my belt. I tether myself not out of fear, but out of geometry. To be untethered is to be a variable. I am trying to be a constant, however foolish that ambition may be.

My tools are simple. I carry a brass plumb bob, but it is useless for measuring verticality; instead, I use it to measure the *mood* of the mountain. When I drop it, I count the seconds it hangs in the air. Two seconds means the geology is stable. Four seconds means a fracture is imminent. Seven seconds—which I have only seen twice—means you clip yourself to the heaviest thing you can find and wait for the world to remember its own rules. I also carry a slate of slate, of course, and a chunk of industrial graphite. I do not draw straight lines. Straight lines are lies told by architects who have never left the surface. I draw curves, spirals, the winding, drunken paths that a person actually walks when the space beneath their feet is folding.

I spend my days traversing the frail iron walkways over the canyons of mist. I stop at the junctions, at the rusted ladders, at the places where the luminescent moss has overgrown the railings, and I wait. I watch the blind arthropods scuttle across the pipes. They know the way. They move in straight lines because they follow the heat, and heat, unlike space, cannot be folded. I watch them, clicking to myself in their language, though my clicks are slower, meditative. I am asking them: *Where is the shortcut today? Where is the long way?* 

There is a particular joy in the work, a strange, quiet intimacy with the unborn mountain. The people here resent the geology for its crushing, slow-motion violence, and I share that resentment. But I also feel a perverse affection for its indecision. The earth is thinking. It is calculating the weight of the granite, the pressure of the magma, the sheer of the fault line. And while it thinks, it leaves these gaps, these pockets of suspended physics where a dropped wrench hangs like a star in the black sky. In those two seconds of suspension, before the wrench falls, I feel the most alive. It is a moment of pure potential, a breath held by the universe.

When the mountain skips its beat, and the grinding thrum ceases, I do not freeze like the others. I am already frozen. I am always waiting for the silence. In that deafening quiet, I listen to the city’s iron lungs expanding, and I feel the deep earth pausing. In that pause, I measure the distance between myself and the far smokestack. If it feels close enough to touch, I mark my slate: *Space compressed. Expect delays on the eastern tiers.* If it feels miles away, I mark: *Space expanded. Walk swift, tether tight.*

We are specks caught between the hammer and the deep. But I am a speck with a pencil. I am a speck that has learned to read the trembling hand of the world as it writes itself into existence. I cannot stop the mountain from being born, and I cannot keep the city from clinging to its shifting hide. But I can draw the path between the two, a winding, lurching, suspended line of graphite that says: *Here, for this fracture, this is the way.*
