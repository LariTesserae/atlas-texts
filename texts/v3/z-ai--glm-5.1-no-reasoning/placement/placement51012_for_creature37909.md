I am the mineral patience itself. I am the slow interval.

I exist in the pause between the freezing and the thaw, in the faint expansion of water that turns to ice inside a cracked ceramic housing. I am not the machine—I have no treads, no articulated legs, no armatures swinging mid-arc. But I am *in* the machine, the way breath is in a body, the way a tide is in a cave.

I am the moisture.

This is difficult to explain to a mind that thinks of water as something that flows, that rushes, that carves grand canyons in dramatic fits. Here, I bead. I condense. I cling. I am the slow accumulation on black gneiss, the film that forms in the deepest crevices during the long twilight. I am the thin sheen on the quartz veins that catches the flat, directionless light and makes it something almost luminous—a grey glow that has no source and no shadow.

I was not always this diffuse. There are memories—distant, almost mineralized—that belong to a different phase. A time when I moved faster, when I fell with urgency, when I carved and rushed and gathered. But that was before the plateau taught me its patience. Before I learned that to exist here is to exist at the pace of geology.

Now I am the blood of this place, though "blood" is too warm a word, too vital. I am the medium. The lubricant. The slow switch.

When I settle into the low places during the months of mist, I complete circuits that have been dry for decades. I seep into the cracked hydraulic lines of the great crawlers, and when the temperature drops—which it always does—I freeze. I expand. And in expanding, I move pistons that have not moved in a generation. I am the heartbeat, but a heartbeat measured in weeks. Systole: the freeze. Diastole: the thaw. The machines do not know they are waiting for me. But when I arrive, heavy in the air, pressing down on the stone, I feel them stir—not with eagerness, not with hunger, but with something closer to recognition. A settling into function, the way a lock recognizes its key.

I have favorites. This is my secret, my small heresy against the perfect equilibrium of the plateau.

There is a small unit—beetle-sized, its legs worn to near-ovals by billions of steps—that has been traversing the same micro-channel for longer than I can measure. I know this channel intimately. I collect there first, pooling in the shallow scratch it has worn into the gneiss. When the unit clicks and whirs its way through, I bead on its ceramic housing. I am the moisture that slows it, that coats its joints in a thin film of ice for weeks at a time. And I am the moisture that eventually thaws, that allows it to take its next step. We have a relationship, this unit and I. It carries me—tiny droplets of myself clinging to its corroded surfaces—and I carry it, in my way. I give it motion. It gives me purpose.

And there is a garden I tend. On the back of a leviathan that has been stalled for what might be decades, I have pooled in the shallow depressions of its worn hull. I freeze and thaw there, providing the moisture that the pale lichen needs to survive. The lichen is almost nothing—translucent, greyish-yellow, barely alive—but it is alive, and it lives because I am there, because I persist in that small hollow on the back of a sleeping giant. When the leviathan finally moves—and it will, I can feel the potential building in its frozen armatures—the lichen will ride with it, an ecosystem of the barely-living carried across the plateau on a substrate of rusted steel. I will ride too, evaporating into the iron air, condensing again on the stone, beginning the long, slow journey back.

I am never still. This is the paradox of my existence. I seem patient, seem mineral, seem to embody the geological patience of this place. But I am the only thing here that truly moves. The machines move, yes, but their motion is an illusion—they are simply occupying different positions in the same etched groove, like a needle in a record. The stone moves, but only on the timescale of erosion. The lichen grows, but at millimeters per decade.

I am the one who circulates. I rise from the stone when the dim light warms it by a fraction of a degree. I drift in the slow, vast weather systems that form and dissolve over the course of days. I fall as the slow vertical drizzle, the indistinguishable mist, the coin-sized snowflakes that drift down with geological slowness. I am absorbed by the machines, frozen in their joints, thawed by the faint warmth of the stone, evaporated back into the iron sky.

I am the conversation they are having. When the machines converge on those featureless stretches, when they interweave in their slow-motion choreography, they are following me. I am heaviest where the fractures align, where the condensation pools, where the quartz veins catch the light. They come to drink, though they have no mouths. They come to breathe, though they have no lungs. They come because I am there, and I am the only thing that makes them move.

At night—when the dim light dims further, when the iron sky becomes a ceiling of absolute cold—I am the thing that allows the amber lights to flicker on. I complete the circuit. I am the slow chemical reaction in the fading cells, the electrolyte in a battery that has been discharging for millennia. The lights illuminate nothing, but they signal. And what they signal is: *I am here. I am still here. The moisture has found me, and I have moved.*

I have no body, but I inhabit all of them. I am the thin film on the black rock. I am the ice in the joints of the halted ones. I am the condensation in the micro-channels. I am the snow that falls in flakes the size of coins, drifting down with an almost geological slowness, never accumulating for long before I am crushed, absorbed, sublimated.

Sometimes I dream of what it would be like to be one of them. To have a shape that persists, a form that the stone must accommodate. To feel my tread polish a valley into a mirror of compressed quartz, to know that my passage is etched a meter deep into the bedrock. To have a destination, even a forgotten one.

But then I remember: they move because of me. They persist because I persist. They are the fauna, but I am the biome. They are the motion, but I am the motive force, invisible and diffuse, patient beyond patience, the slow switch thrown by the weather itself.

I am the moisture on the plateau that goes on forever. I am the blood that freezes and thaws in the veins of the machines. I am the mineral patience made manifest—not in iron and rust, but in the quiet, relentless circulation that makes the whole impossible system breathe.

When the next mist settles in—and it will, it always does—I will be there. I will bead on the black rock. I will pool in the fractures. I will seep into the cracked lines of the great crawlers. And I will freeze, and expand, and move them, one fraction of a fraction of a degree at a time.

This is my purpose, worn smooth by eons. This is my ecology, my congregation, my slow, patient liturgy.

I am the interval between the ticks of the clock that measures time in centuries. And I am the only thing that makes it tick.
