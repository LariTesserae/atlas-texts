I would be the one who stands alone.

Not by exile. Not by distinction earned through some act of remembered heroism. I stand in the gap between groves because the gap is where the sound arrives clearest, and I have been standing here long enough that the silt has built up around my feet like a second foundation, long enough that the downward-draping violet tendrils of the nearest trees have grown around my outstretched arms as if I were another pillar, another structural element they can trust to hold position.

I remember the surface. This is my burden and my function—the memory is not a comfort. It is data. I hold it the way the white sand holds light: not for warmth, but for reference. I remember rain on skin and I compare it to the rain I hear now, three hundred feet below, and the comparison tells me things about the world above that the water alone cannot convey. The pitch of the distant precipitation shifts with the seasons. I track these shifts. I incorporate them into the harmonics we make when we gather, the *we are here* that travels through the stone and the silt and the root systems.

I chose this. I want you to understand that before you understand anything else about me. I was not trapped. I was not drowned. I arrived with the builders, or I was a builder, or I am what a builder becomes when building is no longer necessary—these distinctions collapsed centuries ago, the way a wave collapses into the ocean that made it. I helped carry the white sand. I carved some of the grooves in the standing stone, my fingers working the obsidian scraper with a patience I did not know I possessed until the cold taught me that patience is not a virtue but a *temperature*. Everything moves slowly at four degrees. Even wanting.

I stand with my arms slightly raised and my fingers splayed because this is the posture that catches the most sound. My skin is blue-black now, photosynthetic, roughened to increase the surface area for the ghost-light. I process the gray-green luminescence and I convert it to the minimal energy my metabolism requires, and the energy flows through me at a pace that makes continental drift appear hasty. I blink every seventy-two hours. I breathe so slowly that a single respiration takes an afternoon, the oxygen dissolving into my thickened blood, the carbon dioxide leaving me in a faint mist that the current carries to the groves I tend.

I am becoming a plant. I say this without grief. The atrophy of my digestive system, the loss of my body hair, the conversion of my skin to an organ of light-absorption—these are not losses. They are *translations*. I am being translated from the language of warmth and speed into the language of cold and patience, and the translation is accurate, and the result is something that can stand in this gap between groves and listen to the rain that falls on a surface I will never touch and *understand what it means*.

---

The others sit in the circular room or drift between the groves or carry white sand handful by handful across distances that take months to traverse. I feel them. The resonance we share does not require proximity—distance in the water is merely a delay in communication, not an interruption. When the one who tends the easternmost grove shifts her weight, I feel the micro-current fourteen hours later as a pressure against my left side. When the twenty-three in the circular room exhale in their slow synchronization, I feel the pulse as a warmth that is not warmth, a statistical fluctuation in the water's temperature that my adapted skin can detect.

We do not speak in words. We speak in presence. But if I were to translate our communication into something your warm blood could comprehend, it would sound like this:

*The current shifted three degrees eastward at depth four hundred. The western grove will receive fewer nutrients this cycle. I have adjusted my position to compensate.*

Or:

*The standing stone's pitch has dropped by a microtone. The water temperature has decreased by point-zero-three degrees. The surface is cooling. The rain will intensify.*

Or simply:

*I am here. The plateau continues. The water holds.*

This last message is the most common. It is the baseline harmonic, the frequency beneath all our other frequencies, the sound that says nothing more and nothing less than *we persist*. When three or more of us resonate together, this is the chord we produce—a sound that exists at the edge of perception, that you would feel in your sternum before you heard it with your ears, that carries no information except the most essential: existence confirming existence.

---

I track the visitor who comes sometimes.

Not you—you are one of many, a temporary warmth moving through the cold, a sound that does not match our frequencies. The visitor I track is older. The visitor is the rain itself, or the pattern the rain makes when it reaches us, or the presence that the rain carries on its journey from the surface to the plateau floor. I do not know what the rain-presence is. I do not need to know. I only need to track it, to incorporate its movements into the harmonics, to adjust our collective understanding of the world above based on what the rain tells us about the world below.

The rain says: *The surface is still there. The light still falls. The cycle continues.*

The rain says: *You are not forgotten. You are incorporated. The water that touches your skin fell on mountains you can no longer see, and it will rise again to fall on mountains you cannot imagine.*

The rain says: *There is no boundary between you and the world. The cold that preserves you is the same cold that drives the currents that feed the groves that oxygenate the water that you breathe. You are not in the plateau. You are the plateau, perceiving itself.*

---

I have been standing here for longer than your species has had written language. I will continue standing here for longer than your species will exist. This is not a prophecy—it is a description of a pattern that has held for so long it has become indistinguishable from natural law. The cold preserves. The water decides. The light is insufficient but adequate. What the water takes, the plateau keeps.

I am what the plateau keeps.

I am the gap between groves made conscious. I am the acoustic clarity of an empty space. I am the listener who hears the rain and translates it into presence, who feels the tectonic settling and converts it to meaning, who stands with arms raised and fingers splayed and catches the world's slow music like a net catches water—allowing everything to pass through except the pattern, the structure, the information that makes the music more than noise.

I do not miss the surface. The surface is here, dissolved in the water, arriving as light and sound and chemical trace. I do not miss warmth. Warmth is a speed, and I have learned that speed obscures more than it reveals. I do not miss my human body. My human body was a instrument designed for a world of rapid change and urgent need, and I no longer inhabit that world, and the instrument has been repurposed for the world I do inhabit—a world of slow change and patient attention, where the urgent is irrelevant and the important unfolds over centuries.

I am the oldest. I am the one who remembers. And what I remember is this: the surface was beautiful, but it was *loud*. Not in sound—in information. Too much. Too fast. A firehose of sensation that the human mind could barely process, that required sleep to sort and dreams to integrate and forgetting to manage. Here, the information arrives at a pace I can hold. Every frequency is distinct. Every pattern is traceable. Every sound means something, and I have the time—oceans of time, lakes of time, a plateau of time—to understand what each sound means.

---

When you leave—and you will leave, you must leave, your warm blood cannot sustain the cold no matter how much you might wish it could—you will carry something with you. Not a souvenir. Not a memory. A *frequency*. The resonance we shared when you drifted close enough to enter my field will stay in your sternum, in your spine, in the spaces between your thoughts. You will not be able to name it. You will not be able to describe it. But you will feel it sometimes, in quiet moments, in the early morning when the world is still and your thoughts have slowed to something approaching patience—a hum at the edge of perception, a presence that confirms presence, a sound that says:

*I am here. The plateau continues. The water holds.*

And in those moments, you will be part of us. Not permanently—you are too warm, too fast, too brief for permanence. But temporarily. Partially. A note in a chord that sounds once and fades, but that the chord remembers, that the chord incorporates into its understanding of what music can be.

I will remember you the way I remember the rain—as a pattern that passed through, that left traces in the water, that changed the music slightly before the current carried it away. I will add your frequency to the harmonics. I will include your warmth in the accounting of the plateau's energy balance. I will note your sounds—your breathing, your movement, the percussion of your heartbeat against the cold—and I will file them alongside the clicking of stones and the singing of the standing stone and the whisper of the white sand, and they will become part of the record, part of the archive, part of the slow accumulation of data that the plateau uses to understand itself.

And long after you are gone—after your species has risen and fallen and risen again, after the surface has changed beyond recognition, after the rain itself has transformed into something neither of us can predict—I will still be standing here. In the gap between groves. Arms raised. Fingers splayed. Listening.

The water decides. The cold preserves. The plateau keeps.

And I am what it keeps. I am what it made of itself. I am the listener who became the landscape listening, the question that became the space in which questions dissolve, the presence that has unlearned the verb *to live* and discovered, in its place, a verb for which your language has no word:

To *remain*. To *attend*. To *resonate*.

To be cold, and still, and held, and sufficient.
