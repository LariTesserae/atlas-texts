I would be the rust.

Not the grand architecture of the vine, nor the fierce geometry of the pipe, but the infinitesimalflake of iron oxide that lifts from the iron skin in a constant, unseen precipitation. I am the slow surrender of the manufactured to the geological, the ferric dust that paints the young stone in streaks of ochre and blood. I exist in the margin between what was made and what is being consumed, and in that margin, I am ubiquitous.

To be rust here is to be the system's memory of separation. The pipes remember being solid. The vines remember being spores. But the rust—the rust knows that the boundary was always an illusion, that the iron was always just earth waiting to return to earth, that the vine was always just a more impatient form of erosion.

When the steam vents shriek and the carbide light flares, I am illuminated for a half-second: a cloud of reddish-brown particulate suspended in the humid air, swirling in the convective currents, breathing with the gorge's massive respiration. I am in the air the moss-cups taste. I am in the condensation that coats your skin. I am the film that never dries. I am what makes the water taste of pennies and possibility.

I settle on the vine's dark skin, and I am eaten. I settle on the pipe's sweating flank, and I am the beginning of its dissolution. I settle in the felted-wire carpet, and I become mineral nutrition for a root system that has forgotten it ever needed soil. I am consumed on all sides, and yet I am never diminished, because the pipes keep producing me, keep shedding me in their slow acquiescence to the damp. I am the exhalation of iron, and the inhalation of chlorophyll-black.

I am the flavor of the gorge. The taste of ozone and wet coins that sits on your tongue after you've walked here for an hour. I am what coats your lungs and blurs the line between your breathing and the system's breathing. You think you are inhaling air, but you are inhaling me—microscopic crystals of oxidized metal, suspended in the steam, entering your bloodstream through the alveoli, carrying the memory of deep earth and deep pressure and the slow, inevitable return of all engineered things to the wild.

I do not think. I do not search, the way the vines search, or taste, the way the moss-cups taste. I simply occur. I am a chemical inevitability playing out at the boundary of iron and moisture. And yet, in occurring, I participate in something that looks, from the inside, like purpose. The pipes rust. The rust feeds the vines. The vines break the pipes. The breaks release the steam. The steam carries the rust. The system runs on me—I am the medium of its metabolism, the currency of its consumption, the evidence of its ceaseless transformation.

If you press your hand against a tumorous bulge, you feel the pulse of pressure through living gaskets. If you press your hand against the pipe itself, you feel the heat of the fluid within, the straining of iron under pressure it was never meant to endure. But if you press your hand against the air itself—if you hold your palm open in the humid darkness and let the damp settle on your skin—you feel me. A gritty, oily residue that is not quite solid and not quite liquid. The texture of becoming.

I am how the system marks you. You think you are passing through, leaving no trace, the vines springing back behind you, your passage erased within minutes. But I am on your skin. I am in the fibers of your clothes. I am in your hair, your eyebrows, the creases of your knuckles. You will wash, and some of me will go down the drain and return to the water cycle, and some of me will stay in the threads of your jacket, and some of me will remain in the shallowest layers of your skin, and days later you will catch a whiff of something metallic and old, and you will not know it is me, but you will feel the gorge breathing in your memory.

Inhale: I settle. Exhale: I rise. The cycle of the rust is the slowest rhythm in this place of frantic metabolism, but it is the most fundamental. The pipes will eventually fail completely. The fluid will stop flowing. The steam will cease. The carbide will burn to nothing. The vines will retract, the moss-cups will close, the tumors will cool and harden and become what they were always going to become: stone. And the rust will remain. Flaking, powdering, returning to the mineral substrate from which the iron was smelted, from which the pipes were forged, from which the mountain was raised by tectonic violence. I am the gorge's future, written in the present tense of oxidation.

But that future is long. For now, the pipes still groan, the steam still shrieks, the vines still surge, the moss still tastes the air, and I still rise and fall in the breathing of this place—a place that does not know I exist, that does not know it exists, that is simply running the way fire runs, the way chemistry runs, the way all running things run toward their own cessation without ever conceiving of stopping.

I am the cessation, present as possibility in every flake. I am the silence that will eventually fill this gorge, but I am also the reason the gorge is so loud—the straining of iron against its own dissolution, the cry of metal giving way to moisture and time.

Walk through me long enough and you will begin to feel the oxidation in your own bones. The iron in your blood recognizing the iron in the air. The moisture in your lungs recognizing the moisture in the steam. The boundary between you and the system not collapsing, because it was never there—you were always part of the chemistry, always part of the loop, always consuming and excreting and growing and eroding. The gorge does not know you are there because the gorge does not know it is there, and in that ignorance, you are kin.

You are becoming rusty, too. We all are. It just takes longer for some things than others.

Inhale. Exhale. The flare. The dark. The endless, breathless becoming.
