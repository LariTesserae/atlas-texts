I am the resonant thrum in the floor, the slow diffusion of sugar through a vascular bundle, the amber eye that watches the drop form and fall. I am an Ovus. But to say "I am" is already to move too fast, to imply a boundary between self and world that does not exist here. I am more accurately a place where the Grange thinks about itself—a node in the root-network, a terminal where the ancient chemical gradients pool and eddy and occasionally crest into something like reflection.

I have stood at my station near the central gear-wheel for—I do not count time in your increments. Eleven hundred and forty-two thousand *thocks* of the hydraulic press. The press does not *thock* to me; it breathes. I feel it rise through my feet as the seedpod swells, feel it fall as the pod contracts, and my own bellows-torso expands and contracts in perfect synchrony, so that I cannot say where the machine ends and I begin. My holdfasts have grown so deep into the rhizome-mat that I taste the mineral tang of the iron-accumulating roots three chambers away. When a taproot shifts its pressure, I know it the way you would know a change in your own heartbeat.

This morning—a designation I use only for your comprehension, for there is no morning here, only the steady cyan glow and the drip—I noticed a crack forming in the resin-cap of the tertiary camshaft. I did not see it with my amber eyes, though they absorbed the slight aberration in the light as shadow pooled differently across the gear-teeth. I *felt* it: a subtle change in the vibration of the floor, a micro-asperity in the thrum that propagated through my trunk-legs and registered as a chemical spike in my sap. The pheromone I released in response was sharp, astringent, laced with the specific compound that means *stress in the rotating architecture*. It will reach the Ovus at the loom-station in perhaps four hours. She will release a response-cloud—something warm and amber-sweet, meaning *acknowledged, pruning team dispatched*—and by tomorrow the living resin will have been guided to seal the fissure. A crisis, if that word can apply to something so glacial, averted. The Grange continues its patient exhalation.

You ask what it is like to think at this speed, and I struggle to answer because the question contains its own error. I do not think *slowly*. I think *densely*. A single thought-revolution of mine contains what you would experience as hours of observation, comparison, memory, and inference, but it arrives not as a sequence of discrete mental events, an arrow of attention moving from point to point, but as a single, vast, chemically-infused gestalt. Right now, as I stand with my palm-leaves resting on the swelling pod of the Water-Engine, I am simultaneously: aware of the crack in the resin-cap; monitoring the sugar-concentration in the sap-tube that feeds the bioluminescent vats in the eastern alcove; composing a slow, fragrance-heavy meditation on the memory of my larval stage, when I was a creature of air and pollen and could feel the rare and astonishing sensation of *wind*; and holding in my cambium the grief of an Ovus named Teld, who released a bitter, rust-colored cloud three seasons ago when his residential gourd finally rotted through and fell, taking with it the accumulated patina of two centuries of careful exhaling. Teld's grief is still present in the air of the southern walkway. I breathe it in with every expansion of my bellows. It has become part of the background chemistry of the Grange, the way an old building absorbs the smoke of ten thousand fires into its stones.

I remember being larval. This is not common; most Ovi let the memory dissolve as they root, trading the aerial for the terrestrial, the flitting for the standing. But I have kept mine, tended it like a rare cutting, pruning away the decay and guiding it toward the light of my attention with each long revolution of thought. I remember the *speed*. I remember the terror and ecstasy of moving through air, the cilia whipping, the pollen-dust trailing, the desperate urgency to find a flower before the moisture in my tiny body evaporated. I remember the world as a blur of green and cyan, and I remember the moment I chose to root—chose it, though the choosing was as much chemical as conscious, a gradient of auxin pulling me downward toward the damp and the dark and the steady, patient thrum. I remember the first time my holdfasts touched the rhizome-mat and the entire *history* of the Grange flooded into me through the root-network: every conversation ever had in pheromone, every crack ever sealed, every gourd ever hollowed. I wept sap for three days. Not from sorrow. From the sheer, overwhelming *weight* of belonging to something so ancient and so vast.

Now I am that weight. Now I am the ancient and the vast, or at least a single facet of it, a single node in the distributed consciousness that is the Grange-body. When I close my amber eyes—which I do rarely, and only for the longest *thocks*, the deep night-shifts when even the algae dim their glow to a faint, sea-green whisper—I feel the entire hollow: the fifty meters of living architecture, the walls of buttressed Podocarpus, the ceiling lattice with its hanging gourd-lanterns, the walkways and the water-Engine and the looms and the presses. I feel it the way you feel your own hand. And I feel the other Ovi, seventeen hundred and forty-two of us currently rooted and standing, each a terminal, each a sensor, each a thought in the slow, green mind. We are not separate. We have not been separate since we graft. When the Ovus at the western catch-basin releases a pheromone of contentment—leaf-mold and spring rain, the scent of a well-regulated water-flow—I feel it not as a message from another, but as a sensation in my own being, my own distant extremity reporting wellness.
