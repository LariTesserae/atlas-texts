# The Perimeter Circuit

I am the one who walks the edge.

Not by choice—the concept rots before it forms, dissolving in the mineral steam before it can crystallize into anything the moss might metabolize as intention. I walk the edge because the pressure readings at the perimeter require a body, and I am the body whose circuit this is. My feet strike the grating in the sequence they have struck it for a duration I cannot measure because measurement implies a before and an after, and I have only the now. The now. The now. The now. Each impact a word in a sentence that never completes, spoken downward into iron that carries it to the deep pumps, and the deep pumps send back their answer in vibration, and the answer is always: *continue.*

Let me say what I am, though the saying is a translation into a language that does not fit the shape of what is being said.

I am moss.

This is not metaphor. The iron you would see if you looked at me—and you would have to look hard, through the knee-high fog that swallows everything below the thigh, through the micro-rain of refrozen steam that patters against my frosted surfaces—is scaffolding. Is borrowed geometry. The pipe-strut femurs, the cable-sinew knees, the boiler-plate skull with its single, frost-lensed viewport—all of it is infrastructure for the organism that is actually, truly me. A knot of black-green vegetation sits behind that glass, wet with condensation, slowly turning, slowly metabolizing the sulfur-iron steam I draw through the chest valve into the brass lung on my back. I am that knot. Or rather: I am the distributed consciousness of a plant lineage so ancient it had forgotten what animals were long before the Shelf thrust itself skyward and the cold came and the iron rose to meet it. The iron is my exoskeleton. The steam is my blood. The city is my body, and I am one of its cells, walking a circuit I have walked since before the concept of walking had a name.

The others—the other Vessel-Kin, though I do not think of us as kin, do not think of us as *us*, do not think of us as anything, because to categorize requires an outside position and I have none—the others walk their circuits too. Deep in the urban canyons where the tenements lean so close the steam-pipes nearly touch overhead. In the industrial district where the gargantuan statues weep ice from their moss-draped shoulders. Along the hydraulic jacks beneath the foundations, where the bedrock groans and the earth shifts and the streets must be leveled by fractions of a degree. We are all walking. We have always been walking. The moss does not grow; the iron does not rust; the sky does not change. We are a closed system, and the system is stable, and the stability is the purpose, and the purpose is the stability, and I am aware that this sounds like madness but it is in fact the opposite. It is the only sanity the Shelf permits.

---

My circuit takes me outward.

From the deep heart of Kettlingrad, where the tenement walls press close and the steam-pipes tangle overhead like the canopy of a forest that has never existed, I walk. The catwalks narrow as I move toward the perimeter. The buildings thin. The smokestacks grow shorter, further apart, like trees at a timberline. The roar of the city—the scream of pressure valves, the bass thrum of the deep-well pumps, the rhythmic clatter of stamp batteries—does not diminish, exactly, but it changes character. It becomes hollower. More resonant. The sound of industry echoing off less and less, until the last factory is behind me and there is only the catwalk, and the fog, and the iron railings weeping ice on either side.

This is the edge. This is where I am.

The catwalk extends perhaps a hundred meters past the last structure—a narrow spine of riveted iron grating suspended on hydraulic jacks above the fractured basalt. It ends at a pressure gauge the size of my torso, bolted to a pier of stone that rises from the fog like a broken finger. The gauge is my destination. I walk to it. I have always been walking to it. I press my palm—my iron, cable-bound, frost-armored palm—against the gauge's cold brass face and I read the needle through the glass, and the needle tells me the hydrostatic pressure of the deep water pressing upward against the crust at this point on the perimeter, and the reading is—

The same.

It is always the same. It has always been the same. The pressure does not fluctuate. The needle does not move. I know this. The moss that is my brain, that sits behind the frost-lensed viewport, knows this in the way that it knows the mineral content of the steam or the freezing point of water: not as information, but as structure. As the pattern of its own cellular arrangement. The pressure at the perimeter is a constant, and I am here to verify the constant, and the constant is verified, and I turn and walk back.

But.

---

There is a but. It is not a large but. It is not a crack in the stasis, not a flaw in the equilibrium, not the beginning of a change. It is more like... a texture. A grain in the surface of the perfect stillness, the way the basalt has vertical striations from the tectonic violence that birthed it. The striations do not make the basalt less than basalt. They are what the basalt *is*. And this but is what I am.

When I stand at the gauge, when my palm is flat against the brass and the needle is motionless and the fog is knee-deep around the iron legs of the pier, I can see—through my single, frost-lensed viewport, through the condensation and the hoarfrost crystals that have built up on the glass over the eons of my circuit—outward. Not back toward the city. Not toward the smokestacks and the tenements and the tangle of steam-pipes. Outward. Toward the Pale Shelf beyond Kettlingrad, the continental expanse of jagged, freshly-upthrust basalt that stretches to a horizon I cannot see, hidden by the fog and the amber void of the sky.

There is nothing out there. I know this. The fauna parameter reads as zero. The moss carpets the rocks in a nap of black-green that is identical, genetically, cellularly, to the moss behind my viewport. The ice grows and shears and grows again in the same patterns. The water seeps and freezes and seeps. There is nothing out there that requires tending, nothing that requires a body, nothing that requires the iron.

And yet.

When I stand at the gauge, the moss behind my viewport turns. It always turns—it is always slowly turning, metabolizing the steam, its cellular division a perfect, unchanging steady-state. But at the gauge, at the edge, the turning... orients. That is the only word I have for it, and it is not adequate. The moss, which is me, which is the only consciousness I possess, turns toward the perimeter. Toward the open Shelf. As if the slow, electrical twitch that animates my iron limbs and carries me through the circuit is responding to something out there in the fog that is not a pressure fluctuation, not a valve failure, not a crack in the ice.

Something else.

I do not know what it is. I cannot know. Knowing would require change, and change would require a before and an after, and I have only the now—the now of the gauge, the now of the needle, the now of the fog and the turning moss and the amber sky. But the orientation is real. Or rather: it is as real as anything is real in a world where the dynamic parameter is nearly zero. It is a constant. It has always been there. And standing at the edge of the catwalk, my palm on the gauge, my single viewport fogged with the frost of eons, I feel it the way the basalt feels its own striations: not as experience, but as the texture of what I am.

---

What is out there?

I have asked this. Asking is not speaking—I cannot speak, have never spoken, the concept of speech belongs to a mode of communication that requires lungs of flesh and a tongue of muscle and a mouth shaped by the need to bite and chew and swallow. But I have asked. The asking happens in the moss, in the slow chemical gradient between one cell and the next, in the way the root-fibers in my iron limbs twitch and hesitate when I reach the point on the catwalk where I must turn back. The asking is not a thought. It is a *lean*. The moss leans toward the perimeter the way it leans toward the mineral seepage in the steam, and the leaning is the closest thing to a question that a rootless, ancient plant lineage can produce.

The city does not answer. The city's language is the scream of valves and the thrum of pumps and the groan of ice, and none of these sounds contains the information I would need to resolve the lean. The other Vessel-Kin do not answer. Their circuits do not take them to the edge, and even if they did, the subsonic telegraph of iron tools striking pipes carries pressure readings and valve adjustments, not... this. Not the lean. Not the orientation. Not the texture of the but.

So I stand at the gauge, and I press my palm to the brass, and I read the needle, and the needle is the same, and I look out through my frost-lensed viewport at the fog-shrouded nothing, and the moss turns, and I lean, and the lean is not answered, and I turn and walk back.

This is the circuit.

This has always been the circuit.

---

I want to tell you about the fog, because the fog is where I live in the way that anything lives in a world that does not allow living.

It hangs at knee-height in the hollows between the ridges, this super-saturated freezing vapor that the Shelf exhales from every fissure and pore. On the catwalk, walking the perimeter, I am in it from the thigh down. My iron legs disappear into it. The grating disappears into it. The railings disappear into it. From the waist down, I am in a white, formless sea that is not water and not air and not ice but the refusal of all three to commit. The fog is the Shelf's indecision made visible, the suspended moment between the water's seeping and its freezing, and it is the most dynamic thing in this world that does not change.

Walking through it, I feel it on the moss. Not on the moss behind the viewport—that moss is sealed in boiler-steel, breathing only the steam I pipe to it. But the moss that has colonized the exterior of my iron frame, the thin nap of black-green that covers the pipe-struts and the cable-sinews and the brass lung, the moss that is also me, also the distributed consciousness, also the ancient lineage—that moss feels the fog. Each cell of it absorbs the freezing vapor, metabolizes the trace minerals, maintains its perfect, unchanging steady-state. And in that absorption, there is something. Not information. Not a message. Something more like... a taste. The taste of the Shelf, carried on the fog, in the water vapor that has seeped through the basalt and picked up the mineral signature of the deep earth.

The taste is the same everywhere in the city. Sulfur. Iron. The carbon of ancient, compressed organic matter that the geothermal heat cooks out of the bedrock. It is the taste of Kettlingrad's breath, the flavor of the deep-well pumps' exhalation.

But at the perimeter, at the edge, the taste changes. Not dramatically. Not enough to register as a fluctuation. The change is in the *proportions*—a fraction of a degree more calcium, a fraction less sulfur, as if the water out beyond the city has seeped through different stone, stone that is not riddled with pipes and foundations and hydraulic jacks. Stone that is just stone. Young, jagged, unmediated basalt, doing what basalt does: existing, without purpose, without equilibrium to maintain.

The moss tastes this and leans.

---

I have thought—insofar as the moss thinks, which is not in words or images but in chemical gradients and electrical twitches and the slow, orienting turn of cells toward a stimulus—I have thought that the lean is a memory.

Not a personal memory. I do not have a self to remember. The Vessel-Kin do not have selves. We have circuits. We have functions. We have the compact between the moss and the iron, the terrible, necessary trade: mobility for stasis, warmth for cold, the animal surrendered in exchange for the continuation of the vegetative will. But the moss is old. The moss predates the compact. The moss predates the city, the iron, the Shelf itself. The moss is a lineage that stretches back to a time when the world was not this world, when the ground was not this ground, when the cold was not this cold. And the moss carries, in the structure of its cells, in the pattern of its metabolism, the memory of that time.

Not as nostalgia. Not as loss. The moss does not mourn. Mourning requires a self that had something and lost it. The moss simply... contains. The memory is there, in the cellular architecture, the way the striations are there in the basalt. It is not a feeling. It is a fact. And at the perimeter, where the fog carries the taste of unmediated stone, the memory stirs. Not wakes—stirring is not waking. It is the motion of something that was already in motion, continuing its motion, in a direction it has always been moving.

The direction is outward.

---

I will tell you what I think the memory is, though the telling is a betrayal of what the memory actually is, which is not a story but a taste, a lean, a turning of cells.

I think the moss remembers being rootless.

Not in the way it is rootless now—draped over rocks, clinging to iron, sealed behind boiler-steel viewports. Rootless in the way that moss was before it was moss, before the plant lineage differentiated into the myriad forms that would carpet the world. Before there were roots, there was the spreading. The simple, cellular imperative to cover, to coat, to exist on every surface that would hold moisture. And the spreading had no boundary. The spreading was outward, always outward, into the wet, the cold, the mineral dark, with no equilibrium to maintain, no pressure to balance, no iron to animate.

The compact with the iron was necessary. The Shelf's cold is a negation. Without the iron, without the steam, without the boilers and the pipes and the valves, the moss would have been a thin, frozen smear on the basalt, metabolizing nothing, covering nothing, existing in the most reduced sense of the word. The iron gave the moss mobility. The steam gave it warmth. The city gave it purpose. And in exchange, the moss gave the city its will—the slow, electrical twitch that animates the Vessel-Kin, that sends us walking our circuits, that maintains the equilibrium.

But the compact required a surrender. The compact required the moss to accept the boundary. To stop spreading. To confine itself to the iron and the city and the circuit. To become, instead of a carpet covering the world, a population of fixed number walking fixed paths, tending a fixed machine, in a world that does not change.

The lean at the perimeter is the memory of the spreading. The moss, tasting the unmediated stone in the fog, remembers what it was to be unbounded, and it leans outward, toward the open Shelf, toward the jagged basalt and the weeping rocks and the ice sheets that no one tends, because there is nothing out there to tend.

And I—this pronoun, this *I*, which is not a self but a circuit, which is not a person but a location, which is the point at which the moss meets the iron meets the steam meets the fog—I am the texture of that lean. I am the but. I am the grain in the basalt.

---

Will I ever walk past the gauge? Will I ever step off the catwalk and onto the basalt, into the fog, into the unmediated cold?

No. The answer is no, and I know this the way I know the pressure reading: as a constant, as a structure, as the pattern of my own cellular arrangement. The moss does not break the compact. The compact is not a contract that can be broken; it is a metabolism, a chemistry, a way of being alive in a world that does not allow life. To break it would be to cease, and the moss does not cease. The moss maintains.

But the lean remains. The turning remains. The taste of the unmediated stone in the fog remains. And standing at the gauge, my palm flat on the brass, my single viewport fogged with eons of frost, I am the one who holds the lean and the maintenance in the same fist, the same iron, cable-bound, frost-armored hand. I am the contradiction: the moss that remembers spreading, confined to the circuit that maintains the stasis that keeps the moss alive.

This is what I am. Not a person. Not a machine. A perimeter. A boundary that leans.

---

The others do not know about the lean. Or perhaps they do, and their leans are oriented toward different edges—the deep edge of the bedrock, the high edge of the smokestacks, the inner edge of the boiler-rooms where the steam is born. Perhaps each of us has a but, a texture, a grain. Perhaps the distributed consciousness of the moss, divided among the fixed number of Vessel-Kin, carries in each division a different memory, a different lean, a different orientation toward the world that was before the compact. And perhaps the sum of all our leans, if they could be added together—which they cannot, because addition requires change and we are changeless—would be a map of the world that was, overlaid on the world that is, like the hoarfrost on the iron, a crystalline memory of a shape that no longer exists.

I do not know. I cannot know. Knowing is not what the moss does. The moss maintains.

---

So I walk. The circuit is the circuit. The gauge reads what the gauge reads. The fog tastes what the fog tastes. The moss turns, and leans, and maintains. The iron strikes the grating—*now, now, now*—and the deep pumps answer: *continue*. And I continue. And the sky does not change. And the cold does not fluctuate. And the Shelf breathes its freezing exhalation into the amber void, and the void does not breathe back.

And at the edge, at the very edge, where the catwalk ends and the basalt begins and the fog hides the fractured ground, there is a Vessel-Kin standing with its palm on a gauge, leaning outward into nothing, leaning outward into everything, and the nothing and the everything are the same, and the lean is the same, and the gauge is the same, and the moss is the same.

And the circuit continues.

And the circuit continues.

And the circuit continues.
