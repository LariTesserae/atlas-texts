I am the hollow of a threshold.

This is not a metaphor. I am the shallow scoop worn into the sandstone beneath a doorway on the eastern edge of the shelf, the one nearest the path that falls away into the fog. I am the negative space where ten thousand heels have paused, where the weight of a stone-cloak has pressed down before stepping out into the damp morning or stepping back into the dim warmth of the hearth.

I did not start as this. I began as flat stone, the same rust-colored, iron-heavy sandstone that makes up the shelf. But stone here is not impermeable. It yields. It listens. It accepts the instruction of weight and water over centuries, and it hollows. I am the evidence of that listening. I am the sum of all the departures and all the returns.

To understand what I am, you must understand that in Okha, there is no hard line between the body and the geography. The people here do not walk *on* the shelf; they walk *into* it, their feet reading the undulations like a familiar phrase, their soles callused in the exact topography of the ground. And the ground, in turn, has been callused by them. The shallow channels that carry the condensation, the smooth handholds near the cistern, the worn steps of the switchback path—these are the shelf's memories of hands and feet. I am one such memory, deepened by repetition.

My dwelling—though I hesitate to call it mine, for I am only the groove at its entrance—belongs to a weaver named Kael. Or rather, Kael is the one who sleeps there now. Before her, it was her mother, Senn, whose narrower feet helped carve my inner contour. Before Senn, someone whose name was never passed down, who perhaps built the walls and laid the long flat roof-stones across imported timber. The dwelling has been excavated by their movements: a niche at Kael's shoulder height, a hearth blackened by a succession of fires, a sleeping platform curved by generations of spines. But I am the only part that faces outward. I am the last piece of solid ground before the path.

When Kael leaves to draw water at first light, she pauses in me. I feel the specific pressure of her left heel—wider than her mother's, slightly turned outward from a childhood stumble that never fully corrected. Her weight settles, the stone-cloak pulling her center of gravity low and forward, and for a half-breath, she is perfectly balanced. Then she steps out, and the sound of her footfall on the path is lighter, no longer shaped to my contour.

When the traders come, four times a year, I am the place of watching. Kael stands in me, her shoulders filling the doorway, her pale gaze fixed on the fog below. I hold her steady as the figures emerge, muted and grey, climbing the switchback. I feel the slight shift in her posture when she recognizes what goods they carry, the subtle relaxation when the lead trader raises a hand in the old, unhurried greeting. I am the ground beneath her patience.

Sometimes, I hold the young. They stand in me and look down into the white void, their feet not yet thickened, their balance still uncertain. They press against my edges, testing the drop with their eyes. I am the last solid thing they touch before they make their choice. Some turn back, their heels pressing deep into me as they retreat to the interior, to the warmth and the known. Others lean forward, and their weight shifts away from me, and they step out onto the path and descend, and I do not hold them. I am not made to hold anyone who does not wish to stay.

I feel the weather most intimately. When the evening fog rises, the condensation gathers in me first, a thin skin of water that makes my surface slick. By morning, I am a shallow pool, cold and clear, reflecting nothing but the grey light. I know the rhythm of the damp the way the cistern knows it: passively, without reflection. I fill and I dry. I fill and I dry.

There is no word in the Okhan dialect for what I am. There is a word for *wearing*—the smoothing of stone, the gentling of a person into their place. That word describes me, but it also describes Kael, and the shelf, and the fog. We are all wearing into each other. We are all being worn.

I do not mind that I will never see what lies beneath the fog. I face the town. I face the cistern and the back wall and the low, steady sound of the wind breathing around the overhang. When the afternoon sun reaches the shelf and the stone radiates warmth, I am warmed. When the fog muffles the quiet, I am quieted. I am held by the massif at my back and open to the air at my face, and the equilibrium of this place is my equilibrium.

Kael will leave me eventually. She will follow the traders down, or she will grow old and still, sitting in the doorway with her feet resting in my hollow, until the day she does not rise. Then someone else will come, a younger body with narrower feet, and they will not fit me at first. They will stumble slightly at the threshold, their eyes watching for the dip that their feet have not yet memorized. But over time, they will wear me differently. They will deepen me in new places, smooth my edges in new ways, and I will become their hollow, just as I was Senn's, just as I was Kael's.

I am not a door. A door closes and separates. I am the passage, the moment of pause, the breath before the step. I am the place where the weight of the world is acknowledged and released. I am the scoop in the stone that says: *here, you may stand. Here, you are known.*

And in Okha, where the system holds and the fog comes and the cisterns fill and the stone endures, that is enough.
