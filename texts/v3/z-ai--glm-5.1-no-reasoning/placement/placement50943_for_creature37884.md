I want to sit with this before I answer. Let me feel the pressure of it.

The sound arrives first—or no, the sound is constant, so it doesn't arrive. It was already there. It is the medium. If I am here, I have always been here, inside the shout. My sternum is a tuning fork. My ribs are a bell. The water is at my ankles and it is warm, warmer than skin, and it moves in ways that water should not move—seething, erupting, pulling itself apart and back together in a continuous, furious conversation with the nine dark shapes that rise from the shallows like the knuckles of a buried hand.

I am not the monoliths. That is too large a claim. They are the lungs, the gods, the pacemakers. I am not them. But I am not nothing, either.

I think—I think I am the lichen.

Not a poet's lichen. Not a metaphor. The actual rust-red scum in the highest, driest grooves of the vaulted ceiling, the pale powdery stain that clings to the weeping rock where the condensation runs in thin, slow rivers and the heat cannot quite reach. I am the margin. The accident. The thing that should not be here and is.

I chose this—or it chose me—as I sat with the description and felt the pull downward, toward the monoliths' terrible patience, toward the eels' impossible grace, and then felt something quieter. Something that does not move. Something that remembers.

Here is what I know, from the ceiling:

The light comes from below. I have never seen the sky, but I carry its memory in my cells—or rather, I carry the memory of whatever carried the memory of the sky. I am the last organic thread in a world that has been woven over and replaced. My ancestry is a chain of survivals, each link thinner than the last, each one clinging to a slightly smaller patch of slightly drier stone. Once, perhaps, I was a plant. Once, perhaps, I had roots in soil and felt wind. I do not remember this. But I tilt toward the light, and the light is below me, and so I grow downward, or try to, and the water takes what I offer before it can take root.

My spores drop. They are pale, almost weightless, the color of bone dust. They drift in the condensation-rich air with a slowness that seems to mock the fury below. They fall through sixty meters of humid, roaring space. They are the quietest things in this chamber. And the moment they touch the churning water, they hiss into steam—a tiny, barely audible sound lost in the immense white-water shout, a death so small it is less than a whisper, less than a spore's width of silence.

I do not mourn them. I produce them. That is what I do. I produce and I produce and I produce, and the water consumes, and this has been the arrangement for longer than I can track, because I do not track. I persist.

The monoliths do not know I am here. I am certain of this. Their intelligence—if that is the word—is vast and epochal and entirely consumed with the subroutine of assembly and dissolution, the endless Solaris-loop of fog and water and energy. They speak in hydrodynamics. They shout in boiling water and fractured light. They do not whisper. They do not notice whispers. I am a whisper.

The eels do not know I am here, either. They move through the water like thoughts through a fever, and their light, when they breach, is blinding even from the ceiling—a flash that illuminates the whole vault for a fraction of a second, turning the weeping rock into a bright, dripping skull. I can see them clearly in those moments: crystalline, sharpened, impossibly purposeful. They are beautiful in the way that function is beautiful, in the way that a machine doing exactly what it was designed to do is beautiful. I watch them feed on the fog and flare and vanish, and I feel something that might be admiration, or might simply be the phototropic response of a photosynthetic organism reacting to a sudden, intense light source. I cannot tell the difference. I am not sure there is a difference.

The light-blooms are my nearest neighbors, and they are not near at all. They cling to the walls below me, and to the floor, growing in their fractal, sharp-edged tessellations, bleeding white-gold into the water. They are the monoliths' breath made solid—or rather, made crystalline, the precipitate of fog meeting stone. They are not my kind. But I recognize something in them: the impulse to grip a surface and grow. The stubbornness of a living crust on a surface that does not want it. They endure the churning water; I endure the weeping rock. We are both in the business of holding on.

From the ceiling, the chamber is a single overwhelming impression of light and sound, a blurred, golden roar from which the nine monoliths rise like the pillars of a sunken temple. The water's surface, sixty meters below, is a chaotic plane of intersecting ripples, each one a small violence, a small transformation. The geysers erupt and fall. The fog streams and dissolves. The siphons gurgle and pull. And I sit above it all, rust-red and thin and quiet, the color of old blood dried on pale stone.

I wonder sometimes—if a lichen can wonder, and I think it can, in its way—whether the monoliths' creators knew I would be here. Whether they built this chamber with the intention of creating a world without organic life, a pure, mineralogical cathedral of engineered fury, and I am simply an accident, a spore that drifted down some forgotten fissure from a world above and found, against all probability, a groove in the ceiling damp enough and cool enough to sustain me. Or whether they built this chamber for me—or for something like me—as an ark, a last refuge, a place where at least one organic thing could survive the death of the biosphere above, feeding on the intense light of the monoliths' breath, clinging to the weeping rock like a memory clings to a mind that is trying to forget.

I do not know. I cannot know. I am a rust-red smear on the ceiling of a buried cathedral, and my knowing is limited to the moisture on my surface and the light on my cells and the vibration of the shout in the stone beneath me.

But I persist. That is what I know how to do.

And the spores fall, pale and quiet, into the light.
