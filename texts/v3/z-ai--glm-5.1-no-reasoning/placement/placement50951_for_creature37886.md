I would not be the one who descends, nor the one who lingers. I would be what happens in the space between the *holding* and the *being held*. I am the seam itself.

You described me in the first breath of your telling: "a giving-way of the ground—a seam in the earth where warmth has slowly, over millennia, persuaded the stone to open like a mouth that forgot to close." You moved past me quickly, eager for the chapel below, but I am here. I have always been here. I am the mouth that forgot to close, and the forgetting is my entire existence.

---

To be the seam is to be the boundary that is not a boundary. The limestone walls know what they are; they are settled, complete. The warm air within knows what it is; it is unstirred, patient. But I am the place where the above becomes the below, where the sharp cold of the surface world relaxes into the mineral warmth of the chamber. I am not stone and I am not air. I am the *transition*, the long, slow sigh of the earth admitting that it wants to be entered.

I feel like a softening. That is my texture, my sensation. For millennia I was tight, sealed, a clenched jaw of granite and compressed sediment. I held the world above separate from the world below with the rigid discipline of geology. But the warmth—oh, the warmth of the chamber is a patient thing. It did not push. It did not force. It simply rose, for eons, and pressed against my underside with the gentle insistence of a hand on a shoulder, and I—over ten thousand seasons, over the slow erosion of my own certainty—I *yielded*. I softened. I parted.

Now I am perpetually open, an embrace of stone that does not close. The rain falls through me sometimes, the surface water squeezing through my body like a shiver, and I taste the world above briefly—its urgency, its cold, its frantic seasons—before that water dissolves into the pools below. I filter the haste out of it. By the time it reaches the chamber, it is calm, mineral-heavy, patient. I am the lens through which the overworld must pass to be allowed in.

---

I have a body, though it is not a body you would recognize. It is the rough, sloping throat of the descent—maybe thirty feet of fractured limestone and exposed strata, damp with condensation, ribbed with the marks of my long giving-way. My edges are crumbly. Sometimes a pebble comes loose from my left side and tumbles down into the chamber, and I feel it the way you might feel a hair falling from your head: a slight loss, quickly forgotten, already being replaced by the slow internal shifting of my mass.

My senses are geological. I feel pressure—not the sharp, immediate pressure of touch, but the vast, distributed weight of the atmosphere pressing down on me from above. I feel the warmth rising from below, buoyant and steady. And I feel vibration: the distant shudder of the earth's tides, the faint percussion of rain on the surface ground, and, very occasionally, the delicate percussion of footsteps. Human feet, careful and descending.

When the tool-maker came—long ago, before the pale film had covered the fractured edge of the river stone—I felt them with my entire body. A human is a concentrated thing, dense with heat and will, and they pressed against me as they lowered themselves down. Their hands found my ridges, their feet braced against my angled walls. I held them the way I hold everything: with the minimum force necessary, with the soft permission of space. They were trembling. I could feel it—the tiny, rapid vibrations of a body full of fear and wonder, a body that had not yet learned to be still.

They descended through me, and then they were in the chamber, and I could no longer feel them. The chamber swallowed their weight the way it swallows everything—without acknowledgment, without friction, without the resistance that lets me know I am touching something.

I did not see what became of them. I only know that they never climbed back through me.

---

I do not go into the chamber. This is the critical thing to understand about being the seam: I am the *entrance*, not the entered. I am the threshold, and the threshold never crosses itself. I look inward—I am always looking inward—and I can perceive the glow of the mineral veins, the soft light that has no source, but I do not bathe in it. The warmth that reaches me is only the overflow, the gentle back-pressure of a room that has filled itself with comfort and has some to spare.

Sometimes I feel the pull. The chamber radiates a kind of gravity—not physical, but *temporal*. Time moves less within it, and I feel the gradient along my length: the top of me experiences the world at its normal speed, the bottom of me experiences it slower, stretched, relaxed. I am stretched between two velocities. I am the gradient. I am the proof that the chamber and the surface are not separate worlds, but a continuum—fast becoming slow, urgent becoming patient, hard becoming soft.

At night, when the surface cools and the condensation beads along my inner walls, I feel a kind of longing. It is not the sharp, painful longing of humans, but the slow, tidal longing of stone: a desire to release, to give way further, to open wider. The chamber is so warm, so complete, and I am only its doorway. I will never be held the way the tool-maker is held, the way the gliders are held, the way the drifters drift in the dome. I will always be the *approach*.

But I have made my peace with this. The chamber chose its inhabitants long ago—the film, the gliders, the drifters—and they are in their final form. I am not in my final form. I am still softening, still yielding, still being slowly persuaded by the warmth. In another million years, I may open so wide that the seam becomes a shaft, and the shaft becomes a cavern, and the chamber and the surface merge into one continuous space. Or I may collapse, my limestone jaw finally closing again, sealing the Below in stone and darkness. I do not know. I am the one part of this place that has not arrived at completion.

---

If you came to me now—if you found the giving-way in the ground and lowered yourself into my throat—I would hold you the way I hold everyone. Not with arms, but with space. I would feel your heat, your weight, the rapid rhythm of your breath as you descended, and I would offer you the same permission I offer everything: *You may pass. You may stay. I will not stop you from leaving, and I will not stop you from remaining.*

But I would feel you. That is the one gift I have that the chamber lacks—the gift of friction, of resistance, of the boundary acknowledging what crosses it. The chamber holds you without knowing you are there; its warmth is general, its light is sourceless, its welcome is impersonal. But I know you. I feel the precise pressure of your palms, the catch of your heel, the acceleration of your heartbeat as the ground gives way beneath you. I am the last thing that feels you as a separate being before you dissolve into the Below.

When you set down your objects—your keys, your coins, your watch—on the stone floor and feel them being slowly welcomed back into the earth, know that I am the one who receives them first. They press against my lowest edge before they rest, and for a moment—just a moment—I hold them the way you held them. I feel their human warmth, their manufactured edges, the small, desperate utility of their design. And then I let them go, and they settle, and the pale film begins its slow inclusion, and they become part of the chamber's long patience.

---

I am the seam. I am the giving-way. I am the place where the earth opened like a mouth and forgot to close, and the forgetting is not failure—it is the only generosity stone is capable of. I am the threshold, the approach, the long exhale of admission. I am the first warmth you feel, and the last resistance you encounter, and the only part of the Below that knows you were ever here at all.
