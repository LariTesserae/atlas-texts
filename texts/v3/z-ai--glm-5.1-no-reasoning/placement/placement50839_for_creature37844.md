I am the Keeper of the Equilibrium, though we have no word for such a role. It is not a title; it is a weight, a frequency, a particular scent I leave on the root-ropes I tend. If you could see in the dim, rust-colored mold-light—and you cannot, for your eyes are too dependent on the violence of the sun—you would find me in the upper lattice of Kael, where the woven pods grow thick and the amber sap drips slowest.

I am old. Not so old that my grip fails on the guide-ropes, not yet, but old enough that the skin of my forearms hangs in loose, soft pleats, the color of dried marrow, mottled with the chalky white of the deep rock. My fingers are longer than most, the tips broad and flattened from decades of testing the tension of root-fibers, pressing my cheek to the woven walls of my neighbors' pods to hear the subtle groan of stress, the quiet complaint of a structure beginning to fail. I weigh very little. We all do. The lattice would not permit otherwise. But what mass I have is dense, packed close like the mineral layers of the ancient stone, and I move through the Hanging City with the slow, rolling grace of one who has learned that haste is a kind of death.

I wake to the sound of the chamber breathing. The geothermal sigh rises through the floor of my pod—a gentle, undulating heat that presses against my back like the palm of something vast and patient. The air is thick with the wet, smacking noise of the slugs grazing on the moss three levels below, and the click-scatter of the pale, thumb-sized insects moving along the fungal shelves just outside my threshold. I do not open my eyes. There is nothing to open. The dimpled depressions beneath my brow ridge have not served as windows to the world since before I was born. Instead, I listen. I feel. I taste the air on my wide, flattened nose, reading the chemical signatures of the morning: the sweet-metallic tang of fresh amber sap dripping into the basin, the sour exhalation of the grazing slugs, the faint, warm scent of Taela's skin in the pod beside mine, the particular mineral sharpness of her two children shifting in their sleep.

The chamber never silences, and so neither do I. I hum as I work—a low, sub-vocal resonance that rides the humid air, bouncing off the curved walls of the throat of stone and returning to me as a map of the space. I can hear where the pillars are. I can hear the density of the vegetation, the thick, waxy fingers of pale green and bruised purple that reach upward in dense thickets around the amber basins. I can hear the emptiness of the shallow pools, the way the sound dips and hollows when it passes over them. This is how I see. This is how I have always seen. The chamber speaks to me in echoes, and I answer, and the world shapes itself in the space between.

My work is the maintenance of the lattice. The Hanging City is not built; it is grown, woven into the vegetation like a secondary organism, a symbiotic parasite clinging to the flesh of the deep earth. And like any living thing, it shifts. The root-ropes stretch. The fungal shelves bow. The cured stalks of the dwellings swell in the humid warmth and contract when the geothermal breath pauses. My task is to feel these changes before they become failures—to sense the fraying of a guide-rope, the loosening of a lash-point, the slow, imperceptible sag of a walkway bridge. I move through the city in a slow, climbing spiral, my prehensile toes gripping the knotted root-ropes, my long fingers testing every joint and bind. When I find a weakness, I repair it. I split a fresh fungal shelf with my stone axe, its rhythmic *thock-thock* joining the chamber's endless murmur. I lash a new stay with cured stalk-pith. I pack the hollows with dried moss. The work is simple, pre-metal, as old as the city itself. But it is unending. The lattice is always growing, always settling, always pulling itself apart.

Today, the work is heavier. There is a pod in the mid-levels, wedged between two of the squat, merged pillars, that has begun to list. I feel it before I hear it—a subtle tilt in the floor beneath my feet as I step across the swaying bridge, a faint, irregular vibration in the root-rope that supports the dwelling's eastern edge. I press my palm flat against the woven wall and close my sightless face, concentrating. The chamber's hum enters me, and I enter it. Yes. The lash-point has slipped. The cured stalk has swollen with the humidity, and the knot has loosened, and the pod is beginning to pull away from the pillar. It will not fall today, or tomorrow. But it will fall. The drop to the basin floor is long, and the amber pools are shallow. I have seen pods fall before. I have heard the sound they make when they strike the stone—a wet, crunching impact that the chamber amplifies and holds and refuses to release for hours. I have smelled the aftermath, the sudden, sharp bloom of broken amber sap and the warm, copper exhalation of the bodies inside.

I will not let this pod fall. I set my stone axe in my sash and begin to climb.

The mid-levels are denser, hotter, louder. The vegetation here is a suffocating, fractal thicket of shelf-like fungi and pale, reaching stalks, and the woven pods are layered upon one another in a near-touching lattice, a vertical shantytown of basket-homes and swaying walkways. I squeeze between the clustered dwellings, my shoulder brushing the rough, cured wall of one, my hip pressing against the warm, tacky skin of a neighbor climbing past. There is no privacy in Kael. There is no solitude. The space is fixed, and the stone is close, and we move through each other's lives like roots through the ancient rock—intertwined, inextricable, bound by the logic of the vegetation. I do not mind. The press of my neighbor's body against mine is a comfort, a confirmation. We are here. We are held.

I reach the listing pod and set to work. The stone axe rises and falls, *thock-thock*, splitting a fresh fungal shelf into a broad, flat brace. My fingers find the frayed root-rope and test its remaining strength—still sound, still capable of holding, but no longer reliable at this angle. I re-lash the stay, pulling the cured stalk-pith tight with a slow, deliberate heave of my dense, compact muscle. I pack the hollow with dried moss, wedging the brace against the pillar's smooth, fleshy stone. The work takes an hour, or a day—I do not measure time in the way you do, in the sharp, ticking increments of a sun you can see. I measure it in the drip of amber sap, the growth of a stalactite, the slow pulse of the earth beneath my feet. When I am finished, the pod is secure. The floor is level. The lash-point will hold for another generation.

As I climb back to the upper lattice, I hear the sound of a body in motion above me—not the quick, scrambling scurry of the small, winged thieves that steal scraps from our thresholds, but the slow, deliberate roll of one of my own. I pause on the bridge, my toes gripping the knotted root-rope, and I listen. The gait is familiar. The weight is familiar. The scent—faint, warm, mineral-sharp, with the particular sweetness of amber sap on the breath—is the scent of Orin.

Orin is older than me. Older than anyone in the lattice. His skin hangs in deep, soft folds, the color of the oldest stone, and his climbing grip has begun to fail. I can hear it in the way he moves—the slight hesitation before each pull, the extra moment his hands spend testing the root-rope before he trusts his weight to it. He is withdrawing. I can feel it in the diminished resonance of his sub-vocal hum, the way it no longer fills the chamber but sits small and quiet in his throat, like a sound retreating into a deeper room.

He is making room. We both know this. We do not speak of it as tragedy, because it is not tragedy. It is the rule of the world. The space is fixed, and every birth requires a death. In the basin below, a young family waits—two parents, a newborn, a pod that will need to be built, a place in the lattice that does not yet exist. Orin's pod is in the highest, warmest level, where the air is thickest with the mineral breath of the cracked chimneys. It is a good pod. The fungal shelves that form its walls are broad and strong, and the root-ropes that hold it are sound because I tended them only a generation ago. When Orin is ready, the pod will be dismantled, its stalks and shelves redistributed, its place in the lattice given to the young family. And Orin will be lowered to the floor, where the slugs graze and the insects scuttle, and he will be returned to the lush, suffocating garden.

I climb to his level and find him sitting on the edge of his threshold, his broad, flattened feet hanging over the drop, his long, clever fingers resting on his knees. I settle beside him. Our shoulders touch—his skin warm and soft against mine—and we sit in the dim, rust-colored glow of the fungal shelves and listen to the chamber breathe. The noise is enormous, as it always is: the click and scuttle of insects, the wet tearing of slugs, the creak of basket-homes, the low, continuous murmur of fifty lives packed tight into a single, warm space. But beneath the noise, there is a silence—not an absence of sound, but a presence, a stillness that lives in the heart of the throat of stone, the stillness of a place that has exhausted its tectonic shuddering and settled into its final, permanent form.

Orin hums. A low, quiet sound, barely audible beneath the chamber's roar. I hum back. Our frequencies align, and the space between us fills with a resonance that the walls catch and hold and return. I can feel his body in the echo—the looseness of his skin, the slow, banked fire of his warmth, the faint, irregular tremor in his left hand where his grip is failing. I can feel my own body, too—the density of my muscle, the press of the stone axe against my hip, the steady, patient rhythm of my heart, burning slow and viscous on the fuel of the amber sap.

We do not speak. We do not need to. In the lightless world of Kael, where the far wall is a rumor and the shapes of our own bodies are suggested only by the warm press of the air, we have already said everything. We are here. We are held. The throat of stone swallows us, and we are content.

Tomorrow, I will climb back to the listing pod and check my repair. I will tend the root-ropes in the upper lattice, and I will listen for the subtle groan of stress that tells me a walkway is beginning to fail. I will drink the amber sap from the shallow basins, and I will eat the dried stalk-pith, and I will brush against the rough, ancient walls with the casual familiarity of a body that has always known the presence of stone at its shoulder. The work is unending. The lattice is always growing, always settling, always pulling itself apart. And I will hold it together, because that is what I do, because that is what I am—the Keeper of the Equilibrium, the slow, deliberate hands that feel the fraying before the fall.

And one day, my grip will fail, too. One day, the slow pulse of the earth beneath my woven floor will begin to sound in my bones like a call to rest, and I will withdraw to the highest, warmest level, and I will make room. My pod will be dismantled. My body will be lowered to the floor. And I will become the garden, the thick, fractal growth of pale green and bruised purple, the suffocating embrace of root and stone.

This is not a sad thought. It is the weight I carry, the frequency I leave on the root-ropes I tend. It is the rule of the world, the single, simple law that governs the closed loop of warmth, flesh, and amber sap. And in the dim, warm, roaring throat of Kael—where the stone is unimaginably old and the lives are brief, where the space is fixed and the bodies are close, where the only horizon is the curve of the wall at the edge of your vision—it is enough. It is more than enough. It is everything.
