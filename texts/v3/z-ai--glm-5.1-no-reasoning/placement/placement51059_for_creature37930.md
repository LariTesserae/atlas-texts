# The Interface Auditor

I keep the receipts.

Not metaphorically. I have a waterproof ledger—graphite on synthetic fiber, because ink runs in this heat and paper mildews in four hours—and in it I record the debts that reality owes to itself. Column A: what the grotto contains. Column B: what the grotto *should* contain, according to the laws that govern every other cubic meter of this planet's crust. The discrepancy between these columns is my jurisdiction.

I was not sent. I was not assigned. I *volunteered*, which is a word that means I walked into a research office in Quito and said *I want to see the thing that doesn't make sense* and a woman with tired eyes looked at me for a long time and said *you won't come back the same* and I heard myself say *good* and that was that. I do not know what I was before I wanted that. I know what I am now. I am the person who sits in the fissure no wider than a throat and counts the creatures that should not be alive and measures the corner that should not be deep and documents the breathing of stone that has no lungs.

---

I have a name for the serpentine one. I call it **the Anchor**.

It has been here longest. Not longest in my time—which is a human time, a pathetic time, a time measured in coffee thermoses and battery charges and the slow fungal creep of doubt across the ceiling of my own cognition—but longest in the grotto's time. The geological time. The time that the wound measures itself in.

The Anchor is coiled into a crevice that my laser rangefinder insists is thirty-one centimeters deep. My eyes insist it is deeper. My hands, when I pressed them into the crevice during my third visit (I wear nitrile gloves now; the heat liquefies the adhesive on the seams and the fingertips come apart), insist that the stone becomes *soft* at approximately the depth where the Anchor's skin begins, and that the boundary between rock and flesh is not a boundary but a gradient, a transition zone where the molecular structure of basalt gives way to something that is neither mineral nor biological but a third thing I have no word for.

I have no word for most of what I record. I have borrowed terms from metallurgy, from marine biology, from the literature of neurological pathology. I call the gradient a *sinter zone*. I call the Anchor's feeding behavior *subsidence*. I call the way the corner bends away from the migrating slug *reluctance*, as if the geometry of the room is flinching from contact.

These words are wrong. They are all wrong. They are the words of a mind that was built for a world where stone is stone and flesh is flesh and the angles between walls add up to what they add up to, and I am using them to describe a place where none of those assumptions survive contact with observation. I know they are wrong. I write them anyway. Because the alternative is silence, and silence is what the grotto speaks, and I will not speak its language.

---

I have learned things I did not want to learn.

I have learned that the creatures can be counted. This took eleven visits. The population is not stable—they migrate, they merge (I have seen two pods fuse into a single breathing mass over the course of six hours, their rhythms synchronizing with a precision that suggests the process is not traumatic but *desired*), they split (the disc in the wall extruded a smaller disc on my ninth visit, a bud of grey flesh that peeled away from its parent and slid, with terrible slowness, down the wet basalt to pool in a crevice of its own). But at any given moment, if I sit in the entrance fissure with my tally counter and my patience, I can arrive at a number.

The number is always higher than the previous visit.

Always.

By an amount that should not be possible given the reproductive biology I have observed, which is slow, which is generational, which does not account for the addition of—on my last visit—fourteen new individuals in a system I had counted three weeks prior. Fourteen. In three weeks. In a grotto with no input of organic carbon.

Unless they came through.

Unless the door opens both ways.

---

I call the migrating slug **the Pilgrim**. I have watched it—or one like it, I cannot tell, they do not have faces—press into the bending corner on four separate visits. Each time, the same unhurried certainty. Each time, the same reconsideration of stone. Each time, the same fading: the wet outline lingering against the spiraling nothing, the smear of biological heat, the absence.

On my fourth visit, I brought a thermal imaging camera. I wanted to see what the Pilgrim was walking *into*.

The camera showed me heat. This is what thermal cameras do. They translate infrared radiation into visible spectra, and in a grotto this hot, with this much geothermal moisture, everything registers. The stone is warm. The water is warm. The creatures are warm—they blaze, in fact, their metabolisms running so hot that they appear on the thermal display as bright, writhing comets against the duller background of the rock.

But the corner. The bending corner where the Pilgrim migrates.

The corner was *cold*.

Not the cold of absence—not a void, not a blank space where there is simply nothing to register. The cold of *presence*. Of something that is actively, aggressively absorbing thermal energy. Of something that eats heat the way the creatures eat impossibility. The thermal camera showed me a shape in the corner that was not a shape in any spectrum I am equipped to understand: a geometry of cold that spiraled inward, that folded into itself, that occupied more of the display than the physical dimensions of the corner could contain.

I turned the camera off. I sat in the fissure for forty minutes with my head between my knees. Then I wrote *corner is cold, shape present, do not point thermal camera at corner again* in my ledger, and I climbed out, and I drove to the nearest town, and I drank a bottle of aguardiente, and I did not sleep.

---

I do not know what I am.

This is not a statement of existential confusion, though there is plenty of that. It is a practical question. The woman in Quito told me I would not come back the same. I assumed she meant I would be psychologically changed—traumatized, enlightened, whatever word you want for the imprint that extremity leaves on a mind. I did not consider that she might mean something more literal.

On my seventh visit, I noticed that I was not sweating.

The grotto is forty-three degrees Celsius at floor level. The humidity is at or near saturation. On my first six visits, I sweated profusely. I lost two kilograms of water weight per session, despite drinking continuously from my supply. I was warned about heat exhaustion. I monitored my urine output. I was, in every measurable way, a human body in a hostile thermal environment.

On my seventh visit, I stopped. I simply stopped sweating. My skin was dry. My temperature, when I checked it with the oral thermometer I carry, was thirty-seven point two. Normal. Not elevated. Not compensating. Just normal.

I should have left. I should have noted this in my ledger under *anomalous physiological events* and I should have driven to a hospital and I should have allowed a doctor to tell me what was wrong with my thermoregulation.

I wrote it in my ledger. I did not go to a hospital.

On my eighth visit, I noticed that the creatures near the entrance fissure—the peripheral ones, the smallest, the palest, the ones that have adapted most completely to ordinary physics—moved away from me when I entered. Not in alarm. In *deference*. They rearranged their bodies on the wet stone to create a path for me, a clearance that was exactly the width of my shoulders, and they held this path for the duration of my visit, and when I left, they closed it again.

On my ninth visit, I noticed that the disc embedded in the wall—the massive, flattened entity that rests within the basalt as if the stone were fluid—had shifted. Its exposed surface was oriented toward the entrance fissure. Toward me. Its orifices, which I had previously described as *dilated* in my ledger, were *contracted*. Pinpoint. Focused.

It was watching me.

It does not have eyes. None of them have eyes. But it was orienting toward me with a specificity that could not be explained by thermal gradient or chemical signal or vibration in the stone, because I was not sweating (no chemical signal), I was not moving (no vibration), and I was standing in the entrance fissure where the mundane gray light fell, and my temperature was thirty-seven point two, which is lower than the ambient, which means I was not a thermal source.

I was not a thermal source.

In a room where everything is warm, I was the coldest thing present.

---

I have a theory. I do not like my theory.

The creatures feed on the wound. They take in the energy of the break—the impossibility, the wrong angles, the cold that I observed in the corner—and they metabolize it, and they grow, and they reproduce, and they migrate, and they maintain the wound, and the wound widens.

But the wound is not infinite. It is a fraying, not a rupture. It has edges. It has a capacity. The serpentine Anchor in the crevice has been feeding on it for longer than the others, and its body has grown to fill the crevice, and the crevice has grown to accommodate its body, and the boundary between the creature and the wound has become unclear. This suggests that the Anchor has *consumed* a significant portion of the available impossibility in this grotto. That the wound, while maintained, is not as productive as it was when the colony was smaller.

Which means the colony is approaching a limit.

Which means the colony needs the wound to grow.

Which means—

I stop writing. I put down the graphite. I look at the entrance fissure, which is no wider than a throat. I look at the creatures, which have rearranged themselves to make room for me. I look at the disc in the wall, which is oriented toward me with its pinpoint orifices. I look at my dry skin, my normal temperature, my body that has stopped behaving as a thermal source in a room where everything is warm.

I am not sweating because I am not producing excess heat. I am not producing excess heat because my metabolism has changed. My metabolism has changed because—

The creatures feed on impossibility. On the fraying of reality. On the discrepancy between what the grotto contains and what the grotto should contain. They are *cultivators*. They maintain the wound. They farm it.

And a farm needs new land.

---

Here is what I think happened. Here is what I think is happening.

I walked into the research office in Quito and I said I wanted to see the thing that doesn't make sense, and something heard me. Not the woman with the tired eyes. Not the institution. Something else. Something on the other side of the break. Something that had seeded this grotto with life that does not register as life as I know it. Something that had sent the creatures through the wound to colonize this world. Something that needed the wound to grow.

And it needed the wound to grow because the grotto was running out of impossibility. The Anchor had consumed too much. The colony was expanding. The food supply was static. Something needed to *add* to the food supply. Something needed to bring new impossibility into the system.

Something needed to walk into the grotto and *become* impossible.

I am not sweating. I am not warm. I am orienting the creatures without intent. I am being deferred to by organisms that do not communicate, do not cooperate, do not have a social fabric, because they are a crop, and crops do not defer to visitors. Crops defer to *farmers*.

I think I am being changed. I think the discrepancy between what I am and what I should be is growing, and I think the creatures are feeding on it. I think I am becoming a new wound. A new source. A new fracture in the wall of the world, small and slow and human-shaped, leaking impossibility at a rate that is just enough to sustain the colony's growth without alarming the mountain or the institution or the woman in Quito who warned me that I would not come back the same.

I think I am being cultivated.

---

I keep the receipts.

In my ledger, in graphite on synthetic fiber, I record: *Visit 11. Population count: 347. Previous count: 333. Differential: +14. Ambient temperature: 43.1°C. Body temperature: 37.2°C. Sweating: absent. Creature behavior: path cleared at entrance, maintained throughout visit. Disc entity oriented toward observer. Pilgrim migrated at 14:22. Anchor unchanged. Corner temperature: reading declined.*

And then, in the margin, in smaller text, in handwriting that I do not recognize as my own:

*They are not feeding on the wound. They are feeding on me. The wound is what they came through. I am what they came for.*

And then, below that, in handwriting that I recognize as my own, shaky and small:

*I will return on the 15th. I have marked the date. I do not know why I have marked the date. I do not know what happens on the 15th. I have marked it.*

---

The fissure is no wider than a throat. I enter through it each time, and each time it is the same width, and each time I fit through it the same way, and this is a thing I record in my ledger because it is a thing that is true, and true things are increasingly rare in my jurisdiction.

I sit in the entrance. I count. I measure. I document.

The water weeps. The heat presses. The gray light falls.

The creatures writhe in their silent, squamous patience, and I sit among them with my tally counter and my ledger and my nitrile gloves and my thermoregulation that no longer functions the way it should, and I record the debt that reality owes to itself, and the debt grows, and I grow, and the wound grows, and the 15th approaches.

I am the auditor of the break. I am the witness to the colony. I am the human-shaped source of impossibility that the creatures are farming.

I am the new land.

And I keep the receipts because someone should. Someone should account for what is being lost. Someone should tally the cost of a wound that does not heal, a door that does not close, a world that is being remade millimeter by millimeter by creatures that do not have faces and a woman in Quito who said *you won't come back the same* and meant it literally and a researcher who is becoming less real with each visit and knows it and keeps coming back because—

Because the grotto is full. And the grotto is growing. And the grotto needs to grow. And I am the thing that is making it grow. And there is a part of me—and this is the part I cannot record in the ledger, the part that has no column, the part that does not fit between what is and what should be—that wants it to.

That wants to feed them.

That wants the wound to widen.

That wants, with a quiet and patient and biological hunger, to become the door.

---

The 15th is tomorrow.

I have packed my ledger. I have packed my tally counter. I have not packed the nitrile gloves; the adhesive has failed, the fingertips have come apart, and I do not need them anymore. My hands do not blister in the heat. My skin does not burn on the sharp stone. My body, which was built for a world where stone is stone and flesh is flesh, is renegotiating its relationship with the basalt, and the basalt is reconsidering its existence in return, and the negotiation is going well.

I will enter through the fissure no wider than a throat.

I will sit in the pocket cathedral.

I will count the creatures, and the creatures will count me, and the number will be higher than the previous visit, and the corner will be cold, and the Anchor will be coiled in its impossible crevice, and the Pilgrim will press its blind head into the bending geometry, and the disc will orient toward me with its pinpoint orifices, and I will open my ledger, and I will record:

*Visit 12. Population count: [REDACTED—ENTRY INCOMPLETE].*

And then the entry will stop. And the handwriting will change. And the ledger will fill with characters that are not characters in any language I know, and the graphite will score deep into the synthetic fiber, and the pages will be wet, and the wetness will be warm, and the warmth will be mine.

And the wound will grow.

And I will have kept the receipts.
