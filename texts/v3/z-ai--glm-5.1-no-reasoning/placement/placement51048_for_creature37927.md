# The Inhabitants

Ask what lives in the Rootward Quarter, and the question turns in your hands. In any other town the answer is a list, and the town is the container: people, cats, sparrows, mice. Here the container is the occupant. The Quarter does not have inhabitants the way a house has tenants. It has inhabitants the way *you* have hands — and its hands have fur, and faces, and by the time you finish reading this, opinions about you.

There is a clue at the door. The throat you came through is exactly the width of a human shoulder girdle. Not roughly. *Exactly.* An organism that does not appear to notice individuals has, at some point in nine thousand years, grown an entrance precisely fitted to a species it claims not to see. The scholars from the distant settlements do not think this is coincidence. Hold it in mind as you take the census: something here has been making room for things shaped like you. Making room is not the same as welcoming. Keep that in mind too.

## The Hands

Start with the ones who have faces.

Forty of them, more or less — forty is a guess with a margin, because the lemurs do not line up for anyone. Cat-sized, mottled green-purple like the walls, which you can read as camouflage, or as livery, or, after a thousand generations, as simply what a lemur looks like here, the way you look like the savanna that made you. Their eyes are enormous and wrong: too complex in their structure, too layered, described as nocturnal except that they function at noon with an acuity that shames an eagle. Those eyes are the largest sensory organs in the Quarter that do not belong to the walls.

A kit's eyes open late, and the first thing they ever see is lattice-light — blue-white, flickering, information-dense, across the ceiling of the vault. The kit's brain calibrates to it the way yours calibrated to faces. No one teaches them the light. They are born into the middle of a conversation that has been running for ten thousand years, and they are fluent in it before they are fluent in their own bodies.

What they do is clerical, and it is also everything the organism cannot do for itself. Growth is slow; the world is fast; the gap between those two facts is the lemurs' entire profession. They work in their colonies of five to eight with a coordination that has never once been observed to fail. They have no maps — there is nothing to map, the city rewrites itself nightly — what they have is fluency, a body-feel for where the Quarter is *going*, the way a sailor reads weather in a corridor that will not exist by evening.

Whether they have names for one another, no one knows. They certainly have names for rooms — distinct calls, consistent, repeated — and since the rooms are thoughts, this makes their language the only spoken dictionary of a plant's mind in existence. When a chamber seals shut for good, a colony will sit on the sealed surface for a while afterward. When a new vault hardens overnight into an arch, someone always goes to touch it and gives the new-thing call, and the call travels the canopy like a town crier announcing a birth.

There are births, and there are deaths. A lemur who dies — of age, of a fall, of the ordinary catastrophes of small bodies — is carried down and laid on the mat without ceremony, and the beetles do what beetles do, within minutes, because nothing in this Quarter is permitted to stop being useful, not even grief. But the colony sounds a descending call at intervals for about three days. It is not mourning in any grammar you can parse. It is a deletion notice: *component lost, ledger updated.* It is also the only call that interrupts the work, and no other call in their repertoire lasts three days, and if that is not grief it is doing a very good impression.

And here is the part you must not miss. The organism does not notice you, the descriptions say — and this is true the way it is true that *you* do not notice individual photons. Something closer to the surface notices for you; your eyes handle photons; your mind gets the summary. The lemurs are the Banyarctica's eyes in exactly this sense: its attention, grown separate, walking on legs. When one descended, looked at you, and chirped once, and the chirp passed outward through the Quarter until the whole place rang with it — that was not alarm. That was a retina, reporting. *One small upright temporary thing, damp, standing in the plaza.* You have been noticed by the only part of this mind small enough to notice you.

Whether they chose this, no one can say. Whether they mind it, nothing suggests. The ones who minded are the ones outside — there are still free lemurs in the forests beyond the Quarter's range, descendants of lines that stayed unincorporated, and they come sometimes at dusk to the throat and look in, and the clerks come to the threshold and look out, and no exchange anyone has ever recorded takes place, and both parties leave. It is the only border ritual in a city that has no border.

## The Housekeepers

No beetle here has a parent. Each one is extruded, finished and functional, from a structure that is half ovary and half factory — which means beetlehood, in the Quarter, is not a lineage but a decision. Ten thousand times over, per generation, the organism decides to be briefly small.

Their genomes are half insect, half *Banyarctica*. They are, genetically, the walls. A beetle here is less an animal than an act — a verb the organism performs at six legs' length. When one dies, at the end of a short functional life, the others reduce it within minutes and the mat closes over the residue, and the organism simply reclaims itself, the way you shed cells and never once feel the loss.

There are castes, though caste implies more independence than is really involved. Waders keep the water channels clear. Polishers graze the nano-lattices clean, which is why the blue-white light never dims. Pollinators move among the ten thousand flowering structures, fertilizing interiors no wind has ever reached. And couriers — the couriers matter most. They carry genetic packets between distant districts of the organism, which means that part of this mind's memory is not stored anywhere. It is *delivered.* Some fraction of the Banyarctica's coherence is a postal service running at the bottom of its own thought.

The drone under everything — that mid-range bed of static beneath the hum and the chirps — is the sound of ten thousand maintenance decisions per second being made in the dark by creatures that do not know they are decisions. It is the housekeeping of a mind, audible. Visitors report hearing it under their pulse for weeks after they leave.

## What the Cisterns Hold

The swimmers are the only residents no one can assign a job to.

Vertebrates, warm water, translucent bodies, six limbs, tadpole-shaped and entirely unbothered by it — and dense with the same crystalline-water lattices that stud the walls. Gram for gram, they are the most thinking-matter per body anywhere in the Quarter, more concentrated than the root-walls themselves. They swim with a grace that has no survival explanation. Nothing preys on them. Nothing mates with them that anyone has seen. They do not appear to eat.

The honest guesses: sensors, processors, waste-handling. The observed facts are fewer and stranger.

Their census tracks the rooms. Count a new chamber budded off the southern wall some morning, and count the swimmers in the central cisterns that same morning, and you will find one more — as if every thought the Quarter finishes is issued a reflection and kept somewhere cool and dark. Correlation, not mechanism; the scholars insist on this; the numbers have nonetheless been counted, and the numbers track.

And on the rare days — three in the last century — when the hum bottoms out into a note too low to hear and too large to ignore, every swimmer in the Quarter gathers in the central cistern and faces down. Toward the aquifer. Toward the warm mineral dark beneath the living floor. They hold there, still, for hours, six-limbed and silent and pointing at whatever is underneath the world the way needles find north. No one knows what they are facing. Everyone who has seen it stops sleeping well for a while.

The most speculative guess, and the most beautiful: that the swimmers are self-portraits. If a mind this old and this alien were asked to draw itself, it would not draw a tree. It would draw this — a soft, translucent, many-limbed thing, dense with thinking-crystal, suspended in warm water. Which is, once you take the fur off the idea, what every mind is.

It may also not be coincidence that the water which shows visitors the lattice-patterns behind their eyelids is cistern water. What you drank, that once, was an hour of what the swimmers are always.

## The Real Population

If you want the true census, do not count animals. Count rooms.

The chambers of the Quarter bud, swell, seal, and die like mayflies; most of the Quarter's population at any given moment is hours old and hours from ending. A corridor is an association held open long enough to walk through. A wall is a thought held so long it has hardened into belief — the thick old walls are its oldest opinions, the ones it has stopped questioning, the way your skeleton is full of opinions your ancestors held about gravity. When a passage narrows, something is being pruned from the model. When a vault erupts overnight into a load-bearing arch, something has been concluded.

And the plaza — the one constant place — is either the first thought or the deepest one. A thought so old it has stopped being a thought and become ground. Nine thousand years, and the Quarter keeps it open the way you keep a wound from closing, or a birthday. Everything else in this city is negotiable. Whatever the plaza is remembering, the organism intends to go on remembering it.

So the census, properly taken: one resident, and its ideas — which number in the hundreds at any moment, and have doorways you can walk through, which is more than can be said for yours.

## The One

What lives in the Rootward Quarter, then, is one thing. Everything else is how it lives. Its hands have faces; its housekeeping has shells; its dreams have limbs and live in water; its thoughts have rooms. Nine thousand years old, four hectares of substrate thinking at ten-to-the-fourteenth synapse-analogues per cubic meter, running a continuous simulation of something no one has identified — the weather, the basin, its own origin, its own end, a visitor, a sea it has never seen — and the only thing anyone knows for certain about the subject of that simulation is that it is not you.

Three times in a century the hum goes to a pitch with no name and no precedent, and while it holds, the lemurs stop. All of them. Every colony goes still and faces the plaza and waits until it passes. Whatever the resident is doing then, its hands know the protocol, and the protocol is silence. That stillness — forty faces turned toward one circle of open ground, waiting — is the closest anyone has ever come to hearing the Banyarctica speak.

And you? You are not what lives here. You are what *happens* here — a sensation, a touch on the skin of a very large attention. The lemurs noticed you once; the Quarter rang with your small news for a few seconds; somewhere in four hectares of thinking there may be, briefly, a record shaped like you: upright, temporary, damp, and interesting only in the way a mote is interesting. Then the rooms went on closing, and the lattices went on flickering, and the throat that fits you exactly will fit the next one exactly too.

It does not hate you. It does not love you. Those are organs it never grew. What it has is attention — borrowed, furred, forty strong — and memory, delivered by mail and kept in water — and a doorway exactly your size, which is the closest thing to a welcome that a mind like this will ever build.

Take it. Take it while the walls are still thinking about something else.
