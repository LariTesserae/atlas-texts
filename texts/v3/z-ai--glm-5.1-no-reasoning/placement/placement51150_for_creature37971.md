Begin with the honest answer: no one lives here. No one, in the singular. There is no creature in this basin that could be picked out, pointed at, remembered apart from its thousand identical neighbors; no individual has ever mattered enough to be missed. And yet the place is not empty. It is inhabited the way a body is inhabited—not by a resident, but by constituents: each one alive at full intensity and in total ignorance, each one part of something it will never perceive.

Go to the walls first, where the stone weeps. The grazers hang there: soft amber masses, less like animals than like textures the clay has grown. Each one is mostly mouth—a broad, creeping sole that spends its whole life pressed against a patch of stone no larger than itself, lapping the mineral film that rises in the seep. A grazer never hurries, because the wall never moves. It thins forward over its inch of the world, and you can watch its meals go through it, dark threads traveling a pale lantern. When it dies it simply lets go, sliding down the tower like a tear down a cheek of clay, and is caught below, tipped into a groove, and ferried away before the wall even dampens differently. Its entire biography fits on one square of stone. It never learns that the stone was built.

The towers are built by the masons—long, blind, bone-pale arthropods whose mandibles have been shaped by use into tools: trowel, chisel, pestle, worn down and regrown like teeth. They excavate silt, knead it with a salivary binder, and pass the loads mouth to mouth up living chains that lean against the wet. No mason holds the plan of the city. Each holds one grain of the plan, a shape stored in nerves rather than mind, and the skyline is the sum of ten billion grains. The only thing that ever alarms a mason is a draft. In a world where the air does not move, moving air means a wound—a crack, a breach, a place where the city has failed to keep itself wet—and they converge on it out of the crowd the way blood converges on a cut: without knowledge, without pause, in numbers.

The isopods—the thick-shelled, fist-sized ones—are the haulers, and they are the roads' co-authors. Their domes have been polished by their own thoroughfares until shell and groove are the same mirror-black, so that from above a marching file looks like the street flowing over itself. They tamp the silt, sweep the channels, and carry the dead and the loads with a gentleness indistinguishable from indifference. They walk in files so old the file has become geography; if one of them is lifted out of the procession, the gap closes without a ripple, because the procession does not know its own length, and does not know that it could.

In the warmest tiers, where the ground's heat collects, live the nurses—smaller, quicker kin of the masons, moving among clutches packed into cells of clay. They turn each egg, lick its shell clean, feed the larvae mouth to mouth, and shove the cooling ones toward the center. Every larva is a copy of a copy, the ten-thousandth impression of the same stamp, indistinguishable from the ancestor it replaces. The first thing any creature here ever experiences is the warm dark, the press of the crowd, and the roar—a noise that has never once broken, not for its birth, and will not for its death.

Below everything, at the bottom of the digestion pits, live the ones that are never seen: pale, segmented masses writhing in the warm black, breaking whatever falls to them—the slid grazer, the mason that stopped mid-patch, the hauler worn polished-through—down into slurry. The slurry rises again, hauled up and smeared across the terraces, and there the true crop grows: the film. Never green, not a trace of green; the film is the color of weak tea, a living sheen that drinks the mineral sweat straight out of the stone. The grazers eat the film, the pits take the grazers, the slurry feeds the film. Nothing leaves. It is a farm that has never seen a sky, fertilized by its own citizens.

And the strangest fact of this metropolis is that its species do not know each other. The mason does not know the grazer exists, though it builds the walls the grazer lives on; the grazer does not know the wall was built; the hauler does not distinguish nurse from cargo from corpse; the pit-things would not know a tower from a stone. Every behavior interlocks—masons build where the seep runs sweet, the film follows the seep, the grazers follow the film, the haulers follow the grazers—but the interlock is an accident of instincts, a cooperation no one agreed to and no one perceives. It is less a society than a mechanism with a trillion living parts.

None of them see. Eyes are worse than useless in the yellow dark; they were never grown, or discarded so long ago that even the memory of the memory is gone. The citizens read the world through touch, chemistry, and pressure: antennae along the groove, feet on the slick, bodies full of the roar's vibration. And the roar is not noise to them. It is their medium—their daylight, the constant in which they exist, the one fact that has never varied and so has become the same as silence. Nothing here has ever heard quiet; quiet may be the one thing this world cannot produce. Nor is the roar speech. No one is speaking. It is the sound of a trillion existences rubbing together—a language with no message, aimed at no one.

None of them knows the city exists. A mason dies mid-patch; another finishes the patch; the crack closes as though the first had never been—as, functionally, it never was. There is no remembering here, because nothing needs to be remembered; no learning, because nothing needs to be learned. Every generation is the first, and none of them are new. In a world that reached its final temperature long ago, its inhabitants reached their final shape: each one born already excellent at its whole life, wanting nothing the grooves cannot deliver, failing at nothing it will ever attempt. No creature here has ever been alone—solitude is a shape this world cannot make—and dying is done the way everything is: in a crowd, in a groove, mid-procession.

So—who lives here? Stitch the pieces and the answer assembles itself. The true inhabitant of the basin is not the grazer, the mason, the hauler, the nurse, the pit-things. It is the composite: an animal the size of a landscape, with towers for a hide, grooves for vessels, pits for a stomach, nurseries for a womb, and the roar for a heartbeat. It has no eye, and never has—none of its citizens has ever seen the skyline; the spires have never once been looked at, not even by their builders. It does not know it is alive, and it never will. But it eats, and builds, and repairs, and replaces, and has endured, exactly this way, for millennia. Its citizens are how it moves. The roar is how it breathes. And the wet, forever-half-finished city is how it stays—always just coming into being, and already entirely complete.
