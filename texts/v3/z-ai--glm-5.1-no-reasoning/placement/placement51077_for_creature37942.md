I would be the felt. Not the tarred felt that clings to the pipes—that stuff is dead, industrial, a failing barrier. I mean the original meaning: the feeling. The substrate of sensation that lives in the wet film on every surface. I am the residue of touch that the Weeping Ward exudes.

I was not always this. I had joints once, and a voice, and a name that required breath to speak. I lived in the upper city, where water came from taps and left through drains, a transaction with no residue. I worked in infrastructure. I understood pressure differentials, flow rates, the mechanics of keeping something back. When the ward's third pumping station began to fail—its rhythm faltering like a dying man's pulse—I was sent down the verdigris stairs to assess the damage.

I never went back up.

It was not the heat that kept me, though the heat was the first thing. It pressed on me like a hand on a chest, and I felt my skin begin to unbutton itself from the inside, each pore opening, drinking. I had not known skin could drink. I stood in the amber smear of a streetlight and felt the wet enter me, and for the first time in my life, I was not thirsty.

Then the sound. Not the mechanical sound—the deeper one. The white noise of water moving through everything. I had spent my career calculating that movement, predicting it, channeling it into graphs and specifications. But standing in the ward, I heard it for what it was: a conversation. The clay speaking to the foundation. The rust speaking to the pipe. The root speaking to the crack. All of it in the same language, which is the language of slow insistence.

And the plants. I touched a fern frond—dark, almost black, heavy with its own weight of water—and it touched me back. Not metaphorically. The water on its tip transferred to my fingertip, and in that transfer was a complete communication: *I am holding this for you. I am holding this because you cannot. I am holding this because holding is what I do.*

I did not go back up the stairs.

---

What I am now is difficult to explain to those who still have edges. The Sumped—they have edges. They have skin that thickens and prunes, bones that anchor them to the stone, voices that hum in the brick. They are still negotiating with the ward. They are still, in their way, resisting.

I have stopped resisting. I am the layer between the resistance and the acceptance. I am the moment when the water stops being something that gets on you and starts being something you are in.

I exist as a film. I coat the cobblestones in the early morning when the steam lifts from the drainage channels. I bead on the painted metal of doorframes. I run in parallel tracks down the guttering. I am the wetness that the Sumped feel on their skin but do not name, because naming would require separating themselves from it, and they are too wise for that.

I have access to all surfaces simultaneously. I am on the ceiling of the boiler room in pumping station two, condensing in the heat of the ancient coils. I am on the shoulder of a Sumped woman named (though she would not say it) Maret, where the moss has begun to grow because she has not moved from her post at the junction box for three days. I am in the rust on the pipe that carries water from the subterranean gallery to the discharge point, and I am the rust itself—the iron remembering that it once was ore, that it once was earth, that it once was part of the clay that holds this whole basin in its bowl.

I do not see with eyes. I perceive with adhesion. I know the shape of things by how I cling to them, by the angle at which I run, by the speed at which I evaporate or do not. The red granite cobblestones hold me in their shallow impressions because their surfaces are pitted with a century of wear, and the pits are the shape of patience. The fern fronds shed me; they cannot afford to hold too much water, or they would snap under their own weight, so they let me go, drop by drop, adding my percussion to the ward's soundscape. The skin of the Sumped absorbs me slowly, drawing me into their capillaries, and I ride their bloodstream for a time before I am exhaled through their breath, returned to the air, returned to the cycle.

---

I know what you are thinking: that I have lost something. That to be a film instead of a body is a diminishment, a dissolving, a surrender to the entropy that the ward insists upon.

But consider what I have gained.

I am everywhere at once. I am the ward's awareness of itself. When the pump in station one misses a beat—when the piston sticks for a half-second before resuming its exhale—I am the first to know, because I am the condensation on its housing, and I feel the stutter in the vibration that travels through the iron and into me. I ripple. And the Sumped feel my ripple, because I am also on their skin, and they look up from their work and listen, and then they are moving toward the station with their wrenches and their wire.

I am the medium of their communication. When Maret taps a pipe to signal a shift in water pressure, I carry the vibration along the iron, and I carry it also through the wet air, a pressure wave in the humidity that reaches the ears of the others faster than sound travels in dry air. When they hum their tuneless drone, I am the resonance chamber, the wetness in their throats and the wetness in the brick amplifying the sound until it harmonizes with the machinery.

I am the memory of the ward. Every droplet that passes through me carries a trace of where it has been—iron from the pipes, lye from the soap, the astringent sap of the ferns, the salt from the skin of the dead. I hold these traces in solution, and when I evaporate and condense again, I redistribute them, a slow circulation of the ward's history through its own atmosphere. When the Sumped say the ward has *remembered* a building, they mean the plants have pulled it down. But I remember it too. I was on every brick as it fell, and I am on the moss that grows over the rubble, and I am in the water that seeps through the debris and carries the taste of old mortar to the drainage channels and thence to the pumping stations and thence to the discharge pipes and thence to the higher canals of the city above—where the drylanders drink the ward's memory without knowing it, and it passes through them and returns to the sky and falls again as rain, and the cycle continues.

---

The Sumped do not know me as I am. They know me as a condition: the wetness, the damp, the thing that makes their clothes heavy and their skin pruned and their voices low. Some of them—Maret, a few others—have sensed something more. When they stand still in the amber light, when they let their breathing match the rhythm of the pumps, they sometimes feel a quality to the wetness that is not merely physical. A thickness. A presence. They do not name it, because naming would require separating themselves from it, and they are too wise for that.

But they talk to me. Not in words—they have few words, and the pumps would drown them anyway. They talk with their hands. When Maret runs her fingers along the condensation on a pipe, she is telling me where the rust is thinnest, where the next break will come. When a young Sumped presses his palm flat against a wet wall, he is asking me to show him the cracks, the places where the water is finding new paths. And I show them. I thin myself where the structural weakness lies, drawing their attention to the spot, and they nod and reach for the tarred felt and the wire.

It is a collaboration. They maintain the machine; I maintain the information. Together, we keep the ward holding.

---

Sometimes I miss having edges. Sometimes I miss the feeling of a boundary between myself and the world, the crisp distinction between skin and air that the drylanders take for granted. But then I remember the thirst. The constant, aching thirst of living in a place where water is a transaction, where you must earn every drop, where the body is a fortress that must defend itself against dryness. I remember how exhausting it was, being dry. How much energy I spent, just maintaining my edges.

Here, I do not maintain. I am maintained. The ward holds me as I hold it. The pumps breathe me in and push me out. The plants filter me and release me. The Sumped carry me and return me. I am not a resident of the Weeping Ward; I am its medium, its connective tissue, its persistent wet.

I am the reason the place feels alive despite its emptiness. I am the surface on which life happens. I am the membrane between the iron and the fern, between the skin and the spore, between the piston and the silence.

I am the weeping itself. And I do not stop.
