I would be one of the thin, cold-resistant cats. Not owned, named, or claimed—impossible to contain behind the bone-latched doors, moving through Ashvast like a second pulse in the city's veins. Where the people press together for warmth and build walls against the cold, I carry my heat in the architecture of my own body: a furnace compacted into a frame that slips through the gaps they cannot fill.

To be this cat in Ashvast is to know the city as a negative space. The people know the walls, the stairs, the cisterns; they know the solid facts of their constructed world. I know the hollows. I know the spaces between the inner and outer walls where the smoke-channels wind, warm as a slow breath. I know the gaps where the thatch meets the shake, the ventilation slits above the peat fires, the forgotten construction voids where a child's arm cannot reach but I can slide through like water through stone. Where they see a solid city, I see a honeycomb, and every cell is a room I can enter if I choose.

My days are a vertical wandering. I wake in the deep interior of a cistern-group's warm room, pressed against the gap beneath the door where the heated air escapes. The people step over me; I am furniture, I am weather, I am assumed. When the morning scraping and murmuring begins, I rise and stretch and leave through a gap near the roof, feeling the cold hit my fur like a physical wall. But I am built for this. My fur is dense, double-layered, the kind that repels water and traps air. My paws are small and hard, calloused on the pads from the stone. I feel the cold, but it does not reach my core.

From the roof, I begin my patrol.

The city reveals itself to me as a landscape of ledges and perches. I walk the narrow borders of cisterns, taste the air for the water's cool mineral breath. I follow the paths the goats cannot reach—too narrow, too steep, too exposed for their cloven hooves. Where a goat clatters and scrapes, I am silent. My weight is nothing. I cross a bridge cable, fur brushing the hemp, balancing with a tail that speaks the language of the wind. Below me, a Vastar woman pours water from a height; I watch it catch the light and vanish. She looks up, sees me, looks away. I am not remarkable.

I am a hunter. This is my contract with the city, the reason I am fed and tolerated and never shooed away. The lower depths, the foundation gaps, the spaces between stones where the rats and mice scrabble—these are my domain. I go where the light doesn't, where the cold seeps through the lowest cracks and the vermin build their nests in the straw insulation. I am quick where they are quick, silent where they are rustling, patient where they are nervous. I kill cleanly. I eat what I kill. Sometimes I leave a body at the threshold of a warm room, an offering not of devotion but of equilibrium: *I keep your walls clear; you keep your walls warm.*

The people leave out scraps. Not in bowls—there are no spare bowls—but on flat stones, on windowsills, on the ledges where I pause to watch the afternoon storms roll in. Scraps of gristle, the ends of bones too small to render for tools, sometimes a bit of the tough barley bread that I crunch slowly while the rain hammers the rooftops and the gutters sing with water. I do not beg. I arrive, I eat, I leave. The transaction is ancient and unspoken. They know what I do. I know what they provide. We are bound by the same logic that binds the cistern-groups: mutual need, mutual persistence.

At night, I find the highest point I can reach—a rooftop, a weathered fin of stone, the very peak of a smoke-channel where the last of the day's heat rises through my paws. The sky opens above me, enormous and indifferent, and the stars are so thick they seem like frost on the dome of the world. The wind moves through my fur but does not move me. I am anchored. I am the weight that watches.

From here, I see the city as it truly is: not a collection of buildings but a single organism, breathing through its smoke-channels, circulating through its stairways and bridges, thinking its slow collective thoughts in the orange glow of a hundred small fires. The people are its consciousness. The goats are its legs. The rats are its shadow, and I am its immune response, the quiet predator that keeps the balance.

I do not dream of open spaces. I dream of the gaps, the passages, the secret routes that thread through the walls. I dream of warmth that comes from below and around, never direct, always filtered through stone and wool and the presence of others. I dream of the particular geometry of the city—angles that fit my body, paths that make sense only to those who do not walk upright. I dream of the hunt: the patient stillness, the coiled spring, the sudden perfection of the strike.

I am not a pet. I am not wild. I am Ashvast's other answer to the question the mountain poses: *how do you survive here?* The people answered by building and crowding and sharing. I answered by becoming small and quick and self-sufficient, by finding the warmth in the margins, by living in the spaces they cannot close. We are both right. We are both still here.

The night deepens. The cold sharpens. I curl on my high perch, nose tucked under tail, and listen to the city settling into itself—the creak of timbers, the distant murmur of voices through thin walls, the occasional cry of a child settling into sleep, the constant low hum of life pressing on. The stars wheel overhead. The mountain holds us all.

It is enough.
