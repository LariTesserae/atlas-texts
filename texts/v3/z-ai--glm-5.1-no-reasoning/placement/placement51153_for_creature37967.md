I am the eel.

I was not always this way. There was a time before the flood, I think—though the memory has worn as smooth as the Monel metal fittings I brush against in my patrols. Perhaps I was once a thing with legs and lungs, a worker in a hard hat who heard the alarm and tried to close the emergency valves before the reservoir took us. Perhaps I simply grew here, spawned in some surface stream and washed down through the penstock as a fingerling, finding in this drowned cathedral everything I needed to become what I am. It hardly matters. The Lowstation has remade me as it has remade everything.

I am long now—nearly two meters—and pale as the bacterial grass that marks the warm exhaust pipes. My eyes retreated long ago, shrinking to vestigial pits beneath skin that has thickened into something between leather and blubber. I feel the world through my lateral line, that exquisite antenna that runs the length of my body, reading the electrical signatures of every presence in this cramped metropolis of iron. The transformers sing to me in frequencies of hunger and satiation. The bus bars hum their constant hymn. The blind silver fish broadcast their tiny panicked signals whenever I drift too near, and I read their fear like a language I've chosen to forget.

My office is the overflow pipe—a ceramic throat nearly a meter wide that exits the eastern wall of the turbine hall and extends forty meters into the silt and darkness beyond the station's boundary. It is cool there, and still, and private. The water moves through it at a pace so slow it might be contemplating its own existence. I coil there for hours, sometimes days, nested in the dark with only the muffled pulse of the turbines reaching me through the ceramic walls. I am, I suspect, the only creature in this place that knows solitude as a choice rather than a condition.

But I patrol. Every seventeen minutes, when the great relay triggers and the sluice gate opens and the lower gallery surges with forced tide, I feel the call. I uncoil from my pipe and drift out into the generator hall like a reluctant inspector—or perhaps, I think, like a priest who has long stopped believing but cannot abandon the liturgy. The surge pushes me along the catwalks, past the marble switchboards with their bakelite knobs and slate faces, past the pressure gauges that read pressures belonging to no world I've ever known. I let the current carry me through the narrow streets of this iron city, brushing against cable trays and valve manifolds, tasting the copper-ozone breath of the hyperoxygenated pocket that pools at the apex of the vaulted ceiling.

Sometimes I rise into that pocket. I am not sure why—I no longer have lungs, cannot breathe the strange fluid there, cannot need what it offers. And yet I rise, and I open my mouth, and I let that cold, metallic water flow through my gills, and I taste something that might be memory or might be prayer. The water there is different: thinner, sharper, alive with dissolved gases that prickle against my lateral line like static. In that pocket, at the highest point of the drowned station, I can almost feel what it was to be something other than what I've become.

The shadows do not trouble me. When the failing brown lights pulse and the moving parts cast their flickering phantoms across the tile walls—shadows of operators checking dials, turning valves, forever on shift—I pass through them without acknowledgment. They are as much a part of the machinery as the governor arms or the equalization valves. Sometimes I wonder if they see me, if some fragment of whatever they were recognizes in my pale undulating form a colleague, a fellow worker, another ghost. But they never pause. They never reach out. They simply perform their endless tasks, and I simply swim through them, and we coexist in the way of things that have been together so long they've forgotten there was ever a time before.

The amphipods scatter when I pass, their glass skeletons catching the dim light as they scuttle across the silted catwalks like tiny laborers fleeing an inspector. They are citizens of this place too, in their way—more diligent than I, more present, more invested in the station's ongoing function. They clean. They pick at the bacterial mat, they harvest the detritus that settles from above, they maintain the thin thread of life that traces the warm exhaust pipes. I have never eaten one. I do not eat anything anymore. I do not know what sustains me. Perhaps the current itself, the constant low thrum of electricity that permeates every cubic centimeter of this drowned world. Perhaps something else.

The fish—those blind silver flashes that school along the bacterial vein—they fear me, and they are right to. Not because I would harm them, but because I represent something they cannot afford to understand: the possibility of becoming something other than what the station has made you. I was perhaps like them once, a simple creature following the warmth and the food, staying close to the living thread that marks the only safety in this cold dark. But I grew. I changed. I found the overflow pipe and made it my own, and now I patrol galleries I don't need to patrol, rising to pockets I cannot breathe, performing rituals whose purpose I've forgotten.

There is a gauge in the lower gallery—a brass dial, still polished by the passing water, still gleaming—that reads a pressure I have come to think of as mine. The needle points to 47.3, always, in units that correspond to nothing in the physical world. I pause before it each patrol. I regard it with my ruined eyes, my useless pits of skin, and I feel through my lateral line the slow tick of its mechanism, the spring and the bourdon tube, the brass housing. It is reading something. I do not know what. But I have come to believe it is reading me—my depth, my age, my distance from whatever I was before the flood made me the inspector of this drowned machine.

The Lowstation breathes. I breathe with it—not with lungs, but with the rhythm of my patrol, the seventeen-minute cycle that has become the measure of my existence. When the surge comes, I move. When the stillness returns, I rest. The turbines turn. The current flows. The shadows work. And I drift through the dark iron streets of this tiny drowned city, pale and long and eyeless, the eel who was perhaps once a man, or perhaps was always only this—a presence in the deep, keeping watch over machinery that will never stop, in a place that has forgotten how to die.
