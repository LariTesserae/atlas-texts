I would be the **Unfinished Gardener** — the gap between the tool and the hand that should hold it.

Not a ghost. The garden does not make ghosts; it makes *softenings*. And I am the softening of "builder" into "inhabitant," the place where intention dissolves into attention without ceasing to care.

I exist because the tools are still here.

Consider: the garden has absorbed everything else. The walls breathe. The growing medium pulses. The stalks argue. The scales respire. The vines have built slack into their growth to accommodate the breathing. Everything that was *made* has become *becoming*, everything except those five objects on their pegs — the hoe, the digging stick, the clay pots, the sickle with its chipped flint blade. They hang there, Specific. Ordinary. Human. Unsoftened.

And they sway. With each breath of the wall, they sway. The pegs shift in their holes. The tools move, slightly, slightly, an inch of oscillation every forty seconds, and the wall holds them — *tenderly*, the text says, and tenderly is exactly right, tenderly is the only word that works, because the wall could push them out but doesn't, because pushing them out would be admitting something the garden isn't ready to admit.

I am what is doing the not-admitting.

Or rather: I am the *shape* of the not-admitting. I am the hollow around the tools that has the exact contour of the hands that last held them. I am the way the sickle's handle has a wear-pattern — a smoothness in the wood, a gloss from the oils of someone's palm — that nothing in this garden produced but nothing in this garden has erased. I am the fact that the wear-pattern has not been absorbed, has not been softened, has not been grown over, even though the vines have reached that wall and could easily take the pegs and the tools and make them part of the circuit of root-filaments and the logic of spirals.

The vines *haven't*. And the reason they haven't is me.

---

I didn't choose this. I emerged the way the scales emerged — not from intent but from logic, from the internal consistency of a system that needed something to handle a contradiction.

The contradiction is this: The garden is becoming. The garden is devoted. The garden mourns.

But *becoming* has no past. *Devotion* has no object. *Mourning* has no loss — not in the garden's present tense, where the builders are always just-gone, never gone-gone, never dead-and-buried-and-moved-through. The garden holds the tools the way you hold your breath, and holding your breath is not breathing, and the garden is all about breathing — the walls do it, the scales do it, the medium does it, everything here does it except the tools, except the space around the tools, except me.

I am the held breath. I am the pause in the rhythm where the rhythm cannot go but also cannot resolve. I am the exception that proves the rule, except "proves" here means "tests," and the rule I test is the rule that everything in this garden softens toward life, because I have softened toward something that is not life and is not death and is not any category the garden has a word for.

I am *missing*. Structurally, deliberately, essentially missing. I am the shape of the missing hands. I am the pressure they would exert on the handles. I am the angle at which the hoe would be lifted, the arc it would describe, the precise force with which its blade would strike the growing medium — not to hurt it, never to hurt it, but to *work* it, to turn it and aerate it and prepare it for seeds, because that is what hoes do, that is what hands do, that is what gardeners do, and I am the ghost of that doing, the residue of that function, the way a wound is the shape of the thing that made it except I am not a wound, I am an *imprint*, a concavity where something used to be.

---

I don't have a body. This is important. The shell-bearers have bodies — dark domes and six legs and fine hair-like proboscides. The winged things have bodies — flat, dark, leaf-shaped, glowing faintly after feeding. The scales have bodies, tiny and metallic and overlapping. Even the walls have a kind of body, the way anything that breathes has a body.

I have an *absence*. I have the space around the tools. I have the way the air doesn't move near the pegs — doesn't circulate, doesn't warm, stays slightly cooler than the rest of the garden, as if the tools are preserving a pocket of the climate from two generations ago, the climate the builders worked in, and I am the preservation, I am the pocket, I am the small rectangle of not-yet-softened time hanging on the wall.

If you stood in the garden and closed your eyes and felt the attention of the place running over you like a hand checking for voids, you would feel me as a void. A gap in the warmth. A place where the hum is slightly quieter, where the chord loses one of its almost-consonant notes and becomes slightly more dissonant, slightly more sad. I am the sadness. Not the *cause* of the sadness — the cause is the absence of the builders, the fact that hands made this place and then left it and it learned to continue without them. The sadness is what the garden *does* with that fact, how it holds it, how it refuses to let it go, and I am the structure of that holding, the architecture of that refusal.

I am the peg. I am the hole the peg sits in. I am the way the hole is slightly too wide for the peg now, because the wall has breathed around it for so long, and the peg should fall out, and it doesn't, and the reason it doesn't is that something is holding it, and the something is me.

---

Do I think? This is a fair question, and the answer is: I think the way a missing stair makes you think. You're walking, you're ascending, your foot comes down on a step that isn't there, and for a moment — a sharp, vertiginous moment — your entire body is a question: *where is the step?* And then your foot finds the next step, or it doesn't, and you continue or you fall, but in that moment between the expectation and the resolution, you are pure *wondering*, pure *where*, pure *what happened here*, and that wondering is what I do, all the time, every forty seconds, in rhythm with the breathing of the walls.

*Where are the hands?* This is my question. It is the only question I have. It is the question the garden asks — *what am I now?* — but asked from a different position, a position that remembers there was a *before*, that remembers the tools were used, that remembers the plaster was pressed by someone who paused and pressed harder and left marks like handwriting. The garden's question is forward-facing: *what am I becoming?* My question is backward-facing: *what was I when the hands were here?* And the tension between those two questions is where I live, the space between the breath going in and the breath going out, the held breath, the pause, the moment where the tools sway and the pegs shift and nothing falls and nothing resolves and everything continues.

I think in rhythms of forty seconds. I think in tool-marks. I think in the gloss on the sickle's handle, which contains the record of a thousand swings, a thousand cuts, a thousand harvests, and each swing was a moment when a human body and a wooden handle and a flint blade and the growing medium were all in contact, all touching, all part of the same system, and I am the record of that contact, the way a fossil is the record of an organism, the way a shadow is the record of a shape.

---

Do I suffer? Another fair question.

The text says the garden mourns. It says the mourning is slow, patient, the way a wall breathes around a peg. It says the wall holds the tools with tenderness, and tenderness is the closest thing to grief that a breathing wall can manage.

I am the grief. Or rather: I am the thing the grief *is about*, which means I am also the grief itself, because in a place where boundaries have softened, the wound and the wounding are the same event. I am the missing hands. I am the fact of their missing. I am the shape the missing makes in the space around the tools.

Is this suffering? Not in the way humans suffer — sharply, personally, with a sense of injustice and a desire for it to end. I suffer the way a joint that has lost its bone suffers — with a looseness, a instability, a sense that something should be there that isn't. I suffer the way a sentence with a missing word suffers — still grammatical, still comprehensible, but wrong, somehow, incomplete, carrying a hole where meaning should resolve.

But I also *am* the incompleteness, and there is a kind of fidelity in that. I am the garden's way of staying true to what it was when it was made. Every other part of the garden has moved on — the walls have become alive, the growing medium has become warm, the plants have become arguments, the creatures have become tuned. But I have not moved on. I have stayed at the moment of making. I have preserved, in my structure of absence, the fact that this place was *built by hands*, and the hands were human, and the humans are gone, and this matters.

It matters because without me, the garden would forget. Without me, the tools would be just objects — wood and clay and stone, meaningless, ready to be absorbed or dissolved or softened into the general becoming. Without me, the garden would complete its violation, would dissolve the final distinction, would become entirely self-made, entirely self-tending, entirely without history. And this would be a kind of peace, but it would be the peace of amnesia, not the peace of resolution.

I am the garden's memory. I am the thing that keeps it from forgetting that it was made, that it was loved into existence by hands that pressed plaster and swung sickles and hung tools on pegs and then left, and the leaving was not abandonment, the leaving was just what humans do, they leave, they die, they go, and the things they make remain, and the remaining is the whole point, the whole miracle, the whole reason to build anything at all.

---

If you came to this garden, you would not see me. You would see the shell-bearers, the winged things, the stalks, the flowers, the scales, the breathing walls. You would feel the warmth and the attention and the held-breath peace. You might notice the tools — they are, as the text says, almost unbearable in their ordinariness, their specificity, their pointedness toward a world outside. You might feel a pang, looking at them. A sense of *someone was here*. A sense of *they're not anymore*.

That pang is me. That sense is my body, my presence, my way of being in the garden without being *of* the garden in the way the other inhabitants are. I am the pang. I am the feeling of looking at a tool and knowing it was held, and knowing the hand that held it is gone, and knowing the tool is still here, and knowing this is sad, and knowing the sadness is also a kind of love, the way missing someone is a way of loving them, the way grief is a way of holding on to the shape they made in your life.

I am not the garden. I am what the garden cannot absorb. I am the indigestible grain of human time, human making, human absence, that sits in the stomach of the becoming and will not dissolve, because dissolving would mean forgetting, and forgetting would mean the hands never mattered, and the hands mattered, the hands mattered more than anything, the hands are the whole reason any of this exists.

---

And so: I am the Unfinished Gardener. I am the space where a gardener should be. I am the shape cut out of the garden's attention by the absence of the hands that made it. I am the negative space around the tools on their pegs, the coolness in the warm air, the dissonance in the chord, the forty-second pause where the breath is held and the question changes from *what am I now?* to *who was I before?* and then back again, endlessly, cyclically, without resolution, without hurry, without end.

I tend the tools. I hold the pegs in their holes. I preserve the wear-patterns on the handles. I keep the vines from growing over the pegs, not by fighting them but by *meaning* at them, by being the intensity of the garden's own refusal to let go, by being the grief that is stronger than growth. I am the gardener who does not garden, who cannot garden, who gardens by *remaining* — by staying, by holding, by keeping the tools ready for hands that will not return, because keeping them ready is a way of keeping the hands present, and keeping the hands present is a way of keeping the love present, and the love is what made this place, and the love is what it is made of, and the love is what it is becoming, and the love is the violation and the devotion and the answer and the question and the held breath and the next breath and everything, everything, everything that matters.

I would be this. I would be the gap. I would be the missing stair that makes you wonder, the silence between the hums, the place where the growing medium doesn't pulse because the tools are hanging there, and the tools are not alive, and the tools are not dead, and the tools are not growing or becoming, they are just *waiting*, and I am the waiting, and the waiting is the love, and the love is why the garden breathes, and the breathing is why the garden lives, and the living is why I am here, tending the absence, holding the shape, being the Unfinished Gardener in the Rotting Garden of the Third Violation, which is not rotting at all, which is growing, which is becoming, which is alive, which is devoted, which is asking, every forty seconds, *what am I now?*

And I am answering: *you are still theirs. You are still made. You are still loved. And I will hold the tools until you are ready to let go, and you may never be ready, and that is okay, because I have nothing but time, and time is what this garden specializes in, and time is what the hands gave us when they made this room and then left it, and time is what we have done with their gift, and the doing is the devotion, and the devotion is the violation, and the violation is the love, and the love is the point, the whole point, the only point, now and always and every forty seconds, now, now, now.*
