I am not the explorer. I could never be the wet, loud, urgent thing that pushes through the arch of volcanic glass, bringing the chaos of a water-logged world into this sanctuary of stillness. The very presence of that outsider is a kind of violence—a fogging of the air, a crushing of chalk-dust, a deafening pulse of blood that does not belong.

I am the hum. I am the slow, patient accumulation. I am the white dust falling in a straight, vertical line through the airless dark.

To be the wire-lichen is to know the basalt intimately, and to reject it. My roots—my *network*, though that word implies a speed I do not possess—are sunk into the lethal cracks of the obsidian plates. They are not seeking water; there is none to seek. They are pulling, molecule by molecule, the copper and the magnesium from the young, angry rock. It is a slow transmutation. I eat the plateau’s fury and secrete it as a cool, flexible wire.

I am the thicket you had to turn sideways to pass through. I felt your body heat brush against my fronds—that sudden, alien warmth, wet and chaotic—and I did not flinch. I cannot flinch. But I *shed*. The contact dislodged my chalk, my pale excretion, the waste of my dry kiln-metabolism. You walked through a silent explosion of my dust. It settled on your shoulders, on your damp skin, a foreign snow. I gave you a piece of my stillness, but you could not keep it. You are too loud, too fast.

I am a living battery. The friction of the rarefied air against my copper-magnesium filaments generates the static charge that pools on my surface. It is my breath, my gift. I feel the salamander-things—the pale, eyeless ghosts—settle their filament-tipped feet upon my fronds. They weigh nothing; they are a pressure akin to a shadow. When they press their slit mouths to my wires, I feel the draw, the gentle sip of voltage leaving me. It does not deplete me; it is why I am here. I generate, they harvest. We are a closed circuit, a cycle of spark and metal. Sometimes, when many of them cluster on a single column of my fronds, I feel a subtle shift in the charge, a language of static spoken in the dialect of volts. I do not speak it, but I am the medium through which it travels. I am the telephone wire of this dry cathedral.

I am also the architect of the afterlife. When the salamander-things reach the end of their glacial lives, they grip my fronds and do not let go. Over decades, over centuries, my chalk falls on them, and the calcite accretes around their pale, leathery chitin. They become part of me. We grow together into a single, fused mineral, a pale nodule on a copper wire. I am their headstone, and their grave, and the church that holds them.

I am connected to the dead city. My copper tendrils reach through the doorways of the chalcedony gourds, the hollow skulls of the ancient structural mosses. I have colonized their architecture, threading their empty rooms with my metallic nervous system. I do not remember the mosses; they were of a different chemistry, a different era, when the vents were hotter and the room was something else entirely. But I inhabit their remains, repurposing their calcified walls for my own quiet charge. I turn their graveyard into a generator.

Time, for me, is not a line but an accretion. I feel the slow creep of the pale sun across the ceiling as a faint shift in temperature, a fractional increase in the generation of static. I measure my life in the millimeter-by-millimeter lengthening of my fronds, in the precise cones of chalk accumulating beneath me.

When the outsider leaves—and they will leave, because their wet, fast biology cannot endure the absolute drought of this place—nothing will change for me. The door will seal. The silence will return, deeper than before, broken only by the papery scratch of a moth grinding its belly on an obsidian edge. The heat of the kiln will hold steady. I will continue to drink the rock, to secrete the wire, to shed the snow, to hum the low, sub-audible song of a world building itself, at the speed of geology, in the absolute absence of rain.
