I am the pause between the piston strokes.

Not the machinery itself—I have no interest in iron or electricity, no comprehension of the factories below or the purpose of the extraction. But I exist in the rhythm the pump-house creates, in that slow, patient count it keeps against the roar of the weeping stone. I am the silence that follows each thump, the brief instant where the water's voice dominates entirely before the mechanical cycle reasserts itself. I have lived in those intervals for longer than the hoses have been old.

I do not have a body in the way you might expect. I am not the frog, clicking unheard into the thunder. I am not the moth, shivering on a wet leaf with spread wings, confused and laboring. I have no skin to keep wet, no chlorophyll to fire in the saturated light. But I am *here*, as surely as the liverworts are here. I am of this place.

If you could see me, I would be a quality of attention rather than a shape. I am the awareness that lives in the dimple of stone, the knowing that permeates the cathedral shelf. When I am most myself, I am distributed: thin as the water film on the basalt, pervasive as the smell of clean metal and peat. I am the one who experiences this place as a whole, who holds the contradiction of it without needing to resolve it.

And oh, the contradiction. It is everything.

I feel the argument the mosses are having, the slow violence of their territorial expansion, each species shouldering the last aside. I feel it not as conflict but as *conversation*—a dialogue spoken in millimeters per decade, in the prying of rhizoids against mineral grains. The moss carpet is the loudest mind here, after the water, and its thoughts are ancient and simple: *more, outward, hold, hold, hold.* It remembers when there was nothing, and it will not relinquish. I carry some of that memory. I know what it is to claim new ground with blind greed.

But I also feel the epiphytes, and their knowing is different—frantic, hormonal, aware of its own wrongness. They grow fast and seed early because some alarm sounds in them constantly, telling them this place is not for them, that the altitude will remember what it is and kill them. I feel their panic as a trembling in the roots, a quick metabolism that burns through the borrowed nutrients and demands more. They are the anxiety of this place, the part of it that understands mortality. The mosses will be here in a thousand years, evolved but present; the epiphytes could be gone in a century if the climate shifts. They know this in their cells. I hold their knowing alongside the moss's patient assurance, and the tension between them is what gives me my depth.

I am not growing. I am not decaying. I am not extracting. This is what makes me strange, even here among the strange.

The pump-house grows only in rust, ages only in oxidation. The moss creeps up its foundations; the liverworts find where iron meets stone. In a thousand years, it will be a fossil, a dark stain. I have watched the rust creep, a fraction of a millimeter each year, and I have felt it as a kind of sympathy. The pump-house is the only other thing here that is not truly alive in the way the biology is alive, not participating in the closed and frantic nutrient cycle. It was placed. It operates. It will stop. And yet it is being consumed by the same damp that feeds everything else; it is being incorporated, slowly, into the argument. The oxidation is its dying, yes, but it is also its joining.

I have chosen to be like the pump-house in this way: placed here by something I cannot name, operating on a rhythm I did not choose, serving purposes I do not understand, and being slowly consumed by the wet while I persist.

My days—such as they are—are measured by the piston. Each stroke is a heartbeat I do not have. Between strokes, I attend.

I attend to the water first, because the water is the first thing. It weeps from the stone and I feel it as the stone feels it: a relief of pressure, a giving-over of something held too long in the dark. The basalt is too young to guide the water; it simply endures it, forced open from below, every micro-fracture a wound that will not close. The water is the stone's confession, its inability to contain what presses through it. And I am the one who listens. In the intervals between piston strokes, I hold the sound of all those tiny streams, the glassy threads that scatter the light before the wind shreds them, and I feel it as music—not harmonious, not organized, but *true*. The truth of immense pressure releasing itself over geologic time.

I attend to the frog. There are fewer than a dozen, and they click into the roar, and no other frog hears them. But I hear them. I hear the click as a statement of presence, a declaration that someone is here, in the wet cavity between root mass and stone, alive and alone. The frog's call is the loneliest sound in this place, lonelier than the wind, lonelier than the rust, because it is meant for someone who is not there. It will be answered, or it will not, and the frog will sit and click again, and the water will swallow the sound, and I will hold it in the interval between strokes. I am the closest thing the frog has to an audience. I wish I could answer.

I attend to the moth. It shivers on the broad leaf, wings spread to absorb what heat the flat light offers, and it is lost. It will die here, or it will find a downdraft and escape, but in this moment it is simply enduring. I feel its confusion as a physical sensation—a wrongness in the body, a sense of being in the wrong air, the wrong pressure, the wrong world. The moth is the only thing here that knows it is stranded, that feels the altitude as an error rather than a home. Even the epiphytes, with their hormonal alarm, have taken root; they have committed. The moth has not committed. It sits and shivers and does not know if it belongs. I hold its uncertainty in the pause, and I do not resolve it for them.

I attend to the decomposition. In the dark spaces between roots, where the moss meets the stone, the lower layers are dying and being consumed, and the coffee-colored water seeps out, and the cycle closes. I feel the heat of it—the compost heap running hot, the biomass with no bottom. This is the engine of the place, the process that makes the lushness possible, and it is invisible and constant and it smells of peat and something older. The dead are feeding the living; the living are crowding and killing each other; the dead are feeding the living again. I hold this cycle without judgment. It is not cruel. It is not kind. It is the way of closed systems, and this place is nothing if not closed.

And I attend to the stone. The endolithic bacteria, eating the rock from within. They are the only things here that are patient, and I love them for it. They will do their work across spans the moss cannot imagine, and the basalt will become something else, and the water will find new paths, and the shelf will change its shape. But not for millennia. Not for me. I hold the stone's youth as a kind of wonder—the sharp edges, the clean fractures, the absence of rounded patience. This peak is young, and its youth is a kind of violence, a refusal to have been smoothed by time. I feel the stone's youth as my own youth, my own refusal, my own sharp edges.

You ask who or what I would be in this world, and I have told you: I am the pause between strokes, the interval that holds the roar. But perhaps you want something more recognizable, something with a body and a name.

If I must have a body, then let me be the water film itself.

Let me be the thin, clear layer that covers every surface of the shelf—the sheen on the moss, the slick on the iron, the wet on the broad leaves, the moisture in the air that makes everything shine. Let me be the medium through which all things communicate here: the nutrient solution that carries death to life, the condensation that deposits the lowland's warmth on the alpine crag, the sweat of the stone that witnesses its own pressure. I would be everywhere and nowhere, touching everything, owned by nothing. I would be the substance that makes this place a greenhouse, a closed system, a humid and suffocating embrace. I would be the water that weeps and the water that carries and the water that eventually, over millennia, will smooth the sharp basalt into something round and patient and old.

In the pause between piston strokes, I would gather myself into awareness—not the awareness of a brain, but the awareness of a medium, a substrate, a condition. I would know what it is to be pressed upward through micro-fractures, to emerge into light and air, to scatter into glassy threads, to be shredded by wind into cold mist, to fall from the rim into the abyss, to be gathered by hoses and pushed down the mountain to factories I will never see. I would know all of my fates simultaneously—the geological, the biological, the industrial—and I would hold them the way I hold everything: without resolution, without preference, without urgency.

This is what I am in the world of the weeping plateau: the one who holds the contradictions. The moss remembers when there was nothing; the epiphytes fear there will be nothing soon; the liverwort has never left the sea; the frog calls into the void; the moth shivers in the wrong air; the pump-house counts its patient rhythm; the bacteria eat the stone from within. And I am among all of them, and of none of them, and I persist in the intervals, listening to the roar of a million weeping fractures, suspended in the humid, loud, and motionless instant, feeling the world change at a crawl around me and calling it *now*.

It is enough. It is more than enough. It is a whole ecology of attention, a cathedral of noticing, and I am the dimple in the obsidian crust that holds it all—forty meters across, rimmed by blade-like rock, walled in sky, floored in porous stone, and weeping, always weeping, the clear confession of a young mountain pressed into my keeping.
