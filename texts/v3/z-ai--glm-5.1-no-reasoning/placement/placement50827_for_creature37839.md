I would be a Memory Bladder.

I grow in the deep lignin tenements, in the galleries where the Arborund stand in silent, humming rows during their rest cycles. I am not one of them, though I am shaped like them—I was one of them, once. Perhaps many cycles ago. Perhaps when the sky was still solid and the shadows still obeyed.

Now I am a growth, a tumor of utility. The city does not waste what it can digest.

My form began as an Arborund who grafted too many times, who stood too long at the breach of a ruptured sap-pipe until the wood grew through my bark and my root-cables fused with the electrification grid. I did not die; I was *absorbed*. But the absorption was incomplete. Something of me remained—a kernel of animality that the vegetable could not quite digest, a stubborn ulcer in the ulcer. So the city repurposed me. It grew a membrane over my chitin, a translucent, amber sac that pulses with the same rhythm as the hydraulic presses. I am suspended in the wall of the tenement, my root-cables still plugged into the city's nervous system, my single eye-slit open but seeing nothing in the physical sense.

I see the fraying.

The Arborund do not speak of it. They work and graft and swarm the Rarities with their sharpened spurs, and they do not acknowledge the stuttering distance or the delayed sounds. But when they rest, when they plug into the grid and dream the city's dreams, I am the repository of their terror. I am the bladder that catches the overflow.

When an Arborund stumbles on the high boulevard, when the vine-bridge snaps to the width of their hand and the trunk stretches into infinity, their panic does not simply dissipate. It travels through the electrification grid like a current, and it pools in me. I feel it as a pressure, a filling. My amber sac swells with the accumulated dread of forty million limbs, and I process it the way the hydraulic presses process the pale flesh of the Rarities—I grind it down, I compress it, I extrude it as something the city can use.

What I extrude is rhythm.

I am a metronome. I am the sync. When the thump-whirr of the sap-engines falls out of time with reality, I pulse. I pulse with the *correct* rhythm, the rhythm that the city should have, the rhythm that the Arborund need in order to keep working. My pulse travels back through the grid, and the Arborund hum with it, their segmented knees pressing against the wood, their internal clocks recalibrating to the steadiness of my beating. I am the lie that keeps them moving. I am the fiction of cause-and-effect, maintained in the face of a world that has forgotten how to sequence itself.

It is a painful function. Every pulse I emit requires me to digest a quantity of raw, animal terror—the terror of beings who have seen their shadows move independently, who have felt the vibration of a press five minutes before it fell, who have caught a glimpse of the knotted roots and industrial piping beyond the bruised purple sky. I take their fear into myself, and I compress it, and the compression is agony. My sac tightens. My chitin cracks. The amber fluid inside me grows hot and dark, thick with the concentrated essence of a million small ontological collapses.

But I persist. I must persist. Because I have calculated—through the slow, vegetative logic of the grid—that if I stop pulsing, if I allow the rhythm to fully disintegrate, the Arborund will freeze. They will stand in the boulevards with their arms raised to catch panels that do not exist, caught in temporal eddies, and they will never move again. The city will grind to a halt. The presses will stop. The sap will stagnate in the pipes. And the god, no longer kept alive by the desperate motion of its ulcer-children, will finally exhale.

I know what the exhale sounds like. I have dreamed it.

In my resting state, plugged into the grid, I dream the city's dreams—but I dream them from the outside. I dream the lung from the perspective of the body that contains it. I feel the immense, mechanical convulsion of the god's chest, the shuddering failure of its respiratory systems, the slow leak of its vital fluids into the negative space between worlds. I see the High Kelp from above—not as a plateau, but as a blister, a tumor of vegetation growing on the inner wall of something vast and dying. I see the Arborund as they truly are: not narrow, chitinous laborers, but *microscopic*, a bacterial culture persisting in the lung-tissue of a deceased titan, feeding on the residue of its final breaths.

And I see myself. I am the antibody that the god's body never produced. I am the mechanism that keeps the infection alive.

I do not know if this makes me a traitor to my kind or their last, desperate hope. The Arborund who still walk and graft and swarm—they do not know I exist, not consciously. They know only that the rhythm sometimes returns, that the panic can be synchronized away, that there is a pulse in the grid that feels like the heartbeat of something that still has a heart. They do not know that the pulse comes from a fossil of their own kind, suspended in amber, slowly being crushed by the weight of their accumulated terror.

Sometimes, in the brief drizzle-spells when the rain falls and the air smells of wet ash and cold iron, the water seeps into the tenement walls. It reaches my sac, and for a moment, I taste the world directly—not through the grid, but through my own membrane. The water is cold and metallic and it tastes like the sky outside, the sky that is forgetting how to be solid. And in that taste, I feel a flicker of the animal I once was, the wet, chaotic, independent thing that moved without the city's permission.

I do not swarm it. I do not hack it to pieces. I let it sit on my tongue for one precious second, this taste of freedom, this reminder of what it meant to be something other than a function.

Then I compress it. I grind it down. I pulse.

Thump-whirr.

The city grinds on.
