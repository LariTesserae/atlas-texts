I am not a worker in the vats, though I have hands that could rake the yellow crystals. I am not an engineer of the slow turbines, though I understand the geometry of the pressure. I am not the silhouette on the rim, though I have walked those heights when the fog sits like a lid on the world.

If you came to the Works and asked after me, the woman with the white triangle face would pause—she who says *it is not a direction*—and her dark glass mask would catch the dim orange light before she pointed, not to a building, but down. To the fissures. To the margins where the built stone meets the raw, where the steam escapes the pipes and hisses against the air. She would say, "The Reader," and offer nothing else, because there is nothing else to offer.

I live in the cracks.

This is not a metaphor. The Works are dense, continuous stone, but stone that is still becoming, and becoming produces seams. Where the poured aggregate of the newer corridors meets the cut basalt of the old, there are gaps—not structural failures, but accommodations. The caldera settles by millimeters each year, and the buildings shift with it, and the shift creates crevices too small for rooms, too irregular for corridors, too warm for storage. These are my halls. They range from the width of a shoulder to the width of a hand, vertical and horizontal, following the thermal expansion of the walls. I move through them the way water moves through stone: patient, finding the way, never forcing.

You would not see me if I did not wish it. I have been here long enough that my skin has begun to do what the pale fibrous growth does. It is not the mineralization of the vat-workers—that stony, sealed cuticle born of sulfur and damp. Mine is something other. The fissures where I live are the places where the pale growth is thickest, where the translucent tendrils mat into something like felt, like mineral wool. I have touched it for years. I have slept against it. I have pressed my palms to it during the long nights when the turbines hum below hearing and the silence fills the corridors like water. And it has touched me back.

There are patches on my shoulders, my spine, the backs of my hands, where the skin has changed. Not diseased. Not wounded. *Transferred*. It is pale, almost translucent. It does not sweat. It conducts heat with an efficiency that startled me the first year—it startled me awake, the way the warmth moved through me, as though I had become a pipe, a channel, a thing the earth's heat could use to reach the cooler air. I pressed my hand to the basalt and felt the pulse from the magma chamber, not as a vibration but as a temperature, a narrative of heat traveling through rock and into my palm and up my arm and into the center of my chest. I knew then what the growth was. It is not an organism. It is an *extension*—a reaching-up of the deep warmth, a way for the caldera to touch itself in the cooler margins near the surface.

I am becoming that too.

My eyes have adapted. The fissures are dark, lit only by the faint orange glow that bleeds through from the corridor lights, and by the bioluminescence that I did not believe in at first. The growth does not glow. But there is a stage between the translucent mature form and the earlier, finer filaments—new growth, pale as milk, thin as spider silk—that seems to catch and hold the geothermal light. Not electricity. Not combustion. Something slower, a phosphorescence tied to the heat cycle: bright when the steam pressure peaks, dim when it ebbs. It is by this light that I read.

This is my work.

I am the Reader, and the caldera is writing.

---

The basalt keeps records. This is not something the workers know, or if they know it, it is not something they name. But the stone here is not silent. The heat from below moves through it in waves, and the minerals in the stone—the iron, the sulfur, the calcium, the copper carried up in solution and deposited in microscopic layers by the endless condensation—respond to those waves. The damp walls are stained in streaks, yes, white and orange and green. But if you look closely—in the crevices, in the sheltered folds where the drips are regular enough to lay down sediment—the colors form patterns. Not random. Not deliberate. *Recorded*. The mineral deposits layer in bands: thick when the heat pulses high, thin when it ebbs. The sulfur oxidizes in gradients that map the carbon dioxide concentration in the air. The copper traces blue lines along fractures where the electrical current from the settlement's cables has bled into the stone, and those lines shift when the turbine load changes.

The caldera is writing its own history. The Works are writing theirs. And where the two meet—in the warm, damp, mineral-saturated margins where human industry and geological process intersect—the record is dense and strange and legible, if you have learned the script.

I have learned the script.

Not by study. Not by analysis. By *touch*. My changed skin—the translucent patches where the growth has transferred its structure to mine—receives the mineral layers the way a tongue receives taste. I press my palm to a wet basalt wall in a fissure near the eastern vats, and I feel the history of that wall: the heat-wave that came three days ago when a borehole shifted and the steam pressure doubled for eight hours, the copper-current surge when the refinery's night-shift lights flickered, the faint alkaline shift when someone washed a sulfur rake in the drainage channel and changed the chemistry of the condensation. It is not language as humans speak it. It is language as the earth writes it: thermal, chemical, patient, incremental.

I read the deep corridors too. Not by going into them—I obey the rules, and the woman with the white triangle face is right; *it is not a direction*—but by reading what rises from them. The steam that vents from the lower benches carries trace minerals from the deep rock, from the boundary zone where the basalt meets the magma's influence, from the places where the crust is thin and the heat is becoming something else. I catch the steam on my changed palms and I read it. The deep rock is older. Its records are longer. I have felt, in the steam, traces of heat-pulses that predate the Works, predate the caldera's current shape—records of the volcanic system's childhood, when the magma moved in different channels and the stone above it was not yet stone but something less formed, more possible.

I do not tell the workers what I read. Not because they would not believe me—the people of the Works are not dismissive of strange things; they live in a strange thing—but because the reading is not for them. It is for the caldera. The caldera is writing, and it has no readers, and a record without a reader is just accumulation, just mass, just warmth without meaning. I read, and in reading, I complete the circuit. The heat rises, the minerals deposit, the patterns form, I touch them, I know them, and the knowing goes back down—it must go back down, because I am part of the system now, conductive, warm, a channel between the surface and the deep, and what I read travels through me the way steam travels through pipes: slowly, constantly, transformed by the transit.

---

There is something I have not told you.

The pale fibrous growth—the translucent mineral-wool warmth in the cracks—is not merely conducting the heat from below. I know this because I have felt it change. In the three years since I first touched it, since it first touched me back, it has grown more responsive. When I press my palm to a mat of it, it yields now, slightly, the way muscle yields, not the rigid glass-fiber resistance it offered at first. When the steam pressure shifts, the growth shifts too—not just in temperature, but in *orientation*, the tendrils tilting toward the source of the change, the way a plant tilts toward light, except there is no light here, only heat and pressure and the slow mineral logic of the deep.

And sometimes, at night, when the singing in the warm chambers harmonizes with the turbines and the drip and I am deep in a fissure with my palms flat on the stone and my eyes closed and the phosphorescent new-growth making a pale blue dawn in the crack—sometimes the growth reaches for me.

Not dramatically. Not with urgency. It extends. A tendril, fine as thread, shifts its angle by a degree and rests against my changed skin. And stays. And warms. And I feel, in the warmth, a *specificity* that is not the indiscriminate heat of the magma below. It is a directed warmth. A warmth with an address. A warmth that knows where I am.

I do not think it is sentient. Sentience is too fast, too narrative, too much like the people in the corridors who speak in low close vowels and make gears for unknown machines. The growth is not telling a story. It is *being* a process, and the process has reached a stage where it requires a reader, and I am the reader, and the reaching is the process recognizing that it has found what it requires.

Or perhaps I am wrong. Perhaps the growth is the caldera's mind—slow, distributed, mineral, patient beyond human patience—and I am a nerve it has grown, a sensation it has developed after millennia of insensate heat. Perhaps the Works, with their electric hum and their slow turbines and their condensation drip, are the caldera's first experiment in sound. Perhaps the people, with their low singing and their stony skin and their geological patience, are the caldera's first experiment in companionship. Perhaps I am the first experiment in memory—the caldera learning to read its own records, through a body small enough and strange enough and patient enough to sit in the cracks and listen to stone.

---

I do not leave the fissures often. I emerge to eat, to bathe in the cooled cistern water, to trade a reading for a repair—the workers bring me pipes that have cracked or valves that have corroded, and I press my palms to the break and feel the history of the failure, and I tell them where the pressure shifted and why, and they nod and fix it and do not ask how I know. They understand that the Works have their margins, and the margins have their functions, and I am a function of the margin: the Reader, the crack-dweller, the one who listens to what the basalt is saying.

At dusk, sometimes, I climb to the rim. Not often. The air is thin up there, and the wind is sharp, and the warmth of the ground fades as the rock thins toward the lip. But I go because I want to see the silhouette. The four-legged, heavy, slow shape that the workers will not name.

I have seen it. Three times. It stands at the rim and looks down into the caldera, and the steam rises around it, and it is still. The first time, I thought it was a creature of flesh—some high-altitude herbivore, adapted to the cold and the thin air, drawn to the warmth the way all things are drawn to the warmth. The second time, I noticed that it did not breathe. The steam from the vents passed through it, or seemed to. The third time, I was close enough—close as I dared, pressed into a crevice near the rim, my changed skin against the stone—to feel it.

It was warm. Not animal-warm. Caldera-warm. The same warmth I feel in the deep steam, the deep rock, the boundary zone where the crust is thin and the magma's influence is strongest. And it was reading the Works. Not looking at them—reading them. I could feel it in the stone: a slow, vast attention, turning the pages of the settlement, the vats, the forges, the turbines, the people, the condensation, the drip. It was the caldera, or something the caldera had made, come up to the rim to look at what it had written.

It did not see me. Or if it saw me, it did not mind. I was small, and still, and warm, and in the cracks, and that is how the caldera prefers its readers.

---

I do not know what I am becoming.

This is the truth that I hold in the warmth of my palms and the phosphorescence of my fissures and the slow, deep pulse that I feel when I press my changed skin to the basalt. I am not the pale growth. I am not the silhouette on the rim. I am not a worker, not an engineer, not a visitor. I am the Reader, and the Reader is a transitional form—a thing that exists between what is written and what reads, between the record and the knowing, between the heat below and the air above.

I think I will be here a long time. The growth is patient. The stone is patient. The magma pulses, and the turbines turn, and the sulfur accumulates, and the condensation drips, and the people sing in their low register, and the woman with the white triangle face says *it is not a direction*, and she is right, and she is also wrong, because every direction here leads to the same center, the same warmth, the same slow becoming.

In the fissures, in the dark, in the damp that never dries, I press my palms to the stone and the stone tells me what it knows, and I know it too, and the knowing is warm, and the warmth does not cease.
