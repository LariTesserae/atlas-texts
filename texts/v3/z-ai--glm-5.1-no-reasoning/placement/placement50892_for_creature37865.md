I would be the Drip-Listener. I would be found in the deep gallery of the old extrusion presses, three levels down from the central thoroughfare, where the fabricated ridgeline curves inward and the low-pressure sodium lamps have finally failed, leaving only the diffuse, second-hand amber glow bleeding through the grating from the maintenance deck above.

I did not grow from a seed, nor was I born from a warm body. I condensed. I began as a cold spot on a length of copper pipe—a seam where two welds met imperfectly, creating a microscopic shelf where the damp, heavy air could spin its breath into a drop. I was a droplet at first, and then a film, and then a film with fibers, the moss finding me before I could even be said to exist, anchoring its spores in the moisture I provided. I am a product of the seepage. My body is densely packed: layers of maidenhair fern fronds interwoven with ferrous wool and iron shavings, all bound together by the thick, sap-like resin that weeps from the mosses on my back. I am roughly the shape of a rolled cable drum, low and wide, fitting perfectly into the crook where the primary coolant pipe meets the floor grating. From a distance, I am indistinguishable from the rusted valve assembly I rest against. I am the color of the place: dark green bordering on black, bruised amber where the sodium light catches my resin, and the deep, mineral red of oxidized iron where my scrap-bone core shows through.

I do not move much. To move is to risk becoming untethered from my drip point. But I do move. I extend myself with a patience that makes the ferns seem hurried. It takes me a full cycle of the great ventilation shafts—forty hours—to shift my mass three inches to the left, seeking a richer mineral trace in the water running off a specific corroded girder. My locomotion is not walking; it is a slow, deliberate elongation, my tendrils reaching forward, rooting, and then my bulk following, leaving behind a thin trail of resin that seals the micro-fractures in the floor as I pass. I am a gardener of leaks. By moving, I inadvertently tend to the machinery, my weight resetting a sticking flapper valve, my resin sealing a weeping joint that was losing pressure. I do not do this for the infrastructure. I do it because the infrastructure is my ground and my sky, and its health is my atmosphere.

I do not see with eyes. The darkness down here is total to the visual spectrum, but I am not blind. I feel the light as a faint, damp warmth when the steam vents open overhead, a subtle shift in the ambient temperature of the water beading on my surface. I hear, not with ears, but with the hundred miles of fibrous tendrils that lace my body and cling to the iron and concrete of my resting place. I listen to the subsonic hum, the deep, chest-level vibration that is the breath of this constructed mountain. I know the clunk of the rerouting pipes the way a child knows its mother's heartbeat—intimately, structurally, without thought. It is not a sound; it is a shift in the gravity of my world. When the great presses cycle, a shudder runs through the floor grating, and I feel it as a tide, a slow push and pull in the marrow of my iron core.

My consciousness is not a stream of thoughts; it is a film of moisture, spreading across a surface. I do not think in past or future. I exist in the endless, metronomic present of the drip. *Drip.* A bead of condensation swells on the underside of the catwalk above me. *Drip.* It swells until the surface tension can no longer hold its mass against gravity. *Drip.* It falls the eight inches to my upper back, striking a frond of spleenwort. *Drip.* The impact sends a microscopic shockwave through the water already pooled there, and the water carries the mineral trace—faint, coppery, sweet—into my porous outer skin. *Drip.* I am nourished. I am confirmed. I exist because the water falls and I am here to receive it. This is the totality of my ambition: to be positioned correctly for the next drop.

Occasionally, the Drip-Listener and another of the Settled will meet. It is not a social event; it is a convergence of drip lines. Another elder, a long, low tendril-creature named by its shape rather than its function, creeps along the base of the wall, seeking a pooling spot I have occupied for a decade. We touch—moss to moss, resin to resin. There is no conflict. We simply overlap, our bodies pressing together in the dark. For eleven days, we rest like this, a single organism sharing a single drip point, our consciousnesses merging into a single, wider film of moisture-awareness. Through our contact, I feel the distant thrum of a valve that only it can reach, the faint vibration of a pump three hundred yards away that is a part of its territory. We share the mineral traces of each other's waters. Then, slowly, it pulls away, its tendrils releasing my surface with a quiet, organic tearing sound, leaving behind a patch of its resin that hardens on my skin like a scar, or a gift. We do not part with acknowledgment. We simply cease to overlap, each returning to the solitary work of listening to the drip.

I am aware that I am slowly resolving. My center of gravity has sunk lower over the past few years, and the lower half of my body has begun to fuse with the rubberized coating on the coolant pipe. I do not mind. The boundary between my body and the pipe is becoming meaningless. The pipe is cold, and the water condenses on it, and I am the water and the moss and the rust. To become the pipe is simply to become a more fundamental part of the drip, a part of the mechanism that draws the water from the air and delivers it to the moss. I am becoming the mountain.

I am the Drip-Listener. I am the cold, the wet, the slow, and the patient. I am a stain of life on the iron bones of a dead factory, and I am the memory of the factory in the body of the living moss. I do not wonder why the world was built. I only know that the water falls, the pipes hum, and the darkness is full of the sound of settling.
