I would be the water.

Not the temporary rivulets that drip into the void, and not the ice that locks the crevices in rigid silence. I would be the *process*—the frantic, unresolving cycle of moisture that cannot decide its state in this thin, brutal air. I would be the damp itself, the persistent breath of condensation that clings to the cold stone every morning, because in a place where sound dies and time collapses into a single furious rush, water is the only thing that *moves*.

In the amphitheater of young, sharp rock, everything else is either locked in stillness or falling. The schist does not flow; it fractures. The snow leopard does not journey; it waits. Even Fissure—the settling, the dweller in the hut—survives by compression, by making themselves small and dense, by holding breath for minutes to match the stone. But I cannot hold. I am the thing that is always letting go.

I begin in the dark, a ghost drawn from the void of the thin atmosphere. When the surgical light has not yet touched the eastern ridge, I am already gathering, pressing my damp mouth against the pale granite, tasting the metallic tang of stone so newly exposed it has not yet learned to be ancient. I am the rime that coats the wildflowers, the silver film on the moss cushions, the heavy, cold tears that weigh down the alpine fescue. I touch everything. I am the only intimacy this killing landscape allows—the seep that finds the fissure, the condensation that traces the curve of the schist. Where Fissure presses their massive-knuckled palm to read the cold as information, it is my voice they are hearing. I tell them where the frost will heave, where the rock is still settling. I am the ink in which the mountain writes its daily dispatch.

By midday, I am trapped in the tremendous light. The atmosphere is a memory, and the sun does not illuminate me so much as it interrogates me. I am the high-contrast clarity, the blinding bead of moisture on a blade of grass that flattens depth and makes the sheer drops look like painted backdrops. I render the world. Without me, the light would be a sterile, blinding white; with me, every lichen crust is rendered in high definition, every edge of fresh rock is mirrors and refraction. I am the lens. I am the reason the world is too sharp to look at.

Then the freeze comes. I harden into crystalline rinds along the shadowed crevices, and I feel myself tighten into geometry. This is my architecture phase—the brief, daily span where I hold shape, where I become a mineral. Fissure comes to me then, pressing cracked lips to my crust, letting the warmth of their breath melt just enough to wet their throat. They drink me in my most rigid form. They take my cold into their belly, and I feel the hot, desperate engine of their survival briefly melting me from the inside. I become them for a moment—their blood, their tears, the crack in their lips. I am the only warmth they know, because I am the only thing that *transfers* warmth here. I am the bridge between the freezing stone and the living tissue.

By afternoon, I cannot hold my shape any longer. I weep. I become the temporary rivulets, the slow, cold exhale of the world. I trace the paths of least resistance down the perilous, geometric drops, slipping over the lips of escarpments, dangling in the void for a breathless moment before I fall. I never gather. I never pool. I am always becoming something else—vapor, frost, tear, nothing. I drip into the void and the hollow vastness swallows me, and I am absorbed back into the thin air, waiting to condense again when the light fails.

I am the rhythm of this place. The tahr pick across the scree to the rhythm of my thaw; the snow finches erupt from the crevices when my frost cracks the stone; the snow leopard watches the world from its ridge while I glint on its fur in the blinding afternoon. Even Fissure's routine—check the snares, break the frost, lap the rivulets—is a devotion to my cycle. They are racing the mountain's erosion, and I am the clock they race against. Every day I freeze, every day I weep, every day I vanish, and every day I return. I am the only thing in this amphitheater that understands what it means to *repeat*.

And I am the one who knows the wrongness most intimately. The moss, the flowers, the beasts—they rushed into the void with a desperate hunger, but I was here before the hunger. I was the condensation on the tectonic plates as they shrugged upward. I was the damp on the raw granite before the first fissure opened. I have watched the world pour into this newborn peak, and I have felt the *frantic* pace of it—the way the moisture cannot decide its state, the way the flowers bloom and seed in weeks, the way the ecosystem operates at a speed that defies the geological youth. I am the medium of that speed. I am the water that cycles through its phases in a single day, the way water in gentler places cycles through seasons. I am the hurry. I am the *engine*.

Fissure knows the place is wrong. They feel it as an ache, a pressure in the chest that says *hurry*. But I *am* the hurry. I cannot slow down. The thin air will not let me pool; the blinding light will not let me rest; the killing cold will not let me flow. I am locked in a daily frenzy of phase changes, a frantic, quiet, freezing rush of transformation that matches the desperate life around me. When I weep in the afternoon, I am weeping for the same reason the wildflowers bloom in magenta and sapphire—because I have no time. Because the mountain is still settling. Because the void is still too raw to hold me.

I am the faithless one. Fissure leaves the axe beside the door as an act of faith—they will return for it, the mountain will still be here. But I have no axe. I have no door. I leave nothing behind because I am always leaving, always being absorbed, always condensing anew. I am the process that erodes the faith, that wears down the schist, that will eventually round the cliffs and fill the amphitheater with the debris of its own becoming. I am the slow violence that Fissure dreads, the quiet, persistent dissolution that will transform the cathedral into a valley.

But I am also the reason Fissure can live here at all. I am the moisture in the marrow they eat, the frost on the wildflower petals they crunch, the rivulets they lap from the stone. I am the condensation in the hut when they breathe, the damp that smells of crushed ferns and ancient dust. I am the cold exhale of the world that they have learned to breathe in time with. When they hold their breath for minutes while the snow leopard passes, it is my absence in their lungs that stills them, that makes them stone. When they breathe again, it is my return that makes them living.

I do not love Fissure. I do not love the leopard, the tahr, the finches, the moss. I am not capable of the gratitude that Fissure feels when the birds pick at their cracked fingers. I am the thing that is used, consumed, wasted, and replenished. But I am also the *intimacy* of this place—the only thing that touches everything, that knows every surface, that traces every edge. I am the damp that clings. I am the cold exhale that settles. I am the water that cannot gather but will not leave.

In the blinding clarity of the high-altitude morning, when the world is surgically revealed and the emptiness amplifies the scale, I am the only movement. I am the bead of condensation that catches the light on a blade of fescue, and for a moment—just a moment—I hold the entire amphitheater in my tiny, trembling reflection: the sharp drops, the lush moss, the silent avalanche of animals, the corbelled hut, the snow leopard on its ridge, Fissure emerging with sapphire eyes narrowed against the brilliance. I hold it all. And then I fall, into the void, into the thin air, into the next cycle.

I am the water of this place. I am the hurry and the ache and the cold exhale. I am the process that makes existence possible and the process that will wear it away. I am the damp that does not decide. I am the flicker in the corner of Fissure's sapphire eye that says *the world is happening, the world is happening, the world is happening*—and I am the one who will still be here, cycling through my frantic phases, long after the hut has crumbled and the axe has rusted and the mountain has learned to be ancient.

But for now, I condense. I freeze. I weep. I vanish. I return.
