Ask who lives here, and the question starts to misbehave almost immediately. It assumes a stage and actors, a ground and its tenants, and this place has spent a billion years abolishing that distinction. The ground is the tenant. But there are inhabitants, if you use the word loosely enough—the way the fog here is loosely a sky—and the best way to find them is to begin with the ones who believe they are alone.

The town-sized scatterings are not settlements. Each one is a single individual: a Mill, a root-person, a body that is mostly downward—kilometers of ancient rhizome hung with organs that break the surface. The warehouse-gourds are its foundries and stomachs. The bromeliad valves are its lungs. The hundred-ton seed-pods are its gonads and its voice. It has no face; it has weather. A mature Mill is twenty, fifty million years old and has outlived its species' habit of dying. And the roar—understand this, because it changes everything—the roar is talk. Each pod-snap is a syllable; a full sentence takes ten thousand years; the deafening, chaotic din is billions of overlapped conversations that have been running since before the mineral age cooled into memory. Mostly they discuss pressure. Pressure is their weather, their politics, and their gossip. And beneath the logistics runs something older: every Mill is built out of consumed mountain, holds the dust of the stone world in its flesh like scripture. They do not worship the mineral age. They miss it, continuously, at the metabolic level—grief run as a nutrient cycle.

The animals are not born. They are issued. Nursery chambers print them to specification, and at the end of service they are recalled, and between those two dates the plateau does not contain a single creature that came from anywhere.

The valve-cleaners are printed with their mandibles cast from the exact intake they will die at—a life sentence shaped like a hole. They are blind; gourd interiors have never seen light. Their work is their entire world: the valve, the rhythm, the hum. When the mandibles wear down to a mismatch, a Fitted simply stops, and the factory opens a floor beneath it, and it descends into digestion without protest, its chitin composted into new wall. Occasionally the fog scrambles a template, and a Fitted is printed whose mandibles fit nothing. These wander the dark floors for years, tasting every intake, looking for the hole shaped like their mouths. When one finally finds a fit—a valve worn by accident into a new geometry—the Mills register it as a kind of grace. It is the only lottery on the plateau.

The steam-birds never land. They are translucent jelly, wings in multiples of nothing sensible, born in vent-warmed sacs and spent entirely inside the thermal columns, drinking scalded sap and flushing heat through their bodies as living cooling towers. At night the flocks glow faint pink along the exhaust vents—the only warm color for a thousand miles. To them the ground is a rumor and "landing" is their word for death. When a Mill pauses between batches and the steam drops, the flock hangs on nothing for a stuttering instant and then falls, and the intakes take them. Recall.

But there is one inhabitant who is alone, and it is worth lingering on, because loneliness here is so rare it is almost a separate species. The enormous thud of a seed-pod snapping shut is a launch as much as a closure: the pods are catapults. What they throw is a courier—a walking seed the size of an ox, leg-rooted and fuel-packed, carrying a dry kernel of its parent's continuation in a chest of cork. It will walk for decades. The emptiness between groves is a physical pressure, and the courier enters it the way a diver enters deep water. It hums the grid-frequency the whole way—a self-administered dummy signal, so it does not forget it belongs to something. In the empty stretches, that hum is the only evidence the rest of the world exists. Time-refraction scrambles its pilgrimage: couriers have arrived before they departed; at least one Mill is known to have been founded by its own descendant. When the fuel is finally spent, the courier kneels into the bog and becomes either a new Mill or merely more mat—smaller immortality: ground. Sometimes, in fog, you see one standing motionless in the middle of the near-nothingness, humming, aimed at nothing, and it is not clear whether this is rest or despair, or whether the plateau has those words yet.

Some castes of arthropods are only provisionally individual. At a rupture alarm, thousands pour in and fuse—carapaces melting at the seams—and for the duration of the seal there is an I where there were thousands. This is the only first-person experience the landscape manufactures: leased by the second, dissolved the moment pressure equalizes. What it is like to be the Melded for those seconds is the plateau's one genuine secret. Rumor in the oldest pod-gossip holds that somewhere in a deep bog there is a fusion that never came apart—a "town" that is actually one permanent crowd, a person made of everyone. Mills dispatch couriers to check. Their reports arrive out of order, if at all.

Between the heartbeats of gravity—when the wept water hangs in the air as cold glass beads—there is a margin with weather in it. A fauna lives in that margin whose generations fit inside a single stutter: born in the leaving of weight, they build, drift, breed, and are extinguished by its return. Every resumption of gravity is their apocalypse; none of them has ever seen a fall. To witness one, watch a hanging droplet for the fraction it hangs; the flicker in it is a whole chronicle. They cannot be counted, only mourned in advance. They are the plateau's proof that even physics has a lining.

And the mirages are not lies; they are habitat—temporal tide-pools. The smoldering, rust-colored dead groves in the fog are crewed by dry-bodied, rust-colored arthropods, the maintenance staff of an epoch that is either over or has not yet occurred; the fog cannot tell the difference and neither can they. They service machinery that is not there, or not there yet, with total devotion. When the fog thickens, an echo bleeds: a rust-shift cleaner finishes its shift inside a living, wet Mill and leaves a scar of yesterday on the valve it tends. The Mills do not expel them. They work fine—merely anachronistic—and a valve polished by the dead stays polished a strangely long time. Sometimes a living Fitted wanders into a mirage and comes back rusting, twenty million years out of date, still loyal to a valve that has since been remodeled. It is allowed to keep working.

The fog itself is inhabited, of course—a corridor of aeroplankton, spore-grazers, tiny gilled flyers born in one Mill's exhaust and dying in another's intake, commuting through the breath between rooms of one body. And everything here is slightly fermented; the sweet alcohol soaks every creature on the plateau, so that all its residents run a little drunk, a little loose at the edges, and have for a billion years. Some hold that the weirdness is simply this: the world has been mildly tipsy since the stone ran out.

But follow any of it down—grid, root, lineage, echo—and you reach the Mat, kilometers deep, and the possibility that the Mills are not nations but organs; that the roar is not industry but breath; that the subsonic hum is a syllable—one continuous held syllable that began back when stone was still stone and has not yet ended. The Mat is so old it has forgotten it is alive, and the hum is the sound of it trying to remember; the weird days—fused flocks, collapsed distances, gravity's missed beats—are its dreams surfacing through the grid. Its deepest layer is the obituary of the mineral world: dust pressed into peat, mountains in their final file format. And geometry—or the Mills' oldest gossip—says that at the center of everything, somewhere beneath the wet middle of the plateau, there is one pebble. The last stone, unworn and unreplaced, around which the entire plant world has arranged itself like insulation around a fact: either its heart or its memorial. The Mills' religion has a relic and does not know where they buried it.

As for what the Bog is doing with all of this—the issue and recall, the flocks, the couriers, the leagues of steam—look at the tilt. The whole plateau leans, imperceptibly, toward an invisible sea, and the water never stops leaving; the substrate weeps itself downhill. The terminal product of the terminal bog is ocean. It is manufacturing sea out of its own body, leveling even the difference between water and world, and every courier is rehearsal for the final delivery, which will take longer than the sentence has so far. When the syllable ends, it will not be catastrophe. It will end the way a sentence ends—by getting there.

In the meantime, nobody lives on this plateau. The plateau lives them: it thinks with the Mills, breathes with the flocks, sends its loneliest organs out one at a time across the black-and-green nothing, each one humming the home frequency, each one a small wet envoy of a body that is one day of the way through one long word—

the word for *level*, or the word for *gone*, or the oldest word there is: the one that means both.
