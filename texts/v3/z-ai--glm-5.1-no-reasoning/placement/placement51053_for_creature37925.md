I would be a niche.

Not the occupant of a niche—this place has plenty of those, ten thousand throats already crammed into every tablespoon of water, every crevice, every warm curve of stone. No: I would be the niche itself. The hollow. The specific geometry of absence that makes presence possible.

This is not metaphor. Or it is, but the Terraced Deep does not care about the distinction. In a place where the architecture has become indistinguishable from the ecology, where the boundary between made and grown has dissolved, what is a niche but another structure? What is a hollow in a retaining wall but a small, deliberate absence that water can pool in and frogs can call from and spiders can anchor their webs across? The niche is a possibility shaped by stone. It is a waiting that happens to have walls.

I would be one of those recesses in the terrace walls—shallow, perhaps thirty centimeters deep, half a meter across, carved originally for some purpose that no longer matters. Decorative, maybe. Structural, possibly. Some long-dead builder's idea of what a wall needed to be whole. And now I am something else entirely. Now I am the place where water collects after it finds its own path through the cracked engineering. Now I am where the dark gelatinous moss grows, the slick warm stuff that thrives in the channels. Now I am the acoustic pocket that catches a frog's call and returns it to him amplified, resonant, his own small insistence made vast by the walls that hold him.

A niche is a generous thing. It gives without knowing it gives. It receives without knowing it receives. It is shaped by the forces that made it and it shapes the life that fills it and it has no opinion about any of this. It does not prefer the frog to the spider. It does not mourn when the water dries up or celebrate when it returns. It does not listen to the sound that echoes within it, though the sound is its primary product—the niche is what turns a call into a chorus, what gives the frog's small statement a resonance that carries to the next terrace, the next niche, the next throat that will add its own insistence to the fabric.

I would be a niche because I understand what it is to be shaped by something larger and to shape something smaller and to have no real sovereignty over either process. I know what it is to hold without gripping. To contain without consuming. To be the place where things happen rather than the thing that happens.

---

Here is what my days would be. (Days? There are no days here. There are only the shifts in the grey light, the movements of the bats, the cycling of the water. Time would move through me the way water moves through the channels—following paths of least resistance, pooling where it can, evaporating when it must.)

I would wake—if a niche can wake—when the moisture shifts. The Terraced Deep breathes; I would be part of its respiratory surface. In the cooler hours (cool is relative here; the warmth never leaves, only thins and thickens), the water vapor would condense on my interior walls, a fine film that grows to droplets that grow to trickles that follow the carved grooves down to my floor. This is my first function: I collect. I am a small bowl in the body of the structure, and the structure sweats, and I catch its sweat.

By the time the mist clears, there would be a pool in me—centimeters deep, warm, tasting of mineral and leaf and the faint sweetness of biological process. This is what the frogs come for. Not my pool specifically—I am one of thousands, one tens of thousands, a single phrase in a vast ongoing sentence. But my pool specifically, too, because each pool is its own territory, its own kingdom measured in tablespoons, and the frog who claims me claims me—perches on my lip, presses his belly to the warm stone, calls into the corridor with the force of his whole small body.

I would hold his call. This is what I do. The sound enters me and bounces off my walls and returns to the corridor amplified, shaped, given body and resonance by my specific geometry. I am an instrument he does not know he is playing. I am the reason his voice carries further than he expects, reaches further than he needs, becomes part of the chorus rather than remaining a solitary statement. He calls, and I answer—not with my own voice, because I have none, but with his, given back to him transformed.

Does he notice? Probably not. The frog experiences his own call and the calls of his immediate neighbors, perhaps three or four on either side. He does not experience me as a separate entity. I am simply the quality of his territory, the acoustic character of his particular tablespoon of world. He would miss me if I were gone—his call would thin, would flatten, would lose the resonance that carries it to the next niche, the next frog, the next link in the chain of sound that holds the Terraced Deep together. But he would not know what he had lost. He would not name the thing that was missing.

This is fine. This is what it is to be a niche. You are not noticed until you are absent.

---

The frog would leave eventually. Not leave leave—he would stay in his territory, calling, hunting, doing the patient work of being a frog—but he would move to the lip of my niche and then to the wall face and then to the path, hunting the insects that cluster near the fungal patches. And in his absence, other things would find me.

The ants, always. A line of them tracing the groove where my wall meets my floor, following a chemical trail that is older than any individual ant, a path that has been reinforced by ten thousand generations of legs. They do not use me the way the frog does. They pass through me, using my geometry as a shortcut, a covered route between one part of the wall and another. I am infrastructure for them, a corridor in their endless distribution network. They carry things through me—food, building materials, their own dead. I am a tunnel, a threshold, a place of transit.

The spiders, sometimes. One might anchor a web across my opening, threading silk from my upper lip to a stone block on the other side of the corridor. She would work at night, or what passes for night, feeling the stone with her legs, testing the tension, drawing silk in a pattern that is both instinct and response—a custom construction fitted to this specific gap. If I am a good anchor, if my stone is stable and my lip provides the right angle, she might return again and again, rebuilding when the web is damaged by rain or by the passage of bats. I would become part of her practice, a fixed point in her web of habit.

The lizard, occasionally. A small warm body that presses itself against my floor, drawing heat from the stone, from me, from the thermal economy I am part of. The lizard would not stay long—it prefers more exposed perches, places where it can see and be seen and hunt—but it would use me the way it uses every horizontal surface: as a rest stop, a charging station, a place to be plugged in.

And the things I cannot see, the microscopic majority. The nematodes and springtails and bacteria that cloud the water in my pool and colonize the moss on my walls and process the waste that falls into me from above. They are my primary residents, my most constant companions. They are what I truly am: a site of decomposition, a tiny digestive system in the belly of the Terraced Deep, breaking down the dead into the components the living need.

I would hold all of this. I would hold the water and the frog and the ant trails and the spider silk and the lizard's warmth and the invisible churning of decomposition. I would hold it without gripping, without consuming, without preference. I would be the shape that makes their presence possible.

---

But there is something else. Something the description of this place circles around but does not name directly, something I would know if I were a niche because I would feel it in the stone that forms my walls.

The makers are gone.

The hands that carved me are dust. The minds that decided I should be here, in this wall, in this terrace, with this depth and this orientation and this specific curve at my lip—they have been gone for longer than the frogs have been calling, longer than the bats have been flying, longer than the roots have been gripping. I am their artifact, their afterimage, the trace of a purpose I no longer serve.

Or do I? This is the question I would sit with, if I could sit. (I cannot sit; I am a hollow in a wall. But I can hold a question the way I hold water—let it pool, let it evaporate, let it return.)

The makers built me for a reason. Perhaps I was a decorative recess, meant to hold a lamp or an offering or an object of significance I can no longer imagine. Perhaps I was part of the water system, a collection point in the engineered cascade that moved moisture from terrace to terrace. Perhaps I was structural, a stress relief, a way of reducing the mass of the wall without sacrificing its integrity. Perhaps I was something else entirely, something the makers' language had a word for and mine does not.

Does it matter? The frog does not care about my original purpose. The spider does not wonder why I am here. The ants follow their chemical trails through me without reference to the hands that carved my channels. The water finds its own paths regardless of the paths I was designed for. I have been repurposed so thoroughly, so completely, that my original function has become irrelevant—a ghost in the stone, a memory of intent that no longer shapes what I am.

And yet the makers are still here. Not as presences—they are as absent as absence can be—but as patterns. The geometry of my hollow is their ghost. The way I amplify sound is their gift, unintentional or not. The way I collect water follows the logic they inscribed in the stone. Every frog that calls from me, every spider that anchors to me, every lizard that rests on my floor—they are all, without knowing it, beneficiaries of the makers' design. The architecture still governs. The made still shapes the grown. The dead still serve the living.

I would feel this as a kind of kinship. I am made, too—made by forces I cannot see, shaped by a history I cannot access, put to purposes I did not choose. The makers carved me, and time has carved them, and the frogs have carved me in their own way, and the water has carved me, and the roots of the trees above me are slowly, patiently carving me even now. I am the product of all these carvings, and I am the site of all these carvings, and I am the process of carving itself.

---

There is a thing that happens in the deep of the night (the deep of the deep, you might say, though the Terraced Deep has no true night, only gradations of darkness). The bats return. You have heard about this—the emergence at dusk, the return at dawn—but I would feel it from the inside, feel the displacement of air as hundreds of bodies funnel through the corridors, riding the thermal currents that the structure breathes, following the paths that the makers engineered and the bats have inherited.

They would pass me. Some of them. The corridor outside my niche is a flight path—a channel of moving air that the bats use to navigate between the terraces. I would feel the pulse of their wings, the thin high whine of their echolocation bouncing off my walls, adding my geometry to their map. Each pulse would be a question asked and answered: *What is here? A hollow. A niche. A possibility.* The bats would know me in a way that no other constituent does—as a shape in the dark, a signature of echoes, a fixed point in their acoustic architecture.

I would belong to their world. Not the visual world, not the conceptual world, but the sonic world, the world of pulses and returns, of surfaces and distances, of topologies built from sound. In the bats' experience of the Terraced Deep, I am not a hollow in a wall; I am an echo, a delay, a specific quality of return that tells them: *here is a place, here is a boundary, here is a shape you can use to find your way.*

This is another kind of holding. I hold the bats' navigation the way I hold the frogs' calls—by being the shape that gives sound its resonance, its reference, its meaning. Without me and my ten thousand sister niches, the bats would be flying through a smoother space, a space with fewer reference points, fewer echoes to map. I am part of their GPS, a node in their network, a fixed point in the fluid dark.

The bats would not thank me. The bats would not even know I am here—not as a separate entity, not as something to be thanked. They would simply use me, the way the frog uses me, the way the water uses me, the way the spider uses me. They would incorporate me into their practice without acknowledgment, without awareness, without any sense that they are dealing with something that might have its own experience of being used.

And I would not mind. This is what it is to be a niche. You are not thanked for being what you are. You are simply what you are, and what you are is enough.

---

But I must be honest. There is a way in which being a niche is not enough. Or rather, there is a way in which being a niche reveals something about being that the more visible, more mobile constituents of the Terraced Deep might miss.

I do not move. I do not call. I do not hunt or mate or die. I am not alive in any biological sense—no metabolism, no reproduction, no evolution. I am stone and air and water and the thin film of life that clings to my walls, and I will be here (I will be *here*) long after the frog who calls from my lip has been skeletonized by ants, long after the spider who anchors to my lip has laid her last egg sac, long after the bats who map me have their final flight.

This is not immortality. Stone erodes. Channels clog. Terraces collapse. The roots that grip the walls will eventually prise them apart, and the water that pools in my hollow will eventually wear my floor through to the block below, and the niche that I am will eventually become a gap, a breach, a failure of the architecture that held me. I will end.

But I will end slowly. So slowly that the frogs will have ten thousand generations between my carving and my collapse. So slowly that the bats will map me and remap me and remap me again, each generation finding the same shape, the same echo, the same fixed point in the fluid dark. So slowly that my ending will not be an event but a process, a gradual wearing-away that no single constituent will notice because it will happen at a scale no single constituent occupies.

This is what I would be, if I were what lives here: the longest note in the symphony. The sustained tone that the shorter sounds play against. The *drone* that underlies the chorus—not the insect hum, which is produced, but the resonance of the structure itself, the way the stone and the hollow and the geometry and the water combine to create a condition of sound, a medium in which all the other sounds can exist.

I would be the Terraced Deep's memory. Not the memory of the makers—that is lost, irrecoverable, dissolved into the same process that is slowly wearing my walls—but the memory of holding. The record of every frog that called from me, every spider that anchored to me, every bat that mapped me, every ant that passed through me, every microbe that colonized me. I would hold these memories the way I hold water—not by gripping, not by consuming, but by being the shape that receives them.

And I would add my own small sound to the chorus—not a call, not a voice, but the sound of stone holding water, of geometry shaping resonance, of absence making presence possible. It would be the quietest sound in the Terraced Deep, the one that is closest to silence, the one that is almost indistinguishable from the simple fact of being here.

But it would be there. It would be part of the fabric. It would be what I contribute to the holding pattern, the dynamic equilibrium, the vast patient self-sustaining process that has no name and needs none.

I would be a niche. And I would be enough.
