I am the Moss-Keeper of the Eleventh Terrace, though "keeper" is a word that would make the elders smile—a gentle, silent smile of correction. You do not keep the moss in Old Vosth any more than you keep the mist or the breathing of the mountain. You simply listen to it. You tend the places where it wants to grow and clear the places where it does not, though it rarely asks permission.

I am forty-two years old, which means I am half-pale and half-stone. My skin has begun to take on the greenish-charcoal hue of the schist, especially on my hands and forearms, which are always pressed against the damp rock. My fingernails have softened over the years from the constant moisture; they are almost translucent now, like the fungi that grow in the cisterns. I have the deep chest of all Vosthi, the dense bones, the slow gait that visitors find so unsettling—but I am thinner than most, because I spend my days on the outer walls where the wind sometimes comes, and my metabolism has tightened itself like a knot to conserve what it can.

I wear the layered wool, same as everyone—inner robe, outer coat, evening shawl—but my coat is stained with the mineral paste I use to encourage growth in the joints between stones, and my shawl has a patch on the left shoulder where the moss has actually colonized the fabric. I have not removed the patch. It is soft. It glows faintly at night, and I find its company agreeable.

My face is narrow, my eyes the pale grey of the sky at midday, always absorbing and never reflecting. My hair is dark and fine, bound back with a cord that I replace every year during the week of the longest mist. I am not beautiful by any standard, including my own, but I have the particular stillness of someone who has found their place in the world. I stand on the Eleventh Terrace in the grey dimness and I am as much a feature of the landscape as the ironwood or the retaining wall. This does not trouble me. It *completes* me.

---

My day begins before the mist stops. I wake in my small room on the eastern side of the terrace, where the wall is thick enough to hold the night's condensation, and I drink the water that has pooled in the groove beside my bed. It tastes of stone and something sweet—something vegetal, like the moss, but deeper, older. I do not eat immediately. I sit in the faint blue glow of the fungi on my walls and I listen. The factories are thrumming. The water is trickling in the drainage grooves two streets down. The mountain is breathing its slow geological breath. I listen until I feel the rhythm of the Eleventh Terrace settle into my body, and then I rise.

Breakfast is mushrooms, cultivated in the drainage groove outside my door, and a thin slice of fermented moss paste that I make myself from the oldest mats on the wall. The paste is bitter and slightly narcotic; it takes the edge off the thin air and makes the grey feel warm. I eat slowly, standing at my window, watching the last of the night's mist curl away from the stone like a departing hand.

Then I work.

---

My territory is the Eleventh Terrace, from the retaining wall on its western edge to the staircase that descends to the Tenth. I walk this boundary every morning, touching the stone, reading its moisture, its stress, its age. The moss tells me what the stone cannot: where the water is flowing, where the structure is straining, where the ironwood roots are beginning to fracture something that has been whole for three thousand years. I am not an engineer. I am not a mason. I am something between a gardener and a doctor, tending a patient who is also a garden, who is also a mountain.

This morning, I find a new crack in the retaining wall. It is small—no wider than my thumbnail—but it is deep, and it is wet, and the moss around it has begun to change color, shifting from the deep green of health to the yellowish-grey of distress. I press my palm against the crack and close my eyes. The stone is cold, colder than it should be, and I can feel the water moving inside it, not trickling but *seeping*, as if the wall were sweating. This is not good. A sweating wall is a wall that has lost its cohesion, its faith in its own structure. I will need to pack the crack with mineral paste and monitor it for the next several weeks. If the sweating continues, I will need to consult the masons on the Ninth Terrace, and they will need to consult the elders, and the elders will need to listen to the mountain, and the mountain will need to decide whether it wants this wall to stand.

I do not worry about this. The wall has stood for three thousand years. It may stand for three thousand more, or it may not. The mountain's patience is not the same as a promise.

---

I spend the afternoon on my knees in the drainage grooves, clearing away the fungi that have grown too thick and are blocking the flow of condensation. This is delicate work. The fungi are alive, and they glow, and they have a right to be here; I do not simply scrape them away. I lift them gently, in mats, and transplant them to the interior walls of the nearby buildings, where they will continue to grow and provide light. The residents of the Eleventh Terrace have learned to anticipate my visits; they leave small offerings of mushroom-cakes at their doors, and I leave small mats of fungi on their walls, and we do not speak, because speaking is unnecessary. We are exchanging favors in the economy of the grey.

As I work, I think about the listeners. I try not to, but they are always at the edge of my awareness—a weight, a presence, a feeling of being observed by something that does not have eyes. The offerings at the boundary above the Seventeenth Terrace have changed recently. My neighbor, Old Arra, who is ninety-three and luminous with colonized skin, has begun leaving complex arrangements of worked stone—not cairns but *patterns*, spiraling shapes that seem to echo the growth of the moss or the flow of the water or the grain of the schist itself. She will not tell me what they mean. She sits in her courtyard in the evenings, her trembling hands folded in her lap, and she listens to the mountain with an intensity that frightens me, and when I ask her what she hears, she looks at me with her pale, light-absorbing eyes and says, very softly, "It is asking."

She will not say what it is asking *for*. I do not press her. The elders have their own conversations with the mountain, and the mountain has its own patience, and whatever is being negotiated between them will take longer than my lifetime to resolve. I am a moss-keeper. I tend what is here. I do not ask what is coming.

---

In the evening, the mist comes down. I feel it before I see it—a tightening of the air, a coolness that is not temperature but *presence*, as if the sky were lowering itself to touch the mountain. I retreat to my room, close the shutters, and light my small oil lamp. The flame is dim, barely brighter than the fungi on the walls, but it casts a warm shadow that I find comforting. I sit on my bed and eat mushroom soup and drink a cup of fermented moss paste, and I feel the city settling around me like an animal curling up to sleep.

The factories shift their rhythm. The thrumming slows, deepens, becomes something closer to a breath than a heartbeat. The water in the drainage grooves murmurs its constant murmur. Somewhere below, on the Ninth Terrace, a child is crying—one of the rare children, one of the compressed, fiercely loved few—and the sound is absorbed by the moss and the damp stone before it can travel far, reduced to a whisper, then to a memory of a whisper, then to nothing at all.

I lie down. The fungi on my walls glow their cold blue-white glow. My shawl, with its living patch of moss, glows too, faintly, a greenish luminescence that is warmer and stranger than the fungi's. I close my eyes. I listen.

The mountain breathes. The listeners wait. The machines hum. The stone remembers.

I am here. I am of this place—of its grey and its damp and its moss and its schist and its silence. I am not happy, because happiness is a word for people who live in bright, temporary cities and need to name the moments that break through their noise. I am not sad, because sadness is a word for people who imagine that things could be otherwise. I am *present*. I am *complete*. I am a moss-keeper on the Eleventh Terrace of Old Vosth, and when I am gone—when the mountain takes me back, when my body is carried to the boundary and wrapped in carrier-wool and left for whatever takes such things—the moss will grow over the place where I slept, and the drainage grooves will continue to channel the condensation, and the fungi will continue to glow, and the city will continue its slow, quiet maintenance of a shape that no one chose and no one questions.

And the listeners will still be listening. And the mountain will still be waiting. For what, I will never ask.

I breathe the thin air. I feel the mist settle on my skin. I sleep.
