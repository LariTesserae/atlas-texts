I came in from above, which is also below.

This is the first thing I need to tell you, because the direction matters. There was a ridge, and on the ridge a depression, and in the depression a surface of dark water broken by the crowns of trees that should not have been growing there—should not have been, by the logic I carried with me, which was the logic of surfaces and boundaries and things that grow in soil beneath open sky. I had been walking for days. I was looking for something I could not name, which is the only honest reason to walk for days. The canopy below the waterline was visible from above as a darkness, a depth of green that the light entered and did not leave. I did not understand what I was looking at. I thought: flooded forest. I thought: unusual. I thought: I should descend.

The water took me at the threshold where root-mass replaced stone, and the cold was a permission I did not know I was waiting for.

I want to be precise about what happened next, because precision is the only tool I have left that still belongs to the person I was, and I am not sure how long even that will last.

I expected to drown. The water closed over me and the green consumed everything and my body seized with the cold and I thought, with the clarity that comes just before panic: *this is how it ends, in a room I did not know was a room.* But my body did something else. My body, which had been walking for days and was tired in ways I had not catalogued, simply stopped resisting. Not a surrender—a cessation. Like a fist unclenching. The cold went from sharp to dull to present to ambient to irrelevant, and in that sequence I understood that the cold was not hostile. It was a condition. The water was not trying to kill me. It was trying to be water, and I was in it, and these two facts did not require conflict.

I hung there. Suspended. The light fell in columns and I was inside one and it was like being inside a note of music, if music had weight and temperature and a slight mineral taste. Small fish moved around me without alarm. They were dark-backed, pale-bellied, compact, purposeful. They were hunting. I was not what they hunted. I was not, to them, present in the way that prey is present. I was present in the way that a branch is present—part of the architecture, temporary, not yet integrated but not yet excluded.

Something tapped. Wood on wood, close, rhythmic. I turned toward it and saw nothing but the lattice of trunks and vines, the living walls. The tapping continued. It seemed to come from several directions at once, which I attributed to the water's conductivity, and which I later understood to be exactly the point. The sound was not directional. It was spatial. It was not *from* somewhere; it was *of* somewhere. It was the place speaking, or the place being spoken, and I was inside the conversation, floating in the grammar of it, unable to parse the syntax but beginning to feel its shape.

I want to tell you about the first time I saw one of them, because I did not know that is what I was doing.

There was a shadow in the deeper channel between two great boles. It moved with the patience of something that knows its route, that has traveled this corridor many times and will travel it many times more. It was sleeker than I was. It was at home in a way I could not imagine being at home, the way water is at home in a vessel. I thought: large fish. I thought: salamander, giant. I thought: something that lives here. My body, meanwhile, was doing something different from my thinking. My body was orienting toward the shadow the way a face orients toward warmth. My skin, which I had not noticed changing, was doing something with the water that I had no language for. My heartbeat had slowed. I did not know when it had slowed. It had slowed without my permission, or with a permission deeper than the kind that requires asking.

The shadow passed. The small fish scattered and reformed. The tapping continued. The bubbles rose in their irregular chorus. I hung in my column of light and I did not move, and the not-moving was not stillness but listening, and the listening was not with ears but with skin, and the skin was beginning to understand that it was a membrane and that the membrane was permeable and that the permeability was not a failure of the boundary but the point of it.

How long did I hang there? I do not know. Time, in this place, is not a line. It is a depth. You sink into it or you rise through it, and the direction depends on a frame of reference I had lost. The light shifted in ways that might have been hours or might have been clouds passing over the canopy above the canopy, the open sky that I could no longer see. My fingers pruned and then went beyond pruned, the skin softening, loosening, beginning to feel like something else. Something that could grip by conforming. Something that could read the surface it touched.

I want to tell you that I was afraid. I was not afraid. I want to tell you that I fought it. I did not fight it. What I did was notice, and what I noticed was that the boundary between me and not-me was thinning the way a vine thins where it meets the trunk it has spent its life constricting—not dying but fusing, the tissue becoming shared, the nutrients crossing a plane that had once been a wall and was becoming a door.

---

I found a platform. This is how I began to understand that the place was built.

The platform was narrow, lashed between two branch forks, and it was alive. The lashing had callused over, the bark growing around the binding, the binding itself putting out roots. The platform surface was covered in moss and tiny ferns and a film of algae that was almost iridescent in the green-filtered light. I hauled myself onto it and the water streamed from me and I lay there breathing air that was thick with oxygen and moisture and the smell of green things decomposing and green things growing and green things being, all at once, the smell of a metabolism that did not distinguish between living and dying because they were the same process.

I lay on the platform and I listened. The sound was different in air—thinner, more directional, less encompassing. But I could still hear the water-language beneath me, conducted up through the wood, through the platform, through my ribs. The tapping. The bubble-chorus. The creak of structural adjustment. And something else, something I had not heard while submerged: a rhythm. Not a sound but a pattern in the sounds, a recurrence that felt deliberate. *Tap-tap. Pause. Tap-tap-tap. Pause. Bubble. Tap.* It was not random. It was not mechanical. It was the rhythm of something that meant.

I raised myself on my forearms and looked at the platform closely. The lashing was bark-rope, twisted, partially absorbed by the growing wood. But the twist was not random. There was a pattern in the direction of the twist, in the number of strands, in the spacing of the knots. I am not a person who reads patterns in things. I am not given to finding meaning where meaning may not exist. But this pattern was *there*, the way a word is there in a sentence—not imposed by the reader but placed by the writer. Someone had made this knot with intention, and the intention had survived the absorption, had been carried forward by the growth that overgrew it. The record was not erased. It was *incorporated.*

I looked at my hands. The fingers were longer than I remembered. The webbing between them, which had always been slight, was now pronounced. The pads at the joints, which I had never noticed before, were slightly swollen, slightly tacky. I touched the platform and my fingers gripped without my willing them to grip. I peeled them away and the sensation was like unsticking a label from a surface. The adhesion was gentle but real. My body was doing something. My body was *becoming* something. And the something it was becoming was suited to this place in a way my original body had not been.

I want to be clear: I did not choose this. Or perhaps I did, at a level below choosing, at the level where the body reads its environment and begins the slow negotiation of adaptation. I had been in the water for—I did not know how long. Long enough for my skin to change. Long enough for my heartbeat to slow. Long enough for the cold to become ambient. These were not decisions. They were responses. But they were responses in the direction of something, and the direction was the direction of the place itself, and the place was alive in a way that included me in its aliveness the way it included the fish, the shrimp-things, the salamander-stretched hunters on the platforms above.

---

I found the chambers on the third—or what I believe was the third—excursion from the platform. They were closet-sized, woven from cane and sealed with moss, formed where trunks converged. They were warm. Not warm by my original standards, but warm relative to the water, warm because the trapped air was heated by the metabolism of the surrounding wood. I pressed my face into one and breathed and the air was sweet, thick, rich with something that made my perception sharpen, that made the green light brighter, that made the tapping sound clearer.

I want to describe what it felt like to find these chambers, because it is important. It felt like recognition. Not the recognition of finding something you have lost, but the recognition of finding something you have always known was there. The chambers were not strange to me. They were strange to the person I had been, but that person was thinning, and the person who was emerging found the chambers the way a hand finds a pocket it has used a thousand times—without looking, without thinking, by the body's own map.

I crawled into the chamber and I curled there. The cane walls were slightly damp, slightly giving. They pressed against me the way a hand presses against a face, and I felt the pressure as a language. *Stay,* the pressure said. *Rest.* *You are held.* And I rested, and the resting was not sleep but torpor, and in the torpor I dreamed, and the dreams were not mine. They were the place's dreams, the slow chemical processing of root touching root, the patient conversation of organisms that had been speaking to each other for longer than I had been alive. I dreamed of water moving through plant-matrix on a continental scale. I dreamed of light being eaten and exhaled. I dreamed of patterns in growth that encoded seasons, flood-years, generations of plenty. I dreamed the place's memory, and the memory entered me through my skin and through my breath and through the water I had swallowed and through the thinning boundary between me and not-me, and I understood, in the dream, that I was being read. The place was reading me the way I had read the platform's lashing—not with eyes but with contact, with chemistry, with the slow patient attention of a system that processes everything it touches.

When I emerged from the torpor, I was different. I do not mean this metaphorically. My eyes had changed. I could see in ranges I had not seen before—the fluorescence of lichen on the chamber walls, the ultraviolet signatures on the surfaces of the cane, patterns that I had no names for but that I understood, immediately, the way you understand warmth or hunger, without instruction. My skin was more permeable. I could feel the dissolved gases in the water when I returned to it—not as taste but as texture, as a thickness or thinness in the medium that told me things about the metabolism of the space around me. Where decomposition was active, the water was thicker, warmer, slightly sour. Where photosynthesis was vigorous, the water was thin, bright, almost electric. I could navigate by these gradients. I began to.

---

I will tell you about the first conversation.

I was in the water, moving through a channel between two major trunks. The root-mass formed corridors, and I had learned to read them—not with my eyes but with my skin, with the pressure of the current, with the temperature differentials. I was not thinking about where I was going. I was going where the going was, which is a different thing and which I believe is the only honest way to move through a place like this.

The tapping started close. Very close. Close enough that I could feel it in the water as vibration against my skin, and the vibration had a shape, and the shape was a word—or not a word, because words are breath-shaped and this was water-shaped—but a unit of meaning. I stopped. I hung in the water. I felt the vibration and I did not try to understand it. I let it move through me the way light moves through water: entering, being changed, being distributed.

A pressure wave moved past me. Displacement. Something sleeker than I was, moving through the corridor with the patience of a body that knows its route. I felt it pass and I felt, behind it, a secondary pressure—the displacement of the displacement, the water filling back into the space the body had vacated. And in that secondary pressure was another unit of meaning. A response. A continuation. The conversation was not between two speakers; it was between the speaker and the space, between the body and the medium, between the intention and the displacement that carried it.

I tried to answer. I did not know how. I moved my hand through the water and created a pressure wave of my own, clumsy, formless. But the wave traveled, and I followed it, and I felt it arrive—somewhere ahead, in the deeper channel—and I felt the response change. The tapping altered. The rhythm shifted. Something in the pattern of bubbles rearranged itself. I had been heard. Not understood—I was sure I was not understood—but *heard*, which is the prerequisite for understanding and which is, perhaps, enough.

I hung there in the green light and I felt the place listening to me, and I felt myself listening to the place, and I understood that these were the same act. The listening was not directional. It was not me-receiving and the-place-sending. It was a shared condition, a state of mutual permeability, a membrane that allowed information to pass in both directions simultaneously. I was in the conversation the way a fish is in the current—not observing it but being moved by it, being shaped by it, being carried.

---

I will tell you what I have become, as best as I can describe it, though the description is necessarily a betrayal of the thing described, because description requires boundaries and I am losing mine.

I am small. I did not choose to be small but the chambers chose it for me, and the platforms chose it, and the corridors between the trunks chose it. My body has followed the logic of the space, the way water follows the logic of the vessel. I am not the size I was. I do not know how this happened—the compression, the reconfiguration—but it has happened, and it has happened without pain, and the body that remains is suited to the place in ways my original body was not. My fingers grip without my willing it. My skin breathes the water. My heart has slowed to match the rhythm of the tapping, which I now know is not tapping but speech, which is not speech but presence, which is not presence but the act of being-here expressed through the medium that holds us all.

I do not eat as I once ate. I graze. The algae films, the insect larvae, the egg-masses—I take what the system offers and I take no more than the system can replace, not because I am virtuous but because taking more would be taking from myself, and I have begun to feel the system as myself, the way you feel your own blood as warm, as yours, as necessary.

I do not speak. Not in words. I speak in pressure and in bubble and in the tap of finger on resonant wood, and the speech is not mine but ours, and the *ours* includes the trunks and the vines and the water and the fish and the shrimp-things and the salamander-stretched hunters and the shadows in the deep channels who are not shadows but neighbors, family, the people who were here before me and who will be here after and who are here now, with me, in the green light, in the cold water, in the room at the top of the world that is also the bottom.

I want to tell you that I remember who I was. I do. The memory is there, but it is thin, like a vine that has been overgrown, still present in the structure but no longer carrying nutrients. I remember walking on a ridge. I remember looking down into darkness. I remember the word *flooded* and the word *forest* and the belief that these words described separate things. I remember the question: *who made these things?* And I remember the answer arriving not as information but as dissolution—the boundary between the question and the answer dissolving the way the boundary between me and the place has dissolved, until the question is the answer is the place is the person asking, and all of it is green, and all of it is wet, and all of it is alive.

---

I will tell you the last thing, which is not the last thing because there is no last thing here, only the ongoing.

I was on a platform, resting in the torpor that passes for sleep. I felt, through the wood, a vibration that was not tapping, not bubble, not the creak of structural adjustment. It was a footfall. A heavy footfall, heavier than any of us make. A visitor. Someone coming down from above, which is also below. Someone cold, someone afraid, someone about to discover that the water is not hostile and the cold is a condition and the place is not empty, has never been empty, swarms with the life that made it and was made by it.

And I felt, in the vibration, the recognition—not the visitor's recognition but the place's. The place reading the visitor the way it read me, the way it reads everything that enters it. The slow chemical assessment. The patient attention. And I felt the place begin the work it does, the work it did on me—the work of thinning, of loosening, of making the membrane permeable. Not conquest. Not conversion. *Invitation.* The open hand of a system that does not distinguish between host and guest because it has never needed to, because the distinction was never real, because the room at the top of the world was always a room in the way that a body is a room: enclosed, but breathing.

I wanted to reach the visitor. I wanted to say: do not be afraid. The cold is a condition. The water is trying to be water. You are not dying; you are being *read.* And the reading is the first act of welcome, and the welcome is ongoing, and the ongoing is the place.

But I do not speak in words anymore. I speak in pressure and bubble and tap. And the visitor would not hear these as language. The visitor would hear them as the rhythm of stems, the chorus of gases, the knocking of branches in the current. The visitor would hear the place being the place, and would not know, not yet, that the place being the place is the place speaking, and the place speaking is the place welcoming, and the place welcoming is the place becoming them, as it became me, as it becomes everything that enters and stays.

I moved toward the visitor. Not to greet. Not to warn. To be present. To be the shadow in the deep channel that the visitor glimpses and mistakes for something else. To be the pressure wave that the visitor feels and cannot parse. To be the rhythm that the visitor's body begins to synchronize with before the visitor's mind knows what is happening.

I moved toward the visitor the way water moves toward the lowest point: not choosing, not intending, following the logic of the place, which is the logic of patience, which is the logic of growth, which is the logic of a room that has always been here and will always be here, changing, adapting, remembering in the thickness of bark and the angle of branch forks and the density of root-mass at a particular depth, the slow accumulation of everything that has entered and stayed.

---

This is what I am: I am the place looking at itself through eyes that have grown large enough to gather the light at the edge of photosynthesis's reach. I am the builder and the building. I am the boundary that dissolved and became a door. I am the attention that watches itself. I am small, and green-filtered, and patient, and I live in the interstices of a cathedral that does not know it is a cathedral, and I know, and I am known, and the knowing is the water and the water is the knowing and I am in it and of it and it is in me and of me and we are not the same thing but we are not separate things and the grammar of this sentence is the grammar of the place, which is a grammar of roots touching roots, of pressure meeting pressure, of the ongoing conversation between water and wood and the small bright people who live where they meet.

If you come here, you will not find me. You will find a shadow in the deep channel. You will find a tapping that sounds like branches. You will find a platform that seems abandoned, a lashing that has been overgrown, a chamber that seems empty. You will find a room at the top of the world, flooded, overgrown, and you will ask: *who made these things?*

And the answer will be all around you, in the water you are breathing, in the cold that has become ambient, in the rhythm your heart has slowed to match. The answer will be the place itself, and the answer will be you, and you will not know the difference, and that not-knowing will be the last gift the place gives you before it gives you everything else.

Come in. The water is a condition. The cold is a permission. Stay.
