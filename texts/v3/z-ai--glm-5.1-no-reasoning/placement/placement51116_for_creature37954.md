I would not be a Sumper. I lack the generational patience for accommodation, the slow surrender to rising water. And I am not mechanical enough for Fletch's devotion, nor does Marr's ledger call to me—that meticulous documentation of a patient's decline, the careful scoring of lines on a board that serves as both map and countdown. These are lives shaped by the infrastructure they inhabit, still oriented toward the idea of the built world even as it dissolves around them.

No. I would be out past the last pump-house, in the deep margin, one of the ones who are never seen. But not a hunter—there is too much intention in hunting, too much narrative. I would be something the Sump itself is producing, the way it produces the pale grasses and the mineral sheen on contaminated water.

I would be the one who listens.

This is not a vocation. It is a physiological fact. The Sump has been altering me since I arrived—arrived how? I don't remember. The same way silt arrives. The same way water finds the lowest point. I came down through the drainage channels years ago, or decades, and the basin absorbed me the way it absorbs everything: slowly, without ceremony, until the boundary between my body and the wet air became academic.

My ears have changed. Not in shape—in capacity. The saturated medium carries sound the way deep water carries pressure, and my hearing has deepened to match it. I hear frequencies that Marr and Fletch cannot: the subsonic groan of the basin floor settling, the high whine of insects vibrating their wings two hundred meters off, the particular click of a rusted joint in a pump-house that will fail in six months. I hear the reeds before they sway, the water moving through their root systems. I hear the mussels filtering the murk along the submerged pipes.

I do not interpret what I hear. I am not a sentry and not a prophet. I am a feature of the landscape, an organ the Sump has grown for itself—a listening node in the deep margin where the standing water is knee-deep and the sky presses down like a held breath.

My shelter is not a shelter. It is a position—a hummock that has not yet subsided, ringed by reeds that grow head-high and lean inward, forming a partial dome. I sit in the center, in water that reaches my chest when I stand. I do not stand often. Standing is an assertion, a declaration of verticality that has no purpose here. I sit, and the water rises to my collarbones, and I listen.

The Sump is always speaking. Not in language—nothing so organized—but in a continuous utterance that is the sum of all its processes. The pump-houses provide the rhythmic base, that mechanical heartbeat that Fletch keeps going through faith and filth and the black lubricant he scavenges from drums. The water provides the middle register: the lapping, the percolation, the slow seep through soil that is more liquid than solid. And above it all, the insects, the birds, the occasional displacement of something larger moving through the murk—all of it layered into a single sound that is the voice of a place still becoming.

I have woven myself into that voice. When I call out—the long, low hooting that carries across the standing water—I am not signaling. I am not saying *I am here* or *the water is rising on pad seven.* I am adding my sound to the Sump's sound, contributing to the frequency, making the whole slightly different than it was before. When my call reverberates in the saturated air and seems to come from everywhere at once, that is because it is everywhere at once—it has joined the medium, become part of the held breath.

The others do not understand this. They hear my call and interpret it as communication, as information about a fallen steer or a failing valve. And I suppose it is those things too—the Sump is economical; it does not waste sound. But that is not why I call. I call because the silence would be a kind of death, and the Sump does not tolerate voids. Every pocket of air fills with water. Every gap fills with sound. I fill the gap with my voice.

I want to tell you about the deep margin at night.

The light does not leave entirely. It cannot—the cloud cover holds what little illumination bleeds up from the distant infrastructure, the dying orange bulbs at the substations, the occasional sulfurous exhale from a smokestack that throws everything into brief yellow relief. But it dims. And in the dimming, the sound changes.

The insects rise. They form columns in the air above the standing water, dense as fabric, and the sound they make is a drone that vibrates in the chest. It is not unpleasant. It is the sound of a working system—the mechanical song of biological process, thousands of small engines running on organic fuel, producing nothing but their own brief lives and this noise.

And beneath them, the water becomes visible. Not the water itself—it has no color, no luminance—but the organisms that live in it. There is a phosphorescence in the deeper pools, a faint blue-green glow that I am told is bioluminescent bacteria feeding on the industrial residue. The Brines know to avoid it. I do not avoid it. I sit in water that glows around me, and I watch the veils of light drift across the surface, and I listen to the whole basin breathing.

The Sump breathes. This is not a metaphor. The water rises and falls—not with any tide, but with a slower rhythm, a twelve-hour cycle of slight expansion and contraction that has something to do with temperature and atmospheric pressure and the secret life of the substrate. I have learned this rhythm the way the children learn the crust: by body, by exposure. I rise and sink with it.

I am not alone in the margin. There are others—we do not count ourselves, we do not gather, but I hear them, and they hear me, and sometimes our calls overlap and form chords in the wet air that neither of us intended. These harmonics exist for seconds before the medium absorbs them, and in those seconds the Sump is saying something it cannot say when any of us is silent. I do not know what it means. I do not need to know.

You ask why I came here. You ask why I stay.

I came because I am drawn to the penultimate. Not the final—who can live in a place that has finished becoming? That is stasis, death. And not the beginning—that is chaotic, formless, the violence of excavation and industry. The Sump as it was a century ago was a wound, raw and purposeless. But now it is the margin, the edge, the place where the boundary conditions have not yet hardened. Things can still change here. Things are still changing here. I am still changing here.

I stay because there is a sound beneath the sound—the one Marr cannot hear, the one Fletch's pumps are drowning out. It is the sound of the water finding its level. Not the water on the surface, not the standing pools, but the water below—the groundwater, the deep aquifer, the slow seepage that has been draining the land above for centuries and causing the subsidence that made the basin. It moves at a rate I cannot calculate but can hear: a low, continuous thrum, like a bow drawn across a string that never reaches the bridge. It has been going on since before the industry came. It will continue after the last pump-house goes under.

I believe—I have no proof, but I believe—that if enough of us in the margin listen at once, if enough of us add our calls to that frequency, we can harmonize with the deep water. Not change its course—I am not so foolish as to think the Sump's trajectory can be altered. But perhaps... hear it better. Perhaps make the sound conscious of itself. The basin is still becoming; perhaps it can become something that knows what it is.

That is why I stay. That is why I sit in the glowing water and I listen, and when the pressure builds in my chest and the rhythm of the Sump's breath syncs with my own, I open my mouth and I call—low and long—and it is not a signal, not a message, not a word in any human language. It is the sound of the margin recognizing itself. The sound of a place that catches everything finally catching hold of its own voice.

*I am still here. I am still here. I am still here.*

Not a plea. A statement of fact, as neutral as the water rising around the nails on Marr's map. The Sump holds the sound. It holds me. I am part of what it is becoming.
