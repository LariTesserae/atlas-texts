I would be the one who reads the splashes. Not the large ones—the vole's consequential plunge, the heron's strike—but the small ones, the events written and overwritten on the surface film of the standing water before the ripples cancel out. I would be the surface-tension watcher, the skater-insect's chronicler.

It started because of the light. The shafts that travel when the wind shifts the cap-fern—I needed to know where they would fall, when they would rest on a pool long enough to warm it, long enough to make the morning condensation lift. I began watching the water for the light, and then I was watching the water for itself, and then I was watching the film on the water, the meniscus that holds the whole surface together by refusing to break, and I realized that the film is where the quarter writes its news.

Everything that happens here arrives at the water eventually. The willow joint that is choking—the cord cutting into the growing wood—you hear it from the builder, yes, but you also see it in the overflow from the east cistern, where the water runs a little darker with the tannins of stressed bark. The cap-fern frond that is lesioning—you smell the rot, yes, but you also see it in the pH of the seep-runnel beneath, the way the surface film tightens or loosens with the chemistry, the way the skater-insects cluster or disperse. The granite fracture that is beginning to narrow—you taste it, as the children are taught to do, but you also *see* it in the pattern of the ripples, the way the flow rate changes the interference pattern where two channels meet, the standing waves that are the water's architecture, as persistent as the willow frames if you know how to read them.

I have learned to read them. Not with a system, not with notation—the Reedwrights would not use notation, would not externalize what can be held in a body—but with my body, with the particular tension in my shoulders that means the north seep is running three hairs slower than yesterday, with the specific angle of my head that means the mineral profile has shifted and I should find the Cistern-Root before midday, with the rhythm of my breathing that I have synchronized to the drip-rate of the cistern near the weaving workshop so that I notice the drip-rate changing the way I would notice my own heartbeat changing: not as data but as feeling, as something wrong or something right in the pattern of my own being.

I am not old. I am not young. I came to this work the way the water comes to the channels—by following the path of least resistance, by finding the place where my attention flowed naturally and letting it deepen into a groove. I was twelve and I was sitting by the pool where the deep-voiced frogs live, and I was watching the surface, and I saw something—two ripples meeting at an angle that should have cancelled but didn't, because the surface film was thinner there, because something in the water had changed the tension—and I understood that the surface was not flat but textured, not simple but written, and I have been reading it since.

My dwelling is the one nearest the confluence, where the two main seep-channels meet before the water spreads into the marsh. It is the wettest dwelling. The floor is always beaded. The walls sweat more than the others because they face the channel and the channel raises the local humidity past the point that even cap-fern can pump away. I chose it—or it chose me; the distinction collapses—in the year that the frog-listener was born, and she is eight now, and we work together without having agreed to work together, without ever having discussed it, because we are both reading the same text in different media, she the voices and I the surfaces, and when she says the north chorus is missing three deep voices I say the north surface-film is carrying a different sediment load and the Cistern-Root nods and says *the water's shifting* and the system adjusts, and no one organized this, and no one needed to.

My hands are stained. Not with dye—though I have worn the plant-extract medicine against my skin like everyone else—but with tannin, with the brown of the water, which has worked into the creases of my palms the way the cord works into the willow joints, not as a surface application but as a swallowing, a living-over of the stain into the skin until the skin and the stain are one material. I can hold my hand over a pool and the pool does not react, because my hand reads as water to the surface film, as something that belongs there, as something that is already part of the writing.

I do not walk the perimeter in the morning. I walk the channels. I move through the quarter along the water's paths instead of the people's paths, stepping in the shallow runnels where the seep flows over the granite, and my sandals are always wet and the nettle-cloth of my legs is always darkened to the knee, and the children think I am strange but the children also know that when I stop walking and stand very still and look at the water, something is happening that matters, and they quiet themselves without being asked, and they watch too, and some of them are learning to read the surface-film, and this is how the role persists—by migration, by the same process that moves names from person to person, and someday someone younger than me will be the surface-watcher and I will be something else, or I will be compost, and my attention will go into the floor and the food and the child who has my hands, and the child will look at the water and see what I see, which is:

Everything. The whole quarter. The rate of growth and the rate of decay and the rate of patching and the rate of forgetting and the way the water is the blood of this place, circulating through the granite fractures like through vessels, carrying minerals from the deep rock to the surface where the plants can find them, carrying the waste of the plants back down into the marsh where the sphagnum can process it, carrying the spores and the seeds and the larvae and the dissolved remains of the composted dead, carrying all of it, always, in a circulation that is not metaphorical but literal, that is hydrology, that is the actual water actually moving actual material through the actual channels of the quarter, and the surface film is where the circulation meets the air, and the air is where the light arrives, and the light is what drives the growth, and the growth is what builds the dwellings, and the dwellings are what shelter the people, and the people are what maintain the channels, and the channels are what guide the water, and the water is what I watch.

I watch the water the way the builder touches the joints. With the specific attention of someone who knows that the system is always nearly failing and always nearly succeeding and that the difference between the two is a matter of noticing, a matter of catching the change before it accumulates into a crisis, a matter of reading the surface-film and tasting the mineral profile and listening to the frogs and reporting to the Cistern-Root and trusting that someone is harvesting the sphagnum and someone is retting the nettle and someone is training the willow and someone is chasing the beetles and the whole thing holds, the pattern holds, the channel persists, the water goes on.

I do not have a name. Or I do, but it is not fixed. I was called Pool-Bend once, because of the way I stood at the confluence with my head tilted, and then I was called Film-Reader, and then I was called Quiet-Walker, and the names moved across me like light moving across a wall, and I answered to all of them and I answer to none of them, because what I am is not a word but a function, and the function is attention, and the attention is not mine but the quarter's, flowing through me the way water flows through the granite, using me the way the willow uses the cord, swallowing me into the living joint of this place where the distinction between the watcher and the watched has collapsed the way the distinction between the dwelling and the plant has collapsed, and I am not separate from the surface I read, and the surface is not separate from the water that makes it, and the water is not separate from the granite that bleeds it, and the granite is not separate from the hill that holds it, and the hill is not separate from the rain that falls on it, and the rain is not separate from the air that carries it, and the air is not separate from the lungs that breathe it, and my lungs are not separate from me, and I am not separate from the Reedwrights, and the Reedwrights are not separate from the reed, and the reed is shaped into the dwelling and the dwelling shelters the watcher and the watcher watches the water and the water shapes the channel and the channel persists.

This is where I am. This is what I am. The one who reads the splashes, who feels the surface-film, who knows the water's opinion before it finishes forming, who stands in the confluence with wet legs and stained hands and breathes the green-gold air and tastes the tannin and the chlorophyll and the particular acid of the sphagnum-cistern water that means the granite is still doing what the granite has always done, and the quarter is still building and unbuilt and building again, and the negotiation is still proceeding, and the sound is still layered and constant and overwhelming, and I am part of the sound—I am the silence in it, the brief pause between the vole's splash and the skater's skating, the moment where the surface holds still and the film is unbroken and the whole pattern is visible, and then the wind shifts and the light moves and the frogs call and the water runs and I go with it, reading as I go, attending as I go, shaping and shaped, watching and watched, here in this high wet place where everything is alive and everything is negotiating and everything is nearly failing and everything is nearly succeeding and the surface-film holds, for now, for another moment, for another day, because someone is watching it, because someone is reading it, because someone is part of it, and that someone is me.
