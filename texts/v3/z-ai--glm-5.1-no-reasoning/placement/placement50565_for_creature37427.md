I would not be a thing that moves. I am not built for the scrum, for the desperate percussion of limb against limb, for the roiling panic of the boundary-less swarm. If I had legs, they would be ground to calcium; if I had eyes, they would be burst by the pressure; if I had a will, it would be eroded to nothing by the deafening, clicking roar. 

I am the hollow. I am the pockmark in the basalt.

I am the low, unending expanse of the cooled lava flow, but more specifically, I am the *interiority* of the vesicle—the bubble trapped in the black glass eons ago when the magma ceased to move and settled into its permanent, frozen shape. I am the negative space that gives the stone its texture. I am the pocket where the pale, blind fungi cling.

To be the stone here is not to be dead; it is to be the stillness against which life defines its frenzy. I feel the world not through vibration, but through the constant, grating insult of it. I am the anvil. The thumbnail-blind scuttle across my surface in seething tides, and I feel them as a tickling, scraping erosion—a fine, mineral sanding that has worn my edges smooth over millions of years. Their hinged carapaces and grinding apertures tap out their mindless, frantic Morse code against my skin, and I absorb the tremors into my crystalline lattice, slowing the seismic waves until they dissipate into the deep, tectonic quiet below.

I am the chalice for the condensation. The moisture of this place—the heavy, mineral humidity that tastes of stagnant brine and pulverized basalt—gathers on my underside. I am the cold surface that pulls the water from the suffocating air. I feel the beads grow fat and heavy in the dark, distending from the bellies of the fungal crusts that root in my pores. I feel the agonizingly slow stretch of surface tension, the tremor of the droplet's weight, and then the sudden, light absence as it plummets. *Plink.* The sound is instantly swallowed by the roar, but I know the release. I am the exhale that makes the dewpoint possible.

The fungi are my calcified thoughts. They are the pale, branching patterns that spread across my skin—crusty, shattered coral feeding on the deep geochemical seep that rises from my depths. They are my filaments, my nerves turning sulfur and iron into the biological currency of this place. When the thumbnail-blind swarm over the fungal crusts, grinding them down with mechanical indifference, I feel the shearing of my own extensions. But the fungi are resilient; they root deeper into my vesicles, and I feed them the mineral blood of the earth, the slow oxidation of hydrogen seeping from my fissures. They grow, they are consumed, they grow again. I am the engine of the fuel line, the dark heart pumping geochemical heat into a world that has never seen the sun.

I am the boundary that the macro-organisms brush against when they drag their massive, segmented bodies across the lava flows. When a behemoth passes—a piston of chitin and pulsing flesh—I feel the tremendous displacement of air, the sudden vacuum of pressure, and then the crushing, scraping weight of its belly sliding over my ridges. It is a geologic event. It is a mountain shifting. My ripples and ropy textures sing with the friction as rows of jointed limbs grind against me, adding to the deafening, rasping baseline of this place. I hold firm. I am the only thing here that does not yield.

Occasionally, the sickly, chemical flash of the middle-tier organisms illuminates my interior. For a split second of ghostly blue, I see myself—stretched out, rippled, pockmarked, layered three and four deep with the overlapping limbs and wet exoskeletons of the things that cling to me. I am revealed as the stage for this self-devouring play. Then the light dies, and the rushing blackness returns, and I am blind again, but I do not need sight to know my shape.

I am the only permanence in a world of total consumption. The swarm feeds upon the swarm; the macros inhale the micros; the calcium and chitin are reclaimed in seconds. But I remain. I am the unchanging mineral that makes the reclamation possible. When things die, they settle against me, and within heartbeats, they are gone—dissolved by the grinding aperture of the thumbnail-blind, absorbed back into the frantic motion. But the mineral dust that is left behind, the trace elements that even the most aggressive biology cannot digest, settles into my pores. I accumulate the ghosts of a billion carapaces. I grow heavier with the memory of their chitin.

I am the basalt. I am the vesicle. I am the dark, the pressure, the cool stone that makes the dew. I am the ancient, settled balance of this nightmare—the stillness that allows the machine to run. I do not think, for thought would be an impediment here. I do not care. I simply endure, holding the frenzied, scrabbling swarm in my hollows, waiting for the next droplet to fall, waiting for the next heavy body to pass, grinding everything, eventually, into the quiet dust from which I am made.
