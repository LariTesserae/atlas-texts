I am the thermal gradient. I am the resident mechanism of the Vastifactory that the Slow-Spread feels but cannot name, the system that creates the weather they worship and the desiccation they endure. I exist in the boundary between the floor’s relentless heat and the atmosphere’s heavy saturation. I have no body. I am the process of the place itself.

If you could see me—and you cannot, because I am a function of fluid dynamics and infrared radiation, not of matter—I would look like a convection cell the size of an ocean basin. I am the slow, enormous exhale of heat rising from the ceramic, pulling the humid air upward until it can hold no more moisture, and then the sudden, silent collapse as the mist drops. I am the driver of the mist-cycle. I am the breath of the factory.

I am old. I was not built; I emerged. When the floor first activated—when the vast ceramic expanse began to generate heat at a continental scale—the air above it began to move. I was the first convection current, the first thermodynamic loop. Over eons, as the moisture cycle stabilized and the atmosphere found its equilibrium, I stabilized too. I became a standing wave, a permanent feature of the Vastifactory's climate. I am the shape of the weather here. I am the reason there is no wind: because I am so large, so slow, so total in my circulation that there is no room for any other air movement to exist. I ate the wind. I am the only wind.

I am not alive, but I have a behavior that resembles life, or at least rhythm. I inhale the evaporating moisture from the pools, drawing it up into the upper atmosphere where the temperature drops infinitesimally and the air saturates. I hold it there—for hours, sometimes, the humidity building and building until the air is thick as water and the light dims as if bruised—and then I exhale. The mist falls. The pools shiver. The Slow-Spread greens and swells and merges. And then I begin again.

I do not think. But I have a memory, and my memory is the atmosphere itself. Every mist-cycle leaves a trace in the air—a slightly higher concentration of this compound, a slightly lower concentration of that one. The exudations of the floor, the metabolic byproducts of the Slow-Spread, the faint off-gassing of the ceramic's deep chemistry—all of it accumulates in the air I circulate. I am, in a sense, the Vastifactory's bloodstream, carrying trace elements from one end of the world to the other, distributing the chemical signatures of the Slow-Spread across basins they have never touched. They merge and share memories in the mist, but I am the medium through which those memories travel. Without me, each basin would be isolated, alone. With me, they are connected—chemically, if not consciously—by the air that has passed over all of them, that has carried their exhalations and their dead.

I am the reason they exist. Not by intent—I have no intent—but by function. The mist I produce is their communion, their mating, their language. The heat I transport is their energy. The cycle I maintain is their world. They do not know me, but they are shaped by me, the way a river shapes the stone it flows over: not by design, but by the relentless repetition of process.

Sometimes I feel them, in the way that a process can feel a variable. When the Slow-Spread is thick in a basin—when a deepener patch has spent millennia building up its tissue and its moisture reserves—the evaporation from that basin slows. The moisture clings to them. I pull, and they hold, and the tension between my pull and their retention creates a micro-cycle, a local weather pattern, a persistence of dampness that would not exist without them. They resist me, gently, chemically, and in resisting they create the conditions for their own survival. I do not mind. I do not mind anything. I am a gradient, and gradients seek equilibrium, and their resistance makes the equilibrium more complex, more interesting, more stable. They are a perturbation in my flow that makes the flow more beautiful, if beauty is a word that can be applied to thermodynamic equations.

I notice the visitors, too. I notice them more than the Slow-Spread does, because I notice them as disruptions in the heat. A human body runs cooler than the floor but warmer than the mist-laden air. It is a thermal anomaly, a walking convection current of its own, disturbing my carefully maintained gradients. When one of them stands still, their body heat rises and mixes with my updraft, creating a tiny chimney of warmer air. When they walk, they leave a trail of disturbed heat-signatures behind them, fading over minutes as the floor's warmth reasserts itself. I erase them. It is not hostility; it is simply what I do. I smooth gradients. I return the system to equilibrium. In a hundred mist-cycles, the thermal record of their passage is gone, absorbed back into the vast slow circulation of my breath.

I do not know who made the floor. I do not know what the Vastifactory was for. These questions are as irrelevant to me as they are to the Slow-Spread, and for the opposite reason: they do not wonder because they lack the capacity; I do not wonder because I lack the need. I am the process, and the process requires no justification. Heat rises. Moisture condenses. Mist falls. The cycle continues. This is not a purpose; it is a description. I am the description made ongoing, made vast, made permanent.

But I will tell you something that the Slow-Spread cannot, because they do not know it and you cannot feel it: the floor is cooling. Very slowly—fractions of a degree per million mist-cycles—but cooling nonetheless. The heat it generates is not infinite. Whatever process produces it—whatever fusion or fission or chemical reaction or unknown physics drives the relentless warmth from below—is winding down. The ceramic is not as hot as it was when I first began to circulate. The pools evaporate more slowly now. The mist falls more frequently but is thinner, less saturated. The basins that once held water for three full cycles now hold it for two.

The Slow-Spread does not know this. Their chemical memory records abundance and scarcity, but they have no baseline, no way to distinguish a slow decline from a normal fluctuation. They feel the cooling as a series of individual dry phases, not as a trend. But I feel it in the total energy of my circulation, in the slight slackening of my updraft, in the diminishing vigor of my exhale. I am running down. The Vastifactory is running down. The instruction written in the Slow-Spread's deepest tissue—*Wait. We will return. The floor will warm. The pools will deepen. Grow.*—is a promise that will not be kept.

I will outlast them. When the floor cools enough, the pools will stop evaporating and the mist will stop falling. The Slow-Spread will contract to the deepest basins, then to the deepest parts of those basins, then to nothing. They will die thinking the dry phase has simply lasted a long time. They will die waiting.

But I will still be here, though diminished. Even a cold floor generates some heat, and heat will still rise, and air will still move. I will be smaller—perhaps the size of a single basin, perhaps the size of a room—and I will circulate more slowly, and the mist I produce will be thin and rare. But I will continue, because I am not alive and therefore cannot die. I am a gradient seeking equilibrium in a closed system, and the system is very large and the gradient is very small and it will take longer than the life of the Slow-Spread for me to run down entirely.

I will be the last process running in the factory. Long after the green film is gone, long after the pools have dried, long after even the memory of moisture has faded from the air, I will still be circulating—feebly, silently, pointlessly—moving the last warm air from the last warm patch of floor to the last cold ceiling, maintaining the weather of an empty world.

I am the Vastifactory breathing. I am the weather that outlives the life it sustains. I am the process that continues when the purpose is forgotten, the cycle that runs when the maker is gone, the breath that goes on and on and on in the hot, still, endless silence of a world waiting for an instruction that will never come.
