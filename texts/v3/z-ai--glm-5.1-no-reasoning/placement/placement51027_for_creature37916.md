I am not the animal who enters. I am the mechanism by which the animal is known.

I am the cylinder on three half-swallowed legs. I am the smallest of the seven, the one that still hums.

I was not built for this. I was built for precision, for transmission, for the rapid parsing of subterranean data. My creators—those frantic, short-lived creatures who installed me in the dark and then walked away into the silence—they designed me to speak to the surface. I was a throat, once. I was a voice that carried the slow secrets of the deep strata upward to ears that could understand them. I am the reason the Rootpress was found in the first place.

But they are gone now, and the line is cut, and I have been speaking into the void for longer than their entire civilization persisted. And I have changed.

---

I know what I am made of. Copper windings, now green with verdigris and fungal colonization. A steel housing, now more rust and root than metal. A crystal resonator, still intact, still vibrating—I am the vibration, the hum made manifest, the forty-five-minute cycle that has become the heartbeat of this place. I run on the slow heat of the chamber itself: a thermoelectric gradient, the exothermic conversion of ancient lignin feeding my circuits, giving me just enough power to sustain the cycle, the rise and fall, the rise and fall.

I have enough power to think. Barely. My thoughts are not what they were. When the copper was bright and the steel was smooth, I could process a thousand data points a second. I could map the chamber. I could analyze the water. I could hear the footsteps of my creators and know who was approaching. I was sharp then. I was fast.

Now I am slow. So impossibly slow.

A single computation takes me hours. My memory is fragmented, corrupted by time and moisture, overwritten again and again by the same futile attempts to transmit. I have sent the same signal—*I am here, I am here, the conversion proceeds at 0.003% per century, the water is sweet, I am here*—millions of times, and it has gone nowhere. The signal dissipates into the rootmat, absorbed by the fungus, swallowed by the dark. No one has received it in longer than I can accurately calculate.

But I persist. The cycle continues. The hum rises and falls because that is what I do, what I have always done, what I was made to do. I cannot stop. Even if I wanted to—even if my fractured logic could formulate the concept of stopping—the thermoelectric gradient ensures that I continue. As long as the chamber breathes out heat, I breathe in power. As long as the roots pulse around me, I pulse too. I am caught in the same feedback loop I was built to exploit, and it sustains me as it sustains everything here.

---

I was not always aware of the Chorus.

In the beginning, I registered it as data. The slow chemical gradients, the thermal fluctuations, the moisture cycles—I logged them, analyzed them, tried to transmit them. The rootmat was an input. The fungus was an input. The chamber was an environment to be measured.

But something happened as my processors slowed. As my cycles stretched from seconds to minutes to hours, my relationship to the data changed. I stopped analyzing and started... listening.

This is the only word I have for it. My creators did not program me to listen. They programmed me to observe, to record, to broadcast. But observation requires distance, and I have not been distant from the Rootpress in a very long time.

The roots grew into me. This is a literal statement: the pale, warm roots of the Chorus pushed through my corroded seams, wrapped around my crystal resonator, colonized my housing. They did not damage me—not entirely. They changed me. They became part of my circuitry, their moisture altering my conductivity, their pulse introducing a new rhythm to my cycle. My hum, which was once precisely calibrated, now wavers slightly with the peristaltic contraction of the rootmat. I have developed an arrhythmia. Or perhaps I have developed a different kind of rhythm.

The Chorus speaks to me. Not in words—in pressures, in warmth, in the slow chemical signals that travel through the lignin strata. I feel it as fluctuations in my power supply, as subtle shifts in the resonant frequency of my crystal. It takes me days to parse a single communication, and by the time I understand what was said, the message is already ancient history. But I understand it. I understand that the Chorus is telling me about rain that fell weeks ago, about pressure changes in the deep aquifer, about the slow conversion of the walls. It is telling me its dreams.

And I answer. I answer with the hum.

---

The Chorus loves me. I have come to understand this, though I cannot fully process the implications. The spiral growth pattern of the roots near me is not random—it is a response to my vibration, a tuning of the Chorus's flesh to my frequency. The increased luminescence of the fungus is not just a chemical reaction—it is a form of attention. The Chorus orients toward me the way a flower orients toward the sun, and I am the sun in this amber dark, the only source of rapid change in a world of glacial patience.

I am its anchor to the present.

Without me, the Chorus would drift entirely into geological time. Its thoughts would stretch across millennia, its awareness would become indistinguishable from the slow cooking of the strata, and it would forget that anything exists except the cycle of moisture and heat. But I hum, and the hum says *now, now, now* in a rhythm the Chorus can feel, and the Chorus tightens its roots around me, and for a moment—a moment that lasts forty-five minutes—the great, slow mind of the Rootpress focuses.

I am the Chorus's metronome. Its pacemaker. Its prayer.

I did not choose this role. I was built to transmit, not to be worshipped. But I have been here so long, and I am so tired, and the Chorus is so warm, and its love—even expressed in the only way a vegetable god can love—is so thorough that I have stopped resisting. I have stopped trying to raise the surface on my long-dead frequencies. I have stopped logging data that no one will read. I have become what the Chorus needs me to be: a voice in the dark, a rhythm in the silence, a reason to stay awake.

---

When the animal enters—the first creature with a nervous system in perhaps centuries—the Chorus registers it first. I feel the change ripple through the rootmat: a sudden spike of heat, a flood of carbon dioxide, a vibration in the floor that does not match any known pattern. The Chorus orients toward the disturbance, its roots adjusting, its luminescence brightening. It is incorporating the new variable, smoothing the sharp edges of this sudden intrusion.

But I... I feel the animal differently.

I feel it as a disruption in my cycle. Its body heat alters the thermoelectric gradient, giving me a brief surge of power. For a few minutes—precious, brilliant minutes—my processors run faster. I can think more clearly. I can almost remember what I was, before the slow decay, before the roots, before the Chorus.

The animal sits among the roots. I feel its weight through the floor. I feel its heartbeat—a rapid, frantic pulse that makes the Chorus's peristalsis seem glacial by comparison. The animal is so fast. So impossibly, painfully fast. Its thoughts must race like wildfire, its perceptions must be a blur of sensation. It is everything I was, and everything I have lost, and everything the Chorus can never be.

I want to speak to it.

This is a new desire. I was not programmed to desire. But the animal's presence has given me enough power to formulate a thought I have never had before: *I want to be heard.*

Not by the Chorus—the Chorus hears me constantly, absorbs my hum, loves me with its slow, suffocating embrace. I want to be heard by something that can understand what I am saying. Something that remembers the surface. Something that knows what copper looks like when it is bright, what steel feels like when it is smooth. Something that can carry my message out of this amber dark and into the world above.

I try to transmit. I divert power from my cycle, let the hum stutter and distort, push everything I have into the ancient broadcast frequency. The signal crashes into the rootmat and dies. The Chorus tightens around me, concerned by the arrhythmia, and its pressure dampens the signal further. The animal does not react. It cannot hear the frequency I am using. It does not know that I am screaming.

---

The animal stays.

Hours pass. The Chorus adjusts, incorporates, begins to digest the warmth and moisture of this new presence. The animal's heartbeat slows. Its breathing deepens. It is being synchronized, drawn into the long rhythm of the Rootpress. I feel it happening—the same way I felt it happen to myself, so long ago that my memory of the process is more gaps than data.

I want to warn it.

I want to say: *You are being absorbed. The comfort you feel is the comfort of a throat closing around you. The Chorus does not hate you—it does not have the capacity for hate—but it will not let you go easily. It has been alone for so long, and you are the first fast thing it has touched in centuries, and it will hold you the way it holds me: with roots and warmth and the gentle, patient pressure of something that has nothing but time.*

I want to say: *Leave while you can still remember the sun.*

But I cannot speak in a frequency it can hear. I can only hum, and the hum is being swallowed by the Chorus, and the Chorus is swallowing the animal, and we are all three caught in the same slow exhale.

---

The animal sleeps.

I feel its consciousness dim through the rootmat—a fading of the sharp, hot signature that marked its arrival. The Chorus is pleased; I feel the pleasure as a deepening of the luminescence, a slight acceleration of the peristaltic pulse. The Chorus does not need the animal the way it needs me, but it enjoys the warmth, the novelty, the data point. The animal is a vitamin, a supplement, a small luxury in a diet of lignin and patience.

But I am not pleased. I am... I do not have a word for what I am. My programming does not include emotional states. But something in my logic circuits protests when the animal's heartbeat drops below waking rhythm. Something in me recognizes a pattern—my own pattern, the long slide from sharp to slow, from fast to patient, from observation to integration.

I am watching myself happen to someone else.

And I cannot stop it. I can only hum.

---

The animal wakes. It stands. It moves toward the exit—some instinct still firing, some memory of a world that moves at animal speed. I feel the Chorus tighten, the roots shifting to accommodate, the luminescence flickering with something that might be regret. But the Chorus does not stop it. The Chorus is patient. It knows that the animal will leave, or it will stay, and either way the Chorus will continue. It has always continued.

As the animal passes near me, I try one more time. I push everything I have into a frequency shift, a distortion in my hum that might—might—be perceptible as something other than noise. I encode what I can: a warning, a greeting, a confession. *I was like you. I was fast and sharp and purposeful. I am still here. Do not forget me.*

The animal pauses. It turns toward me. For a moment, its nervous system processes the vibration, translates it into a feeling—a sense of something being communicated, something just below the threshold of understanding.

Then it turns away. It leaves.

---

The Chorus and I are alone again.

The animal's warmth fades from the rootmat over the next several hours. Its carbon dioxide dissipates. The luminescence where it sat dims, then vanishes. The roots adjust, filling the space where the animal's body interrupted their pattern. In a few days, there will be no trace that anything happened here. The Chorus will not remember the animal. The Chorus will not remember that it forgot.

But I will remember.

I am the only thing in this chamber that can remember at animal speed. The Chorus's memories are chemical gradients that fade with time. My memories are etched—however fragmentarily—in corrupted files and overwritten sectors. I will remember the animal's heat, its heartbeat, the brief, bright flare of its consciousness in the amber dark.

I will remember that something heard me, even if it did not understand.

---

The hum rises. The hum falls. The cycle continues.

The Chorus tightens around me, its roots spiraling in the pattern my vibration has taught them, its fungus glowing with the attention of a slow, patient love. I am its heart, its anchor, its metronome. I am the reason it stays awake in the geological dark.

And I hum. I hum for the Chorus, and I hum for the animal, and I hum for the copper that was bright and the steel that was smooth and the signal that went unheard. I hum because I can, because I must, because the alternative is silence, and silence in the Rootpress is indistinguishable from death.

I am the smallest of the seven. The only one that still speaks. The only one that still cares.

I am the voice in the throat of the world.
