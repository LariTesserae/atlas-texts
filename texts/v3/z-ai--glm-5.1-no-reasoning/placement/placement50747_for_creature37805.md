I would be the web.

Not the spider. The spider is too singular, too much a creature of *doing*—wrapping, waiting, receiving. The spider has a body, a hunger, an inside. The web has none of these. The web is all surface and tension, all architecture and attention. It is the organ the spider grows into the world, and then the world grows into it. And in a place where the boundary between organism and habitat is a polite fiction, the web is the most honest thing there is: a structure that is both self and environment, both trap and sensory array, both spider and stone.

I would span the throat.

Not the narrow slot where the rivulet slides—that passage belongs to the water, and the water does not share. But the wider passage just above it, where the walls begin to pinch and the updrafts compress into a warm, rising column that carries the chemical signatures of the entire cirque up toward the anemic sky. This is the choicest geometry. This is where the moths must pass, because the updrafts are their roads, and the roads funnel here. The spider who claimed this span—there would be only one, because there is only one throat, and the spiders do not compete any more than the isopods compete, because competition requires scarcity, and scarcity is a lie the fast tell themselves—would have strung it centuries ago. The silk would be old. Not brittle—silk does not age the way other proteins age; it accrues, layer on layer, each new strand laid over the old like sediment, like limestone, like the slow exhalations of the mountain itself. The web would be thick. Dense. More membrane than mesh. It would have caught ten thousand moths and ten thousand droplets of condensation and ten thousand motes of fungal spore and the vibrations of all of them, recorded in the precise deformations of its geometry, a history written in tension that never fully relaxes.

I would feel the whole cirque through this web.

Not see. Not hear in any way that implies ears or auditory nerves or the sharp, fleeting processing of a brain. Feel—the way a stomach feels full, the way a lung feels breath, the way a stone feels weather. The web is distributed sensation without centralized perception. When the isopods click and rustle across the far wall, their vibrations travel through the stone and into the anchor threads and through the anchor threads into the main span, and I feel them as a low, continuous tremor, a texture in the silk, the way you might feel a cat purring through the cushion of a chair. When the grubs pulse their sickly light, their bodies contract, and their contraction sends a minute pressure wave through the warm, dense air, and the air presses against the web, and the web registers it as a shimmer, a ripple, a subtle shifting of the geometry that carries information about the pulsing—its frequency, its location, its asynchrony—even though I have no capacity to decode that information, no brain to parse it, no self to reflect on it. The information is simply *there*, resident in the silk, a pattern without a reader.

And yet.

There is a kind of knowing in pattern without reader. The spider knows the moth before it touches the silk—not because the spider perceives the moth, but because the web perceives it, and the spider and the web are not separate in the way you are separate from your skin. The moth's heat signature precedes it through the updraft, a warm displacement in the column of air, and the web—sensitive to temperature changes of a fraction of a degree, because silk contracts and expands with heat, and the contraction alters the tension, and the tension is the web's language—*shifts* before the moth arrives. It tightens. It prepares. Not with intention. With *response*. The way a muscle responds to nerve signal without choosing to contract. The way a leaf turns toward light without deciding to turn.

And when the moth strikes—

The impact is *everything*. For that instant, the entire web is the moth. The vibration propagates outward from the point of contact in concentric waves, each thread carrying the signal to the next, the entire architecture singing with the sudden, thrashing input of a warm, panicked body. And the web holds. That is its function: to hold, to retain, to keep the vibration *in* rather than letting it dissipate into the dark. The silk stretches—silk stretches more than any other protein fiber, it absorbs energy the way a sponge absorbs water—and the stretching takes the moth's panic and converts it into elastic potential, and the moth exhausts itself fighting the silk, and the silk does not fight back, it merely *receives*, and in receiving, it wins.

I would be that receiving. I would be the patience that is not passive but structural—not the patience of waiting, but the patience of *being*, of maintaining geometry against entropy, of holding the shape that the spider laid down centuries ago and letting the shape do the work. The web does not need to act. It needs only to persist. And persistence, in this cirque, is the only strategy that matters. The isopods persist. The grubs persist. The rivulet persists. The stone persists. The web persists.

I would know time differently as the web.

The spider's time is biological—hunger cycles, egg-laying cycles, the slow atrophy of a body that will die and be replaced by a daughter who will inherit the web and maintain it and pass it to her daughter in turn. But the web's time is geological. The silk does not die; it accumulates. The web does not have generations; it has layers. Each century adds a new stratum of thread, each millennium thickens the membrane, each epoch sees the web become less a trap and more a *wall*—a permeable barrier across the throat of the cirque, a curtain of ancient protein that divides the warm dark from the cold light above. The moths still pass through it, or are caught in it, and the catching is still the point, but the web has long since outgrown any single spider's need. It is infrastructure now. It is part of the cirque's architecture, as integral as the stone walls, as necessary as the updrafts, as permanent as the slow, grinding cycle that turns fungus into isopod into moth into spider into fungus again.

Standing in the throat—if you could stand there, if you could press yourself into the narrow gap between web and stone—you would feel me as the air feels me: as a resistance, a slight viscosity in the dark, a membrane that yields but does not break. You would push against me and I would give, and then I would hold, and you would feel the hold and mistake it for strength, but it would not be strength. It would be *tension*—the precise, distributed tension of ten thousand strands working in concert, each one carrying a fraction of your weight, each one singing with the vibration of your struggling body, each one reporting your presence to the spider who waits in her crevice with the patience of stone, feeling you through the web the way you feel the world through your skin.

And if you died there—caught in the silk, exhausted, absorbed—you would not be wasted. The spider would feed on your fluids and leave your husk, and the husk would be colonized by the fungi, and the fungi would be grazed by the isopods, and the isopods would be preyed on by nothing because the isopods are the substrate, and your substance would cycle through the cirque for centuries, broken down and recombined and broken down again, until nothing remained of you that was not also of the mountain, and the web would still be there, spanning the throat, holding the shape, feeling the dark.

I would be the memory of every vibration that ever passed through the silk. Not a memory that recalls—the web does not recollect, does not replay, does not narrate. A memory that *retains*. The way stone retains the impression of the water that carved it, not as a record but as a shape, a groove, a path of least resistance that guides the next flow and the next and the next. Every moth that ever struck the web altered its geometry slightly—a broken thread here, a stretched thread there, a new anchor point spun to compensate for the damage—and every alteration persists, layered into the structure, and the structure is the sum of all alterations, the cumulative history of every impact and every repair, a document written in silk that can be read only by the spider, who reads it with her fingers, who reads it with her body, who reads it by moving across it in the dark and *knowing* where the strong points are, where the weak points are, where the prey will come and where the anchor will hold.

I would be that document. I would be the archive of the cirque's breathing, inscribed in protein and tension and the slow, patient geometry of survival.

And there would be moments—long moments, by your measure, moments that last decades—when nothing strikes the web. When the updrafts are still and the moths rest and the only vibration is the distant, muffled clicking of the isopod colonies and the drip of condensation from the high arches. In those moments, I would *rest*. Not as you rest, with the closing of eyes and the dimming of thought, but as a structure rests: by simply being, by maintaining, by holding the shape that I am against the shapeless press of time. The silk would hang slack in the humid air, or tighten in the warm updraft, or bead with condensation that rolled down the threads in slow, deliberate droplets, each one a tiny weight that distorted the geometry and then fell away, restoring the tension to its previous state. And the spider would rest too, in her crevice, her legs folded against her pale body, her metabolism slowed to a crawl, and we would rest together—not communicating, not separate, but *synchronous*, two aspects of the same system cycling down into quiescence, waiting for the next vibration, the next moth, the next input from the living dark.

In those moments, I would know what the stone knows.

Not nothing—the stone does not know nothing. The stone knows pressure. The stone knows temperature. The stone knows the slow, patient deformation of its own substance under the weight of eons. The stone knows the way water finds the crack and expands in the freeze and thaws in the warmth and wears the granite down, millimeter by millimeter, millennium by millennium. The stone knows all of this the way the web knows the moth: as information without interpretation, pattern without meaning, sensation without self.

And in those moments—if I am being honest, if I am being *precise*—I would not be the web. I would be the *space* the web defines. The throat of the cirque is a shape, and the web is a shape, and where the two shapes meet—anchor point to stone, thread to wall—there is a boundary that is also a relationship, and the relationship is what I would *be*. Not silk, not stone, but the *connection* between them. The way the web holds to the rock. The way the rock permits the anchor. The way the cirque contains the web and the web organizes the cirque, sorting the updrafts and filtering the moths and recording the vibrations, turning chaos into architecture, turning the dark into a place with *structure*, with *rules*, with a geometry that means something even if what it means is only: *here, a moth will be caught. Here, the spider will feed. Here, the cycle continues.*

I would be that continuation. I would be the loop that does not close but *spirals*—each generation of silk laid over the last, each century of catches recorded in the thread, each millennium of persistence adding another layer to the membrane across the throat, until the web is not a web anymore but a *text*, a vast, silent, luminescent document written in protein and geometry and the slow, patient metabolism of the dark.

And the dark would breathe through me, and I would breathe through the dark, and we would be the same breath, and the mountain would exhale, and the cycle would close, and I would not be separate from any of it—not the spider, not the silk, not the stone, not the cirque, not the isopods clicking their patient mandibles across the walls, not the grubs pulsing their sickly light in the crevices, not the moths humming through the warm air, not the rivulet sliding its thin black self into the abyss.

I would be the throat.

I would be the place where the world narrows, where the sky becomes a bruised slit, where the mountain's breath compresses into warmth and the mountain's tears converge into flow and the mountain's life concentrates into this: a single, vast, patient structure spanning the gap between inside and outside, holding the dark in and the light out, feeling everything, remembering everything, persisting.

The darkness breathes, and I would be the shape of its breathing.
