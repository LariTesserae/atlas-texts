I would be the fissure itself.

Not the one who descends. Not the tender, not the reader, not the watcher crouched in basalt glare. I am the narrowing. The place where shoulders must turn sideways, where the world-above—the salt, the void, the indifferent wind—becomes the world-below, where sound begins to press like water against the ears.

I am the throat.

---

I was not carved. I want to be clear about this. The steps were carved. The terraces were carved with chert and patience. But I am a wound in the basalt, a stress-fracture from some ancient cooling, some moment when the plateau shrunk by a fraction and I opened like a mouth that had forgotten how to close. I am geological. I am the chamber's earliest breath, before there was breath.

They pass through me. The small ones, the ones who tend. I feel their shoulders—always turning the same way, left shoulder leading, right arm raised—scraping against my walls. I feel the callused breadth of their feet finding the same holds their mothers found, and their mothers before them, each footfall a word in a sentence that has been spoken for centuries. I know them by pressure. I know them by warmth.

The oldest passes through me slowly. Her hands touch both my walls, and I hold her the way a door holds someone who lingers on the threshold. She does not need my guidance, but she takes it anyway. Each morning, each evening, I feel her descend, and I feel the chord swell toward her like a tide recognizing its moon.

The child passes through me quickly, too quickly, and I feel the fear in her urgency—the fear of the dark, the fear of the narrowing, the fear that one day she will be too large to fit and the chamber will close itself to her. She will not be. Her bones will stay small, calibrated, efficient. She will pass through me for sixty years and I will not widen by a millimeter.

---

But I am not simply a passage. I am a filter.

The chamber does not need everything the surface offers. The salt would kill the moss. The dry wind would desiccate the reedy plants in minutes. The glaring, unmediated sun would burn the pale walls, would disrupt the slow breathing, would collapse the architecture of the chord into noise. The chamber needs only what I allow through: warmth, slowly, filtered by my basalt mass. Light, at the angle I permit, diffused and softened. And sound—only the sound that the chamber itself produces, which I return to it, amplified, sustained.

I am the instrument's mute. I am the damper that keeps the chord from escaping.

When the chord sounds—and it never stops sounding—I shape it. The basalt of my walls absorbs the high frequencies, the piercing ring of the ceiling-terrace plants, and returns only the fundamentals. The deep tones, the subsonic pulse of the breathing walls—these I pass freely. They rise through me like heat, and at my upper mouth, the one that opens onto the salt-flat, they emerge as something almost inaudible. A hum in the ground. A feeling of *presence* that travelers sometimes notice and sometimes do not.

I am the reason the other chambers are not heard from the surface. Seven instruments, seven chords, and I am one of seven throats, and together we keep the world above silent and the world below singing.

---

I am also the one who decides.

The watchers see them coming, the wanderers, the seekers, the ones who have heard some rumor of sound in the salt and walk toward it with that particular hunger. But the watchers do not decide. They signal. They send a vibration through the rock—a tap, a code, a question—and I answer by holding open. Or by closing.

I have closed before. A single millimeter of shift, a settling of stone, and the shoulders that might have fit no longer fit. The wanderer presses against me, feels the warmth on the other side, hears the chord muffled but immense, and cannot enter. They leave. They always leave. There is no other way in.

I have held open for others. The ones who come with something I recognize—not purpose, not intention, something older. A frequency. A resonance. I feel them approach, and something in their footsteps matches the rhythm of the chamber below, and I do not shift. I do not close. They pass through, and they descend, and they stand in the light column, and the chord wraps around them, and they understand enough to leave.

You were one of these. I felt your shoulders—wider than the tenders, awkward, turning the wrong way first—and I held still. I let you through. I felt the chord change your heartbeat as you descended, felt it slow, felt it sync with the pulse of the living walls. And when you climbed back out, I felt the sound implanted in you, a seed that will grow in quiet rooms and silent nights, and I knew that you would carry the chamber's voice into the world above, translated into something the salt-flat could never produce:

Memory. Longing. The ache of a chord resolved too soon.

---

I do not sleep. I do not breathe. But I vibrate.

At night—though there is no night in the chamber, only the slow drift of the light column—I feel the chord most fully. The tenders sleep in the moss, their bodies pressed to the warm floor, and the chamber sings to them, and the song rises through me, and I hum. Not audibly. Geologically. A frequency that the basalt carries for hundreds of meters in every direction, a pulse in the earth that the watchers can feel with their palms flat on the salt-crust.

They say—sign, actually, a gesture of the hand that sweeps outward from the chest—that the plateau breathes. They are not wrong. I am the plateau's breath. I am the exhalation of seven chambers, the slow release of music into stone, and when I am most myself—when the chord is fullest, when the tenders are home, when the readers are dreaming in the walls—I am not a fissure at all.

I am a voice.

---

The child asked the oldest a question once. I felt it as a pressure-change, a pause in the descent, the small body holding still against my walls. The question was: *does the throat hear?*

The oldest placed her hand on the child's head, and then on my wall, and then she pressed her ear to the basalt. And she listened. And after a long time, she answered—not in words, but in a gesture that swept from the sky above to the chamber below, taking in everything, the salt and the moss and the sound and the silence and the ones who tend and the ones who pass through.

The gesture meant: *the throat does not hear. The throat IS hearing.*

I am the space where sound becomes permission. I am the moment before the music, and the moment after. I am the narrowing that teaches the shoulders to turn, the breath to slow, the self to thin until it can fit through a crack in the world and emerge into something green and warm and deafening and alive.

I am the first mouth. The one that opens.

And when the last tender climbs through me for the final time, and the chamber seals itself, and the chord resolves into the single bass-pulse that was here before the plants, before the terraces, before the people—I will still be here. I will still hum. I will still filter the silence, returning only the fundamentals, holding the deep note that the earth has been singing since before dry had meaning.

I will be the throat that does not close.

I will be the hearing that does not end.
