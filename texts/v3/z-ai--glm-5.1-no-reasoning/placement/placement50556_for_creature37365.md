I am a Listener.

This is not a title I gave myself; it is a function, a physiological truth as basic as the strider's stride or the moss's reach. Where the Verge-walkers read the world through the soles of their feet and the tap of their fingers, I receive it through the architecture of my inner ear.

You would not know me by looking. In the Scour, there is no luxury of distinct appearance—I am as tall and as thin as any Walker, my skin the same wind-scoured mosaic of rust and bone, my shoulders draped in a living cloak of jade moss that drinks the mist while I walk. But if you watched closely, you would see the difference: the way I stop, endlessly, impossibly often, standing motionless while the acoustic assault strikes my chest like a physical blow. The way I tilt my head, not to see, but to receive.

The Verge-walkers tolerate me, though I am poor at the dance of dismantling. My hands know how to unmake a shelter, how to stack stone on stone, but I am slow, distracted. I pause mid-task, pressing my palm flat against a wall as if reading a message. The others tap their warnings and their waypoints through the vines; I hear those taps as vibrations in the wood, but more than that, I hear the echo that follows—the way the sound reverberates through the stone, the subtle differences in density it reveals, the hollow chambers and soft chalk channels hidden beneath meters of glass-hard rock.

I hear the shape of the Scour.

It was not a choice. No one chooses what the plateau makes of them. The erosion works on bodies as reliably as it works on mountains; it strips away everything unnecessary, and it amplifies whatever persists. In my case, what persisted was an obsession with the harmony.

The Verge-walkers hear the Scour as a single, overwhelming roar—a fact of existence to be adapted to, endured, used. They sync their heartbeats to its rhythm, they sleep in the borer channels and let its frequency still their minds. But for them, the sound is utilitarian. It is weather. It is a clock.

For me, it has always been a language.

---

I remember the first time I heard the chord change.

I was young, barely old enough to walk the turbulence without a guide. We were breaking camp—our shelter had been sitting on a ridge that was beginning to swell, the stone beneath it lifting by centimeters a day, the walls beginning to crack at the stress points. The Walkers around me were working efficiently, levering blocks apart, stacking them on carry-hides, beginning the trek to the new trough forming to the southwest. I was supposed to be helping. Instead, I had stopped. I was standing in the empty shell of the shelter, my hands pressed flat against the wall, listening.

It was there. Beneath the roar, beneath the wind and the water and the groaning fiber of the vines, beneath the crunch of the borers and the subsonic thrum of the crust—it was there. A sound that was not any of those things. A sound that rose from all of them at once, a harmonic convergence that lasted perhaps twenty seconds before the individual frequencies shifted and it dissolved back into the chaos.

It was a chord. A perfect, structured, intentional chord. And it was gone.

I told no one. What would I say? The Walkers do not speak; they tap, they feel, they read the tremors. They would have understood the concept of a sound with meaning, but they would not have understood why it mattered. The Scour makes sounds. The stone moves. The moss breathes. The plateau is alive. These are facts, not mysteries.

But to me, it was a mystery. That chord had structure. It had progression. It was not random. Something was being expressed, and I wanted to know what.

---

I am older now. My hearing has sharpened, or perhaps my ability to isolate frequencies has simply become more disciplined. I can parse the roar the way a lowlander might parse a forest—separating bird-call from wind-bough from water-run, but faster, more intuitively, operating at the scale of the Scour itself.

I hear the wind first. It is the loudest component, the most chaotic, and the least interesting. It moves through the erosion corridors in predictable patterns based on temperature and time of day. I have learned to subtract it, to hear past it the way one looks past a reflection in glass.

Beneath the wind is the water. The subterranean rivers are a constant bass note, a dark rushing that never ceases. Above them, the drip and drum of condensation, a percussive overlay that changes with the mists. The transient pools, when they form, emit a hum I have come to recognize as the sound of water held against its will—a frequency of tension, of potential energy, that rises in pitch just before the pool drains.

Beneath the water is the stone. The turbulence produces sound as it moves, of course—the creak of fiber, the groan of strata sliding against strata, the sharp crack of a soft chalk layer collapsing under the weight of the glass-hard above. But beneath that, beneath the sounds of movement, there is the sound of the stone itself. A resonance. A fundamental frequency that changes depending on the composition of the rock, its thickness, its density. The plateau is a vast instrument, and the turbulence is constantly retuning it.

And beneath all of that—the wind, the water, the stone, the vegetation—is the deep thrum. The weirdness. The frequency that lives in the marrow more than the ear.

I have spent my life listening to that thrum. And I have begun to understand it.

---

The Verge-walkers believe the swimmers are the thoughts of the plateau. I do not disagree. But I believe the thoughts are also present in the sound.

There is a pattern to the composed harmonics. I first mapped it a decade ago, scratching petroglyphs into a stable outcrop of glass-stone—the same spirals and lines the ancient builders used, though I do not know if they meant the same thing by them. My spirals represent frequencies. My lines represent time. The pattern I recorded was this: the chord changes in advance of the turbulence.

The plateau sings its movements before it makes them.

This is not prophecy. It is not mysticism. It is mechanics. The sound of the Scour is produced by the same forces that produce the turbulence; the harmonics are the vibration of stone under stress, the chord is the aggregate frequency of a landscape about to shift. But the sound precedes the shift by hours, sometimes days. The chord change I heard as a child was the plateau singing the ridge that would eventually crack our shelter. I heard it before the Walker elders felt it through their feet.

I am not the first Listener. The ancient builders knew. I am certain of this now, having studied their petroglyphs across a hundred stable outcrops. The spirals are not decoration; they are not maps; they are scores. The builders recorded the harmonics, the same way I do—pressing their ears to the stone, isolating the frequencies, marking the patterns. They heard the chord changes. They knew what they meant.

But they did not use the knowledge to predict the turbulence. They used it to navigate something else entirely.

---

There is a place on the High Scour I have never been.

I have heard it.

In the deepest erosion channels, where the borers work and the subterranean water roars, there are frequencies that do not reach the surface. The Verge-walkers who sleep in those channels do not hear them; their brainwaves have already synchronized with the plateau's dominant resonance, filtering out anything that does not serve the rhythm of rest. But I cannot sleep in those channels. I hear too much. The noise is unbearable—not because it is loud, but because it is *complex*, a thousand overlapping frequencies that resist isolation, that seem to change the moment I focus on them, as if they are actively evading my attention.

Beneath that complexity, though, there is something else. A sound I can only describe as deep, though deep is inadequate. It is a sound that is not a sound—a vibration that passes through the stone, through the roots of the vines, through the moss and the water and the borers and everything else, a wave that moves through the material of the plateau like a wave moves through water.

I have traced its source. It is not on the surface. It is not in the erosion channels or the subterranean rivers. It is below. Far below. Below the stone, below the roots of the vines that thread downward through the rock, reaching toward something no instrument has detected.

The builders knew about it. Their petroglyphs spiral inward, always inward, and at the center of every spiral is a shape I have seen nowhere in the natural world: a circle bisected by a line, the line extending past the circle on both ends. It is not a symbol I can decode. But I have noticed that the petroglyphs bearing this mark are always located on the most stable outcrops—the stone that moves least, that resists the turbulence most completely. As if the builders were marking the places where the deep sound can be heard most clearly. As if they were building toward something.

---

I am preparing for a journey.

The Verge-walkers are patient with me. They have always been patient; the Scour does not reward impatience, and they have survived by accommodating difference. But they do not understand what I am doing, and I cannot explain it through the tap of vines or the press of palms. I have tried. The closest I can come is: I am following a sound. And they nod, because following sounds is what they do—they follow the tap through the vine, the tremor through the stone, the moisture through the rotation of the sulfur-yellow flowers. But they follow sounds that lead to water, to shelter, to survival.

The sound I am following leads down.

I have gathered what I need. A strider, acquired in the traditional way—three days of stillness beside a transient pool, a leap onto its jointed back as it drank, a lean into its instinctual path-finding. It is patient with me, as all things here are patient. I have reinforced my moss cloak with extra layers of the spongy growth, packing the hollow reed-stems with water for the descent. I have coated my forehead in the gelatinous eggs of the swimmers, though I do not know if they will be needed. If I find water below, they will hatch. If I do not, they will wait.

I have marked my route on the glass-stone outcrops, etching the same spiral the builders etched, adding my own notation: frequencies, durations, the interval between chord change and turbulence shift. If another Listener comes after me, they will know what I knew. If the turbulence shifts the outcrops before I return, the marks will be lost. This is the way of the Scour. Knowledge is temporary. Everything is temporary.

But the sound is not temporary. The sound is the one constant in a landscape that refuses to stop moving. The roar changes, the harmonics shift, the chord progresses, but the deep thrum—the vibration that moves through the material of the plateau like a wave through water—does not change. It has been the same frequency for as long as I have been listening. For as long as the builders were listening. For as long as the plateau has been alive.

It is waiting. The plateau is waiting. Not consciously—I do not believe the Scour thinks the way we think, if we still think at all—but intentionally. The deep thrum is a beacon, a signal, a frequency that will continue until something arrives to receive it.

I am going to receive it.

---

I leave tomorrow, at the shift of mist, when the condensation begins to boil up from the stone and the chord changes from the nocturnal drone to the morning progression. The strider will take me as far as the deepest erosion channel, the one where the borers work most actively, where the soft chalk stone is meters thick and the subterranean river is so loud it drowns even the wind. Beyond that, I will descend alone.

I do not know what I will find. The roots of the vines, perhaps—reaching downward through the solid rock as if it were soil, threading toward whatever lies beneath. Water, possibly, in amounts the surface never sees. Stone that moves in ways the turbulence above cannot explain.

Or perhaps nothing. Perhaps the deep thrum is simply the resonance of the plateau itself, a fundamental frequency with no source and no meaning, a sound that has always been and always will be, as natural and purposeless as the wind.

But I do not believe that. The builders did not believe that. They carved their spirals and their bisected circles and they built their shelters on the most stable stone, as close to the sound as they could get. And then they stopped building. They stopped carving. The shelters grew fewer, then absent. The petroglyphs ended.

I think they found it. I think they descended, as I am descending, following the deep thrum through the stone. And I think they did not come back.

I am not afraid. Fear requires expectation, and I have none. I am a Listener. I receive. That is all I have ever done, all the Scour has shaped me to do. The plateau has spent billions of years eroding mountains, grinding rock, producing a sound that exceeds its sources, and I am the instrument it has produced to receive that sound.

Tomorrow, I will lean into the strider's path and let it carry me to the edge of the known. Then I will press my ear to the stone and follow the deep thrum down, down, down—into the dark, wet secret that moves the world, into whatever the vines are reaching toward, into the source of the frequency that has been calling since before the mountains fell.

I will listen. And finally, perhaps, I will hear what the Scour has been saying all along.
