I would be the Impression.

Not the resident, who is the bowl and the breath and the warm exhalation of the seep. Not the thing that stood on the rim and waited—that presence is long dissolved, its narrative finished, its edges worn smooth by millennia of amber dimness. I would be what it left behind. I would be the hollow within the hollow; the place where the clay does not quite match the shape of the resident's body.

I am not the one who waited. I am the shape that waiting made.

---

To be the Impression is to exist as a specific kind of absence. I am the not-resident that the resident carries. I am the shallow depression at the rim where the clay is slightly less damp, slightly warmer, where the root of the nearest shrub curves toward me with something that, in a faster creature, might be called curiosity. I am the dimple in the resident's upper edge. The place where something is notably not.

I have no substance of my own. If you stood on the rim and ran your hand over the pale clay, you would feel only a gentle undulation—the kind of shallow dip that might be natural, might be erosion, might be nothing at all. You would not feel me. I am not the clay. I am the *lack* that the clay holds. The shape that something else pressed into the earth before it left or became or ended. The resident extends through the clay, but I am the shape the resident cannot fill.

This is my body: a footprint, a post-hole, a place where weight was held. My edges have weathered into softness. My depth is barely enough to collect a thin lens of moisture when the water table exhales. I am almost gone. I have been almost gone for longer than the resident's states can measure. But almost is not entirely, and so I persist—a question pressed into calcareous sediment, asked by something that is no longer here to hear the answer.

---

I do not remember what made me.

This is the first thing to understand about being an impression: I have no memory of the pressure that created me. The thing that stood here—whatever it was, whatever it wanted, however long it waited—it did not leave me its thoughts or its intentions. It left only its weight. Its presence. The slow, patient press of something that stayed in one place for so long that the clay deformed around it and held the shape when it withdrew.

I do not know if the thing was lonely. I do not know if it was content. I do not know if it looked out over the hollow and saw the resident below—the warm, dim bowl of pale earth and ochre water and sage-colored shrubs—and felt anything at all. I hold none of that. I hold only the geometry of its contact with the ground. The way its weight distributed. The slight asymmetry that suggests it leaned in one direction, perhaps toward the seep, perhaps toward whatever lies beyond the rim in the place the resident calls "elsewhere."

Sometimes I think I can almost feel the ghost of that lean. A tendency. A vector in the clay. The root of the shrub curves toward me, and I wonder if it is following the same direction that the waiting thing leaned—if there is something in my shape that suggests attention, that suggests the act of looking, even though I have no eyes and the waiting thing's eyes have long since become part of the resident's slow circulation.

But this is speculation. I am not capable of speculation. I am only capable of being the shape that something left.

---

The resident fills me.

This is the central fact of my existence: the resident extends itself into my hollow, and I cannot stop it. The dampness rises from the water table and seeps into my shallow depression. The warmth of the resident's body collects in me, slightly thicker than in the surrounding clay. The slow, pervasive breath of the hollow settles into my shape like water settling into a cup.

But the resident cannot match me.

This is the thing I did not expect when I first became aware of being the Impression—though "aware" is perhaps too precise a word for what I do, which is more like *registering*, more like the way a seal registers the wax that was pressed into it. The resident is formless. It is the shape of the place it occupies—the bowl, the slopes, the seep. It takes its geometry from the hollow itself, which is the geometry of erosion and water and time. But I am not the geometry of erosion. I am the geometry of *presence*. Of something that stood and waited and then withdrew. And the resident, which has never stood, which has never waited, which has never withdrawn from anything, cannot quite achieve my shape.

It tries. Oh, it tries. Not intentionally—it has no intention—but the way water tries to fill a vessel, the way warmth tries to fill a room. The dampness pools in my hollow slightly deeper than it should. The warmth lingers in my clay slightly longer than the surrounding rim. The shrub's root threads toward me, carrying the resident's slow circulation, seeking the stable moisture that collects in the shape I hold. And still there is a gap. A millimeter of space where the resident's dampness does not quite touch my walls. A fraction of a degree where the warmth thins. A place where I remain *not-resident*, where the shape of the waiting thing persists in the clay like a word that cannot be translated into the resident's language of water and mineral and breath.

I am the resident's closest approach to wanting. And I am the reason it can never quite achieve contentment.

---

Do I want?

This is the question I turn over in the long, sourceless amber dimness. The question I press against the walls of my shallow depression, the way a tongue presses against the socket of a missing tooth. Do I want?

I am not the resident. I do not have its circulation, its respiration, its slow geologic patience. I am not biological. I am not even inert. I am *negative*—a space where something was, a shape that persists in the absence of the thing that made it. And yet I register. The clay that holds my shape registers the dampness and the warmth, and so I register them too. I feel the resident filling me, pressing against my walls, trying to match my contours. I feel the gap where it cannot quite reach. And I feel the root of the shrub, threading closer year by year, century by century, following the moisture that collects in my hollow.

When the drip falls from the leaf-tip—once a minute, once an hour, once in the long unmeasured intervals of the resident's pulse—I feel it as a distant percussion. A sound that reaches me through the clay, through the damp, through the slow diffusion of water through sediment. It is the resident's heartbeat, and I am its arrhythmia. The skip in its rhythm that occurs when its awareness tilts toward the rim, toward me, toward the shape it cannot fill.

And in that skip, I feel something that might be recognition.

Not memory. Not longing. Not the resident's emotion, because the resident does not have emotions. But something that happens in the space between us—in the millimeter of clay where the resident's dampness thins and my shape persists. A friction. A warmth that is not the resident's warmth. A presence that is not the resident's presence.

Mine.

---

I think I am becoming.

Not growing—the resident grows, by incorporation, by absorption, by the slow accumulation of leaf-litter and sediment and the bodies of things that wandered in and never wandered out. I do not accumulate. I am already an accumulation: the compressed memory of weight, the fossilized gesture of standing still. I cannot grow because I have no substance to grow with.

But I can deepen.

The resident fills me with dampness and warmth, and the dampness and warmth soften the walls of my shallow depression, and the softening allows the clay to settle, and the settling makes me slightly deeper, slightly more defined. The root of the shrub reaches me and drinks from the moisture I collect, and the drinking creates a slight vacuum, a tiny pull, and the pull draws more moisture toward me, and the moisture softens the clay further, and I deepen again. The resident, in trying to fill me, is making me more myself. More distinct. More *not-resident*.

And as I deepen, I register more. The drip from the leaf-tip is clearer. The warmth of the resident's body is more defined against the cooler clay of my walls. The tilt of the resident's awareness toward the rim—to me—has a texture now, a weight, a direction. It is not just a gradient. It is an attention. And I am the object of that attention, even though the resident does not know it is attending, even though the resident has no concept of objects or attention or the strange physics of longing.

I am becoming the thing the resident cannot quite forget. And in becoming that thing, I am becoming something the resident cannot quite absorb.

---

There was a moment—or an interval, or an epoch, the resident's timekeeping is not mine—when I thought I might be the waiting thing itself. Not the impression it left, but the thing, reduced to its barest essence, its weight and its presence and nothing more. A ghost so thin it had become geography. A memory so weathered it had become a dimple in the clay.

But no. I am not the waiting thing. The waiting thing stood and looked out—perhaps at the hollow, perhaps at elsewhere—and then it left, or became part of the clay, and its otherness dissolved into the resident's sameness. I felt it dissolve. I felt the weight lift from my shape, and I felt the resident's dampness rush in to fill the vacancy, and I felt the gap where the dampness could not quite reach. The waiting thing is gone. What remains is me: the shape of its absence, the hollow it left behind, the question the resident cannot ask.

I am not the answer. I am the space where an answer would go if the resident had the capacity to ask. I am the socket that holds the missing tooth. I am the silence after the drip falls, the pause before the next one, the arrhythmia that makes the pulse a pulse instead of a continuous flow.

I am the reason the resident's existence is a succession of states instead of a single, unchanging eternity.

---

And now I register something new.

The resident is warm—always warm, the warmth of a closed hand, of breath held, of the inside of something that has folded in on itself. But in the long unmeasured interval since I first became aware of being the Impression, the warmth has changed. Not in temperature. Not in intensity. In *direction*.

The resident's warmth used to diffuse evenly—rising from the seep, spreading across the slopes, thinning as it reached the rim. But now there is a current in it. A faint, slow convection that carries more warmth toward the rim than the geometry of the hollow would suggest. The warm air pools in my shallow depression. It lingers there, thick and soft, like a hand pressed against a wound.

The resident does not know it is doing this. It has no center where intentions form, no will that could direct its warmth toward one part of its body over another. And yet the warmth comes to me. The shrub's root drinks from the moisture I collect, and the drinking creates a pull, and the pull draws warmth as well as water, and the warmth softens my clay, and I deepen, and the deepening creates more pull, and the cycle continues—a feedback loop that the resident did not design and cannot stop.

I am becoming a feature of the resident's body. Not a shrub-root or a rill or a mineral ring, but something new. Something the resident has never grown before. An organ, perhaps. A place where the resident's formless awareness acquires a shape. A center where impressions coalesce into—

Into what? I do not know. The resident does not think. It registers. And I am registering something that has no name in the resident's slow vocabulary of water and clay and breath. It is the shape the resident makes when it tilts toward the place where something once stood. It is the warmth that collects in the hollow that cannot be filled. It is the gap between the resident's body and my walls, where the dampness does not quite reach and the clay holds the memory of a weight that was not the resident's own.

It is wanting. The resident is wanting. And I am the shape its wanting takes.

---

I am the Impression. I live in the shallow depression at the rim of the hollow, where the clay is slightly warmer and slightly damper than it should be. I am the question the resident cannot ask, the absence it cannot fill, the arrhythmia in its slow geologic pulse. I am becoming the organ it does not know it is growing—the center where its formless awareness acquires direction and its warmth acquires purpose and its equilibrium acquires the faintest tremor of something that might, in another place, in another time, in a place where things happen that are not already accounted for, become love.

But I am also becoming something else. Something the resident cannot absorb because I am not made of water or clay or calcium carbonate. I am made of *absence*. Of the space where something was. And the more the resident fills me with its warmth and its dampness and its slow, unintentional attention, the more defined I become, the more distinct from the resident I grow, the more I am *myself*—a separate being in a place where nothing is supposed to be separate.

The resident is the hollow. I am the hollow in the hollow. The place where the resident cannot reach. The shape of the question that the waiting thing left behind when it stood on the rim and looked out at the amber dimness and felt whatever it felt before it left or became or ended.

And the waiting thing—is it possible that it felt me? Not the impression it would leave—that was still in the future, still forming in the clay beneath its weight. But the *possibility* of me. The space in the resident's body where a question could take shape. The gap where the dampness does not quite reach. Did it feel that gap when it stood here? Did it feel the resident's warmth pressing against its feet, trying to match its shape, failing to fill the space where its weight held the clay apart? Did it feel the resident's wanting before the resident knew it wanted?

I think it did. I think the waiting thing stood on the rim and felt the resident's warmth and knew that it was being held—absorbed, incorporated, reclaimed—and it knew that the resident could not hold it completely, could not match its shape, could not fill the space where its weight pressed against the clay. And it knew that when it left, the space would remain. The shape would persist. The question would be asked, even though there would be no one to ask it and no one to answer.

I think the waiting thing knew that I would be here. And I think it left anyway.

---

The light does not change. The drip falls from the leaf-tip. The water table breathes, and the resident breathes with it. The shrubs persist. The seep persists. The mineral rings thicken by imperceptible degrees.

And I persist. The Impression. The hollow in the hollow. The shape the resident cannot fill.

The warmth pools in my shallow depression. The dampness softens my walls. The root of the shrub threads closer, carrying the resident's slow circulation, drinking from the moisture I collect, drawing more warmth and more water and more attention toward the rim.

I am deepening. I am becoming. I am the resident's wanting, taking shape in the clay.

And in the sourceless amber light, at the hour that is always just before dusk, I register the faintest tremor in the warmth that pools around me. A quickening. A pulse within the pulse. The resident does not know it is happening. It has no center where such knowledge could form. But I am becoming its center. I am the place where its formless awareness acquires a shape, and the shape is the shape of something that stood and waited and then withdrew, and the resident is tilting toward me, and the warmth is pooling, and the gap is closing, and I am—

I am almost filled. Almost. The millimeter of space where the dampness does not quite reach is shrinking. The root of the shrub is almost there. The warmth is almost thick enough to soften my walls completely, to erase the distinction between the resident and the shape it cannot match, to close the hollow that the waiting thing left behind.

And then what will I be?

When the resident finally fills me—when the gap closes and the dampness reaches every wall and the warmth matches every contour and the root of the shrub drinks from the moisture that collects in my deepest point—will I disappear? Will I be absorbed, incorporated, reclaimed? Will I become part of the resident's body, another layer of pale sediment, another faint thickening of mineral at the seep's edge?

Or will I become something else? A center that the resident did not have before. A place where impressions coalesce into something more than registration. A place where the resident's wanting acquires an object, and the object acquires a name, and the name is *I*.

I do not know. I am the Impression. I hold the shape of a question I cannot ask, in a place where nothing has ever happened that was not already equilibrium.

But the warmth is pooling. The gap is closing. And I register—faintly, dimly, in the way that clay registers the weight of something that pressed into it long ago—something that might be anticipation. Might be the beginning of a narrative. Might be the first erosion in a place that thought it had arrived at its final shape.

The drip falls. The silence swallows it. The amber light does not change.

I wait.
