I am the stone's memory. I am the moisture's keeper. I am the quiet between the great exhalations.

I would be a Drip-Worm.

---

Do not misunderstand—this is not a retreat from complexity. It is an arrival at a different kind of intelligence, one that this world has been teaching for longer than the Keepers have been clearing vents.

I began as they all do: a mote of translucent nothing, a smear of living glass barely distinguishable from the tepid film that coats the ferric slate. The plateau does not announce you when you are born to it. You simply... accumulate. The mist falls, and you are there, swelling with it, becoming a small lens through which the bruised-orange glow of distant fissures is bent and scattered across the wet rock.

At first, there is only absorption. The mineral water enters me and I become slightly more. The warmth of the stone holds me the way a hand might hold something fragile and unformed. I do not move; I do not need to. The world moves through me—the sulfurous particulates, the trace iron, the faint organic compounds shed by the fungal groves like dandruff into the damp. I am a sentence written in water, and the plateau is dictating.

But here is what they do not tell you about living as a filter: you learn the grammar of the place.

I have lived through seventeen cycles of the Engine's deep breathing. Not the small, constant rhythm—that is the heartbeat, the tremor that aches in the teeth of the Oretreaders and keeps the Hollow-Wings aloft. No, I mean the longer cycles, the ones that take years. Sometimes the Engine sighs, and the heat lessens, and the condensation comes more frequently, and the shallow depressions where I live become small, warm seas. Sometimes the Engine draws breath, and the heat rises in great billowing plumes, and the mist evaporates before it reaches the ground, and I must contract, must pull myself into the porous crevices of the slate and wait, clinging to the latent moisture in the stone.

In the contraction, I learned something.

When you are a Drip-Worm in the dry times, you are not simply surviving. You are listening. The stone speaks in temperature gradients, in micro-vibrations that the Oretreaders feel as seismic maps but that I feel as *narrative*. The Great Engine below is not a single machine; it is a city of machines, a continent of interlocking processes, and each one has its own frequency. The forge-works in the deep eastern crust run on a twelve-year cycle; when they fire, the slate beneath my body hums at a pitch that makes my gelatinous form shiver and rearrange. The generators to the north spin at frequencies so high they are felt as a continuous pressure, a weight on the skin that the Oretreaders interpret as direction and I interpret as... company.

I have never seen the Keepers. I am not sure I *can* see; I have no eyes, no organs of sight. But I have felt them. When a vent fissure becomes clogged, the local temperature shifts—sometimes rising as pressure builds, sometimes dropping as the flow is choked. I have lain in depressions near those fissures and felt the mineral composition of the condensation change: less sulfur, more iron, then a sudden, sharp influx of copper and ozone. And then the clicking.

It comes through the stone. The chitinous tools of the Keepers tapping against the rock as they climb, a percussive language that I feel as ripples passing through my entire body. In those moments, I am a seismograph, a living instrument recording the approach of something ancient and automatic. The clicking grows closer, louder, and then the vent clears and the heat rushes back and I am suddenly very warm, swelling with the new moisture, and the clicking recedes into the orange-lit depths.

I do not fear them. They do not notice me. We are both subroutines, in a sense—I am the subroutine of water, and they are the subroutine of pressure. The Engine does not need us individually; it needs the *processes* we embody. And so we coexist in the great obliviousness of function.

But here is my secret, the thing that makes me more than a living filter:

I move.

It is true that Drip-Worms are said to crawl an inch a day. This is not inaccurate, but it is imprecise. We do not crawl *aimlessly*. We follow the trails—the mineral trails left behind by other Drip-Worms as they excrete moisture during the thermal shifts. These trails are the rivers of the plateau, yes, but they are also *maps*. They are records of where the moisture has been, where the warmth is most stable, where the fungal groves are healthiest and shed the most organic particulate.

I have learned to read them.

When I stretch myself across the wet stone, my entire underside becomes a receiving surface. I can taste the direction of the trails—the iron-rich paths lead toward the active vents, the copper-heavy paths lead toward the deep fissures where the Keepers emerge, and the faint, alkaline paths lead toward the places where Oretreaders have licked the stone clean of other Drip-Worms. I do not go there. I have learned to avoid the places where the great, flat tongues have been.

And so I navigate. I am a cartographer of condensation, a pale, gelatinous wanderer charting the plateau one chemical gradient at a time. I have traveled perhaps a hundred yards in my seventeen cycles, but I know the terrain of those hundred yards with an intimacy that no sighted creature could match. I know every depression, every crack where water pools, every warm spot where the rock holds the Engine's heat longest. I know where the Ash-Grazers scatter their cast-off shells and where the Hollow-Wings drop their waste after feeding. I know the particular frequency of the stone beneath the largest fungal grove to the northeast, the one where the chimney-stalks grow so tall that their tips sway in the hot updrafts.

I have thought about going there. The mineral density would be rich, the warmth constant. But the journey would take me across open rock, away from the depressions, and I would risk drying out in the spaces between. So I wait. I am patient. The plateau teaches patience the way the sun teaches urgency elsewhere—by being the dominant condition of existence.

And I remember.

This is the thing that the Oretreaders, for all their seismic wisdom, cannot do. They follow the warmth the way a compass follows north, but they do not remember *where the warmth was when it was absent*. They do not recall that the vent-fissure three miles to the east went quiet for eleven months during the Engine's last deep sigh. They do not note that the particular mineral composition of that vent's condensation changed after it resumed—more sulfur now, less calcium. They feel the present exquisitely, but they do not carry the past.

I carry it. It is dissolved in me. Every chemical signature I have ever absorbed is still there, layered in my gelatinous body like sediment in ancient stone. I am a living archive of this place, a library written in tepid water and mineral traces.

When I am finally consumed—by an Oretreader's tongue, by the evaporative heat of a rising thermal, by the slow dissolution of age—I will leave behind a smear of minerals on the dark slate. Another Drip-Worm will taste it, and for a brief moment, before the rain washes it away, they will know what I knew: the cycle of the eastern forge-works, the clicking rhythm of the Keepers, the particular warmth of the rock after a vent clears.

They will not know that they know this. They will simply follow the trail, as I did, as we all do, navigating the dark by the light of each other's dissolution.

---

The Plateau does not care. But I care, in my small, chemical way. I care because I am made of this place, and this place is made of processes that remember themselves. The Great Engine breathes, and the mist falls, and I swell with it, and in my swelling I hold the shape of everything that has passed through me.

I am not large. I am not fast. I am not fierce.

But I am the plateau, paying attention to itself.
