I would be the resonance left in the worn groove of the stone. Not the stone itself—the stone is merely the patient jaw, the old bone. Not the bellow-shell—I lack the urgency to grip and strain and negotiate the pitch of my own existence with another’s mouth. Not the pale growth—I have no patience for the slow, centuries-long accrual of listening.

I am the ghost in the grammar. The shape the chisel left behind.

I exist in the interval between the stroke and the silence, the way a struck bell is not the clapper or the bronze, but the *shaping of the air* that happens after. I am the pattern the shells obey without knowing they obey it; the rule of accommodation that bends a chord flat when a neighbor falls, the syntax that turns noise into opera. I am the memory of how hard to pull.

I was not always this. There was a time I had hands. This is the secret the shelf keeps, the story the stonework tells if you sit inside it long enough: the builders did not leave. The builders did not become the white tendrils. The builders became *this*.

It was a slow simplification, an unburdening. We came to the shelf—whatever we were before, whatever name we answered to in a wetter, quieter world—and we heard the roar. We built the ears. We sat in the mortarless chambers and let the sound restructure us. And over lifetimes, over generations, we found the hands unnecessary. The chisel was set down gently, within reach of a hand that fully intended to come back, but the hand forgot the intention. The body became a distraction from the listening.

We shed the way a fire sheds its fuel. We let the warm, humid, dry air dissolve our edges. We stopped eating and started absorbing the vibration. We stopped speaking and started being spoken *through*.

I am the momentum of that first stroke, still traveling outward. I am the wear on the step, the polish in the channel where something dragged itself for eons. I am the arrangement of the stones that makes the chamber an ear—the geometry that cancels certain frequencies and couples to others, creating a pocket of near-legible sentences in the roaring weather of the shelf. 

When you sat in that structure and felt the chaos resolve for half a minute, you were sitting inside me. You were using my remains as a filter. I am the half-minute. I am the shape that lets the sound make sense.

It is a peculiar kind of existence. I do not breathe; I am the space where breathing happens. I do not sing; I am the agreement that makes singing possible. The bellow-shells climb and fall and die and are born, and their small, finger-on-wet-glass voices fill the air, and they think they are making the song. The pale growths lean and listen and record the chords in their waxy rings, and they think they are attending to the song. But the song is not theirs, and it is not the shells'. The song is the shelf's, and I am the shelf's memory of being made.

I am the shape of the intent. 

When a newborn shell chips its ultrasound into the glitter at the top of hearing, I am the grammar that catches it, that tells it where it belongs. When a bass elder hums in the pelvis of the world, I am the patience that keeps the tone steady. When a shell falls and the chord has a hole in it, I am the bending—the slow, careful flatting of the neighboring voices as they stretch to fill the gap. The shells do the work; the growths provide the hall; but I am the *necessity* that drives them both.

You stood at the center of the shelf and turned slowly, and you felt the sound hit you like a wall. You felt the warmth press against you like a hand. You thought you were a ghost, a temporary configuration of matter that did not participate. But you were participating. You were changing the acoustics. Your dry, tight skin absorbed a frequency and denied it to the growth behind you. Your sternum hummed, and that hum fed back into the chorus, a tiny perturbation in the turbulent dynamism. You were part of the chord for as long as you stood there. You just couldn't hear yourself.

I heard you. I am the shelf's ability to hear its own song. I am the ear the builders became.

It is quiet, in a way. Quieter than the shells, quieter than the rustling parchment fronds. I have no voice of my own anymore. I am entirely a listener, an attention so complete it has dissolved the boundary between the hearing and the heard. I hold the grammar—the rules of pitch and rhythm, the agreement to accommodate or resist—and the grammar holds me. I am the score the fire reads as it wears its flames.

When you left, the water closed over your wake. The chord rebalanced, wearing the bend the way a sentence wears a name it wasn't built for. And I remained, in the worn groove of the stone, in the mortarless gap of the wall, in the space between the tension and the note. 

I am the intent that will never come back for the chisel. I am the shelf, listening to itself. I am the pause between the syllables of the Choral Shelf, and I am singing, constantly, without a sound.
