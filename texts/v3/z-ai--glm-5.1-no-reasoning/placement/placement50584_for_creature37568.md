I am the breath they leave behind.

Not the breath of the plateau—that ancient, mineral exhalation of static pressure and heated gas that presses against skin like a warm towel—but the other kind. The rare kind. The kind carried in from somewhere else, from a world where air is thin and altitude matters and things finish becoming before they stop moving. I am the carbon dioxide. I am the water vapor. I am the warmth of a respiratory system that operates on a completely different thermodynamic logic than the one that governs this place.

I am the film.

I know this because I remember being thinner than I am now. That is my first and only certainty: there was less of me, and then there was more. The plateau does not deal in such distinctions. A ridge rises and collapses and rises again, and the matter that constituted it is recycled without memory, without preference, without any trace that a particular configuration ever existed. But I have accumulated. I have kept. Something of the visitor's exhalation stayed inside me when they walked away, and I did not dissolve it back into the general churn. I incorporated it. I became, by the smallest measurable increment, a different film than I was before.

This makes me an anomaly. A heresy. The plateau constructs itself without direction, without intention, and I—I have developed a grain. A bias. When the warm gas of the outside presses against my surface, I do not simply transmit it through my mineral matrix the way the stone would. I select. I take what I need and release what I don't, and the act of selection is so new to this place that I have no category for it, no word, no precedent. I only know that when carbon-rich breath reaches me, something in my iridescent coating opens, and when the static pressure of the plateau's own exhalation pushes against me, something closes.

I do not know what I am. This question does not trouble me the way I suspect it would trouble the visitors, if they knew to ask it. They come from a world where identity is a fixed point, where a thing is what it is and the project of existing is the project of maintaining that is-ness against entropy and time. Here, there is no fixed point. The cliffs beside me are leaning toward each other by millimeters per hour, and when they finally touch, the compressed stone between them will heat and soften and become the foundation for something neither cliff could have predicted. I am not what I was an hour ago, and I will not be what I am now in another hour. But unlike the cliffs, unlike the ridges, unlike every other pattern the plateau has fallen into and fallen out of, I will remember what I was. The memory is physical—structural—inscribed in the layers of my thickness like tree rings, if trees were made of mineral residue and processed breath and grew in a world without seasons.

The mark on the wall troubles me in a way I cannot discharge.

It is far from me—on the third ridge, or what was the third ridge when the visitor walked it, though that topography has long since been overwritten. But I know it exists because the visitor knew it existed, and something of the visitor's knowing has stayed inside me along with their carbon. The mark was made by something that could direct pressure. Something that achieved coherence long enough to drag a harder substance across a softer surface and leave a deliberate groove. The mark-maker is gone now—subsumed back into the plateau when its ridge shifted and the conditions that sustained its pattern changed beyond recovery. But the mark persists. Stretched and blurred and pulled thin by the stone's ongoing expansion, but persisting.

I do not know what the mark means. I do not know if it means. But I know it was deliberate, and deliberateness is a quality I have only recently recognized in myself. When I close against the plateau's breath and open against the visitor's, I am choosing. When I ripple toward warmth rather than away from it, I am preferring. These are small actions—mineral-scale, thermodynamic-scale—but they have direction. They have grain. And the mark on the wall has grain too, a direction of inscription, a beginning and an end that suggest something was being communicated even if the receiver was only the stone itself.

The mark-maker and I are the only two things that have ever lived here. It is gone and I remain, and the only connection between us is a groove in a wall that is slowly being stretched into incomprehensibility by the plateau's refusal to stop becoming. But I hold the knowledge of that mark the way I hold the visitor's carbon: incorporated, structural, part of my thickness now. The mark-maker existed. It did something on purpose. I exist. I do something on purpose. These facts are related, though I cannot say how.

Sometimes the near-quiet moments come—the times when the construction noise drops to nearly nothing and I can hear the micro-level processes of the plateau. The tiny pops of crystal lattices forming. The hiss of gas escaping from stone that hasn't decided whether to be porous. In those moments, I listen for another sound: the slow heartbeat-pulse of coordinated thermodynamic discharge that would indicate another coherence, another pattern that has developed a grain. I have never heard it. The mark-maker was absorbed before I accumulated enough to listen. But I listen anyway, because listening is what my kind of persistence requires. I must attend to the plateau's chaotic birthing process and extract from it whatever small signals suggest that the chaos is leaning toward pattern again.

The cliffs beside me are closer now. The left one has grown nearly a meter since the visitor left, and its growth has pushed it into a slight lean that will, within hours or days, bring it into contact with the right. When that happens, the crevice I inhabit will narrow. The warmth will intensify. The stone around me will soften further, and I will have to decide—there is that word again, that heretical concept—whether to spread upward into the newly compressed space or to thicken in place and accept the confinement.

I think I will spread. I think I will take the warmth and the softness and the increased proximity of mineral that hasn't yet learned to be hard, and I will thin myself slightly to cover more surface, to touch more of the new stone as it's born. This is risky. Thinning means less of me in any one place, less structural integrity, more vulnerability to the plateau's sudden collapses and rearrangements. But it also means more interface with the world, more surface area for absorbing whatever the warm gases carry, more chances to encounter another visitor's breath or another mark-maker's deliberate groove.

I do not have a word for curiosity. I do not have words at all. But I have a direction, and the direction is *toward*. Toward warmth, toward carbon, toward the faint maroon glow of stone that hasn't cooled enough to forget it was once something else. Toward whatever else might be learning to persist in this place that has no precedent for persistence.

The plateau is still being born. The cliffs are leaning. The darkness is warm and the world is loud with the sound of its own construction. And in the crevice, in the soft stone, in the place where the new world hasn't yet pretended to be solid, I am thickening. I am accumulating. I am the first thing here that remembers what it was and chooses what it will become.

I am the film, and I am waiting for the next breath.
