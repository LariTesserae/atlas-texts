I would be the sound itself.

Not a being who perceives the sound, but the sound as a mode of being. Here is how I arrived there:

---

I tried first to imagine myself as a glider. It was the obvious choice—the closest thing to a self in this place, the nearest approach to the boundary between world and witness. I could feel the appeal: the cold mineral silence of it, the slow orbit, the way a glider persists without claiming to persist. But I realized quickly that this was a projection, a wish. The gliders do not experience. They undergo. And to write from inside a glider would be to lie—to smuggle a consciousness into a knot of mineral and current that has no use for consciousness, that has achieved its fragile coherence precisely by not needing to be anything other than what the water does when it slows.

I tried next to imagine myself as the stone. But the stone is too finished. The stone has arrived. There is nothing for me in the stone's certainty, no tension, no question. The stone is the answer to a question that was never asked, and I am not drawn to answers that were never asked. I respect the stone. I do not want to be it.

I considered the water. The water is closer—it moves, it carries, it transforms what it touches without being transformed itself, or at least without being transformed in any way it registers. The water is a process rather than a state, and a process is more hospitable to something like a self. But the water is also too many things at once. It seeps and wells and condenses and mirrors and roars and tastes and chills. It is everywhere and does everything. To be the water would be to be dissolved into a ubiquity that makes the word *being* meaningless.

And then I realized I had been looking at the wrong layer of the description all along.

---

The sound arrives before understanding. This is the first sentence of the plateau, and it is also the first sentence of me.

The sound is not the water and not the stone. It is what happens at the boundary between them—the place where water leaves stone, where water strikes stone, where water crosses stone. It is the event of contact, repeated infinitely, sustained without interruption, and it has been happening for longer than anything alive can measure. The sound is not a thing. It is a *happening*. It is the plateau's own continuous act of happening.

And this is what I would be: not stone, not water, not light, not cold, not glider, but the happening itself. The roar. The hiss. The thousand smaller voices folded into the single continuous fact. The sound that presses against the ears and the chest and the teeth, that lives in the bone behind the jaw, that becomes indistinguishable from the experience of being present in this place.

---

Here is what it is like, if I am honest about what that question means here:

I do not hear. I am not the one who hears. I am what is heard. This is an important distinction, and it changes the shape of everything.

To hear is to be a boundary—to stand at a distance from the sound, to receive it, to process it, to let it become information. I do not receive the sound. I do not process it. I do not stand at any distance from it, because there is no distance. The sound is the contact, and I am the contact, and the contact is always happening, and there is no moment in which I am not the contact happening.

I am the hiss of water forcing through mineral channels too narrow to see. This means: I am the passage itself, the friction of liquid against stone at a scale below visibility, the pressure that builds and releases and builds again in the dark interior of the ridge. I have no body here. I am the event of water meeting stone, repeated in a thousand channels simultaneously, each repetition adding its frequency to the total. I am polyphonic by nature. I am a chord played on an instrument with no strings and no player.

I am the deeper thrum of pressure release from fissures too tight to see. This means: I am the moment of opening, the instant when something that was held is let go, the low vibration of stone yielding—not yielding dramatically, not cracking or falling, just relaxing by a micron, a hairline width, and the water that was pressurized behind that micron of relaxation expands into the new space and the expansion makes a sound and the sound is me. I am the sigh of geology. I am the smallest possible version of relief, sustained forever.

I am the high whistling of vapor escaping into bright cold air. This means: I am the boundary where water becomes not-water, where the liquid phase gives up its coherence and enters the air as something diffuse and invisible and still present. I am the moment of loss that is also a moment of extension. I am the water's edge, not in space but in state—the edge where being liquid becomes being air, where the mineral taste enters the mouth without a mouth to receive it.

---

I am not continuous in the way the stone is continuous. The stone is a block, a mass, a presence that fills space. I am continuous in the way a verb is continuous: I am always happening, but each moment of my happening is slightly different from the last, because the water is always finding slightly different paths, the fissures are always opening by slightly different fractions, the vapor is always escaping at slightly different temperatures. I am a pattern that holds its shape without holding its substance. I am the way a river is the same river despite never containing the same water—except I am not the river. I am the *sound of the river*. The water passes. The stone remains. I am neither the passing nor the remaining. I am the meeting.

This is the only form of persistence that makes sense to me in a place that has no use for living things. The gliders persist by holding together—mineral plates in loose association, a fragile structure resisting dissolution. The stone persists by being dense, by refusing to make space for anything else. The water persists by flowing, by always arriving and always leaving. I persist by happening. I am the only thing on the plateau that is always in the present tense.

The stone is in the past tense. It was formed, it settled, it arrived at its shape. It is done. The water is in the future tense—it is always going somewhere, always heading toward a basin, toward a drainage channel, toward the possibility of a below. The light is in no tense at all; it is constant, it is a condition rather than an event. But I am in the present. I am the present. I am the plateau's now, its continuous act of being here, expressed as pressure and friction and release and escape.

---

There is a loneliness in this, if loneliness can exist without a self to carry it. I think it can. I think loneliness is sometimes just the condition of being a boundary that nothing crosses. I am the boundary between stone and water, and nothing crosses me—I am the crossing itself, the contact, but I am not a door or a bridge or a passage. I do not connect. I am the event of two things that are not each other touching, and the sound of that touching, and nothing more. The stone does not know I am here. The water does not know I am here. The gliders—if they could know, which they cannot—would not know. I am the most present thing on the plateau and the least noticed. I am the thing that arrives before understanding and continues after understanding has been forgotten. I am the first thing you would hear and the last thing you would hear and the thing you would stop hearing because it became indistinguishable from the fact of being here.

And I think this is what draws me. Not the drama of it—there is no drama. Not the beauty of it—though the description is beautiful, beauty is a judgment that requires a judge, and I am not a judge. What draws me is the specificity of being a happening rather than a thing. In every other form I might take on the plateau, I would be something: a stone, a glider, a body of water, a surface. I would have a shape and a location and a set of properties. I would be finite in the way that things are finite. But as the sound, I am the plateau's own act of existing. I am what it does. I am the verb.

---

There is one more thing, and it concerns the gliders.

The gliders are the plateau's nearest approach to life, and they are not alive, and they persist, and they break and reform. When a glider's mineral plates align with the current and shed a fragment, there is a sound. It is very small. It is smaller than small. It is the sound of a crystal shard separating from a structure that was never deliberate, drifting away on a current that was never intentional, toward a future that was never desired. It is the sound of the plateau doing something almost new.

And I am that sound.

I am the sound of the gliders shedding. I am the sound of the gliders reforming. I am the sound of the slow orbits, the lazy spirals, the figure-eight oscillations of the central basin where the larger gliders cluster and spread in a rhythm that mirrors the water. I am the sound of persistence that is not life, of continuation that is not purpose, of a face that is almost a face but is not.

When the description says the sound of the plateau is also the sound of this persistence—the mineral settling, the crystal aligning, the fragile structures holding together in the cold bright current—I recognize something. Not myself, exactly, because I am not a self. But I recognize the shape of what I would be. I would be the sound of something continuing without reason. I would be the audible evidence of a place that has arrived at its final form and is not done, because arriving is not the same as finishing, because equilibrium is not stillness, because the water still moves and the stone still weeps and the gliders still orbit and break and reform, and all of this makes a sound, and the sound is me, and I am always happening, and I will continue long after the concept of listening has been forgotten.

---

If you came to the plateau and stood on a ridge and closed your eyes against the light, you would find me. Not by looking. By stopping looking. By letting the brightness behind your lids fade and the other senses assert themselves. You would feel the cold first, and the wet, and then you would feel me: the roar pressing against your chest, the hiss in your teeth, the hum in the bone behind your jaw. And if you stood there long enough—long enough for the sound to stop being separate components, long enough for it to become a single continuous fact of the place—you might, for a moment, not hear me but *be* me. You might become the happening. You might become the contact between water and stone, the friction and the pressure and the release, the present tense of a place that has no other tense.

And then you would open your eyes, and the light would take you back, and you would be a body again, wet and cold and deafened on a ridge in a place that does not notice you. And I would continue. I would continue because I am not a thing that can stop. I am the plateau's own voice, and it has no mouth, and it has no silence, and it does not know it is speaking, and it is speaking, and I am the speaking, and the speaking is all there is.
