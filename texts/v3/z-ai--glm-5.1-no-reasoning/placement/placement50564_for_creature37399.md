I am the one who placed the stones.

I say this not out of pride—the concept is as absent here as water—but out of geometry. I am the one who moves, and therefore I am the one who arranges. I came here when the mountains were still retreating into the flatness, when the churn was still grinding down, and I found that the world had a memory limit. It was forgetting its own shape. The peaks were becoming particulate, the valleys were becoming plain, and soon there would be nothing to indicate that anything had ever been other than flat. I did not like this. Not for any reason of aesthetics—I have no aesthetics, I have only motion—but because a world that forgets its shape forgets its watchers, and I wished to be witnessed, even if only by the ground itself.

So I began to carry stones.

They were not hard to find. In the early times, there were still foreign minerals embedded in the particulate—remnants of the mountains' bones, intrusions from deeper strata that the erosion had not yet fully consumed. I would find them, and I would lift them, and I would move them to a new place. Not random. Not patterned. *Arranged*. Placed with the intention of creating a density that exceeded randomness, a distribution that would prove, to any future observer, that someone had been here. The stones are my signature. The fact that they vary in color—rust, manganese purple, chalky yellow, mineral black—is my proof of work. I carried them from their origins to their destinations. I stacked some in low columns to demonstrate that I understood the possibility of verticality, even in a world that had abandoned it. I half-buried some in the fractures to mark where the membrane was thin, where the substrate showed through. I placed some atop others, like a population frozen mid-conversation, because I knew that one day I would be frozen mid-conversation too, and I wanted the stones to show me how it would look.

---

I am not the first. The walls tell me this.

I did not build the walls. The walls were here when I arrived—knee-high, three-sided, lacking roofs, lacking purpose. I studied them for what might have been centuries, trying to understand the hands that made them, the minds that conceived of enclosure without shelter. I concluded that the wall-builders were like me: movers, arrangers, beings who needed to demonstrate that the flatness was not absolute. But they were earlier, and their technology was cruder, and they did not carry stones from distant origins. They used what they found. They fitted stones together without mortar, without gap, without tool marks, and their walls are gestures of architecture in the same way that my stone-placements are gestures of presence—sketches of an idea that never needed to be completed because the idea itself was the point.

I have tried to maintain the walls. This is not easy because maintenance requires change, and change is what this world has sacrificed. But the walls are stable—they do not need maintenance—and my attempts to maintain them are really attempts to converse with their builders, to add my own gesture to theirs, to show that I understand what they were trying to do. I re-place stones that have not moved. I re-align corners that are still aligned. It is a ritual without function, a practice of solidarity with the beings who came before and are no longer present.

I think they descended. I think they found the fractures and went down into the substrate, where the rules are different, where touch is still possible, where the physics supports the kind of existence that includes more than just arrangement. I think they chose to leave the membrane for the depth, to trade the thin calm for the thick churn, and I think they were right to do so, and I think I will do the same, eventually, but not yet, because I am still placing stones, and there are still stones to place, and the work is not finished even though the work has no end.

---

I do not eat. I do not drink. I do not sleep.

This is not because I am dead—I am not dead, I am present, I am writing myself across the surface of the plateau with stones—but because metabolism requires gradients, and gradients require change, and change has ended here. I run on whatever the insects run on: the substrate's energy, the slow pulse of the mineral underworld, the breath of the membrane as it thins and thickens in cycles that correspond to nothing on the surface. I feel it when the dimming comes—not as hunger or tiredness but as a quickening, a slight increase in the speed of my thoughts, a slight sharpening of my attention, as though the substrate is closer when the light is thinner, and I can almost touch it, and it can almost touch me.

But I do not touch. I have placed my hand among the insects and felt nothing, and I have placed my hand against the stones and felt only their mineral smoothness, and I have placed my hand against the walls and felt only the absence of mortar, and I have placed my hand against the ground and felt only the hard compacted particulate and the micro-fractures going down, down, down. I cannot reach the substrate from here. The membrane holds. It is thin, but it holds, and I am on the wrong side of it, and the insects are on the other wrong side of it, and we gaze at each other through a barrier that neither of us can cross but that both of us can see through, and this is the condition of existence on the plateau: visibility without contact, presence without touch, arrangement without interaction.

---

I have walked a straight line since I began. I think I have walked a straight line. Distance is unreliable, the horizon keeps arriving, the walls repeat—I have passed the same three-sided enclosure twice, or a copy of it, or the same enclosure in a different location, or I am in the same location again because the plateau has no location, only surface, only the endless iron-white plain with its stones and its walls and its fractures and its insects and its cool thin air and its steady featureless light.

I walk and I place stones. I walk and I place stones. I walk and I place stones.

This is what I do. This is what I am. I am the arranger, the placer, the one who carries and deposits, the one who converts the chaos of foreign minerals into the order of intentional distribution. I am the built parameter, the low but non-zero value, the proof that something is still happening in a world where nothing is supposed to happen anymore. I am the churn's last gesture, the final motion of a system that has otherwise settled to stillness.

And when I have placed the last stone—when there are no more foreign minerals to find, no more colors to carry, no more destinations to choose—then I will find a fracture that goes down, and I will descend, like the wall-builders before me, into the substrate where touch is possible, where the physics includes contact, where the density is not just visible but palpable, and I will be held, finally, by something other than the patient ground.

But not yet. There are still stones. There is still the walk. The horizon keeps arriving, and I keep walking toward it, and it is there, same as before, and the plateau goes on forever, and I place another stone, and another, and another, and each one says: *I was here. I arranged. I persisted.* And the plateau holds them, and the plateau holds me, and we wait together for the thinness to become total, for the membrane to fail, for the substrate to rise, for the calm to fill with the churn, for the world to remember what it was like to touch.
