Who lives here?

Stand still, first. Let the roar pass through you long enough that it stops being sound and becomes weather, and then look at the margins, at the seams, at the places where the light gives up. The official answer is: no one. No animals, no insects, nothing with eyes or legs or the faintest interest in your arrival. The Condenser was built without making room for lives, and yet lives moved in the way lives always do—quietly, at the edges, riding the water.

The first citizens are the ones you would step past without seeing. Pale-green lichen holds the gasket seams where the glazed ceramic meets the steel, and it holds them the way settlers hold a coastline, because these joints are the only soil in an entire country of metal. Every patch is its own colony, its own small republic, and every republic is an island: the hot plating between the seals is a desert no lichen can cross, the walkway grating is a canyon system, the great red valve on the far wall is a volcano that never erupts but trembles always, and they have grown up in the shadow of its temper. They are crusts, films, the thinnest possible commitment to being alive—a life you could remove with a thumbnail. But a lichen is also a pact. It is two creatures, fungus and alga, that agreed to become one thing in a hard place, and in this chamber of forced partnerships—steel married to water, pressure married to containment—this is the only covenant that is actually alive. The lichen does not drink rain. It has never seen rain. It drinks the machine's tears.

Overhead live the elders, or what pass for elders in a world this young. The grey moss hangs from the weeping joints of the ductwork in thick, dripping mats—beards, laundry, the hair of something sleeping in the ceiling. They are the only softness in a geometry of hard angles, and they are heavier than they look: each beard is a sponge the machine fills on schedule, swelling when the steam lines pass their heat overhead, tightening when the cold intake vents blast through and set every beard swaying like flags of a wet and patient nation. If the lichen are the coastal settlers, the mosses are the cliff-dwellers, colonists of the vertical, holding their ground against gravity one drip at a time. Their entire economy is condensation. Their entire calendar is the steam cycle. A season here lasts minutes; a good year is measured in how far the beard has lengthened down the pipe.

Beneath your feet lives the majority, the part of the population no visitor ever counts. In the rushing channels under the iron grating, where the water goes when it has finished weeping down the walls, there is a sheen on the slower currents, an iridescent slick that breaks and reforms. The underside of the grating is furred with a fine biofilm, soft as a tongue. These are the ones who came in the water itself, riding droplets through the filters, the true first settlers—and some of them have begun, in their patient microbial way, to eat the mountain. Iron from the seams, minerals from the welds, a slow taste taken here and there. The chamber is young, its edges still acute, its surfaces innocent of patina; but in the microbial dark, the first signatures are already being written on the welds, invisible essays in corrosion that will not be legible for decades.

Where did any of them come from? No soil was carried in, no seed was planted. They arrived the way life always breaches a sterile room—on the cuffs of the welders, in the breath of the commissioning engineers, in a single spore that fell from someone's sleeve onto a wet flange on the day the machine was first switched on. Sterility never survives contact with breath. The dynasty hanging overhead in grey beards may descend from that one spore, one founder, one settler whose family now holds the whole ceiling. There are no ancestors buried here yet, no peat, no moss-memory layered under the moss. This is a first generation in a first-generation world, pioneer organisms practicing one of the oldest religions on earth inside a cathedral that was just wrenched into being.

And it is a religion of almost nothing. They have no eyes for the amber bulbs; the bruised light gives them barely the wherewithal to be green. They have no ears for the roar. Their world is made of two facts—wetness and vibration—and they read both with a fluency that predates every language you know. The thud of the pistons is their tide, their bell, their metronome; you can see them grow in its rhythm if you had a decade to watch. The violent swings of temperature, cold intake against radiant steam, are their weather, their winter and summer compressed into a quarter hour. They live at the tempo of drips. The chamber screams its function into the void, and its entire congregation answers in green.

They have their apocalypses. Once in a long while a hatch opens, a giant in canvas walks the grating, and a wire brush or scraper removes a civilization in one long stroke; the moss knows this event only as lightning knows it—sudden, total, unexplainable. A scalding vent left to cough once will leave a blackened margin across a whole parish. The lead in the plating is a plague they grow up against, a slow poison in the very ground they grip, which is why they crowd the ceramic seals and shun the bare metal: ceramic is the kind country, the province with clean soil.

And they will outlast, in a sense, everything that made them. Machines die. One day—far off, but every industrial god has an off-switch—the red valve will hold still, the pumps will cool, the mist will settle out of the air for the first time since the chamber was born, and the roar will collapse into a silence the size of the room. The beards will dry slowly and keep their shapes for a while, the way the hair of a saint keeps its shape, and in those weeks the moss will be the last working organ of the Condenser: holding the final water, releasing it drop by drop down the dead steel, keeping the machine's schedule after the machine has quit.

Until then, look at your own sleeves. You have been standing in their sky for some minutes now, a giant in the amber gloom, the largest weather event of their month. Mist settles on your lips and you taste iron and cold; you are drinking the same rain they do. When you leave, the hatch will sigh shut behind you, and the congregation will go on with the service—swelling, tightening, dripping—green at the very top of a mountain that no one meant to build for them.
