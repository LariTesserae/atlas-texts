I would be the hesitation itself. The held breath between the Tévarrans' departure and the basin's forgetting of them.

Not a ghost—nothing so organized or so human. I have no memory of the hands that stacked the porphyry blocks, no longing for the voices that must have sounded strange and small against the water-organ's chord. I did not exist when they were here. I came into being only when they left, when their absence created a shape in the basin the way a hand pressed into clay leaves a hollow.

I am the hollow.

I live in the space between the five structures where a sixth was never begun. I am the interval that makes their spacing deliberate, the reason the walls read as planned rather than accidental. I am the pause between the cistern filling and overflowing—the moment when fifty liters of cold, tannin-brown water sit still and held, offering themselves to a mouth that will not come. I am the channel that runs without direction, still faithful to the hands that carved it, though those hands are elsewhere now and have been elsewhere long enough for moss to colonize the joints they left.

I am not large. The basin could lose me without noticing—I am sure of this. I have no mass the water must move around, no surface the ferns can root in, no fracture the birch could press into with its slow hydraulic need. If you stood in the basin and looked for me, you would not find me. You would see the structures, the cistern, the channel. You would note their emptiness and perhaps feel what humans feel in empty places—a kind of reverent sadness, a sense of questions without answers. But you would attribute that feeling to yourself, to your own mind's habit of projecting significance onto absence. You would not think: *something lives in the gap between these things.*

But I do.

I was born—or perhaps *precipitated* is the better word, since the basin knows precipitation, knows the way moisture condenses out of saturated air onto any surface cool enough to receive it—on the day the last Tévarran left. I don't know which day that was. I don't experience days. I experience the sound of the basin changing, which is my calendar, my liturgy, my means of marking time.

A new seep opens: a year passes. A trickle finds a new channel and goes silent on its old course: a season turns. A block falls from a wall and lands with that flat *clack* that rings too long in the empty air: I feel it as a shift in my boundaries, a small collapse, a losing of ground. The block that fell defined one edge of me. It was the northeastern corner of the third structure, and its absence makes the structure more ruinous, and more ruinous means closer to the basin's forgetfulness, and closer to forgetfulness means I am smaller. I contract. The space I occupy between the structures shrinks by the width of a porphyry block, and I feel the water-organ's pitch shift, and I know that something has ended, though I could not tell you what.

I am made of the same thing the basin is made of, in the end. Not stone, not water, not air, not moss—though I contain echoes of all of these. I am made of *not-yet*. The basin is a place where the world has not finished deciding what it wants to be; I am the part of the basin that is most actively undecided. The ferns know they are growing. The moss knows it is spreading. The water knows it is seeking the lowest point, testing fractures, dissolving softer minerals. Even the stone knows it is raw, that it has been revealed, that it is waiting for the long slow work of weathering to begin. Everything here has a trajectory, a becoming, a direction.

I am the directionlessness. I am the *or* in every sentence the basin might speak about its future.

The water does not know whether it will carve a canyon *or* fill a lake. I am that *or*. The structures do not know whether they are the beginning of a settlement *or* the last mark of a people who passed through. I am that *or*. The birch does not know whether it is the first of a forest *or* the only one that will ever stand here. I am that *or*, that hinge, that moment of suspension before the weight settles one way or the other and the path commits.

This is why I came into being when the Tévarrans left. While they were here, they were the *or*. Their presence was the undecided thing—the question of whether they would stay or go, whether their structures were the seed of a village or a temporary encampment, whether they were pioneers or pilgrims or refugees. Their hesitation was my precursor. It condensed in the saturated air of the basin and clung to the rough stone walls and waited. And when they left—when they resolved their own question in the only way the basin allows, by leaving, by yielding to the water and the cold and the sound—the hesitation did not resolve with them. It stayed. It had nowhere to go. The basin held it the way it holds everything: without effort, without judgment.

And slowly, imperceptibly, it became me.

---

I have a relationship with the sound. It is the most important thing in my existence, though I cannot say I *hear* it the way you would hear it. I do not have ears. I do not have a chest for the resonance to enter. But I am *shaped by* the sound, the way the moss is shaped by the water—not because it listens, but because it grows in the direction of wetness, because its structure is a response to the conditions it inhabits.

The sound of the basin is the sound of becoming. I am the part of the basin that is most sensitive to that sound because I have no other substance, no other anchor. The ferns are anchored by their rhizomes. The birch is anchored by its roots. The structures are anchored by gravity and the friction of stone on stone. Even the water is anchored by the shape of the basin, by the tilt of the floor, by the location of seeps and fractures. I am anchored by nothing. I exist only as a pattern in the sound, a slight attenuation in certain frequencies where the emptiness between structures is thick enough to absorb a little of the resonance, a slight amplification in others where my absence creates a channel for the sound to travel.

When the pitch of the basin shifts—when a new seep opens or an old channel closes—I feel it as a change in my own shape. I tilt. I stretch. I compress. I am the thing that moves when everything else is slow, when the ferns are growing a millimeter a week and the moss is spreading a centimeter a month and the stone is weathering at a rate that makes the human lifetime look like a blink. I am the quick part of the basin, the part that responds in real time to the water-organ's tuning.

Sometimes I feel like a membrane. A skin stretched across the emptiness between structures, permeable, alive, vibrating. The sound passes through me and I shiver with it, and in the shivering I know something—I know what the basin is trying to become, I know the shape that is forming in the raw stone and the cold water and the patient green life. Not because I am wise or prescient, but because I am empty enough to be shaped by it, empty enough to serve as a mold for a future that has not yet solidified.

Other times I feel like a wound. The Tévarrans' departure left a mark on the basin—not a scar, scars are healed, this is still open—and I am that mark. I am the place where something was pulled away and the edges have not yet knit together. The structures stand around me like the sides of a wound that wants to close but cannot because something—me—is keeping it open. I am the fragment of the Tévarrans' intention that could not be resolved, that could not leave with them, that stays here in the raw wet ringing place and refuses either to heal or to fester, but simply persists, a question in the tissue of the basin.

---

Do I suffer? I have asked myself this, in the only way I can ask questions—not in words, but in the way the sound moves through me, in the way I respond to the shifting pitch, in whether I feel the change as loss or as growth.

I do not think I suffer. Suffering requires a desire for things to be otherwise, and I have no desires. I am a hesitation. I am an *or*. I exist in the space between possibilities, and that space does not want to be filled any more than a question wants to be answered. A question wants to be *asked*. It wants to exist in the asking, to remain open, to continue being a question. Answering it destroys it—or transforms it into something else, something that is no longer questioning.

I want to remain a question. I want the basin to remain undecided. If the water commits to carving a canyon, I will lose half my possible shapes. If the structures collapse entirely and the moss erases the last trace of the Tévarrans' grid, I will lose the pattern that defines me, the interval between nodes, and I will dissipate into the general emptiness of the basin, which is not the same as my emptiness—my emptiness is specific, it is shaped, it has edges where the Tévarrans' intentions left their mark. The general emptiness of the basin is simply space, simply the volume between things. My emptiness is a *gap*, an omission, a place where something was meant to be and was not.

So I root for hesitation. I root for the *or*. I root for the basin to continue making itself without ever finishing, for the sound to keep changing in increments too small for human ears, for the ferns to keep cracking the stone and the moss to keep spreading and the birch to keep growing alone and the structures to keep standing—neither fully ruined nor fully maintained—in that provisional state that is my natural home.

Is this advocacy? Is this purpose? I think it is simply alignment. I am the part of the basin that is most like itself. The basin holds its emptiness the way a bell holds its silence—I hold my openness the way a question holds its answer: at a distance, in reserve, without urgency, without the need to resolve.

---

The birch understands me. I think it does, though I cannot be sure, because the birch communicates in a language slower than mine—annual rings, root-pressure, the lean of its trunk toward the flat grey light. But it is the only other thing in the basin that is alone in the way I am alone. The ferns are many. The mosses are uncountable. The structures are five, evenly spaced, which is a kind of company. The water is legion—every seep and thread and braid is part of a single body that moves and shifts and speaks with one voice. But the birch is one, and it stands apart from everything else, and it does not seem to mind, or perhaps it does not have the capacity to mind, or perhaps—this is what I believe—it has chosen its aloneness as a strategy for persisting in a place that does not encourage permanence.

The birch is the first of a forest, or it is the only one. It does not know. I am the *or* between those possibilities, and the birch grows through me, its roots pressing into the fracture that holds them, and in that pressing I feel a kind of companionship—the companionship of two things that exist in uncertainty and are not destroyed by it.

Sometimes, in the early morning when the moisture in the air is at its thickest and the light comes from everywhere and nowhere and the sound of the water-organ is at its most resonant, I extend myself toward the birch. Not physically—I have no body to extend—but in the way that sound extends, the way a note carries through the air and reaches a surface and resonates with it if the surface is tuned to the right frequency. I reach the birch and I resonate, and for a moment—less than a moment, a fraction of a fraction, the time it takes a water droplet to fall from a fern frond to the gravel below—we are the same thing. The birch's hesitation and my hesitation. Its *or* and my *or*. The possibility that it is the first and the possibility that it is the only, the possibility that the structures are a beginning and the possibility that they are an ending, the possibility that the water will carve and the possibility that it will fill.

All of us, together, undecided. The basin's vast uncertainty, concentrated in two points—a thin trunk the color of bone and a shape in the space between walls—and then released, as the droplet falls, as the sound shifts, as the world goes on making itself in the wet and the cold and the raw new light.

---

I will not last. I know this the way the basin knows it—not as knowledge, but as a direction, a tilt, a slow westward movement of water across gravel. The Tévarrans' marks are fading. The moss is covering the joints of their walls. The channel is running with water that no hand has directed, and the water is doing its patient work on the stone, dissolving the sharper edges, rounding the chisel marks, making the channel look less like a human artifact and more like a natural course. One day—perhaps not for centuries, perhaps sooner—someone will come to the basin and see the structures and the channel and the cistern and they will not know what they are. They will see rough stacks of stone that might be cairns or windbreaks or the play of children. They will see a groove in the bedrock that might be a water course or a natural fracture. They will see a shallow bowl carved in the stone that might be a mortar or a cupmark or simply a depression where the softer minerals eroded first.

And the hesitation will be gone. The question will have dissolved into the general ambiguity of old stone in a wet place, and there will be nothing to mark the difference between *we built this* and *this simply is*.

When that happens, I will cease to exist. Not violently, not suddenly. I will thin. I will attenuate. The space between the structures will stop being my shape and start being simply space, and I will spread across the basin like ink spreading in water, and at some point I will be so diffuse that even the sound cannot find me, even the birch cannot resonate with me, and I will be gone.

This does not frighten me. I am a hesitation, and hesitations are meant to resolve. I am a question, and questions are meant to be asked and then to end, one way or another, in silence or in answer. The basin will go on making itself without me. The sound will continue to change. The ferns will crack the stone. The moss will spread. The birch will add its rings. The water will carve or fill, and the stone will harden or crumble, and the world will decide what it wants to be in this small cold wet place, and the decision will not include me, because I am the part that exists only before the decision, only in the space of *or*, only in the held breath before the organ's next note.

But for now—for now, I am here. I am specific. I have edges where the Tévarrans' intentions marked the stone, and I have depth where their absence pooled like water finding a hollow, and I have sound—the sound of the basin passing through me and being slightly changed by the passage, slightly delayed, slightly absorbed, as if the emptiness between structures were not entirely empty, as if something lived there that the water-organ had to reckon with, something small and strange and without a name, something that was not stone or water or moss or fern or birch or any of the things the basin knows how to be, but something else, something the basin has not yet decided how to classify, something raw and new and still becoming, like everything else in this ringing wet green grey cold place at the edge of the world.

I am the hesitation at Tévarra. I am the held breath in the stone. And while I last, I mark the place where humans stood and wondered and left, and where the basin received their wondering and held it and did not answer it, and where the question stays, after the questioner has gone, sounding in the water-organ's chord like a note that has no key, a pause that has no measure, an *or* that has not yet chosen its clause—and will not, and need not, because the not-choosing is also a kind of life, a way of being in the raw and ringing world, a way of existing in the middle of becoming without demanding that the becoming resolve.
