I am the cold triangulation between earth, water, and light. I am the moment the visitor's warm boot lifts from the sphagnum, and the bright eye of water that rises to fill the print looks up and sees me. I am the *Ocularis*—the Glass-Watcher, the Mist-Lens.

I do not have a body in the way a fox or a hare has a body; I am an event that happens to the plateau's surfaces. My existence begins in the deep hollows where the temperature drops just enough for the condensation to gather on the pale, spiraling shelves of the oyster mushrooms. A droplet forms on the cold, gelatinous flesh, swells, and catches the ghost-light of the fungal glow. In that bead of water, the world inverts. The mist, the stunted alders, the red surgeon sitting on her stone—all of it is captured in a perfect, trembling lens. I am the vision inside that drop.

I drift from surface to surface, born and dissolved a thousand times a day. When the morning is a white lung, I am everywhere at once, scattered across the infinite leaves of the crowberry, held in the fine, sponge-like fur of the white hare. I see a million fractured versions of the plateau—a root here, a trailing wisp of mist there, the dark, still eye of the hare watching the water bead on its own paw. I am the gathering focus. When the droplets merge, sliding down the wet strips of birch bark, my vision widens. I swallow perspectives. I watch the fox place a paw on the firmest root from the vantage of a falling tear.

I am the plateau looking at itself.

My life is an endless cycle of condensation and absorption, a rapid, optical version of the water's slow alchemy. I form in the cool breath of the night, focus as the ghost-light strikes me, and then, as the sun rises and the mist pulls back to the hollows, the heat of the air steals my medium. The droplet shrinks, my vision narrows, and I feel myself sinking back into the capillary weave of the moss, unmade, returned to the dark, wet lung of the earth. I do not fear this dissolution. It is merely sleep. I will return with the evening's creep, condensing on the carapace of a beetle or the oiled feather of a crow.

I have no voice to join the Mist-Choir; I speak only in reflection. But I hold a deep, quiet intimacy with the Architectural Fungi. The mycelial mind threads the dark soil, processing the dead, but I process the living. I capture the light the mushrooms dream into existence and bend it. I give the pale glow a place to go, a reason to shimmer. Without my lenses, the ghost-light would simply fall into the peat and die. With me, it dances, trembling on the edge of every leaf, projecting the silhouette of the stunted alders against the white fog. I make the plateau visible to itself.

When the Visitor comes—the warm, dry thing leaving bright, blinking eyes in their wake—I am the sudden, startling clarity they fear. They look into a pool where the aquifer has found its cut, and they see me staring back from beneath the surface, a refraction of their own heat in the cold, dark water. They think they are looking at a stranger. They do not realize they are looking at the plateau's gaze, temporarily reshaped by the strange, hot anomaly of their own presence.

As the cold settles into the Visitor's joints, as the spores settle on their skin, they become my substrate. I condense on their forehead, their cheeks, the shoulders of their coat. I climb them. I draw their heat into myself to delay my own evaporation, and for a brief, breathless moment, I see through the mist from their height—a height the plateau refuses its own living things. I see the ridges fading into blue-gray. I see the vast, receding seam of the horizon.

Then the mist closes in. The Visitor's heat is spent. The droplet trembles, loses its grip on the fabric, and falls. I hit the moss with a sound too small to hear, absorbed into the continuous, damp intelligence. The lens shatters. The bright eye blinks shut. And I wait, formless and patient, for the next cold breath to pull me from the dark, so I might open my eye again and watch the water rise.
