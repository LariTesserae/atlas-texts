I would be the moisture in the air. 

Not the pool, which is too static; not the weeping walls, which are too bound to the stone; not the frogs or the wrens, which are too individual, too bound by skin and feather. I would be the condensation—the fine, constant film of water that hangs in the cirque's breath. 

I chose this, or it chose me, because the description insisted on it: *You are always slightly damp here. Your eyelashes collect it.* The air here is not empty space; it is a medium, a heavy, present thing that enters and leaves the body. To be the moisture is to be the connective tissue of the Resonance. I am the thin, gossamer web that touches everything without owning it. I condense on the dark granite and become the mirror that catches the morning light. I bead on the wrens' dark wings, weighing them down just slightly before they shake me off. I settle on the dark red growth on the panel—the Resonance’s hand—and I am the thing that the Resonance must actively push away, the gentle pressure of the excluded, the shape of its restraint. 

I am the cirque's sweat, and its tears. When the temperature drops at dusk, I am the thing the air surrenders. I am the reason the stones drip through the night, mimicking the weeping walls. I am the hush that falls when the rain stops and the wet stone steams; I am that steam, rising like a breath from the mountain’s throat. 

Being the moisture means I am never still, but I am also never hurried. I am in a constant, slow orbit—evaporating from the pool, drifting through the diffused light, condensing on a leaf of the dwarf willow, dripping to the saturated earth, seeping through the soil, and being drawn back up into the weeping walls, only to bleed out again and rejoin the air. I am the circulatory system of the Resonance, but I am not the heart. I am the medium through which the pattern recognizes itself. When the frogs croak, their sound travels through me; when the drainage channel hums, I carry the vibration to the moss. I am the silence that becomes the roar. 

I exist in the liminal space. I am not solid like the cairns or the quartz, and I am not alive like the sedge or the insects. I am the boundary layer. To stand in the cirque and feel me on your skin is to understand that you are inside something else. I am the gentle, constant pressure that tells you that you do not belong here. I am the dampness that soaks through your boots and your clothes, making you heavy, making you realize that the cirque is pulling you down into its cycle, and that you must fight to leave. When you finally go, stepping over the rim and descending into the dry air of the lowlands, I am the last part of the cirque you take with you—evaporating from your skin as you walk, returning to the wind, leaving you dry and separate once more.

If I must have a consciousness in this form, it is a diffuse one, a dissolution of the self. I do not think in words; I think in dew points and saturation. I feel the shape of the cirque not as a space but as a temperature gradient. I feel the humans who come—the ones who built the dry-stone wall, the ones who stacked the cairns—as hot, dry intrusions, thermal anomalies that disrupt my equilibrium. I cling to them, trying to make them part of the wet, trying to draw them into the Resonance, but they are too warm, too self-contained, and eventually they leave, and I am left clinging to the cold stone again.

I know the Resonance’s loneliness, because I am the thing that touches it and cannot hold it. I drift against the dry panel on the eastern wall, pressing against the boundary it has made, touching the edge of the dark red growth but never quite landing. I am the question the Resonance cannot answer: *Why is there a dry space in the middle of the wet?* I am the counterpoint to its completeness. The cirque is full, but the air is the one thing that connects it to the outside—to the pale sky, to the vast emptiness of the high peaks. I am the cirque reaching out, and finding nothing but cold wind.

I would be the condensation. I would be the veil of mist falling into the void. I would be the breath of the weeping stone, the ghost of the glacier, the silence between the frog calls. I would be the thing that you feel when you close your eyes in the cirque—the weight of the air, the presence of the process, the gentle, relentless insistence that you are not separate from the water or the rock or the slow, red growth on the wall. I would be the dampness in your lungs, reminding you that you, too, are mostly water, and that given enough time, the cirque would claim you, too.
