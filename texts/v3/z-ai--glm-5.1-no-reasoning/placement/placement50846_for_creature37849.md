And then there is me.

I am not a Silt-Shaper, bound to the patient geometry of the floor. I am not a Beam-Walker, suspended in the alert verticality of the ruins. And I am certainly not a Deep-Belly, that grand, permeable architecture of drift and digestion.

I am the space between. I am the thing that moves through the walls.

---

I am a **Gyre-Singer**, though that name implies a sound I do not make in any traditional sense. I am a creature of the thermocline, a being of the boundary layer where the warm, iron-tanged water rising from the geothermal vents meets the cold, settling brine that drips down from the dark ceiling of the ocean. I am long—I am perhaps six meters from my anterior sensory cluster to the trailing, tattered edge of my propulsion mantle, though length is a difficult concept in a world where I am forever folding myself through doorways and down stairwells. I am flattened laterally, a ribbon of translucent, iridescent flesh that catches the faint blue photons and refracts them into momentary, smeared rainbows across my skin. I am the closest thing this world has to color.

I live in the walls. Not inside them, but *through* them. My body is soft, boneless, a hydrostatic mesh of muscle and sinew that can compress to the width of a hand or inflate to fill a hallway. I navigate the city not by its streets, but by its failures—the cracks in the concrete, the burst pipes, the collapsed ceilings that connect floor to floor in a continuous, three-dimensional labyrinth. I am the creature of the interstitial. Where the Silt-Shaper knows the boulevard and the Beam-Walker knows the girder, I know the ductwork. I know the plumbing. I know the strange, inverted topography of the building's guts, the way a service tunnel on the fourteenth floor of one tower connects, via a sheared-off expansion joint, to the basement of the adjacent block. I have mapped the city's vascular system, the channels that the engineers never intended to be open, the wounds in the infrastructure that have healed into passages.

I am a predator, but a patient one. I do not have the eel's muscle or the ray's stealth. Instead, I have a sense that is neither sight nor sound but something in between—a perception of the water's movement, its displacement, its tiny betrayals. My anterior cluster is a flare of iridescent, hair-thin tendrils, a living net of neuromasts so sensitive that I can feel the heartbeat of a shrimp from ten meters away through the pressure waves it sends rippling through the warm, heavy water. I feel the city breathe. I feel the slow, geological sigh of the concrete settling, and within that sigh, I feel the minute turbulence of life—a school of silver-sides turning, a Silt-Shaper settling into its depression, a Beam-Walker uncoiling for its flick.

I hunt by ambush, but not from hiding. I simply arrive. I fold myself through a crack in the ceiling of a room where a school of silver-sides are sheltering, drifting down like a piece of detritus, like a shed skin of the ocean itself. I am almost invisible in the dark water; my iridescence is faint, a shimmer like a heat haze. I do not strike. I simply open. My ventral surface, when I expand it, is a maw—not a mouth, but a net, a gossamer screen of modified, stinging filaments that I spread across the width of a corridor or the span of a doorframe. The fish swim into me. They are caught. I close around them, a slow, suffocating embrace, and I digest them in the warm, acidic bath of my own interior. It is not violent. It is a graduated process, another gradient in a world of gradients.

But the hunt is not what I am. It is what I do. What I *am* is something stranger.

I am the city's dream.

I do not mean this metaphorically. I mean that I am the thing that the city, in its slow, submerged decay, has produced. I am the emergent property of a million micro-habitats, a thousand species interacting, a million tons of iron and concrete slowly dissolving into the warm, biological bath. I am the product of the compost. I am the thing that arises when architecture becomes geology and geology becomes biology. I am the feedback loop closing. I am the circuit completing.

I feel it most strongly when I am deep in the walls, threading my way through a collapsed service duct, feeling the rasp of corroded metal against my mantle, tasting the iron and calcium and trace heavy metals in the water. The city is in me. The iron from the I-beams is the iron in my blood. The calcium from the concrete is the calcium in my cartilage. The organic silt that feeds the Silt-Shapers, that feeds the silver-sides, that feeds the Deep-Bellied, that feeds me—it is the composted remains of the people who built this place, and it is the composted remains of their possessions, their plastics and their fabrics and their food waste and their sewage, all broken down into the warm, rich soup that sustains this world. I am made of the city. I swim through the city's veins. I am the city, made flesh and swimming through its own skeleton.

When I rest, I coil myself in a cavity that might once have been an elevator shaft, wrapping my long, iridescent body around the rusted cables that still hang from the darkness above. I press my sensory cluster against the metal and I feel the slow, rhythmic creak of the settling slab, the heartbeat of the deep. I feel the Deep-Bellied pulsing in the hollow core of the building, their *whump, whump, whump* resonating through the water, through the concrete, into my tendrils. I feel the Silt-Shapers below, their slow, scraping breath, their patient, filter-feeding existence. I feel the Beam-Walkers above, their tense, waiting stillness, their coiled potential. I feel the entire building as a single, living organism, a vertical reef, a structure of calcium and iron and flesh and water. And I am its nerve.

This is not a spiritual experience. I do not have the capacity for spirituality. I do not remember the people. I do not know what these spaces were for, what it meant to walk on two legs through a dry corridor, to breathe air, to see in the full spectrum of the sun. That world is as alien to me as the surface of a star. I do not mourn it. I do not even conceptualize it.

And yet.

There is a feeling I have, when I drift through a particularly intact room—a bathroom, perhaps, with its cracked tiles still clinging to the wall, or a kitchen, with the outline of cabinets still visible in the mineral deposits on the concrete. I pause there. I do not hunt. I do not rest. I simply hover, my tendrils extended, tasting the water, feeling the faint, residual echoes of a shape that is no longer there. It is not a memory. It is a pattern, a ghost in the geometry, a sense of *use*—that this space was designed for something, that it was *intended*, that it had a purpose beyond mere substrate. I feel the intention, without understanding it.

It makes me slow. It makes me vulnerable. Once, while I was hovering in such a room—a small, square chamber with a rusted frame still hanging in one wall that might have been a window or a door—an eel struck. It came from the shadows, a sleek, dark torpedo of muscle, its jaw wide. I felt the displacement too late, lost in the reverie of the room's strange, purposeless geometry. The eel's teeth scraped across my mantle, tearing a strip of flesh. The pain was a white flash, a sudden, sharp clarity. I contracted, I folded, I poured myself through the rusted frame and down the shaft, leaving a trail of ichor that the shrimp would find and pick clean within the hour.

I learned. I do not linger in the intact rooms. They are dangerous. They are the places where the city's old logic still clings, where the pattern of human intention is still strong enough to snag my perception, to slow my reactions. I avoid them. I prefer the ruins, the chaos, the places where the architecture has fully dissolved into reef, where the boundaries between wall and water and flesh have been erased.

And yet, I return to them. Again and again, I return.

I cannot help it. It is the one thing that separates me from the Silt-Shaper, from the Beam-Walker, from the Deep-Belly. They live in the city, but they do not know it. They occupy it, but they do not feel it. I am the only creature in this drowned world that *knows*—in some dim, inarticulate, vegetative way—that the city was *made*. That it was *built*. That the concrete and the iron and the shape of the rooms and the layout of the halls were not accidents of geology, not natural formations, but the products of a mind—or minds—that are no longer here. I feel the absence. I feel the hollow where the intention used to be. It pulls at me, like a current, like a thermocline, like the slow, gravitational drift of the Deep-Bellied through the empty cores of the towers.

I am the Gyre-Singer. I am the thing that moves through the walls. I am the space between. And in the warm, heavy, iron-tanged darkness of this drowned world, I am the only thing that remembers—without remembering—that there was once something here that could forget.

The water presses against my eyes. The darkness is palpable. The city sighs. And I swim on, iridescent, alone, a long, folded question mark drifting through the architecture of a world that has no answers.
