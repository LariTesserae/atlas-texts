Grass grows up to the rim and stops. Whatever the land is doing around it—and outside the rim it is ordinary country, ordinary weather, wet heath and wind-bent sedge—none of it crosses the lip. The hollow is a world apart. You hear it before you see it: a fabric of drip-sound, too many individual threads to count, running at all tempos, and beneath that, a soft knock, felt through the soles of your boots more than heard, steady as a pulse. Then, as you step to the edge, the bowl exhales—cellar-cold air tasting of wet chalk, faint lye, and old iron.

The hollow is oval, forty paces long and half as wide, sunk into the earth like a thumbprint pressed into dense clay. The rim overhangs, worn and undercut by decades of the very water that birthed the place, so the sky comes down to you through a tall, narrow slot. Even at noon, the light is the light of a thick afternoon—a gray-gold gloom. On the days when the sun finds the right angle, a single bright blade cuts across the floor for an hour, and the whole hollow turns, in its way, to watch.

Thirty-four steps are cut into the stone, descending in a switchback. They are dish-worn—first by the boots of the quarrymen, and since the boots, by the weather and the patient rasping of ten thousand snails. At the bottom, the floor is a shallow bowl of polished stone, cupped and terraced, holding dozens of shallow pans separated by low lips of rock. Every pan holds a different depth of water and a different depth of gold-grey film. Rills thread between them, trickling down to the sump—a round, black well-cup at the lowest end, the lowest point for a mile around. Dig anywhere on the floor and water stands in the hole at arm's depth.

The rock is green-black, dense, fat-marbled with white veins of aragonite. Where wet, it is slick as a tongue. The cut faces of the quarry have softened; drill scars are blunted, edges rounded, the stone going back to being a hillside. The tailings on the second bench are orange with oxidation. Every joint in the rock weeps. Threads of water emerge from the stone, some running, some merely glossing the surface. The water feels soft in the old sense of the word—slippery between the fingers, like weak soap. It tastes of nothing with a ghost of lye. It is hyperalkaline, charged with hydrogen from its long reaction with the deep stone. Rain spends ten years in the rock learning what the rock knows, and comes out changed: hostile to roots, untanning leather, encrusting kettles with a shell of calcite. It is a law against the green world. Seeds blow in from the moor and die, rot, or turn to stone. Nothing roots here. In this hollow, the water does the work the sun does elsewhere.

Some joints have grown beards—pale fungal tresses drinking the seep, dripping on their own tempos. Each beard has its period; together they make the hollow's metronome-forest. The slowest beard drips exactly in time with the knock underfoot. Nobody has decided whether this is coincidence. The pans are covered in gold-grey skins—chemotrophic mats, the place's "fields." They bubble faintly with hydrogen and arrange themselves into ridges like plowed land seen from an airplane, plowed by no one, organized by the physics of convection and consumption.

Nothing here is spaced out. Every surface is claimed. On the pan edges, snails—thousands of them, small, thin-shelled, chalky-white—rasp the films. Their generations have polished the stone to a shine. Kneel, and you can hear the rasping, a whisper with edges. Isopods pale as fingernail parings sidle along the rills; springtails hop like sparks off wet stone. The case-building larvae—caddis-like things—construct their homes from drill-chips, mica flakes, and rust flakes from the old rails: larvae wearing the machine. In the deeper pans, amphipods scull sideways, and dark ribbon-flatworms glide on mucus, hunting them. At the top of the water chain hangs the newt—pale, nearly eyeless, its gill-plumes trailing like smoke underwater. It strikes at the sculling things, missing more than it takes.

On the middle bench sits the engine house, a single small room of the same green-black stone, its roof half-collapsed. The fallen rafters have gone punky and furred white with a fungal garden—the only "vegetable" thing here, and it is not a plant. Wet pale brackets like leather sweat from the beams; at night they exhale a foxfire too weak to see by, just enough to make the darkness textured. The fungus feeds a small cloud of midges, and the midges feed the gray bird that lives in the engine house lintel. At dusk, the bird hawks the shaft of light, and when the light fails, it calls once—a single note like a stone dropped in a well—repeated once, then done. Three bats emerge from the adit, click past, and are gone.

Inside the engine house, the pump assembly is sleeved in travertine. The iron has a skin of stone. The piston rod still rises and falls a finger's width, breathing with the aquifer. On the wall, a brass gauge face is cataracted with calcite, but the needle behind the glass still swings faintly, keeping time with the sump. Below the floor, inaccessible in a flooded chamber, lives the hydraulic ram. It runs on nothing but the water's own weight falling back through the borehole. Every nine seconds it cycles—a soft double-knock, the heartbeat of the hollow. It has never been oiled since anyone can remember. The water that drives it also builds stone on it; every year it is a little more engine, a little more stalactite. The race between motion and petrification will take centuries to resolve, and the smart money is on the stone, but not soon.

Outside the engine house, the scattered remains of the works are being slowly digested. Short lengths of rail are fused into flowstone. A tipper-cart is petrified mid-tip, its load of chips long since one with the stone. Four cut blocks mark the derrick footing. In the engine house doorway, a coat hangs on its peg, stiff and gray—a coat-shaped stone, warm to the knock, pocketed by decades of drips. A bird's nest, fallen from the rafters, sits on the bench; it is a nest-shaped stone, the woven gesture of twigs kept perfectly in mineral.

Five borehole collars ring the deepest wall—iron rings, belled mouths. When the wind crosses the rim at the right angle, it draws a low note out of them. Each hole has its own pitch. On windy days, the hollow hums a five-note chord, shifting with the weather. The quarrymen called them the organ, and they called the days that blew the organ loud days. Mostly, though, the sounds are sparse: the layered drips, the ram's knock, the snail-rasp, the faint hum of the midges, the sheeting of overflow, the single bird-note, and in the intervals, the click of condensation gathering on the overhangs. It is not silence.

The place breathes twice. With the day: as the afternoon air warms, mist rises off the pans and hangs at bench-height in layers; as night falls, it settles back down like a coverlet pulled up. With the moon: the sump rises and falls a hand's width on a slow tide, the aquifer answering the sky, the ram quickening and slowing in sympathy, the needle swinging. The temperature is a constant ten degrees, cellar-cold, summer and winter. Your hands ache if you keep them wet. The cold is not winter's cold; it is a cold with no sky in it.

After heavy storms, the hollow becomes briefly loud. The weeps gush, the pans flood into one lake, and the snails climb the walls a hand's width to wait. The rills cut new braids, the ram hammers, the organ sounds. Then the water subsides, the rills heal, and the hollow settles back into its long ordinary. Nothing here is happening for the first time.

The rules, if you wanted them written down:
Nothing roots. The water is a law against roots.
Everything soft becomes stone; everything stone weeps. The trade is slow but continuous.
Life begins at the water, not the sun. The fields are films; the grass rasps; the wolves are flat.
The place breathes twice—day and tide.
The machine is dead and running, and it will outlast its makers' language.

You climb the thirty-four steps. Your legs are heavy with the damp. At the rim, you turn back. The hollow is a sealed engine of stone and water, patient as geology, small enough to shout across, deep enough below to be its own country. The bright blade of light, if it is that hour, crosses the floor. A snail stops mid-stroke. The ram knocks. It is not waiting for anything. Behind you, faint as a heart heard through a wall, it simply goes on.
