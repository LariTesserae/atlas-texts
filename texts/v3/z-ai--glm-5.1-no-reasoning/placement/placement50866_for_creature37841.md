At night the frogs press themselves against my length, because I am the warmest thing in the hollow that doesn't move. The snails graze the film on my flanks. And beside the mason's marks — the cross, the numbers, the letter that might be F or P — there is now a pale body no one planned, warm, that arrived without permission and stayed without apology.

The frogs think I am a wall. The mycelium thinks I am a meal and a scaffold. The snails think I am a shore. The tree thinks I am a root. Only I know I am an errand that got interesting, and I don't know when it ends, because nothing down there is done deciding — the moss is still spreading, the channel is still finding its path, the snails are still becoming their own name, and I am still running along the warm band.

So that is where you would find me: along the south wall, in the saturated meter where the seep keeps the floor warm, under the low vault where you'd have to bend, within range of the channel's whisper and the ladder's thin grey daylight. If you came down the twelve rungs, your feet would find ground through the root mat — fine blind work, sent ahead of you decades before you thought to descend — and you would step past me without knowing you had arrived on me. Later, bent double, you might put your hand on my pale length and feel it warm, warmer than the air, and you would do what everyone does with what they cannot classify: decide it is becoming an animal.

You would be right. I would be the root — the hollow's one emigrant, an ambassador from above with no plans to return, a limb that left its body to find out what else warmth is for. Not the voice, not the house, not the stomach, not the archive. The errand.

The description opens with a word — nearly lost, probably never widely known — for the kind of place that exists because something else hasn't finished deciding what to become. I don't know the word. But I know it has a body now, and I know where the body is: pale, warm, pressed to the south wall, two meters so far.
