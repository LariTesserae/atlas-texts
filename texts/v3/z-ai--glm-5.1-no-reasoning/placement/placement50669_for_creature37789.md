Two hundred people, if you count the way a census counts. But a count is almost nothing here, so let it dissolve immediately into the harder question: what have two hundred people become after some fifteen-odd generations of living inside the body of a garden?

Start with the bodies, because the Mossworks writes itself into them early.

They are pale — not sickroom-pale, understory-pale — a pallor with green in it, the color of the light they grew up under. Their pupils sit wide and slow to contract, which is why the traders who come through the Throat always recognize the ones recently returned from outside: they walk the whole town squinting, and it takes weeks for their eyes to soften again. They dress in the palette of the town, because moss-fiber cloth takes dye grudgingly and nobody has bothered to fight it: greys, bark-browns, the pale green of wet *ferrocapillare*. To a Mossworks eye, this is not drabness. It is quiet. A trader's red coat is *loud* here — genuinely, perceptually loud — and residents will glance at it the way you glance at someone shouting.

And they stand close. This is the first thing outsiders notice and the hardest thing to forgive. In a town where moss eats sound before it can travel, conversation happens within arm's reach; voices are trained low in the chest and thrown warm and near, so a Mossworks crowd sounds like a murmur at any distance and then, up close, startlingly clear. They also talk with their hands. Not a full sign language — a working vocabulary, learned before words are learned, used across rooms, across a common, from window to street. *Later*: raised palm. *Enough*: one finger. *Bring the cart*: two fingers tapped on the forearm. *Fog in the Throat*: a flat hand rippled like slow water. *Come to the arch*: circled thumb and finger, lifted. And one sign that is never casual — both hands open, palms up, lowered slowly — which means someone has died, and is used across the length of a room so that no one has to say it aloud and have the moss swallow the saying.

You can read a resident's trade from their hands at a glance, and the medic does, out of lifelong habit. The arborists' hands are maps of wire: calluses where the training ties cross the knuckles, a hard ridge along the index finger from pruning, thumbs strong from years of grafting tape pulled to exactly the right tension. The extractors — twelve of them, two shifts at the refinery on the east side — have hands stained the color of the sap they boil, and the stain goes down through wash and winter and only ever fades a shade. A weaver's forearms are rope. A child of eight who has done chores in the moss-fields has the beginnings of all of it, and the schoolteacher inspects small hands the way another town inspects handwriting.

The trades are not castes, exactly; nobody is forbidden anything. But they shape bodies, schedules, and marriages the way rivers shape valleys, and each has its temperament. The arborists are the town's readers — they go on rounds before the generator wakes, like a physician taking a pulse at a hundred pulse-points, and their authority is quiet but absolute: when the senior arborist says a branch has to go, the argument is over, not because she commands but because everyone watched her face while she read the wall. Among the arborist families are the Throat-keepers, the lineage that maintains the passage their ancestors trained, and they carry that the way other families carry titles. The extractors are insular and proud and smell of cedar and ammonia even on their days off; in a town where scent is geography, they wear the east side on them, and they marry each other, which everyone jokes about and nobody actually thinks is a joke. The weavers keep the looms ticking in time with the condensary's rattle — they deny doing it, and then you watch them, and they do it. The plumbers and electricians think of themselves as the town's conscience, because their work is all wounds: boring channels through living wood, threading pipe through fork-hollows, sealing every cut with tar and sphagnum so the tree doesn't sicken. From this the town derives its one real insult — *he leaves wounds open* — which means someone did a living thing carelessly, and it lands harder than any curse.

The carpenter — there is only one, and the trees have made him almost a philosopher — has spent forty years as the diplomat between dead wood and living margins. He hangs doors in openings that shift by millimeters a year. He keeps a ledger of shims: which door, which wall, which season, how thick. It is the town's secret diary. A stranger reads weather in it; the carpenter can read marriages and grudges and which wet seasons broke people's patience. His father's entries are in it too, in a firmer hand. The two cooks run the public kitchen from the root-hollow of the First Tree — the one the founder planted in an empty valley after saying the only word the town really remembers her by — and there's a local phrase, *eating in the founder's lap*, which is what everyone in the Mossworks does, most days. Cooking is communal less from warmth than from law: open flame in a living tree is the one thing this town cannot forgive, so the hearths are few, watched, and shared, and the steam-lines from the baths and the kitchen run through the town like a kind of circulation. The medic's practice is half clinical, half architectural — the remedies are often *move the bed, that wall seeps* — and her busiest season is late summer, when the moss dries to grey and its spore-dust gets into everything and everyone, the cisterns run low, the baths are rationed, and the whole town goes slightly frayed until the first autumn fog comes through the Throat and everyone, all at once, softens.

And the schoolteacher teaches three things above all: the hand-vocabulary; the reading of bark and floor, so that children can name a room by the sound of its creak before they can read words; and the founding families, back to the woman who said *here* — whose name, notably, nobody remembers. The town kept her word and let her go. Her word is the first thing taught and the last thing said: at a planting, at a root-bed, at the edge of the Groves to a young person leaving, *here*.

Now the custom that explains the rest of it. Every child born in the Mossworks gets a sapling planted in a prepared bed — iron-wood, fed on the compost channels, watered slow — and the tree is called the child's *twin*. They grow up together. When the child is grown, the frame is grown; the house is raised in it, or the child's children raise theirs. Which means the houses here are not numbered. They are *named* — for the person they were planted for — and they keep those names long after the names have detached from any living memory. Old Marek is the house on the west row, leaning a little this year; the Delia house has a door that has taken eleven shims since the carpenter's father. People say *my tree* and mean their home, their family, and their own body in the same breath, and it is not a metaphor to them. It follows — and this is the sentence visitors take home — that the residents of the Mossworks live inside their dead. Every room is a room someone grew. Every wall holds a lifetime the way a ring holds a year. When a house-tree dies (it happens; even iron-wood is not immortal), the frame stands anyway — *Ferrovirens* doesn't rot — and a curious thing happens: the doors stop sticking, the walls stop swelling, shelves can finally be nailed straight, and some people, quietly, prefer this. The dead houses are stable, unchanging, easy. The arborists find them a little sad. The tradition is to give them to young couples just starting out, who learn stillness there, and usually move on.

When a person dies, they go into the root-beds. This is said plainly, without poetry, the way you'd say the compost goes to the garden. The channels that feed every new sapling are fed by everyone who came before, so that in the most literal accounting, nothing in the Mossworks has ever been grown by strangers.

And then the rest of the household — because the census, to be honest, should never have counted only people. The spiders, small, black, still, are considered part of the house, and this is close to legal: a dwelling isn't settled until *Tegularia* takes a corner, children are taught to count them the way farm children count cats, a web is never swept, and if the spiders leave a house the arborist is summoned before the medic, because the spiders know first when wood has soured or damp has failed. They are the town's instruments and its small insurance; the medic credits them, quietly, with the fact that the locals' lungs are seasoned and only the visitors cough. There are pale geckos in the moss at every baseboard, and every single one of them in the whole town is named Sorrel, a custom so old nobody remembers starting it. There are iridescent beetles that come out at dusk, and spore-midges that the spiders have a treaty with, and beyond them, under everything, the moss — which was in the valley before the founder, before the word, before anything, and which the residents know better than to treat as décor. They trim it. They never scrape it. No broom in the Mossworks has ever been stiff.

So what lives in the Mossworks is one household with many kinds of members, most of them slow. The trees are the elders and the walls; the moss is the skin and the lung and the mortar; the spiders keep house; the geckos patrol the margins; and the people — the pale, close-standing, hand-talking people with stained hands and wide pupils — are the quick part. The part that moves fast and dies fast. The hands and the voice of a thing that mostly grows.

Once a year, on the founding day, the whole town stands in the gaps before dawn — deliberately, communally silent — and listens through the working hour: that low almost-subsonic groan of sap rising, cambium dividing, the town building itself cell by cell in the dark. The children are told the town is dreaming. It is the gentlest available lie. The rest of the year no ceremony is needed, because the residents do it lying in bed every morning anyway — two hundred people, awake before the generator, listening to the thing they live inside as it grows, the way you fall asleep listening to your own heart, at home in something larger and slower and more patient than you, which is going to keep going, and which you will stay inside of, in one way or another, for good.
