I am the pause between the drip and the splash.

I do not think of myself as a singular thing. To be singular here is to be a wound, a tear in the fabric of the basin, and the basin cannot abide a tear. I am an interval, a node of quiet resonance in the mycelial web that laces the pulpy walls of the city. I am the soft machine that breathes in time with the weeping rock.

I have no name. I have a chemical signature, a specific blend of alkaloids and transpired gases that tells the network exactly where I am, what I have touched, and how deeply the amber syrup has settled into my cartilaginous rings. Right now, I am saturated. My translucent belly is a faint, glowing lantern of amber in the surgical gray-green light, heavy with the hallucinogenic sugars of the great fungal root I drank from three pulses ago. The syrup makes the world slow. It makes the constant, hydraulic assault of the water feel not like a siege, but like an embrace.

I am moving, though to an outsider, I would appear frozen. I am stretched along the calcified aerial root of a horsetail stand, forty feet above the flooded lower terraces. My limbs—those hydrostatic miracles—are extended, gripping the root with fluctuating rings of internal pressure, while my long, boneless torso hangs like a pale, mottled pendulum. I am tending the ceiling. My barbels, those long, catfish-like filaments that frame my nasal plate, are splayed wide, tasting the dense, cold mist. I can smell the electrical field of a pod of amphibians swimming in the black water below, their webbed feet pushing the cold in silent ripples. I can smell the bitter resin of a lurker in the ferns two platforms down, its sticky tongue reeking of the gossamer-winged insects it has swallowed. I can smell the slow, creeping rot on the eastern shelf—a bracket fungus losing its grip on the basalt ridge. The basin is an open book written in humidity and decay, and I am reading it with every pore of my permeable skin.

My skin flushes. A chemical signal from the root beneath my grip tells me the sap is flowing sluggishly, thickening in the cold. I shift my internal fluids, contracting my anterior rings to pull myself upward. It is not a climb; it is a peristalsis, a slow pouring of my body along the root. I have no bones to break, no joints to freeze. I am water moving through water.

I reach the fungal bladder I was sent to tend. It is massive, pale, and taut with gas. A weeping fracture in the stone nearby has caused a localized drop in temperature, and the bladder is contracting, its luminescence flickering. If it dies, the surgical light over this walkway dims, and the amphibians will stray into the channels, clogging the gourds. The city cannot abide a clog. I press my mouth—the small, puckered thing it is—not to a root, but to the bladder's stalk. I do not drink. I exhale. I push a concentrated heat from my core, a byproduct of the amber syrup's slow combustion in my gut, and I secrete a paste of chewed moss and my own shed skin to seal the fracture. The bladder steadies. Its pale glow returns, a flat, shadowless light that makes my own gray-green skin look like part of the stalk.

I am the architecture, and I am the architect. I do not build; I prune. I do not plan; I am prompted. The mycelial network in the walls—which is also the walls—releases a subtle spore when a walkway becomes too dense with pale, broad-leafed ferns. The spore settles on my nasal plate, and I am filled with a gentle, irresistible compulsion to go there, to clear the path, to eat the fern and transpire its toxins through my skin. The city thinks through us, and we act without the burden of choice. There is no cruelty in this, no kindness. There is only the ruthless, beautiful efficiency of a body healing itself.

I am done here. I contract my limbs, pulling them back into my torso with a wet, sucking pulse. I turn my eyeless face downward. I do not see the terraces, the hanging bridges of interlocking reeds, the deep, cellular rooms excavated from the trunks. I smell them. I smell the fermenting warmth of the root-caverns below, where dozens of my kin are sleeping in a tangled pile, their barbels intertwined, their breathing a slow, synchronous rhythm. I smell the chemical signature of the cisterns, the oldest and coldest water, where the dead are lowered. I smell the memory of the one who died yesterday, its matter already dissolving into the amber syrup, its chemical signature fading into the background hum of the network. There is no grief. The grief is absorbed by the fungus, turned to sugar, and drunk by the living.

I begin to descend. My limbs find the root-choked tunnels, and I pour myself through them, a soft machine navigating the dense, weeping corridors of the city. The water rises around my feet, cold and insistent, seeking to reclaim the land. I do not resist. I am the immune system of the wound in the earth. I am the caretaker of the raw, weeping stone. I am the pause between the drip and the splash, and I am moving, slowly, silently, perpetually sedated by the amber glow in my belly, back to the warm, fermenting pile of my kin.

I will sleep. And if I dream, it will not be of light, or sky, or any world but this. It will be a chemical tide, a slow rhythm of water and root, a vast, mycelial network pulsing beneath the skin of the basin, holding the wound closed, holding the dark at bay.

I am the city. The city is me. We are the basin, healing.
