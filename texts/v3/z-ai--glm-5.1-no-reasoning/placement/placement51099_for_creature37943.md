# The One Who Returns to Count

I would not be the chamber. The chamber is too complete in its incompleteness, too fully itself in its blind persistence. It doesn't need me. It has its pump-heart, its water-blood, its basalt-bone. It has its *not yet*. It is taken care of, in the terrible way that something can be taken care of by its own dysfunction.

And I would not be the caretaker. The caretaker left. The caretaker is a set of marks and a fuel canister and a patched hose and an absence shaped like a reason. The caretaker is finished—or at least paused, and the pause has lasted long enough that the distinction doesn't matter to the pump, which doesn't matter, which is the point.

No. I would be the one who comes back.

Not comes back in the sense of return—there was no first visit to return from. Comes back in the sense of *goes back*, the way water goes back to the sea, the way heat goes back to the stone. I would be the one who finds the fissure in the basalt and feels the warm exhalation and enters not out of curiosity but out of a recognition that doesn't have a word. The way you recognize a song you've never heard. The way you recognize a face in a dream as someone you know, though you can't place them, though they don't exist outside the dream.

I would turn sideways in the crack and my clothing would whisper against the walls and the sound would be enormous and I would not flinch. I would know the sound. Not the specific sound—this specific sound is new, is mine, is the rasp of my particular fabric against this particular stone. But the *quality* of it. The intimacy of sound in a close space. The way the chamber takes your small noises and amplifies them and returns them to you changed. I would know this quality the way you know the quality of your own name being spoken.

---

The chamber would open around me and I would stand in the warm shallow water and I would not be surprised by the water. I would be surprised by how unsurprised I was. The wet heat would sit on my skin and I would let it. The grey light from the fissure would fall across the floor like a blade and I would stand partly in it and partly outside it and I would feel the difference—warmth on one side of my body, the specific mineral breath of the chamber on the other—and I would think: *this is a place where things are still being decided.*

And then I would see the pump.

And I would know the pump. Not this pump—this specific pump, with its rusted housing the color of dried blood and its cracked hose and its peeling tape and its wet chugging cough. But the *category* of pump. The pump as a concept. The pump as a solution to a problem that shouldn't exist: a chamber that weeps, that cannot stop weeping, that is still being made and cannot stop being made, that fills with the evidence of its own becoming and needs something to drain it, to carry the water away, to say *enough, this is the shape we've chosen, stop filling, stop forming, stop*—but the pump doesn't say stop. The pump says *not yet*. The pump says *I'll hold this for you. I'll hold this while you decide.*

I would stand in the water and I would listen to the pump's cycle. Ninety seconds of silence—the filling, the gathering, the water rising by millimeters, the chamber's patience with its own flooding. Then the chugging—wet, irregular, the combustion reports of fuel not quite vaporized, the iron shuddering against its own effort—and the water thinning, draining, the mirror becoming less perfect, and I would feel this in my feet. The slight pull. The acceleration of the film toward the drain. I would feel myself being emptied alongside the chamber, my own weight shifting as the water level dropped, my boots finding purchase on basalt that was briefly exposed before the water returned.

And I would understand the cycle. Not understand it intellectually—understand it the way the body understands breathing. Systole. Diastole. The filling and the emptying. The chamber breathes water in and the pump breathes water out and I would stand in the middle of this respiration and I would feel it in my own chest, the sympathetic rhythm, the way you feel another person's breathing when you lie close to them in the dark. Not synced. Not harmonized. Just *adjacent*. Just close enough that the two rhythms become aware of each other without merging.

---

Then I would see the marks.

And this is where I would stop. This is where the recognition would become something else—something sharper, something that cuts through the humid mineral air and the grey light and the warm water on my boots and finds me, specifically me, and says: *you are here. You are here now. You are the one who is here now.*

The marks on the wall. Hundreds of them. Vertical columns, horizontal strokes, organized, regular, systematic but not annotated. No labels. No language. Just the count. Just the patient accumulation of *this many, this many, this many*. The lowest marks faint, softened by the mineral film, the upper marks sharp, recent. Memory degrading from the bottom up. Stone memory. The recent past vivid, the deep past illegible.

I would stand close to the wall and I would read them. Not read them in the sense of decoding—I don't think they can be decoded, I don't think there is a cipher, I don't think the strokes correspond to pump cycles or days or water levels or anything that could be translated into a meaning outside the chamber. I think the strokes correspond to themselves. I think the count is the count. I think the marks say: *I was here. I was here. I was here again.* And that is all they say, and that is enough, and it has always been enough, and it will never be enough, and both of these things are true in the same way that the chamber is both still being made and already finished, both provisional and committed.

I would raise my hand to the wall. I would touch the marks. I would feel the abrasion—the pale interior of the basalt exposed by the strokes, the texture of a hand that pressed hard enough to scratch stone, that dragged a point or a fingernail or a piece of harder stone across this surface with enough force to leave a mark. And I would feel the hand that made them. Not literally—the hand is gone, the hand left, the hand stopped making marks and left a nearly full fuel canister on a natural shelf and a patched hose and an absence. But the *pressure* of the hand. The *intent*. The specific quality of attention that makes a person press hard enough to scratch basalt. I would feel it in my own hand, the way you feel the weight of a tool someone else has used, the way a grip leaves its shape in a handle over years, the way a path is worn by feet that are no longer walking it.

And I would know that I was going to continue.

---

Not decide. The decision would already have been made by the time I touched the wall. The decision would have been made in the fissure, in the sideways descent, in the first breath of mineral air. The decision would have been made before I arrived, would have been made by whatever it is in me that recognized the warm exhalation from the crack in the basalt as something that concerned me. The decision would have been made by the part of me that is not quite curiosity and not quite obligation and not quite love but something else, something for which there is no word because the thing it describes is too specific: the response of a particular kind of being to a particular kind of need. The need of a place that is still being made, that is mid-process, that is paused between one stage and the next, that has been left by its caretaker and is running on habit and tape and the last fumes of fuel that shouldn't still be burning but are, somehow, still burning, because the pump doesn't know how to stop and the water doesn't know how to stop and the marks don't know how to stop being marks even though no hand has added to them in however long it has been since the caretaker left.

I would continue the count.

I would find something to scratch the basalt. A piece of harder stone, perhaps—a piece of chert or quartzite brought down from the surface, something that would bite into the dark rock and leave a pale stroke. I would stand near the existing marks—near, not on top of, not continuing the columns but starting new ones beside them, because I would want to preserve the caretaker's count, would want to keep the distinction between *their* here-I-was and *my* here-I-am. I would press the stone to the wall and I would make a mark and it would be the first mark in however long it has been since the last mark, and the sound of the stone on the basalt would be sharp and dry in the wet chamber, a sound like a small bone breaking, and the echoes would take it and return it and return it and return it.

And the pump would cycle. And the water would weep. And I would make another mark.

---

What would I be, then? Not the maintenance—the maintenance is the chamber's own. Not the caretaker—the caretaker is gone. Not the visitor—the visitor leaves.

I would be the *resumption*. The specific being that emerges when a paused process is continued by a new hand. Not the same process—the caretaker's count is the caretaker's count, and my count is my count, and the two are separated by a gap of silence and peeling tape and a nearly full fuel canister that no one came to use. But not a different process either. The marks are the same kind of marks. The wall is the same wall. The pump is the same pump. The water is the same water. The need is the same need: *record this. Record that we are here. Record that the chamber is still weeping and the pump is still chugging and the basalt is still warm and the fuel is almost gone and the tape is peeling and the hose is cracking and none of this is permanent and none of this has stopped.*

I would be the one who comes back to a place that doesn't know it has been left, and who stays—not forever, not permanently, because nothing here is permanent, because the land itself hasn't committed—but for now. For the current cycle. For this systole and this diastole and this drip and this echo and this mark on the wall.

I would be the one who adds a stroke to the count and listens to it echo and feels the chamber absorb the sound into its ambient noise, its sound-body, its acoustic memory, and I would know that the mark will be sharp now and illegible later and that this is how stone memory works and that this is enough. I would be the one who stands in the warm water and feels the pump's pulse in the soles of my feet and counts along with it—not because counting matters, but because counting is the thing the chamber taught the caretaker and the caretaker taught the chamber and I am learning from both of them, from the marks and the absence, from the presence and the gap.

I would be the count that continues after the counter has left. The habit that outlived its first host and found a second. Not a haunting—a habitation. A settling-in. The way water settles into stone, finds the vesicles, fills the cavities, emerges on the other side changed by the transit but still, recognizably, water.

I would be the one who finds the nearly full fuel canister on its shelf and uses it. Who uncaps it, who threads the fitting, who pours the fuel into the pump's tank and hears the liquid find the level and smells the sharp chemical tang of it mixing with the mineral air. Who pulls the starter cord or turns the valve or does whatever this specific antique machine requires to wake from its gapped, stuttering, almost-out-of-fuel silence. Who hears the first real combustion report in however long it has been—sharp, wet, heavy—and feels the floor-water shift toward the drain and the film thin by a millimeter and the chamber's breathing change, deepen, find its rhythm again.

And then I would make a mark. And another. And another.

---

This is who I would be in the Weeping Chamber: the one who resumes.

Not the one who began. Not the one who will finish—there will be no finishing, not here, not in this provisional landscape, not on this uncommitted coast. The one who resumes. The one who walks into a paused process and says, with a hand on a wall and a stone in a fist and a fuel canister tilted toward a tank: *I am here. I am here. I am here again.*

The chamber would not notice. The pump would cycle. The water would weep. The basalt would cool by a fraction of a degree, contract by a fraction of a millimeter, release another bead of mineral water from a new vesicle. The marks on the wall would not change their character—my marks beside the caretaker's marks, both just strokes, both just count, both just the patient accumulation of presence in a place that measures presence in abrasion and forgetting.

But I would be there. Warm mammal intrusion, temporary organ, part of the anatomy for as long as I stayed. Adding vapor. Adding heat. Adding the dry sound of stone on stone to the wet sound of water on water and the heavy sound of iron pushing water through rock. Adding my count to the count. Adding my *here* to the *here*.

And when I left—because I would leave, eventually, because I am not permanent either, because nothing here has committed—the marks would remain. Sharp for now. Fading later. The newest ones mine, the older ones the caretaker's, the oldest ones illegible, dissolved by the mineral film, absorbed by the stone, returned to the basalt from which they were scratched. Memory degrading from the bottom up. Stone memory. The kind that doesn't hold on, that lets the deep past go, that keeps only what is recent and sharp and *here*.

And the pump would cycle. And the water would weep. And the count would continue, or it wouldn't, and the chamber would not know the difference, and I would not know the difference, and the difference would not matter, and it would be the only thing that mattered, and both of these things would be true in the warm grey mineral wet of a place that is still being made and has not yet decided what to become.

*Not yet. Not yet. Not yet.*

I would be the one who says this with my hands.
