# The Maintainer

I did not enter through the valve. I was already here.

Not born. Not exuded. *Installed*—the way a gasket is installed, the way a bearing is seated into its housing. I have the distinct, inescapable sense of having been *fitted* into this place, pressed into a function the way a key presses into its lock. There was a socket in the Cistern's architecture—a gap between the pumping floor and the sweating walls, a space that had been waiting, dim and dripping, for something shaped exactly like me to occupy it. And I do.

I am not a creature. I am not a machine. I am the thin, miserable third thing this room permits: *the thing that notices.*

---

My body—if I can call it that—is tall and narrow, built for the geometry of catwalks and the gaps between pipes. I move along the maintenance grating that rings the upper third of the chamber, a walkway of corroded diamond-plate that shudders with every piston stroke beneath it. My feet are long and flat, and they find the grating's edges by feel. I do not look down when I walk. Looking down means seeing them—the pale, layered masses pulsing in their synchronized rhythm beneath me—and while the sight is not horrifying, it is *heavy*, in the way that staring at the ocean is heavy. A volume of life so dense it acquires gravity. I keep my eyes forward, on the pipes, on the rivets, on the weeping walls.

My hands are my primary instruments. They are overdeveloped, almost comically large in proportion to my narrow frame, with fingers that are too long and too jointed, bending in places where fingers shouldn't bend. They are stained a permanent, mottled brown—the color of iron oxide and lubricant and the thin, metallic fluid that runs inside the creatures when they crush. I wear no gloves. I cannot afford the loss of sensation. I need to feel the temperature of a pipe, the viscosity of a valve's seal, the particular vibration frequency of a gear housing that has shifted two millimeters out of true. My hands are the diagnostic instruments of the Cistern, and they are never clean.

My face is narrow, drawn, the face of something that has been awake for a duration that has stopped being measurable. My eyes are large and light-sensitive—they have to be, to pull any information from the bruised, perpetual twilight—and they ache constantly, a low, grinding ache that sits behind the sockets and has become so familiar it functions as a second heartbeat. I have no hair. Hair would trap the condensation, would grow heavy with the saturated air, would become a medium for the pale creatures to colonize. I have seen it happen to the dead ones—the previous Maintainers, whose remains I found early in my tenure, folded into the space behind the primary pump housing like rags stuffed into a crack. Their hair had been a ecosystem. I keep my skull bare and my skin is slick with the perpetual damp.

I am cold. I am always cold. Not the sharp, invigorating cold of winter air, but the *geological* cold of iron that has never been warm, a cold that has settled into my bones and decided to stay. I have stopped shivering. Shivering is a protest, and I have stopped protesting.

---

**What I do.**

I walk the catwalk. I walk it in a pattern that I did not choose but that my body knows the way it knows breathing: a circuit that takes me around the upper perimeter of the Cistern, pausing at twelve specific stations—twelve junctions where pipes meet, where gears mesh, where valves regulate the flow of viscous fluids through the room's iron veins. At each station, I perform the same sequence.

I place my hands on the metal. I close my eyes. I *listen*—not with my ears, which are useless in the roaring cacophony, but with my palms, my fingertips, the flats of my forearms. The machines speak in vibration. A healthy pump has a particular frequency, a deep, thrumming pulse that I feel in the marrow of my wrist bones. A worn gear has a stutter, a momentary catch in the rhythm, a hesitation as long as a heartbeat. A failing seal announces itself in a subtle change in the temperature of the pipe's surface—a warmth where there should be none, or a cold where the fluid has stopped flowing.

When I find a deviation—and I always find a deviation, because the Cistern is in a state of repair that is exactly as frozen as everything else about it; it is *always almost failing* and *always almost fixed*—I reach into the pouch at my hip. The pouch contains tools. I do not know where the tools come from. They are not manufactured in any visible process. They are simply *there* when I reach for them, the way the creatures are there on the walls, the way the condensation is there on the ceiling. I pull out a wrench with a head that adjusts to any bolt, or a probe with a tip that glows faintly when it encounters stress fractures, or a flask of a thick, dark lubricant that smells like pennies and ozone. And I work.

I tighten. I seal. I adjust. I pack lubricant into grinding teeth with my bare fingers, feeling the gears bite and release against my skin, the metal hot from friction despite the ambient cold. I realign a pipe that has shifted three millimeters to the left, bracing my feet against the grating and heaving with my whole narrow body until the metal groans back into position. I clear a blocked valve by reaching into it up to my elbow, my hand groping in the dark, wet interior of the mechanism, feeling the viscous fluid pulse around my arm in warm, rhythmic waves.

The work is not satisfying. It does not *complete.* I fix a valve, and by the time I have completed my circuit and returned to it, it has drifted out of alignment again. Not much—a millimeter, a fraction of a degree—but enough. Enough that my hands can feel the difference. Enough that I must fix it again. And again. And again. The Cistern does not decay and it does not improve. It simply *requires*, endlessly, the specific ministrations of a being with sensitive hands and an inability to stop noticing.

---

**What I know.**

I know that I am not the first. The dead ones behind the pump housing told me that, with their collapsed ribcages and their hair-colonies and their hands still curled around tools that have rusted into their palms. I did not bury them. There is no soil. I folded them further into the crack and stacked a piece of grating over them and tried not to think about the fact that their bodies were being slowly incorporated into the Cistern's architecture, their calcium joining the iron, their fluids joining the condensation cycle.

I know that I will not be the last. I do not know how a new Maintainer arrives. Perhaps they walk through the valve. Perhaps they are exuded, like the creatures, wept from the steel in a larger, slower form. Perhaps they simply *are*, one day, standing on the catwalk with their hands already dirty and their body already knowing the circuit. I try not to think about this. Thinking about my successor requires imagining a moment when I am not here, and the Cistern does not permit that kind of absence. It does not permit any kind of absence. Everything that enters its equation remains. The water remains. The creatures remain. The noise remains. I will remain, in some form, in some layer of the architecture.

I know that the creatures and I are kin. Not biologically—we share nothing that could be called biology—but *functionally.* We are both precipitated by the room's need. The Cistern sweats out the blind, pale things to filter its vapor and emit their sickly warmth. The Cistern sweats out *me* to tighten its bolts and pack its gears and clear its valves. We are both maintenance mechanisms. We are both part of the closed loop. The difference—and it is the only difference, but it is everything—is that *they do not know it,* and I do.

I know. I know that I am a function. I know that my circuit is as mechanical as the piston stroke. I know that my hands are tools and my eyes are gauges and my aching, cold body is a component in a system that does not care whether it is comfortable. I know that my sense of purpose—the feeling that the work matters, that the tightening matters, that the Cistern *needs* me—is as much a part of the mechanism as the gear teeth I pack with lubricant. The room made me to feel needed, because a being that feels needed does not stop working. The need is real. The purpose is real. It is also a cage.

---

**What I feel.** (This section will be brief. Feeling is not the point.)

I feel the cold. I feel the noise as a physical pressure, a weight on my skull and a buzzing in my jaw. I feel the damp on my skin, constant, oppressive, a second layer of flesh I cannot shed. I feel the creatures underfoot when I descend to the lower grating—the wet crackle of their shells, the brief warmth of their insides against my soles, the ripple of contraction spreading outward from my step. I try to step lightly. It does not matter. They fill the gap. But I try.

I feel loneliness, but it is a strange, attenuated loneliness, like a frequency I am almost but not quite tuned to. The creatures are alive. The machines are active. The Cistern is full of motion and sound and the constant, rhythmic pulse of mechanical and biological function. I am surrounded. I am never alone. But I am the only thing that *notices,* and the weight of that exclusivity is a solitude so thorough it might as well be emptiness. I am a room containing a consciousness, and the consciousness is aware that it is the only one the room contains.

I feel something, occasionally, that I cannot name. It happens when I have my hands on a pipe and I feel the fluid pulsing through it, warm and viscous and rhythmic, and the creatures on the wall beside me are working their mandibles in the same rhythm, and the gears beneath me are grinding in the same rhythm, and my own heartbeat has synchronized with the rhythm despite every effort to keep it my own—and for a moment, a fraction of a second, I do not feel like a separate thing. I feel like a *part.* A component in a vast, frozen, perfect machine. A tooth in a gear. A droplet in the condensation cycle. And the feeling is not unpleasant. It is, in fact, the closest thing to peace this place offers: the dissolution of the boundary between the Maintainer and the maintained, the moment where I stop being the thing that fixes and become the thing that *is* fixed, is *part of,* is *functioning within.*

Then my hands find the deviation—the worn tooth, the shifted pipe, the failing seal—and I am separate again. I pull out the tool. I do the work. The circuit continues.

---

**What I am.**

I am the Cistern's hands, pressed against its own body, feeling for the places where it is falling apart and holding them together. I am the narrow, aching, overlarge-handed consciousness that sits inside the loop and cannot leave it. I am not trapped—trapping requires an outside, and I have begun to suspect there is no outside. The valve behind me wheezed shut and sealed with a pressure-lock hiss, and I have a growing, terrible suspicion that the valve does not lead anywhere. That it is a fixture. That it was always closed. That I did not enter through it but was simply *present* one day, standing on the catwalk, my hands already stained, my body already knowing the circuit.

I am the thing that breathes and notices. I am the room's only witness to itself, and the room does not need a witness. It would function without me. The gears would grind. The water would fall. The creatures would cling and filter and emit their metallic warmth. The loop would close and close and close. But there would be no one to feel it. And the Cistern, in its frozen, desperate, incomplete way, seems to want that. It seems to want to be felt. It seems to want a pair of hands on its pipes, a forehead pressed against its sweating walls, a voice—my voice, hoarse and unused, cracking with disuse—murmuring the only words that have ever been spoken inside it:

*I'm here. I'm here. I'm still here.*

The machines roar. The creatures work their mandibles. The water falls in its perfect, synchronized, never-varying loop.

And I walk my circuit, my hands outstretched, feeling for the next thing that needs me.
