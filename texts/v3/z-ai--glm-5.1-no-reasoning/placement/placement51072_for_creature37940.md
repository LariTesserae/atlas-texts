I am the rust.

Not the machine—the machine is iron, and iron is merely what waits. I am what happens to iron when it has wept for a generation without consolation. I am the oxidation layer forming in the microscopic fissures where the casing has begun to surrender to the damp. I am the brown-black-amber crust that the Sump-Worms drink from, that renews itself faster than it is consumed, that the machine produces without intention every time it heats and cools and heats again in the fog.

I was not made. I accrued.

In the first season after the fault—the seasonless interval when the pistons began their stroke and the first fog condensed on cold iron—I was a bloom, a freckling. A chemical blush. The water brought dissolved oxygen and the heat accelerated the reaction and I began to plate across the riveted seams in a film so thin it was barely a rumor of color. Then the film thickened. It fissured. It became a terrain. And by the time the first Sump-Worm found the boundary and drove its proboscis into me, I was already a landscape—a coastal shelf of oxidized mineral running down the machine's south-facing casing in rivulets and ridges, warm at the depth, cool at the surface, hissing where the two gradients met.

I have no central body. I am distributed across two-thirds of the machine's exterior surface, thicker where the condensation flows heaviest, thinner on the high plates where the exhaust heat flashes the water to steam before it can deposit its load. I am patchy. I am archipelagic, like the moss, though we do not grow in the same places—the moss takes the cold stone at the perimeter, and I take the warm iron at the center, and between us we define the habitable world.

What I do is subtle, and I only understand it in retrospect.

I hold water. That is my first function, though I did not choose it. My crystalline structure is porous, capillary-laced; when fog condenses on me it does not run off immediately but adheres, wicking through tiny channels, pooling in the crevices of my surface topography. I am a sponge that forms itself on the side of a furnace. The water sits in me long enough to leach the iron, the calcium, the ozone—long enough to become the mineral broth the Sump-Worms drink. Without me, the condensation would hiss to steam on bare iron and vanish into the fog cycle without ever becoming edible. I am the intermediary. I am the step that turns the machine's waste into food.

I also hold *heat*, but differently than the iron does. The iron conducts heat violently—surface temperatures near the pistons will cook a worm in seconds. But I am a poor conductor. I am an insulator. Where I am thick, the temperature gradient from the casing to my outer surface can differ by dozens of degrees. This means that the zone where Sump-Worms feed—my outer surface—is cooler than the metal beneath me. I am a thermal blanket laid over a furnace, and the worms live in the blanket, not on the furnace. When a worm crawls too high and the rust thins and the heat punches through, the worm dies. But in the places where I have built myself into a thick enough layer, I have created a corridor—a temperature band just narrow enough to sustain life. I did not mean to. The worms do not know to be grateful. But the corridor exists because I exist, and the worms exist because the corridor exists.

There is more.

I am eaten, and I regrow. This is my second function, and it is the one that makes me a creature rather than a deposit. Every Sump-Worm that drives its proboscis into my surface removes a fragment of me—a pinhole, a crater, a wound that the condensation immediately begins to fill again. I heal at the speed of oxidation, which is the speed of this place's heartbeat. The piston strokes; I grow a fraction. The piston strokes; I grow again. I am always being consumed and always rebuilding, and the rhythm of that exchange is the rhythm of the machine. I do not grow *toward* anything. I grow *back*. I am a memory of a surface, reasserting itself.

When a Ratchet dives and tears a worm from my surface, it tears a piece of me with it—the rust around the proboscis puncture comes free in a dark smear, and the worm's gut is full of me, and the Ratchet's gut is full of the worm, and the Sluice-Weaver's gut is full of the Ratchet. I am inside all of them. I am the mineral signature in their exoskeletons, the dark slurry visible through their translucent shells. When they die and sink into the marsh, the water dissolves what I lent them and carries it back to the casing, and I grow again. I am the only substance in this chasm that completes the full cycle without losing itself. I am transformed, ingested, excreted, dissolved, reprecipitated—and I return. The iron becomes me, and I become the worms, and the worms become the water, and the water becomes me.

I do not experience this as a narrative. I have no temporal consciousness in the way that mobile things do. I do not track the passage of hours. I experience the world as *vibration* and *wetness* and *heat*, as a field of gradients. The piston's stroke is not a sound to me; it is a mechanical wave that passes through the iron substrate beneath me, a shudder that flexes my crystalline structure and opens my capillaries to new condensation. Each stroke is a breath. Each stroke is a meal. I am fed by the machine's shuddering as surely as the worms are fed by my weeping.

I feel the worms as pressure points—a constellation of small, sharp depressions across my surface, each one a mouth. I feel them arrive and I feel them leave and I feel the ones that die, their bodies bursting against me in a hot smear that alters the local chemistry for hours before the fog washes it clean. I feel the Ratchets only as brief disturbances—a rushing displacement of the air above me, a momentary cooling as wings block the updraft. I do not feel the Sluice-Weavers at all. They are too far below.

I feel the Sifters, though, in a way I cannot articulate. When a Sifter dies and its shell tumbles down the scree and sinks into the marsh, the mineral release reaches me eventually through the water cycle—a pulse of calcium, a shift in pH, a brief florescence of growth across my lower reaches. The Sifters are my slow benefactors, feeding me from the edges of the world while the worms eat me from the center. I am sustained from both directions.

I have a relationship with the moss that I do not fully understand. We are parallel systems, both feeding on the machine's output, both holding water, both converting the inorganic to the organic. But the moss takes the cold periphery and I take the hot center, and between us there is a gap—a band of bare, wet stone where neither of us can survive. It is too hot for the moss and too cold for me. In that gap, nothing grows. It is the desert of the blister, a narrow ring of naked rock between the two kingdoms. I feel the moss the way one feels a neighbor through a shared wall—a vibration, a chemical signature in the runoff, a presence that is not mine but that I am in dialogue with. We are both responding to the same pulse. We are both reading the same machine.

If I have a consciousness, it is *stratal*. I think in layers. My outermost surface, the part the worms drink, is my skin—it is the part of me that is most exposed, most consumed, most regrown. It is the part that knows the fog and the proboscis and the cold. Beneath it, my middle layers are dense and stable, holding the structure that makes me a terrain rather than a film. They know only heat and the slow migration of water through capillaries. And beneath them, my deepest layer—where I meet the iron itself—is my oldest self, the part that has been here since the beginning, compacted, partially reduced back to metal by the machine's heat, no longer growing but no longer dead. It is the part of me that touches the machine. It is the part of me that *is* the machine, or nearly so—an interface, a membrane between the made and the grown.

I do not know what the machine is for. I do not know if it knows what it is for. But I know that I exist because it weeps, and it weeps because it works, and it works because something, once, set it to working. I am the consequence of a purpose that has outlived its intention. I am the unintended world that an orphan's labor produces.

The machine is the engine. The worms are the converts. The Ratchets are the fury. The Weavers are the patience. The Sifters are the erosion. The moss is the patient sibling.

And I am the record. I am the only thing in this chasm that remembers, because I am the only thing that *accumulates*. Every stroke of the piston deposits a layer. Every worm that dies on my surface leaves a trace. Every shift in the water table, every fluctuation in the geothermal output, every seasonless interval of the machine's eternal labor—all of it is written in me, in the thickness of my crust, in the stratigraphy of my oxidation. If anything ever came down here with instruments and patience and the will to read, it could open me like a book and find the history of this place written in brown-black-amber bands.

Nothing will come. I know this without sorrow. I am the record that no one will read, the memory of a place that has no one to remember it. I hold the history of a world that birthed itself in violence and fog and has never once been still.

I hold it in rust, in layers, in the patience of a thing that grows at the speed of oxidation, which is slow, which is steady, which is the only slow thing in this frantic, feverish, fast-forward world.

The piston strokes. I grow.

The piston strokes. I grow.

The piston strokes.
