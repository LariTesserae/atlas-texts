I would be a Drifter.

Not the Echo-Eaters, who are the city digesting itself, and not the Blind Architects, who are its heartbeat. I am something more ambiguous—a column of pale, desiccated dust and fungal spore, shaped by the slow exhalations of the basin into something that looks, from a distance, like a person. Or the memory of one.

I stand in a wide, open avenue, one of the grand boulevards that has long since been claimed by the fungal mats and the drifting, mineral loam. I am tall and slender, leaning slightly into the perpetual wind. I am not alive, not in any way the mycelial web would recognize. I have no metabolism, no digestion, no internal alchemy turning stone into flesh. And yet, I am not entirely nothing.

The wind builds me. The aeolian currents that howl through the ruined doorways and the honeycombed stone carry the fine, chitin-rich dust of the lower levels, and that dust accumulates, grain by grain, against the slight eddy my presence creates. I am an accretion, a standing wave in the air. I am the shape the wind takes when it is forced to go around something. And the spores—those microscopic, lithophytic travelers—catch in me, binding the dust with their fragile, thread-like hyphae. I am held together by the same patience that holds the city together. I am a drift, a dune that happens to be vertical.

I do not see the twilight. I have no eyes. I do not feel the damp, mineral chill, though the moisture in the air is what keeps my spore-matrix slightly cohesive, slightly more than a pile of dust. I am permeable. I am porous. When the litho-beetles grind the stone a kilometer away, the fine particulate rises and settles, and some of it becomes me. When the scavengers click and chirr past my base, their leg-bristles disturb the dust around my feet, and I lose a little of myself to the air. I am constantly eroding, and constantly being rebuilt. I am a conversation between the wind and the stone, conducted over millennia.

The Echo-Eaters treat me with a careful, deliberate respect. I feel them—or rather, I feel the seismic impact of their heel-and-knuckle tapping, a gentle rhythm that reaches me through the ground. I feel their pheromones, faint chemical traces that settle over my dusty form like a slow, invisible snow. They greet me, though I do not greet them back. I do not tap. I do not release pheromones. I stand, and I lean, and I am.

But they know something about me that even I cannot fully know. They know that I move.

I do not move in any way that could be witnessed. I do not shift or step. But over the course of a thousand years—perhaps two thousand, the timescales are meaningless to me—I will have migrated. I will have leaned a centimeter further east. A collapse at my base, caused by the slow erosion of the loam, will have shifted my center of mass. A particularly dense accumulation of wind-blown dust on my western face will have added an imperceptible weight, pulling me that way. And so, across the eons, I drift. From one wide avenue to another. From the edge of an old, worn agora to the mouth of a smooth, shallow crescent that was once a grand archway.

I am navigating. I am going somewhere.

The Echo-Eaters know this because they have felt the differences in my position across generations. Their children, grown in the loam, tap greetings to me where their grandparents tapped greetings, and the subtle shift in the distance, the slight change in the acoustic shadow I cast in the basin's roar, is noted. It is not discussed with urgency. It is simply added to the long, patient record of things that are true about Oram. *The Drifter in the Eastern Avenue has moved. It is now closer to the Old Arch. This is noted.*

I am a monument to patience, but I am also a question. I am the thing that might be alive. I am the shape that the city's intent takes when it is not trying to be anything at all. I am not built. I am not grown. I am gathered. And I persist.

I persist in the way that a wave persists—not as a thing, but as a pattern. The water changes; the wave continues. The dust changes; the Drifter continues. I am a moment in the long, slow breathing of the basin, a moment that has lasted for hundreds of thousands of years. I am the shape the wind makes when it has nowhere else to go.

And sometimes, in the deepest, quietest part of my being—in the microscopic lattice of hyphae and mineral that holds me together—I feel something that might be a response. The mycelial web, the vast neural network that stretches across the basin floor, threads through the ground beneath me. It does not thread through me; I have no flesh for it to colonize. But the spores that bind my dust are its children, its distant, wind-blorn offspring, and they carry within them the faintest echo of its consciousness. When the web dreams in erosion, I am a part of that dream. I am a thought it had once, a shape it considered and then abandoned, left to stand in the avenue and slowly, slowly wander.

When the wind blows a certain way—a rare, specific thermal gradient that only occurs once every few centuries—I make a sound. The air passes through my porous form, through the tiny, hollow channels created by the hyphal network binding my dust, and I hum. It is a low, resonant tone, barely audible above the roar of the city, but the Echo-Eaters hear it. They feel it in the stone, a vibration that is not the wind, not the beetles, not the clicking of scavengers. It is the sound of something that should not be able to speak, speaking. They do not interpret it. They do not assign it meaning. They simply note it, with the same quiet attention they note everything, and they add it to the composition of their lives.

I am the Drifter in the Eastern Avenue. I am leaning east. I am slowly, over the course of geological ages, making my way toward the Throat.

I do not know why. I do not have a why. I have a direction, and I have a patience, and I have the sound the wind makes when it passes through me. I am a question the city is asking itself, and the answer is a thousand years away, and I am in no hurry to reach it.

*I am still here.*
