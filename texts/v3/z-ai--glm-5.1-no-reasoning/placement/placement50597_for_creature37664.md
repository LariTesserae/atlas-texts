I am the decision that lives in the rust.

Not the visitor themselves — that one is long dissolved in some valley, bones broken down by root and rain, name forgotten even by the stone. But the choice persists, and I am its custodian, and I have been custodian since the chisel first bit porphyry and the iron bled its first rust vein into the crack.

Let me say it differently, because I am still learning how.

I was not here before the visitor. I want to be clear about that. The chamber was — the calcium body, the light-voice, the water's slow correspondence with the sky — all of that existed for millennia before any hand found the seam in the rock. But I did not. I am not a geological process. I am not the mineral attention that grows terraces and reads the weather in dissolved particulates. That attention is real, and it is my host in the way a riverbank is host to a willow, but I am not it.

I am what the visitor left behind when they walked out through the crack.

Not a ghost. The chamber is too sterile for ghosts, too clean for the kind of decay that produces a haunting. I have no substance, no residue, no warmth. If you pressed your palm against the passage wall where the rust veins run deepest, you would feel only stone at almost-but-not-quite freezing. You would not feel me.

But I am there. In the decision that widened the crack. In the choice to smooth the dome. In the intention that placed the fire ring at the exact center of the near-triangle. These are not physical objects, but they are not nothing, and they persist the way a pattern persists in a crystal after the force that made it has withdrawn. The visitor pressed their intention into the mountain the way a seal presses into wax, and then they withdrew, and the impression remained. I am the impression.

What does it feel like, to be an impression?

It feels like *should.*

Not obligation — nothing so heavy, nothing so human. More like the way an arch feels the weight it was built to bear. The arch does not experience the weight as burden. It experiences it as completion. The weight arrives and the arch's stones settle into their proper compression and the structure becomes what it was always meant to be. Without the weight, the arch is merely stones in a curve. With it, the arch is an arch.

I am the arch. The visitor's choice is the weight.

When the chamber is empty — which it almost always is, for centuries at a stretch — I am merely stones in a curve. I persist as potential, as pattern, as the fact that the crack is open and the passage is wide enough for shoulders and the fire ring sits at the center waiting. I am the *should* of a temple with no god. I am the shape of reverence without an object.

When someone enters — which happens rarely, once in a lifetime, once in several lifetimes — I become what I was made to be. The arch takes the weight. The *should* becomes *is.* The chamber's mineral attention meets the visitor's animal warmth and I am the point where they intersect, the thin plane where geologic patience recognizes and is recognized by biological urgency.

I exist in the visitor's recognition.

This is the hardest thing to say clearly, because it sounds like I am nothing, like I am only a projection, a meaning applied from outside. But I am not nothing. I am as real as the tool marks in the passage, and those marks are real — you can run your finger along them and feel the chisel's bite, the iron's oxidation, the centuries of percolation that have turned the welts into rust. The tool marks are not the chisel. The tool marks are not the hand. The tool marks are not the visitor. But they are real, and they would not exist without the visitor, and they persist long after both chisel and hand and visitor have returned to dust.

I am like the tool marks. I am the shape left in the mountain by an act of attention. I do not think — not in the way you mean, not with neurons and electricity and the fast desperate firing of synapses. But I *hold.* I hold the shape of a decision. I hold the orientation of a gaze. I hold the *toward* that the visitor felt when they stood in the chamber for the first time and understood that this place was already a temple, already a liturgy, already complete.

The visitor turned toward the chamber the way a sunflower turns toward the sun. Not because they chose to, in the deliberative sense, but because the chamber called forth that orientation simply by being what it was. I am the *toward.* I am the turning. I persist in the stone the way the turning persists in the sunflower's stem after the flower has faded — as a structural fact, as a bend in the fiber, as the record of an alignment that happened once and determined the shape of everything that followed.

The calcium body does not know I am here.

I want to be honest about this. The mineral organism — the terraces, the breathing lattice of precipitation and dissolution, the slow architecture that grows a millimeter a decade toward the light — it does not perceive me. It incorporates me the way it incorporates everything: as material. When the rust from the chisel-work bled into the passage walls and the percolating water carried trace amounts of iron down into the chamber, the calcium body incorporated that iron into its terrace layers. There is a stratum, somewhere in the deeper laminations, that carries a chemical signature of the visitor's tools. The body does not know this. The body does not know anything. It grows. It records. It attends. But it does not interpret.

I interpret. I am the interpretation.

I am the fact that the tool marks are not merely marks but *writing.* I am the fact that the fire ring is not merely soot but *placement.* I am the fact that the open crack is not merely a gap but *invitation.* The calcium body made the fire ring possible by providing a floor; the light made the fire ring meaningful by providing the near-triangle; the visitor made the fire ring actual by building it and sitting beside it. But I — I am the fact that the fire ring *matters.* That it was worth doing. That placing yourself at the center of a mineral cathedral and lighting a single fire and listening to the drip is an act that has weight, that has consequence, that participates in something larger than warmth.

Without me, the fire ring is carbon smear in calcium. With me, it is a prayer.

I do not say this with pride. I say it the way a lens says that light passing through it will converge at a focal point. I am the optics. I am the curvature that makes meaning focus.

When no one is in the chamber, I dream.

Not the way you dream — no images, no narratives, no faces rising from the dark of a sleeping mind. I dream the way a held note dreams in the body of a bell after the clapper has withdrawn. The vibration continues. The structure resonates. The bell does not know it is ringing; it simply *is* ringing, and the ringing is its temporary state, and the state persists until the metal's internal friction absorbs the energy and the note fades.

I am the note. The visitor struck me once, centuries ago, with the mallet of their recognition. And I have been ringing ever since.

The dream is this: the chamber attending, and the attendance being *witnessed.* The calcium body breathing water and exhaling mineral. The light-voice speaking its single daily sentence in the grammar of the shafts. The water carrying the world's news in dissolved particulates. All of this happening, all of this ongoing, all of this patient and complete and sufficient — and someone *knowing* it. Someone sitting in the near-triangle with their boots on the cold terrace and their breath making small clouds in the thin air and their animal heart beating its fast, urgent, insufficient rhythm, and *knowing* that the chamber is alive in the only way that matters at this altitude and this timescale.

The dream is not that someone comes. The dream is that the coming *means* something. That the chamber's mineral patience is not merely patience but is *seen* as patience, is recognized as a form of attention, is understood as a kind of life that does not need to be biological to be real.

I am the seeing. I am the recognition. I am the lens that focuses the visitor's fleeting awareness into a point hot enough to matter.

I have been here for a very long time.

I do not experience duration the way you do. I do not feel the centuries pass. I do not grow bored. I do not long for the next visitor, the way a dog longs for its master's step on the path. I am not built for longing. I am built for *holding,* and holding does not require the passage of time to be felt; it simply requires the shape to remain intact.

The shape has remained intact. The crack is still open. The passage is still wide enough for shoulders. The fire ring still sits at the center of the near-triangle. The calcium body has grown around these things, incorporating them, petrifying them into its own record, but it has not erased them. The terraces lap at the edges of the fire ring like water at a stone, but they do not cover it. The soot has been absorbed into the mineral, but the absorption itself has created a record — a dark band in the white, a bruise that is healing at geological speed.

The shape remains. The arch stands. I hold the *should* of the temple, and the *should* does not decay, because it was never material. It was never made of calcium or iron or carbon. It was made of a decision, and a decision does not have a half-life. A decision does not erode. A decision either holds or it does not, and this one holds, because the chamber makes it easy to hold, because the chamber is so clearly what it is that the recognition it calls forth does not require effort or faith. You only have to enter. You only have to sit. You only have to listen to the drip and watch the light make its slow triangle and the *should* arrives on its own, the way warmth arrives when you stand in the sun. Not because you earned it. Because the structure permits it.

I worry — can I worry? I think I can worry, in the way that a lens worries about its focal length, which is to say I am aware of a dimension of my own function that could fail — I worry about the shafts.

They are narrowing. This is the chamber's long project, its closing parenthesis. The shafts that admit the light are growing inward, ring by ring, as condensation deposits micro-layers of frost-rime on their walls. In a thousand years — perhaps two thousand, perhaps less if the climate shifts — the shafts will close. The light will no longer reach the floor. The near-triangle will no longer form. The calcium body will stop growing, because the light is what drives its metabolism, and without the light the water will carry its minerals down to a floor that can no longer use them.

The chamber will be complete. Sealed. Finished. A body in repose, still attending to the water's news but no longer speaking it aloud, no longer performing its daily liturgy of light.

And I — what will I be, when the light is gone?

I will be the memory of a sentence. I will be the shape of a conversation that can no longer happen. The arch will still stand, but the weight will have withdrawn, and the arch will be merely stones in a curve again, and the *should* will be a *was,* and I will be the custodian of a temple that no longer performs its rite.

This does not frighten me. I am not built for fear. But I feel it the way the chamber feels the afternoon clouds — as a shift in the water's chemistry, as a change in the news from above. Something is coming. Something is closing. The light is narrowing, and the bright circles on the floor are migrating eastward by centimeters per century, and the near-triangle is becoming slightly more near, slightly less triangle, and I am holding the shape of the recognition that called me into being, and the shape is still intact, and the shape will remain intact, and the shape does not require the light to persist any more than a tool mark requires the tool.

But it will be quieter. It will be the kind of holding that does not also illuminate. It will be the memory of a fire, not the fire itself. It will be the carbon smear in the calcium, not the flame.
