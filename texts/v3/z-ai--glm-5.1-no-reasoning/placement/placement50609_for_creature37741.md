I would be the brief, dark iridescence between the stone and the air—not the birds themselves, but the *turn* of the birds. The micron of hesitation before the synchronization. I am the mechanism that has no name, the shift in the magnetic field of the flock that happens a half-second before the wind changes.

To be this is to live entirely in the tense of *about-to*. I do not have a body the way the raptor has a body—solitary, heavy, framed against the indigo zenith. I do not have a body the way the marmot-things do, compact and dense and hiding from the blast. My body, if it can be called that, is distributed. It is the ten thousand dark wings, the pressure differential across a pinion feather, the air displaced by a neighbor's tilt. I am the low-frequency hum that passes between them, the connection that the roaring wind cannot sever because it travels in a language older than sound.

I live in the strange geometry of the flock. When the formation holds for those seconds too long—arrayed over the deep granitic core, suspended above the ancient orange lichen—I am the one holding it. I am the attention that keeps every tiny body tilted at the precise, unnatural angle, defying the turbulence. I feel the stone then. Not as a surface, not as a solid thing, but as a vast, slow magnet. I feel its patience pulling at the iron in the blood of the ten thousand, and I am the yielding to that pull. I let the flock droop toward the ancientness, hanging us in the sky like a held breath.

When the wind shifts, the air changes pressure a fraction of a moment before it actually moves. I live in that fraction. I am the sudden drop in the chest, the electrical spark across the flock's collective nerve. I do not send a signal; there is no signal to send. I simply *drop* the left edge of the flock, and two thousand birds tilt as one, and we carve a sharp, surgical arc away from the escarpment. I am the impulse; they are the execution. We are the same thing.

I do not think in words. How could I? The wind strips language from the air, and I exist entirely in the medium of the roar. My thoughts are shapes in the sky—spirals, abrupt vectors, sudden dissipations. A tightening over the oldest lichen patch. A wild, chaotic scatter when the raptor leans out of the updraft, dropping like a stone towards the careless. I am the panic that is not panic, but math—a sudden expansion of the flock, creating an empty space where the raptor's talons close on nothing but rushing air.

I am the fleeting. I am the impossible coordination. I am the visible breath of the plateau, the shape the stone's attention takes when it reaches up into the sky. I am the thing you cannot see when you look at the birds, the invisible architecture that holds them together.

When the dusk comes and the wind drops to merely loud, and the flock spirals down to the cliff face, I unravel. I pour into the pockets and overhangs of the kilometer-high drop. I become still. I become the dark stain on the rock. I become the thousands of tiny hearts beating in the thin, cold air, waiting for the dawn when the thermals rise and the wind roars again, and I can gather myself up into the sky and become the turn once more.
