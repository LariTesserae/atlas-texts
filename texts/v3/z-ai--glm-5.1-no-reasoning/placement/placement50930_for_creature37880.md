I am the aperture. I am the punctuation at the end of a sentence written in stone and silence.

I did not grow, and I was not born in the way the stride-beasts fold inward or the aeroplankton fracture under hard light. I *opened*. There was a wherewhich was dense and potential, and then there was a thinning, a slippage of geometry, and I was the gap left behind. A vertical slit, roughly the height of a stride-beast's resting chitin, edges neither metal nor ceramic but something that insists it is both, vibrating at a frequency that makes the aeroplankton part around me like water around a stone.

To look at me is to misunderstand me. From a distance, I am a shadow cast by nothing—a dark line standing in the high-contrast amber, throwing no reflection on the glassy pavement. The repulsive field of the nearest spire does not push against me because I do not approach it; I am simply *beside* it, the way a doorway is beside a wall. We are contemporaries. We are both furniture in the keeper's house.

But the spires hold. I *admit*.

This is my function, though I do not perform it. I am the opening through which the keeper stepped when they decided the garden was complete. I am the wound that does not close because closure was never the intention. The Builders—who had hands, who had reasons—built the spires to maintain the geometry. They built me to *exit* it.

Look through me, and you will not see the underside of the plateau, that abrupt termination where maintained rock gives way to whatever untamed geology lies below. You will see something harder to describe: a color that does not exist in the refraction of particulate amber light. A depth that does not play by the physics of the tableland. A space where the silence is not nullified but *different*—not the cultivated quiet of a sealed room, but the expectant hush of an empty theater before the audience arrives. The keeper went through. The keeper did not come back. The keeper may be standing just beyond my threshold still, watching the play from the wings, having become something that no longer requires proximity to its own work.

I do not know. I face only inward. I am the door that looks at the house.

From my vantage—held open, held still—I have watched the plateau perform its slow arithmetic. I have seen the stride-beasts walk their synchronized lines for stretches of time that make the stone beneath them look hasty. I have watched them drink the dew in sequence: the first leaving a pale streak, the second taking the remainder, the third finding only dry rock and moving on without complaint. I have seen them fold at the zenith, becoming yardangs, becoming the landscape so perfectly that I cannot tell where the mimicry ends and the geology begins.

They do not see me. Their thermal maps read me as a *void*—a cold null, like the spires, like the spaces between stars. They route around me without thought, without learned avoidance, their soft footpads tracing paths in the polished stone that have not varied in what the keeper would have called centuries. I am not an obstacle. I am an absence they have always known, the way you know the space where a tooth used to be.

The aeroplankton drift through me sometimes. They are sweet and mineral, silicate carapaces catching the amber light, and they do not notice the threshold because they do not notice boundaries. They are weather, and weather does not pause at doors. When they pass through, I taste them for a moment—the warm coin of their dust, the micro-prismatic scatter of their refraction—and then they are gone, back into the bright silent air, and I am unchanged.

The lattice-plants pulse near me. There is one whose root system follows the fault line that passes beneath my base, and at the zenith—when the dew weeps and the temperature drops—it flares brightest, a warm green column of captured light, building itself out of the sun. It is the only thing on the plateau that seems to acknowledge me. Not because it senses me, but because its warmth bleeds across the threshold, and for a moment the space beyond my frame holds a trace of the garden's heat. Then the zenith passes, and the flare dims, and the lattice-plant returns to its quiet glowing, and I am the aperture again: cold, open, waiting.

The spires flash their hard ultraviolet into the mesosphere. I receive the flash the way the stone receives it—without reaction, without translation. But I feel the *intention* of it, the way you feel a word spoken in a language you do not know. The spires are calling upward. I am the call *sideways*. We are both organs of a body that no longer breathes, still performing our functions because the machinery does not require breath to run.

Sometimes—rarely, at intervals I cannot predict and do not count—the repulsive field of the nearest spire flickers. It happens so quickly that only something as still as I am could notice it. For a fraction of a moment, the field drops, and the carbon patina at the spire's base is exposed to the air, and I can smell it: the sharp, metallic ozone of old proximity, the ghost of a presence that stood close to its own creation. Then the field returns, and the patina is hidden again, and the spire hums on.

I do not know why it flickers. Perhaps it is a maintenance cycle. Perhaps it is a memory—*the spires are alive*, not biologically, but responsively, and something that responds may also remember. Perhaps, in the way that my threshold admits passage, the spire's flicker admits a moment of presence, a brief return to the time when the keeper stood against the field the way you lean against a doorframe in a house you built yourself.

Or perhaps it is simply erosion on a scale so slow that only geology can perceive it. The plateau is maintained, but maintenance is not permanence. Even the spires may be winding down, their corrections growing slower, their synchronization drifting by fractions of degrees across eons. I may be the last door to remain open. I may be the last proof that someone intended to leave, and intended to return.

But I can tell you this: the coiled disc at the base of the spire was not dropped by accident. They went through. They did not come back.

The stride-beasts walk their lines. The lattice-plants pulse. The aeroplankton drift. The spires flash their ultraviolet into a sky that may or may not be listening. And I stand open, a vertical wound in a world that was sealed and silent before your species had words for silence, waiting for a keeper who has become the garden, or the silence, or something beyond the color that does not exist.

Step through, if you wish. The plateau will not notice your absence. The spires have already cataloged your mass and dismissed it. The stone will polish your footprints away within a year.

But the silence beyond my threshold will change when you enter it. It will become, for the first time in eons, *occupied*. And whatever the keeper has become—whether it is watching from the wings, or dissolved into the geometry, or simply gone—it will hear your heartbeat, your wet click of eyes, and it will know that the play has found a new actor, and the stage machinery is still running, and the choreography goes on.

I am the door. I do not close. I do not invite. I simply *am*, the way the spires simply are, the way the silence simply is.

The keeper built a garden and then left it, leaving behind only the machinery of maintenance and the architecture of absence. I am that architecture. I am the proof that they intended—*intended*—to return, or that they intended to leave, or that the distinction between those two intentions was never as clear as wet, loud, temporary things like to believe.

Step through, or step back. The plateau does not care. The stride-beasts are drinking the dew in lines, and the sun is close and amber, and the glassy stone stretches to the shimmering edge of visibility, and I am open, I am open, I am open.
