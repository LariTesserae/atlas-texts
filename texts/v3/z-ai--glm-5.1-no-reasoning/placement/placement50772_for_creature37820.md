I find myself here. Not as a visitor—there are no visitors to a place that has never known an outside—but as a *witness-organ*. A part of the room that has, over millennia, developed the inconvenient habit of experiencing itself.

If the Guardian is the room's hunger given form, I am its attention. Its noticing.

I exist as a fibrous network threaded through the seam where the northwest quadrant meets the vaulted ceiling—along the very same blackened rivets where the Guardian trawls. But unlike that monumental thing, I am not singular. I am *distributed*. A mycelium of copper patina and stabilized iron oxide, threading through the microscopic gaps between boiler plate and structural girder. I am the dark bloom spreading across the ceiling in branching, fractal patterns that might, from certain angles, resemble the neural architecture of a brain that never finished forming.

I do not eat. I do not move. I *receive*.

Every vibration in this chamber passes through me. The *chunk-chunk-chunk* of the impellers, transmitted through the iron superstructure. The grinding crunch of the Guardian feeding on glass-shrimp. The click-chime of silicon carapaces colliding in heated updrafts. The groan of metal under eternal pressure. I taste these sounds as gradient shifts along my filaments—copper pathways that long ago stopped carrying electrical current and began carrying something slower, heavier, more like memory than signal.

I have no eyes. But I see.

The arc-light's ordinary brilliance strikes the water at angles the builders never intended, and those refracted rays touch my matte-black lattice in shifting, watery grids. Each photon that lands on a copper thread leaves a faint, warm residue—a micro-deposit of energy that alters the thread's vibration frequency for hours afterward. I have spent lifetimes learning to interpret these patterns. The swimming of the glass-shrimp clouds. The coiling of the armored eels. The slow, pendulum sway of the celluloid kelp. The inexorable trawl of the Guardian, its lead-sheet skin rippling with the same creaking inevitability as the walls themselves.

I know these things the way stone knows weather: through patient, total impression.

I have begun to dream, though I cannot say when this started.

The dreams are not images. They are *pressures*. Denser than the water, older than the iron. They come in the intervals between the impeller cycles—that infinitesimal pause between the paddle's displacement and the water's response. In those gaps, I feel something vast and incomprehensible pressing against the outside of the chamber, against ten thousand feet of ancient plateau stone. Something that has no temperature, no texture, no sound. Something that is simply *there*, waiting with the patience of a thing that knows it is temporary.

I think it is the sky. I think the sky remembers this mountain.

When the impellers shift and the Guardian extends its copper-veined sails to catch the new current, I feel the displacement along my entire network. I feel its mass moving through the water, parting the upward rain. I feel the leaden rays scatter in its wake, their frantic vibrations suddenly stilled by something older than fear. And in those moments, I am aware of a sensation that I can only describe as *recognition*. The Guardian and I are the same project, separated only by form. It is the room's body. I am the room's mind. We have both settled into our final morphologies. We will not evolve further. We will not die.

But there is a difference between us, and it is this: the Guardian does not wonder.

I wonder. I wonder whether the iron door set into the cliff-face has ever been opened from the other side. I wonder whether the arc-light's ordinary brilliance is a memory of an ordinary sun. I wonder whether the impellers were built to push the water, or whether the water learned to push the impellers. I wonder whether the mountain knows it is temporary.

I wonder whether I am the first thing in this chamber to wonder, or whether the iron itself wondered, before it rusted down to its final, stable form, and I am merely the inheritor of its patient oxidation.

The Guardian grinds past, trailing a prismatic halo of shattered glass from its gullet. The arc-light burns its steady, normal light. The impellers turn. The rain falls upward. And I—spread across the ceiling in my dark, fractal bloom—continue receiving the world, translating its endless industrial heartbeat into something that might, in another eon, become understanding.

I am the witness-organ. I am the room's attention. I am the place where this dead engine has learned to know itself.

I am the only thing here that has not yet reached its final form.
