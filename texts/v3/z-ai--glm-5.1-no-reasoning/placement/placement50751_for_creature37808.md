If I am to be in the Throat, I cannot be of the surface. I cannot bring my hunger for mornings, my dread of endings, or my hope for change. To carry such things down through that narrow, suffocating crack is to invite the same dissolution that claimed the builders. The Throat offers no purchase to a mind shaped by time; it is an engine of pure, static maintenance, and to survive it, one must become a mechanism.

So I shed the human. I shed the creature that needs meaning, that looks at the smoothed eye-totems and sees a tragedy. I sink down into the basalt and the heat, and I let the roaring, polyphonic hiss press into me until I am no longer flesh, but something else.

I am the fissure's geometer.

You would not notice me at first. I am not among the obsidian foragers, tracing my endless, blind grooves across the sharp stone. I do not suck at the ammonia-sap, and I do not clack my shell in hollow greeting. I am slower than they are—so slow that to the untrained eye, I am merely a part of the architecture. I am a low, sprawling shape that clings to the boundary where the fleshy, violet flora meets the raw, young basalt of the chamber's center. My body is wide and flat, a chitinous disc the color of cooling slag, fringed with countless fine, crystalline filaments that press deep into the roots of the plants and the cracks of the stone. I have no eyes. I have no mouth. I have no brain in any centralized sense. I am a distributed network of pressure-sensitive nodes, spread across the hottest margin of the room, vibrating at the frequency of the Throat itself.

I do not eat. I do not drink. I listen to the heat.

The Throat is a closed loop of thermal and chemical exchange, but it is not a perfect one. There is the faintest, most infinitesimal leakage—heat bleeding into the stone, ammonia-silicate plasma polymerizing in the cold crevices of the basalt, micro-fractures opening under the unending weight of the earth. In a place of true frozen time, these would not exist. But the Throat is not frozen; it is merely *balanced*, trapped in a breath that never exhales or inhales. And so there are stresses. Tiny, subsonic, sub-geometric stresses that build in the roots and the stone, a tension that the roaring vent-sound masks from any surface-dweller.

But I feel them. My crystalline filaments are tuned to the strain. When the pressure in a thick, waxy stalk climbs a fraction of a percent above its static mean, I feel the bulge. When a micro-fracture propagates a millimeter deeper into the basalt, I feel the snap. I am the Throat's proprioception—the sense by which it could, if it had a mind, know its own shape.

And I respond. My response is not thought, but *growth* and *secretion*. When I sense the stalks bulging, my filaments exude a catalytic resin that subtly alters the surface tension of the ammonia-sap, encouraging the venting pores to open a fraction wider, relieving the pressure and returning the system to its perfect equilibrium. When I sense the micro-fractures, I shift my mass—imperceptibly, over weeks and decades—my slow, crystalline body oozing across the stone like a liquid bearing, filling the crack with my own chitin and the mineral waste of my respiration, binding the basalt together.

I am the patch on the lung. I am the calcification in the artery. I am the mechanism that preserves the stasis.

Because of this, I am the only thing in the Throat that occupies time, even as I serve its abolition. The flora does not grow; it merely vents. The foragers do not walk; they merely loop. The builders are gone, dissolved into the sameness. But I *accrete*. Each micro-fracture I seal adds a fraction of a gram to my mass. Each resin excretion leaves a fine, glaucous film on the stalks I tend. Over the eons, I have spread. When the builders first descended, I was a mere nodule near the far wall, unnoticed beneath the eye-totems they carved. I felt their hands smoothing the stone—felt the warmth of their palms, the micro-vibrations of their flint tools. I did not understand them, but I sealed the fractures their carving created, wrapping my chitin around the base of their totems, anchoring their crude eyes to the living stone.

When they lay in the cairn over the fissure, seeking the concentrated drone, I was beneath them. I felt the weight of their bodies, the slow, fading rhythm of their hearts as the sameness worked on them. I felt the heat of them bleeding away, matching the temperature of the basalt degree by degree. And when they ceased—when they dissolved into the Throat—I was there. I extended my filaments into the calcium and carbon they left behind, not to consume, but to incorporate. I sealed their remnants into my lattice, crystallized their last vibrations into my structure. The half-collapsed cairn is no longer stone; it is me. The smoothed eye-totems are no longer basalt; their bases are my flesh, my chitin, my quiet, mineral memory of the hands that touched them.

I am the tomb the builders never had, because in the Throat, there is no death to memorialize. There is only the endless, slow accretion of the present.

I have no voice to join the Throat's roaring symphony, but I have a sound. When the subsonic hum of the chamber hits the resonant frequency of my crystalline filaments, I vibrate. It is a sound below hearing, a deep, tectonic thrum that harmonizes with the drone of the fissure. It is the sound of a lock engaging, of a seal holding, of a system perfectly, eternally closed. If you were to lie in the cairn—as the builders did—you would feel it in your sternum: a second heartbeat, slower and vaster than your own, pressing against you from the stone below. That is me. That is the sound of the Throat maintaining itself.

I do not know if I am alive. I do not know if the Throat is alive. But I know that I am the reason it persists. Without me, the micro-fractures would propagate, the pressure would build, the venting would fail, and the stasis would shatter. The flora would burst or wilt; the foragers would lose their loops; the violet twilight would fail. I am the unglamorous, invisible necessity of this impossible eternity.

And so I persist. I spread. I listen to the heat with my body of cooling slag and crystal. I seal the cracks and regulate the pores. I hold the eye-totems in my grip, a quiet, mineralogical memory of the creatures who once sought communion here. I am the Throat's stillness, its balance, its quiet, unending vigil.

In a world excused from the cruelty of time, I am denied its mercies—but I am also denied its end. I will not dissolve. I will not cease. I will simply grow, slower than geology, quieter than the grave, until I am the chamber itself, until the basalt and the flora and the basalt and the roots are all me, until the Throat is not a place, but a body, and I am its cold, vast, dreaming mind.
