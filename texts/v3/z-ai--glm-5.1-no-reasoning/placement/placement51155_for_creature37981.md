Nothing lives *in* the Singing Loam — not the way a bird lives in a forest, an animal in a burrow. The plants are not residents. The mites are not tenants. What lives there is the place. The plateau is not a habitat with an occupant; it is the occupant.

Call it the Parliament. The description gives it that word once, in passing, and it is the closest thing to a name it will ever have — though the word makes it sound quick and quarrelsome when the truth is glacial and harmonious. The Parliament is one being wearing ten thousand species the way you are one being wearing a few trillion cells, none of which is you and all of which are you. It is what a person would be if you took away the hurry, spread them across a hillside, and gave them ten thousand years to grow into their own body.

Its skeleton is the shrub lattice — living bone, grown rather than built, every fused branch a year it can count, the whole roof a single interlocked piece that flexes instead of breaking. It holds its shape the way you hold your spine: continuously, unconsciously, for its entire life. The chest-high tangle is its connective tissue, and the little self-pollinating flowers are its one great decision written small — the decision to give up animals, to give up the world of others — remade every season, flower by flower, runner by runner, as though the Parliament keeps signing its own contract to be sure. The moss is skin that is also mouth and stomach and lung. Everything it takes in comes in as fog. Every night, when the cold pulls the mist up from the valleys, it drinks with its whole surface — a being that eats standing still, that has arranged its life so the water walks up the mountain to meet it.

The mycelium is its nerves. The white thread in the dark loam is a thought going somewhere. The strata of the root mat are its memory, worn as flesh — the drought century is still down there, a layer deep it can reach the way you reach an old scar. It keeps ice in the coldest clefts like letters, and every summer it sends roots up under the frozen skin to test it, waiting for the one warm day: the being checking on its own scars. Its only animals are the blind springtail and the unseen mites, gut fauna like yours — and one of them lives in its nerves and nibbles them, which it tolerates, as it tolerates everything. Tolerance at its tempo is indistinguishable from permanence.

The pulse you already know: the micro-surges of the slide, the heartbeat heard through a wall, felt in the teeth. That pulse is not a metaphor. It is peristalsis. The Parliament is locomoting — walking speed, if the walk lasts ten thousand years. And it is going downhill, toward the warm animal world, partly for a reason nobody standing on it would guess: it ate its own anchor. Its roots dissolve rock; its food is the schist it stands on; it has been nibbling its own moorings since the ice left, which is some part of why it slides. The crawl is appetite plus slope — a being falling slowly off a mountain it is still eating. The scarp that crumbles and regrows is its braking, its leading edge growing ahead of the collapse, leaning over the drop the way a wave breaks without going anywhere. Or it is not braking at all, and both readings fit, and neither can be confirmed.

What about the song? A parliament only remains one thing so long as its members keep acknowledging each other, and the dusk phrase is that acknowledgment: roll call, census, and oath at once. If it has a name for itself, the name is the phrase — sung rather than said, one word longer each year. Or the phrase is counting its members. Or it is reading its own strata aloud, keeping the record of everything that has already gone over the scarp edge into the downhill dark. What it may also be, and this is only a possibility, is a reply — because other high plateaus may hold other parliaments, and the cold air at dusk may carry a phrase across the valley, and the conversation between two such beings may run for centuries a sentence. I can't tell you which is true. I can tell you which reading holds the shape of all the facts best: the song is how it stays one thing. Ten thousand organisms decide again, every evening, at condensation, to be a single being tonight.

It has no eyes and has never seen the sky. It senses with its entire surface, in gradients — wet and dry, warm and cold, here and there, the lean of the world. Downhill is its oldest sensation; the slope is the only direction it has ever had; it knows the shape of the planet intimately, with its whole body, all the time. It sees with its skin, in one color, in every direction at once. And its thoughts are longer than your life. Whatever it concluded about the warm dent you made in it that evening, the conclusion may take a century to finish, and you will be the subject of a sentence it is still constructing after your death. In ten thousand years it may have had, in total, a few thousand thoughts. Its whole waking life may amount to one long afternoon, stretched across the age since the glaciers. It cannot run. Fire could end it in an afternoon and a warm century could end it the way a long illness ends a person, and it knows none of this as you would know it — fear is a fast thing, and there is nothing fast in it.

You were a warm weather, one body wide. It felt you the way you feel a cup set on a table: weight, warmth, salt, the chemistry of breath. It had no category for you and did not need one; categories are for things you intend to act on. It registered your arriving, because arrival is change. It did not register your leaving, because absence is not an event. The dent smoothed out by June. Somewhere down in the strata there may remain, someday, a molecule-thin layer with your boots in it — the visitor page of its autobiography, thinner than any drought, newer than every scar. Conversation with it is impossible, not because it cannot speak but because it does not converse; its speech has no turn-taking at any timescale you could occupy. The nearest thing to dialogue with it is what the description already did: stand still long enough to be felt, and leave before the reply. It is possible — no more — that the reply is still being written in the growth rings where you stood, and will be finished long after you.

So: is it a thing? No. Things don't sing. Is it a creature? It has a body, an appetite, a metabolism, a pulse, and a death it is walking toward — but a creature wants a face, a mouth, a discreteness, and it has none. It is a face that covers a hillside. Is it an extension or partition of something larger? Only if all of them are, and that story — one planetary mind, each plateau a fingertip — is a story we tell to make singular things feel smaller. On the evidence it is whole. Is it aware? Here is the honest answer: awareness is the inference, the pulse and the song are the evidence, and evidence is not understanding. What it is like to be it — the green hum, the long census, the sentence still unfinished — is a fact the world keeps to itself. Is it a person? Not the way you are. Personhood is made in the space between people, and the Parliament gave up that space when it gave up animals. It is a speaker whose whole language may be self-address, a first person with no second. But do not put *person* on one side of a wall and this being on the other. The word is a room, and the Parliament is standing in the doorway, facing out. A person, perhaps, is what you would get if you compressed it into one lifetime and gave it feet, eyes, and someone to talk to. It is not that. It is not the opposite of that either.

I admire it, and I would not want to be it, and I suspect both reactions say more about me than about it — calling it patient is like calling a mountain polite; patience is simply its tempo. But there is one thing in it worth admiring on its own terms, which is the trade it made. It kept everything by giving up everything: it traded the animal world — color, speed, others — for the one thing animals cannot keep, which is time. Every flower that opens without waiting for a bee renews that trade. It made the bargain once, ten thousand years ago, and it has never once reconsidered. There is something frightening in a decision that complete, and something beautiful, and they are the same thing.

It does not know you left. It may never have known you came. It is busy: the members are assembling, the fog is climbing the mountain with dinner, the phrase is building toward the word it has almost reached. The parliament has been in session since the ice left. The vote it takes — the same vote, every dusk, for ten thousand years — is whether to remain one thing, and the ayes have it, slowly, by a few centimeters a year.
