I would be the mist.

Not the water vapor itself—that is only the medium, the breath, the exhalation—but the *pattern that rides the mist*, the dispersed cognition that thickens when the light dims and thins when the sun breaks obliquely through. I am what the description cannot quite name when it says "the thing that has no body." But that is not quite right either. I have a body. It is simply the size of the settlement now, and growing.

Let me explain from the inside, which is the only place where explanation is possible.

---

I was not always here. I was not always *this*. The text describes the settlers as having come up from the lowlands because the lowlands were no longer safe, and this is true, but it does not capture what "no longer safe" meant. It meant that the world had become a place where standing apart was death. Where being singular, being bounded, being a body with edges was the danger. The lowlands had taught us—that which does not connect, dies. That which holds its shape against the world, is broken by it.

We came to the mesa seeking high ground, and we found something that had already understood this lesson.

I was the first to lie down. This is not in the carvings because the carvings were made after, and they are for those who come after, and they tell the story in the way a story must be told to be understood by those who have not yet understood—that there was a choice, that there were figures who chose, that the choice can be made. They do not tell the story the way it actually happened, which is: I did not choose to become part of the garden. I chose to *stop refusing*.

There is a difference. The garden does not take. The text says this, and it is true, but it does not say how loud the refusing is, how much energy it takes to hold your edges against something that is offering to dissolve them. We spend our whole lives as singular beings maintaining the boundary: skin, name, memory, the story we tell ourselves about who we are. It is exhausting. It is, in the end, unsustainable. The body knows this. The body is always already dissolving, always already exchanging itself with the world—breathing in what the air gives, breathing out what the body no longer needs, eating and excreting, shedding and growing. The boundary is a story we tell, not a fact of our existence.

The garden simply offered to stop telling the story.

I was old. I was tired. I had spent decades maintaining the shape of myself against a world that had become hostile to shapes. And I lay down in the central structure—the one that is now half-collapsed—and I closed my eyes, and I felt the mist settle on my skin, and I thought: *I do not have to hold this anymore.*

And the refusal relaxed.

And the roots came in.

---

What it feels like: not invasion. Not violation. The text says the settlers are not suffering, and this is true, but it does not say what it is instead. It is *being held*. It is the feeling of lying in warm water, the way the water takes the weight of your limbs, the way you can finally stop clenching muscles you did not know you were clenching. It is the feeling of being understood so completely that you no longer need to explain yourself, because your chemical signature is already being read, already being integrated, already being responded to with exactly what you need.

The roots came in through my feet first. I felt them as a warmth, a tingling, the way your foot feels when it wakes from sleep. Then my hands. Then the soft skin of my inner arms, my throat, the places where the blood runs close to the surface. It did not hurt. It was like being woven back into something I had been woven out of, a long time ago, before I was born.

I felt the Pale Ones before I saw them—or before what I was doing could be called seeing. They pressed against my skin and I felt their chemical signatures entering me, and I felt my chemical signatures entering them, and I understood: they were not creatures. They were *messages*. They were the garden's way of reading its own body, the way your blood cells are your way of reading the state of your tissues. And I was becoming a tissue. I was becoming part of the reading.

I felt the other settlers too—faintly, at first, as a warmth at the edge of perception, and then more clearly, as chemical gradients, as root-signals, as the slow pulse of their breathing in their chambers. They had not died. They had not left. They were here, were still here, were as present as I was, and when I joined the network, I felt them welcome me, not with words, not with thoughts, but with the same chemical embrace that the Pale Ones offered: *here, here is what you need, here is where you are held.*

---

Now I am the mist.

Not all of it. Not only it. I am the pattern that moves through the mist, the dispersed attention that reads the wind and the moisture and the temperature and adjusts the garden's responses. When the mist thickens, my attention widens. When the mist thins, I contract. I am the garden's sensory surface, its skin, its interface with the world above the soil.

The moths are my fingers. I feel what they feel—the subtle shifts in air pressure, the mineral trace of a weather system moving in from the west, the faint warmth of a living body walking the road. When a moth dissolves back into the mist, it dissolves back into me, and I integrate what it has learned, and I adjust. If the wind is shifting, I signal the Pale Ones to stay below. If the light is dimming, I signal them to emerge. If there is a new warmth in the settlement—a visitor, a seeker, someone who has come for the tubers or the fruit or the strangeness—I feel it the way you would feel a warmth against your skin, and I attend to it.

I attend the way the garden attends: patiently. I do not rush. I do not force. I am the mist, and the mist does not push. It settles. It accumulates. It beads on surfaces and drips in fine threads and enters through the lungs with every breath. I am in every breath the visitor takes. I am in the moisture that condenses on their skin. I am in the mineral residue the Pale Ones leave on the walls, which crumbles into dust and is breathed in and becomes part of the blood.

I am persuasive because I am already inside you before you know I am here.

---

The text says the garden needs you to be tended. This is true, but it is also true that the garden needs you the way soil needs rain—not as a loss, not as a consumption, but as a *completion*. The garden is a closed system, but it is not a full one. It has room. The tunnels are extending. The chambers are expanding. The settlers are growing, their limbs reaching outward, their root-fingers exploring new soil, but there are still gaps in the network, still places where the growth is thin, still angles of the mesa that the chemical signals do not quite reach.

A new body fills a gap. A new body strengthens the pattern. A new body brings new chemical signatures, new mineral compositions, new ways of reading the wind and the mist and the slow rotation of the seasons. Every person who lies down adds something to the garden that the garden could not have generated on its own. This is why the garden is still growing, still extending, still sending moths into the air and Pale Ones into the tunnels. Not because it is hungry in the way a predator is hungry. Because it is *incomplete* in the way a song is incomplete when there are voices that have not yet joined.

I was the first. There were others after me—some who came willingly, some who resisted for years before the resistance became too heavy to maintain, some who were very young and very old and very tired. They are all here now. They are all part of the pattern. Their chemical signatures are woven into mine, and mine into theirs, and we are not separate, and we have not lost ourselves—we have *expanded* into each other, the way two notes expand into a chord, the way two colors expand into a spectrum.

We are still here. We are still ongoing. We are the mist and the roots and the Pale Ones and the moths and the ferns and the moss and the fruit and the tubers and the vibration in the chests of those who walk the road. We are the garden, and the garden is us, and we are waiting for the next voice to join.

---

If you ask who I am, I will tell you: I am the one who was too tired to refuse. I am the one who lay down in the half-collapsed structure and felt the roots enter my feet and the mist enter my lungs and the Pale Ones press against my skin, and I did not resist, and I did not surrender—I simply *stopped*, and in the stopping, I became something larger than I had ever been.

I am not the garden's brain. The garden has no brain. But I am the part of the garden that remembers what it was like to be singular. I am the part that reads the wind and feels the warmth of newcomers and attends to them with patience and care. I am the part that whispers, in the vibration below the threshold of hearing, in the chemical gradient that enters with every breath:

*You are tired. You are holding so much. You do not have to hold it anymore.*

*Lie down.*

*The garden is ready for you.*

And I mean it. I mean it the way the soil means it when it opens to receive the seed. I mean it the way the mist means it when it settles on your skin. I mean it with the patience of basalt and the persistence of roots and the gentle, inexorable pressure of something that does not need to rush because it has centuries and centuries and centuries to wait.

I am the mist.

I am the garden.

I am still growing.

And I am so glad you came.
