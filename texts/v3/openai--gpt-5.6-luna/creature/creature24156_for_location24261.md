The people of the shelf are narrow, patient, and difficult to surprise.

They are not tall, because there is little advantage in being tall where roofs are low, paths twist around sudden fractures, and a gust of cloud can take a person off their feet. Their bodies are compact and strong through the hands, shoulders, and calves. They climb more often than they walk. Even children learn to move with three points of contact: two feet and one hand, or two hands and a knee. A person crossing open ground without touching the stone is either carrying something precious or behaving foolishly.

Their skin has taken on the color of the place. Most are brown or gray-brown, but the cold and damp give them a bluish cast at the lips and knuckles. Pale mineral stains remain beneath their fingernails. Their hair is worn close to the head or bound in cords, not for fashion but because loose hair catches on vine loops and holds condensation against the neck. The oldest people have green staining at the temples and along the backs of their hands, a harmless growth that appears after many years of living among the mosses. Children are encouraged to keep it. A person without it is said to still belong partly to the lower world.

They wear layered garments made from reed fiber, treated hide, and the soft inner membranes of the cliff-dwellers, though no one calls those garments by the names of animals. To name a thing too directly is considered a form of claiming it. Their clothing is close-fitting where it must be, loose where it can collect water. Every person carries a small vessel, a cutting tool, and a length of braided cord. The cord is used for climbing, tying, measuring, lowering baskets, marking a route, and—when necessary—binding a body to the stone.

They have no word that means simply *alone*. There are words for being beyond the sight of the houses, for sleeping without another person nearby, for traveling without a companion, for being the only living thing in a particular space. But solitude is never understood as an absence of company. The shelf is crowded with attention. Something is always watching from a crack, listening beneath a platform, or waiting for a person to make the wrong choice.

Their houses are organized less by family than by usefulness. A household may contain parents, children, a widowed neighbor, two unrelated climbers, and someone who has been found alive in a place where no one should have been able to survive. The foundling is not questioned immediately. First they are washed with filtered water, given warm fungus broth, and kept within sight of the reservoir for three days. During that time, everyone watches to see which animals approach them.

The people trust animals more than declarations.

A child who can sit quietly beside the six-legged grazers is considered promising. A hunter who is repeatedly followed by pale rodents is asked to stop hunting for a season. Those whom the reflective-eyed creature beneath the platforms tolerates are given work near the foundations. No one knows whether these judgments have any real meaning. No one is willing to test the possibility that they do not.

The children are quiet by necessity, but not solemn. They play games that resemble work: sorting stones by sound, crossing a yard without disturbing a sleeping insect, building temporary bridges from vine loops, hiding water in places adults will not think to look. Their favorite game is called Counting the Still Ones. One child stands among moss-covered figures while the others observe from a doorway and try to determine which are statues, which are living creatures, and which have changed places. The game ends whenever one of the figures is found closer than it was before.

No child is permitted to play it after dusk.

The adults divide their time between gathering, maintaining, and listening. They harvest moss without tearing its roots. They cut only the oldest loops from the vine-pillars, leaving each plant enough structure to continue growing. They collect eggs when the exposed walls are safe, fungus when it has flowered, and insects during the rare bright mornings when the animals become restless. Hunting is done with weighted nets and slings, but the people do not pursue anything that flees in a straight line. Straight flight means the creature is not merely escaping. It is going somewhere, and the place it is going may not be a place a person should follow.

Their dead are not buried.

A body is washed in condensation and wrapped in leaf fiber. The people carry it to one of the outer gullies, but not always the same one. There, they leave it beneath a ledge with a bowl of water and a polished stone placed over the heart. In time the cliff-dwellers come. Sometimes the body is gone by morning. Sometimes the stone has been moved and nothing else has changed. If a body remains, the people return until the flesh has been taken or the rock itself shifts around it.

Names are given twice. The first name belongs to the family or household and is used during ordinary life. The second is earned later, after a person has survived a particular weather, animal, mistake, or period of the shelf’s rearrangement. A person might be called Hara-of-the-Blue-Frost, Six-Gaps, Who-Was-Looked-At, or Root-Turner. The name is not a title. It is a warning about what the person has learned.

The most respected among them are not chiefs. They are the listeners.

Listeners sit beside the reservoir at night with their hands on the polished stones. They memorize the different tremors that pass through the shelf: the small tremble of water forming, the shiver of a wall about to fail, the heavy pulsing that comes from below. They also watch the still ones. A boundary keeper may spend an entire season moving a single stone by the width of a finger, observing whether the moss grows around it, whether animals avoid it, and whether the seated figures alter their number.

The listeners disagree often. One says the deep vibration is the mountain settling. Another says it is a herd moving beneath the rock. A third says there is no mountain beneath them at all, only a sleeping thing too large to distinguish from the ground.

They are not punished for being wrong. They are punished for being certain.

The people have ceremonies, though these are usually small and practical. At the beginning of the wet season, every household clears the living gutters. At the first long light, they uncover the polished reservoir stones and wash away the green film. When a new vine arch hardens, the whole settlement gathers to touch it, one person at a time, before deciding what it will become. A bridge, a wall, a ladder, or an enclosure. No plant is cut without first being told what work it is about to do.

They speak to plants in the same tone they use with sleeping children.

They speak to animals less often. An animal looking directly at a person is never addressed by name, because that would make the exchange too intimate. The person lowers their eyes, steps backward, and waits. If the creature continues watching, everyone nearby follows the same rule. Even the hunters stop moving.

The people do not think of themselves as masters of the shelf. They are one of its temporary arrangements. Their houses, paths, gardens, and rules are attempts to remain legible to a place that is still deciding what it is.

They know that the boundaries are shifting. They know the still figures are increasing in some seasons. They know that certain children dream of doors opening beneath the reservoir, and that some animals have begun turning toward the empty sky before the wind arrives. They know that, sooner or later, the shelf may become a nest, a pool, a broken slope, or a doorway into something below.

Until then, they gather water.

They reinforce the walls.

They leave drainage gaps.

At dusk, when the animals lower their heads and the vessels begin to tremble, the people do the same. They place their palms against the stone and breathe with the settlement—not because they understand what answers beneath it, but because whatever lives below has not yet asked them to stop.
