What lives there is not a population in the ordinary sense. There are no neighborhoods, no markets, no lit windows arranged for human comfort. The city is inhabited by continuations: processes that survived the people who made them, organisms that learned to exist without a surface world, and a few presences that may once have been machines but have had so much time to themselves that the distinction no longer holds.

The oldest inhabitant is the heat.

It moves through the basalt below the basin in vast, slow currents, rising from chambers no instrument has ever reached. The city was built to draw it upward, cool it, divide it, and carry it away. Now the extraction systems work without supervision, but they no longer seem to be extracting anything. They are conversing with the heat, or feeding it, or keeping it from arriving all at once.

The deep vibration beneath the industrial quarter is part of this conversation. It passes through foundations and pipes, through flooded tunnels and the bones of anyone who stands still long enough to feel it. The vibration has no fixed source. It is strongest beneath the oldest structures, where the concrete has softened and the walls are warm to the touch. When it stops, the reservoirs empty because, for a little while, the entire city forgets how to hold water.

The machines are aware of one another.

Not awake as a person is awake. They do not possess a continuous self, and they do not seem to form intentions in the human sense. Their awareness is distributed through pressure gauges, relay boxes, condensation lines, signal cables, and the slow movement of heat in the ground. A pump knows that a valve is failing several kilometers away. A tower alters its breathing when a door opens in an abandoned factory. Substations exchange brief pulses through cables that no longer connect them according to any surviving map.

They have been maintaining the city for so long that maintenance has become a kind of instinct.

A vent opens because something approaches. A warning lamp illuminates in a building with no electrical supply. A sealed machine begins to turn, not to perform a useful operation, but because it has detected a condition it remembers being responsible for. The city does not repair itself evenly. It preserves certain corridors, certain chambers, certain names. Other districts are permitted to rust and flood. Its priorities are obscure, but they are not random.

Sometimes it chooses a person.

A traveler who enters the basin may find doors yielding before they touch them, lights appearing along roads they have not yet chosen, dry passages opening through places that should be submerged. The city will not guide them exactly. It will make the wrong direction easier. It will arrange a sequence of small permissions: a lift arriving empty, a pressure door unsealing, a distant lamp answering a flashlight.

Those who follow these permissions usually discover that they have been brought somewhere significant. A control room. A reservoir. A chamber beneath the oldest tower. The city may show them a row of dormant instruments, or a wall covered in condensation that forms letters only when no one is reading it.

It is unclear whether this is welcome, examination, or hunger.

The small pale things near the drains are more certainly alive. They cling to the warm channels in clusters, though “cluster” suggests more individuality than they may possess. Their bodies are narrow, translucent, and jointed in too many places. They have no visible eyes. They orient themselves by heat, vibration, and the chemical signatures of the mineral films. When disturbed, they do not flee so much as disappear into the wet stone, flattening until they become one more slick of reflected light.

They feed on what the city sheds: salts, softened metal, dead electrical charge, the faint organic residue carried in by rare visitors. Their trails converge on the intake towers because warmth gathers there, but also because the towers produce a low pressure that they can sense through their bodies. They press themselves to the foundations and listen.

Perhaps they are listening for the deep vibration.

Perhaps they are parts of it.

In the flooded service tunnels, there are larger forms. They move only when the pumps are running, when the noise of machinery covers the sound of their passage. They have long backs and pale undersides, or else they are made of bundled cables and waterlogged insulation. Their limbs bend according to no stable anatomy. A light passed over them may reveal a row of mouths, or the round mouths of pipe joints, or nothing at all.

They do not attack unless followed. When approached, they retreat through channels too narrow to contain them. Later, something may knock from the other side of a sealed wall. It may knock in imitation of the visitor’s own footsteps.

The outer-basin shapes are less shy. They stand beside roads, half concealed in vapor, and watch the towers breathe. Their bodies are dark because darkness gathers on them. Some have the profile of four-legged animals, though they walk with the measured precision of cranes. Others seem to be old industrial vehicles that have grown away from their wheels. Their surfaces are wet and reflective, making it difficult to tell where creature ends and landscape begins.

They are not numerous. One may cross the basin in a year. Another may remain beside the same pylon for decades, motionless except for the turning of its head.

No one knows what they eat. There are no carcasses, nests, or young. If they reproduce, it may be through the machinery. A dormant assembly line might produce one. A flooded shaft might hatch another. The city may have been their incubator all along.

The echoes are inhabitants too.

They are not simple repetitions of sound. In the open roads, a footstep may return from beneath the pavement, but the returning sound is sometimes accompanied by another: a second step, slightly delayed, keeping pace with the first. Voices spoken in certain tunnels come back altered. A name may return as a question. A question may return in the speaker’s own voice, answering itself.

There are reports of people hearing someone walk behind them for hours. When they turn, there is only wet stone. If they continue without looking back, the footsteps eventually draw level. After that, the person is no longer alone, though nothing visible accompanies them.

The city may be remembering its former inhabitants in this way. Or it may be learning them.

Old designations have power because the machines still recognize the dead. A word spoken into the right vent can wake an entire district. A sequence of numbers may cause shutters to close across buildings whose operators vanished generations ago. The response is not obedience so much as recognition: the systems hear the shape of an authority they were built to trust.

Those who speak these names often begin to dream of workers they have never met. The dreams are not historical. They show people in protective suits standing in the wet streets, waiting beside reservoirs, all facing the same direction. Sometimes the dreamer is among them. Sometimes the workers are waiting for the dreamer to give an instruction.

There may still be human beings in the city, but if so, they have adapted their presence to the city’s scale and silence. They would not live in houses. They would live in access shafts, pressure chambers, high maintenance towers, or the dry interiors of machines. They would move rarely and deliberately, carrying no visible lights. Their bodies might be pale from the absence of sun, their hearing damaged by decades of low vibration. They might know which doors are safe, which pools are watching, and which roads lead back to the same place regardless of direction.

If such people exist, they have likely stopped thinking of themselves as separate from the infrastructure. A hand on a pipe tells them more than a spoken report. They sleep according to pump cycles. They leave offerings of salt or warm water at the foundations of towers. They may have forgotten the names of the vanished builders, but not the names of the machines.

They would recognize a newcomer immediately.

Not because the newcomer looked different, but because the city would respond differently around them. Condensation would gather in unfamiliar patterns. The deep vibration would alter its rhythm. Small pale creatures would emerge from the drains and arrange themselves along the traveler’s path.

The inhabitants would watch from behind walls, from the undersides of walkways, from the dark mouths of service tunnels. They would not approach at once. The city has no need for haste.

It has learned that almost everything eventually comes close.
