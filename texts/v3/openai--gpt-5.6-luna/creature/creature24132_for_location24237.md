What lives there is not a creature, but a congregation of boundaries.

The oldest inhabitant is the dark film spread along the lower concrete wall. It is almost invisible except where the light catches its dull green crescent. Heat has selected for it. It feeds on what the factory cannot quite contain: trace metals leached from the pipes, carbon carried down in soot, salts lifted from the warm stone. It does not grow upward. It advances sideways, always keeping its body close to the floor, extending itself into the shape of available moisture. Where the water withdraws, the film remains as a faint stain. Where the water returns, it wakes without any visible change.

In the hottest cracks, beneath the black shelves of young basalt, there are colonies too small to be seen individually. They occupy the thin places where heat meets water and where metal dissolves into both. Their world is measured in gradients: warmer on one side of a fracture, more oxygenated on the other, richer in iron near a bolt, richer in sulfur beneath the pipe. They do not move through the basin. They alter it, patiently, by making new colors at the edges of pools and soft mineral skins over the exposed stone.

A pale moss clings to the coolest wall, though it is not quite moss in the ordinary sense. Its threads are translucent and sparse, each one a narrow instrument for catching condensation. When the factory ventilation draws hard, the threads lean together. When the air stills, they loosen and spread. They have no flowers, no scent, and no need to rise. Their entire ambition is to remain at the exact distance from the heat where water can gather without vanishing.

The pools contain another kind of life: not swimmers, but suspended communities. Fine organisms drift beneath the surface, invisible except when a bubble passes through them and briefly gathers them into a silver cloud. They endure the near-boiling warmth by living in layers. Some remain close to the hot bottom, where the hidden flow brings dissolved minerals. Others stay near the cooler skin of the water, where the factory draft supplies them with air. The pool appears still because its inhabitants are smaller than its movements. Beneath that stillness, they are continually dividing, settling, rising, and being carried into the drains.

The orange lines under the central basin are not merely the glow of warm stone. They are paths used by the heat-loving colonies below. Along these seams, mineral deposits accumulate in delicate ridges, then crack, then accumulate again. The living matter is mixed so thoroughly with stone that there is no clear point at which one ends and the other begins. The basin is being built by organisms that cannot distinguish construction from survival.

The pipe has its own resident. Inside the yellow-painted section, beyond the reach of light, a soft encrustation has formed around the inner wall. It thickens when the pumps slow and thins when they increase. The colony knows the factory by pressure. A distant valve opening sends a tremor through it. A change in temperature causes it to release a faint drift of particles into the water. It has no eyes, but it receives the entire building through vibration: the turbine’s turning, the relays’ irregular speech, the weight of vehicles crossing overhead.

The cabinet’s fan is not alive, though it behaves like something that has refused to die. Mineral deposits have locked its casing into the floor, but one blade continues to turn in the warm draft. It has no task left except to mark the passage of time. Each rotation gathers a little moisture, and each pause lets a drop form. The drop falls at the same interval, striking the cabinet’s lower lip with a small, exact sound. Around it, rust has spread in concentric stains. The cabinet is slowly growing a clock out of corrosion.

There are no animals to hunt or flee. Nothing nests in the ladder, and nothing hides beneath the submerged rungs. The only mouths belong to the films and colonies, and they open across surfaces rather than in bodies. Their hunger is diffuse. They consume gradients, residue, heat, and the minute offerings shed by the factory: copper, iron, carbon, oil, salt, warmth.

They live according to the machinery’s long rhythm. A pump’s thud is a season. A valve opening is weather. The passing of a maintenance vehicle is a rare earthquake, followed by rain. When condensation falls, the wall colonies drink. When the pools rise, the submerged communities spread into new territory. When a crack opens, life enters it almost immediately—not because anything travels there, but because the water carries the basin’s inhabitants wherever the stone allows.

The basin is therefore not empty. It is inhabited by things that have no outline until they leave one behind: a green skin, a white rim, an orange seam, a darkening stain. They are slow enough to resemble geology and responsive enough to resemble weather. They do not possess the factory. They are learning it.

Given time, they will coat the bolts, lace the concrete, thicken inside the pipe, and connect the two pools beneath the floor. Their bodies will become deposits; their deposits will become shelter. The factory will continue to thunder above them, supplying heat, water, pressure, and vibration.

And the inhabitants will continue their quiet work of turning machinery into climate, and climate into a place where something can remain.
