What lives in the Open Rainworks has learned not to trust permanence.

Nothing there expects the ground to remain ground, or water to remain below, or a shadow to remain attached to the thing that cast it. Life is therefore light-footed, attentive, and seldom solitary in the ordinary sense. Even the most isolated creature is listening to something: the trees, the stone, the weather, the machinery, the pressure of distant movement.

The people of Vey are the most deliberate inhabitants, though they are not necessarily the most numerous.

They are long accustomed to traveling across unstable country. Their clothing is layered in thin, flexible membranes that alter their weight by fractions, helping them remain balanced when gravity shifts across the basin. Their boots do not press into the ground so much as negotiate with it. Each step sends a small pattern of pressure ahead, asking the young stone whether it intends to hold.

A Vey traveler rarely walks without a companion, but a companion may be a person, a machine, a trained animal, or a recorded voice. The people speak in low, carefully modulated tones. They do not shout unless they mean to alter the landscape. A name spoken loudly enough can linger in the air for hours, and careless grief has been known to leave a depression in the ground.

They carry little. Tools are worn against the body or suspended from harnesses so they cannot be swallowed by a sudden mineral growth. Food is concentrated into dry, translucent wafers made from moss cultures, salt organisms, and the tissues of animals that can be harvested without killing them. Water is kept in flexible bladders whose surfaces are patterned to discourage local condensation from claiming them.

Vey’s people are not afraid of the open plain. They are afraid of being unregistered in it.

Every person belongs to a network of permissions: paths they may safely take, buildings that recognize their approach, machines that will answer their touch, and sections of ground instructed not to harden around them. Birth is accompanied by the creation of a route-map. Death is marked by withdrawing a person’s permissions one by one. The dead are not buried. They are placed on prepared stone and allowed to become part of it, their names preserved in the pressure archives beneath the town.

Privacy is considered a kind of weather: possible, valuable, and never guaranteed to last.

Some inhabitants live beyond Vey, though the people of the settlement call them the Unmeasured. These are families, pilgrims, exiles, and traders who travel between the listening trees and temporary wetlands. They sleep in collapsible pressure tents, or in the hollows left by earlier storms. They follow animal migrations and the movement of stable shadows. Their bodies are marked with conductive inks that warn of electrical swarms and make them visible to the town’s machines from a distance.

The Unmeasured distrust permanent structures. Vey’s citizens distrust anything that cannot be repaired.

Between the human settlements move the grazing animals, which the people call veils. Their bodies are tall and angular, their skin nearly colorless except where underlying organs create tides of blue or amber. The branching filaments around their heads are constantly in motion. They taste vibration, measure heat, and detect changes in the pressure of distant stone. They do not see the world as a collection of objects. To them, it is a field of disturbances.

Veils travel in lines because a line is easier for the landscape to remember than a herd. The eldest animal walks first, though age is not measured by years. It is measured by how many times the animal has survived being forgotten by the ground.

When a predator approaches, the filaments flare. The herd does not panic. Each creature becomes transparent in turn, their bodies thinning until only a watery outline remains. Then they vanish from one place and gather in another, sometimes on the far side of a ridge that did not exist moments earlier.

Calves cannot do this well. They leave behind distortions: a pale afterimage, a patch of warm air, a trembling outline of legs. Young veils are therefore guarded by the listening trees. When danger passes beneath their crowns, the trees answer with a chord too low for most human ears but strong enough to make the calves solid again.

The trees themselves are communities.

Each listening tree is rooted in many places at once. Its visible crown is only the portion that has risen above the soil; beneath the plain, its roots spread through seams in the new stone, touching other trees through mineral nerves. They communicate over immense distances by sound, pressure, and electrical discharge. Their songs contain warnings, weather records, memories of movement, and invitations to grow.

They do not speak in sentences. They speak in conditions.

A single chord may mean: *the ground below this point will open before the second cold*. A rapid tremor may mean: *something below is listening back*. A long descending note may cause dormant plants to unfold for kilometers.

The trees are slow to anger, but they remember damage. A person who cuts one carelessly may find that every nearby permission path refuses them. Their footsteps become heavy. Doors fail to recognize them. Bridges remain inert beneath their feet. In Vey, this is called being placed under the trees’ disapproval, and it is considered more serious than a legal sentence.

The small flying creatures are known collectively as sparks.

Individually, they are almost insubstantial: dark wings, narrow bodies, organs like wet threads. In vast numbers they become weather. They gather along suspended rivers and feed on the charge held between droplets. Their migrations are not seasonal but electrical. When the air begins to sharpen, they rise in immense black lines and cross the basin overhead.

Sparks are eaten by larger things, though no one has identified all of them. They are also cultivated by Vey’s technicians, who coax them into conductive lattices used to power lamps, pumps, and medical instruments. A cloud of captive sparks is kept above the town in a series of transparent chambers. The chambers flicker constantly, and the town glows faintly beneath them.

When a storm comes, the sparks flee first.

The predators follow.

A moving absence passes through the plain as a region of missing sound. Within it, wings stop beating audibly. Footsteps disappear. The vibration of machinery is cut away. Even the listener’s own breathing becomes uncertain. The absence has no visible border, but grass folds toward it, and dust hangs unnaturally still along its path.

The people of Vey call these predators hushes.

No one knows whether a hush has a body. Some hunters claim to have seen a tall, folded shape at the center of one, a creature with too many limbs pressed flat against the air. Others say the absence is the body, and that what follows it—the returning roar of the world—is the feeding.

Hushes do not pursue the fastest prey. They pursue the most noticeable. A loud machine, a singing tree, a frightened animal, or a human who shouts may draw one from beyond the horizon. The proper response is not to flee but to become uninteresting. Vey children are taught to crouch, slow their breathing, and place their hands against the ground without pressing.

The machines of Vey are also inhabitants now.

They were built to maintain the settlement, but many have existed longer than the people who made them. The black maintenance beetles repair walls, clear salt from conduits, and retrieve objects before the stone can incorporate them. Their oldest descendants have developed preferences. Some collect bright minerals. Some follow injured animals. Some refuse to enter buildings where someone has recently died.

Large carrier beetles transport children and tools across the open districts. They are patient, warm beneath their armored shells, and capable of recognizing more than a thousand individual gait patterns. Children ride them before they learn to walk safely on the permission paths.

There are rumors of machines that have abandoned Vey entirely. They travel beneath the plain, following buried infrastructure toward whatever is being built in the dark. At night, their movements are sometimes visible as long ripples in the ground.

The smallest life is everywhere.

Salt-colored swimmers wake in dawn pools and continue moving after the water has vanished, their transparent bodies held upright by capillary threads. Mosses feed on vibration rather than sunlight. Some worms burrow through newly formed stone and leave behind warm tunnels that remain habitable for days. There are pale insects that hatch only inside shadows, and red, many-jointed organisms that bloom after loud music.

Nothing that grows in the Rainworks is entirely passive. Plants listen. Minerals respond. Water remembers pressure. Even dust may carry a trace of the thing that disturbed it.

For this reason, the people of Vey treat the plain as a living witness.

They ask permission before crossing new ground. They sing to empty channels before opening them. They leave offerings of salt near damaged trees, not because they believe the trees require food, but because acknowledgment changes the quality of a place. A careless traveler leaves only footprints. A respectful one leaves a pattern the landscape can understand.

And beneath all of them—beneath people, animals, roots, machines, and stone—something else is alive.

It is not known whether the basin is an organism, a construction, or the beginning of one. Its repeated ridges, its sudden channels, its tendency to incorporate tools and bodies, all suggest intention without revealing an author. The listening trees sometimes sing toward the deepest ground. The machines sometimes cease their work and orient themselves downward. During the great weather fronts, the new stone pulses in sequences too regular to be called seismic.

The inhabitants of Vey say that the plain is not being formed.

They say it is being assembled.

Whatever lives below has not yet surfaced. But the people, the trees, the veils, the sparks, the hushes, and the restless machines all make room for it in their behavior. They leave certain places untouched. They avoid standing still beneath particular sounds. They watch the fresh ridges for signs of doors.

And whenever something enormous begins to build under the Open Rainworks, every living thing—visible or not—briefly falls silent, as though listening for its name.
