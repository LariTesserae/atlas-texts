They are called the **Anchored**, though the name is not quite accurate.

Nothing about them is fixed.

An Anchored body is narrow and jointed, built for ladders, gantries, and the sudden changes in angle that pass through the city. Their feet and hands divide into three long gripping digits, each ending in a pad of dark mineral cartilage. The pads can flatten against a wall or close around a rail. When they sleep, they do not lie down. They fold themselves into recessed brackets along the residence shafts and lock their limbs into place, suspended above the floors in case the floors become walls during the night.

They are pale only where they are unarmored. Most of the body is covered in overlapping plates the color of cooling basalt: gray, blue-black, iron-red at the joints. Under the plates are dense cords of muscle and a network of pressure sacs. These sacs allow them to breathe the thin upper air for a little while, though no Anchored trusts their own lungs near the summit. They carry breathing masks not over the mouth but across the throat, where a pair of narrow vents open and close with every breath. The masks are made of glass, graphite, and the polished shell of climbing fauna. Each one is fitted by hand. A badly fitted mask is not an inconvenience. It is a future burial.

Their faces are small and heavily protected. They have no visible ears; sound reaches them through vibrating plates along the jaw and collarbones. Their eyes are deep-set, adapted to arc glare and the sudden darkness beneath the gantries. In bright light, the pupils contract to fine horizontal cuts. In darkness, their eyes reflect faintly blue, so that a group of them crossing a high bridge may appear to be a string of indicators moving through the air.

They do not speak often in the open streets.

Speech wastes breath, and ordinary speech is easily lost in the stone. Instead, they communicate through taps delivered to railings, walls, and the metal frames of their buildings. A rapid pattern against a bridge means that a load is moving above. Three low strikes mean that the floor has shifted. A hand laid flat against a support can carry a message through an entire workshop if the structure is quiet enough.

Among themselves, they also use pressure signs: a tightening of the throat vents, a change in the rhythm of the chest sacs, a particular angle of the head. These are subtle and difficult for outsiders to read. An Anchored can tell a frightened individual from an angry one without seeing the face. They can recognize fatigue through a change in foot pressure. They know when someone has been working near a furnace by the dry heat stored under the skin.

Children are born in the lower levels, where the air is thickest and the mountain’s movement is slowest.

They are not small versions of adults. For several years they are soft-bodied, with flexible plates and too many grip points in their hands. They cling to their parents’ backs or to the warm sides of pressure engines. Their sensory fans are broad and sensitive, constantly opening and closing as they taste dust, vibration, and electrical charge. A child can locate a hidden fault in a machine long before an instrument can. This is considered useful, though children are forbidden to approach a fault without an escort. The city has lost too many of them to the sudden blue flash of a live cable.

They are raised collectively, but not gently.

Every child belongs to a **brace**, a small household organized around a section of the city rather than a family line. A brace may contain birth-parents, older workers, mechanics, a retired pressure-tender, several children, and one or two domesticated tunnel creatures that have been accepted into the group. The household eats, repairs, sleeps, and evacuates together. If the mountain shifts, it is the brace that receives the warning and decides who carries whom.

Names are given twice. The first is a birth-name, used only in the lower chambers and during medical work. The second is acquired through labor. One may become **Who Holds the West Gantry**, **Who Heard the Third Fracture**, **Who Returns the Fault-Beasts**, or simply **Noisy Hands**. The name changes when the person changes. Some names are inherited as burdens. A child of a famous vent-diver may be called **Not Yet the Diver** until they have earned something else.

The Anchored believe that a person is made of three things: a body, a brace, and a direction.

The body is temporary. It cracks, dries, loses pressure, and is eventually dismantled.

The brace is the set of beings that keep one alive: co-workers, machines, animals, ancestors, and the structures that carry weight when one’s own limbs fail.

Direction is the purpose toward which all that support is applied. To live without direction is to become loose inside the mountain. Such people wander between districts, taking jobs without joining a brace, sleeping in abandoned housings, and following electrical faults as if they were trails. They are not hated. They are feared. A loose person may climb into a forbidden shaft simply because the rock is warmer there.

The city’s workers are divided less by wealth than by altitude.

The lower-level people operate the furnaces, crushers, pressure engines, and waste channels. Their plates are darkened by soot and their hands are permanently scored by mineral dust. They know the mountain through its underside: heat, load, and the slow push of material through the foundations.

The middle levels belong to builders, haulers, machine-tenders, traders, and those who work with the fauna. They move constantly through the vertical streets, carrying sealed cases, coils, tools, and sleeping children. Their clothing is made of layered fabric and thin flexible armor, with many loops for attaching themselves to rails. A middle-level worker can cross a collapsing gantry with the calm efficiency of someone stepping over a puddle.

The upper levels hold surveyors, vent-keepers, signalers, and the people who maintain the adjustable anchors. They are thinner than the others and often suffer from a faint tremor caused by prolonged exposure to the summit air. Their eyes are marked by the blue-white glare of the outside dust. They spend their lives measuring the mountain’s newest movements, making maps that are obsolete before the ink dries.

There are also the **listeners**.

Listeners do not belong to a single industry. They sit in small chambers against the rock and press their hands, feet, and jaw plates to the walls. They feel the deep movements of the mountain: the swelling of a buried seam, the grinding of one basalt face against another, the distant collapse of a shaft no map remembers. They are trained from childhood to distinguish natural movement from structural failure, and structural failure from something stranger.

For the mountain has sounds that do not come from pressure alone.

Sometimes the listeners report a second pulse beneath the city’s mechanical lung. Sometimes they feel a vibration traveling upward through the summit, too regular to be stone and too slow to be machinery. When this happens, nobody asks whether the mountain is alive. That question is considered childish. Everything is alive if it can alter the shape of your future.

The Anchored do not worship the mountain exactly. They maintain it.

Their oldest rituals are forms of inspection. Before a new district is occupied, a group walks its walls and marks every dangerous seam with white ceramic dust. When a fracture appears, the nearest brace brings tools, lamps, and a dish of powdered metal. The powder is scattered into the crack. If it disappears, the fracture is still opening. If it remains, the stone has settled for the moment.

At the end of a worker’s life, the body is stripped of its useful plates and fittings. The bones, if they have bones, are ground with ceramic and pressed into anchor wedges. These are installed in the city’s foundations. No one speaks of this as burial. It is called **taking weight**.

A dead person may therefore remain in service for centuries.

A bridge can be supported by the jaw of a remembered engineer. A residence may hang from the finger-joints of three generations of pressure-tenders. Children are told this plainly. When they complain that a floor is cold or a wall is vibrating, an elder may place a palm against the stone and say, “That is your great-grandmother. She is holding.”

The creatures of the city are not considered property. They are classified as neighbors, tools, hazards, or weather, and one creature may move between categories during its life.

The small furnace-nesters are tolerated because they consume the films that corrode machine housings. Workers leave them bowls of powdered shell and mineral paste in designated niches. If the creatures begin abandoning a furnace, the workers evacuate before checking the gauges. The larger climbing animals are more complicated. Some are trained to carry cable bundles along the cliff faces outside. Others are bred for shell plates, which are cut away after death and used in masks, instrument housings, and children’s protective gear.

The Anchored know the animals are listening to the mountain too.

During a shift, the fauna may freeze all at once. Their sensory fans close. Their bodies flatten against the walls. In such moments the workers stop as well. No alarm is needed. Everyone waits for the message carried through the stone.

Then, if the danger passes, life resumes: the presses strike, the lifts move, the lamps brighten, and a child is scolded for climbing where the floor has not yet been bolted down.

There are strangers in the city, though none arrive by road.

They come in sealed capsules lifted through the summit throat, or in pressure-battered climbing shells that emerge from the dust outside. They are prospectors, exiles, emissaries, salvage crews, pilgrims from settlements lower on the plain. The Anchored receive them without enthusiasm. Visitors bring new tools, new diseases of the machinery, new ways of naming the mountain, and questions that cannot be answered without wasting time.

Most visitors leave.

Some stay and become useful. A stranger is not considered part of the city until they have survived a shift while carrying someone else.

This is the simplest test. When the rock moves, one must choose between saving oneself and keeping hold of another person. No one is asked to be fearless. Fear is a pressure reading, and pressure readings are valuable. But a person who releases everyone else at the first tremor is never again trusted with a bridge, a child, or a living machine.

At the summit platform, where the whole hidden city can be seen at once, the Anchored go alone or in pairs.

They go to measure the dust. To inspect the throat. To watch the plain far below, if the veil thins enough to reveal it. They go to feel how much higher the mountain has risen since their last visit.

From there, the city appears less like a home than a concentration of sparks inside a wound. The inhabitants move along its walls, vanishing into shafts, emerging from vents, crossing one another on bridges that flex with the stone. The pale animals thread between them. Cranes turn. Lamps blink. A new fracture opens and is immediately marked, named, and assigned to a brace.

An Anchored on the platform may place both hands against the summit rock.

The stone is cold there, but deep within it something is still warm.

They listen.

They feel the upward pressure gathering beneath the city, the immense unfinished force that has lifted the mountain into the sky and continues to lift it. They understand that every street, machine, child, animal, and buried ancestor is suspended inside a moment that cannot last.

This does not make them despair.

Permanence, to the Anchored, is not the opposite of change. Permanence is what holds long enough for something else to pass through.
