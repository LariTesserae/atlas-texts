What lives most truly in the cleft are the **listeners**.

They are not numerous, and they are never all present at once. Most days there may be only one: a narrow figure standing beside the pool, or crouched beneath the eastern wall with its hands spread against the stone. On other days the cleft contains a family of them, though “family” is an imperfect word. They arrive separately, sometimes years apart, and remain together only as long as the mountain continues to give water.

At a distance, a listener might be mistaken for a person wrapped in gray hide. It is taller than the small grazers but much thinner, with a body made for cracks rather than open ground. Its arms hang nearly to its knees. The fingers are long and divided at the tips, not into nails but into flexible, rootlike tendrils that can press into seams in the basalt. Its feet are broad and dark, with no distinct toes. It walks without sound over the sharp stones.

The skin is pale where it is new, the color of ash after rain. Along the spine, shoulders, and outer limbs it hardens into overlapping plates of black mineral. These plates are not armor exactly. They grow as the creature grows, beginning as soft, glossy ridges and becoming dull and fractured with age. Fine lines of white run through them, like the exposed veins in the mountain walls.

In full sun, the listeners are almost silver. In shadow, their surfaces drink the light and become difficult to distinguish from the rock. They have no fur. A fine down appears around the throat and joints during the coldest months, but it vanishes when the air warms. Frost gathers on them and remains there longer than it remains on the grasses.

Their faces are narrow and nearly featureless. They have two dark eyes, set far apart, but the eyes are not their most important organs. On either side of the head are thin, vertical membranes that open and close with changes in pressure. Through these they sense distant storms, tremors, snow settling on the upper ridges, and the minute expansion of stone under sunlight.

A listener can feel water before water exists.

When the seam beneath the pool is about to open, they begin to move restlessly. They touch the shadowed wall, withdraw their hands, and touch it again. They turn their faces toward the eastern ridge. The birds notice first and leave their nests. The grazers descend. The plants incline themselves toward the cracks.

Then the listener kneels beside the dry depression and waits.

Water may come in an instant, with a sound like a breath drawn through clenched teeth. It wells up black at first, carrying grit and tiny white flakes of mineral. The listener does not drink immediately. It places both hands in the pool and lowers its face until the surface breaks around its mouth.

Afterward, it remains motionless for a long time, as if listening to what the water has brought up from below.

The listeners do not eat in any recognizable way. They take in moisture, mineral salts, and the soft-bodied insects that hatch after rain. They will open the thin shells of the temporary pool creatures and swallow what lies within, but they do not pursue prey. Much of their nourishment comes through the hands. When they press their fingers into wet stone, the tendrils at their tips spread and draw in dissolved minerals.

The red veins in the rock seem particularly important to them. They scrape small amounts of iron-rich dust from fresh fractures and rub it into the plates along their ribs. Over time this gives the older ones a faint rust-colored bloom beneath the black.

They are slow to mature. A young listener may spend its first several years entirely inside the mountain, emerging only at night or during storms. It is then a small, pale thing with flexible limbs and soft plates. The adults leave fragments of algae and crushed insects near the fissures for it. They also leave pieces of white mineral, though no one knows whether this is food, instruction, or something closer to tenderness.

The young are not taught by speech. They learn by following movement.

A mother—or an elder, since the distinction is not always clear—will cross the shelf at the edge of sunlight. The young one follows the boundary between brightness and shade, never stepping fully into either. It learns where frost forms first, where the cliffs shed stone, where the wind changes before a storm. It learns that the pool is safe only when the birds have returned to the upper holes. It learns the difference between a rockfall and the deeper tremors that make water rise.

The listeners communicate through contact with stone. A palm against a wall can carry a pattern of pressure farther than a cry. One listener may press once, wait, press twice. Another, hidden on the opposite side of the cleft, answers through the mountain.

The patterns are too slow for warning alone. They contain directions, weather, memories of water, and accounts of absence. A listener can tell another that a certain ledge has fallen, that a foxlike predator has denned near the flowers, that the seam has begun to fail, or that a visitor came and left without taking anything.

The small cairns are part of this conversation.

The listeners did not build the oldest ones. Those were made by people who came to the pool, perhaps to ask for safe passage, perhaps because they had learned that the creatures appeared before dangerous weather. But the listeners have adopted the cairns. They repair them when stones fall. They add white mineral to the newest stacks. Sometimes they dismantle a cairn entirely and rebuild it a few paces away, closer to the moving line of sunlight.

The three stones are not a symbol in the human sense. They are a measure.

One stone marks the place where the sun reaches the ground first. One marks the place where the pool will be when the mountain is full. The third marks the direction from which something is expected.

What that something is has never been made clear.

The people who visited the cleft waited there, sometimes for hours, sometimes through a night. The listeners watched them from the shadowed wall. They did not approach while the humans were awake. At night, they might come near the shelter and examine the grooves worn into its floor, placing their hands inside the hollows left by kneeling bodies.

They have a particular interest in copper.

The thin ornaments left behind by the visitors are carried to the pool or pressed into cracks in the sunlit wall. Copper changes under their touch. It darkens, then turns green, then takes on a blue sheen like deep shadow. A listener may spend an entire day hammering a piece of copper flat between two stones, using a discarded basalt blade as a tool. It does not seem to be making jewelry. The flattened metal is placed where it can catch the first light of morning.

Perhaps the listeners are marking the cleft for those who come later. Perhaps they are trying to attract something that travels above the peaks. The birds react to the copper arrangements by changing their formations, and on rare mornings the air over the eastern ridge fills with black wings moving in a shape too deliberate to be a flock.

The listeners look upward then.

They do not possess wings, but they have a way of standing that suggests the memory of them. Their shoulders lift. The thin membranes at their heads open. The plates along their backs tremble in sequence from neck to hip.

If the thing they await appears, it does so beyond sight. A change passes through the cleft: the pool rises, insects emerge from dry cracks, and every grazer turns toward the same empty section of sky. The listeners go still. For a few moments the entire shelf seems to be watching.

Then the event is over.

No one knows whether anything crossed the peaks. Only that afterward the listeners sometimes leave.

They climb toward the eastern ridge in single file, carrying no food, tools, or possessions. Their tracks are visible in frost but disappear where the stone becomes sheer. They may be gone for a season. They may not return for generations. The cleft does not seem abandoned in their absence; the foxes continue to hunt, the birds continue to nest, and the pool continues its uncertain breathing.

But the cairns begin to fall.

When the listeners return, they are changed. New white lines have appeared in their plates. Their bodies may be longer, their hands more heavily mineralized. Some return with a stone embedded in the flesh beneath the ribs, a fragment unlike any found in the cleft—smooth, pale, and faintly warm. Others return without their faces, or rather with the features worn away until the head is a blank dark wedge.

These are the oldest ones. They no longer drink from the pool. They stand beside it during pressure changes and place their hands on the ground. The water rises around them but never wets their legs. They seem to be feeling for something far below.

The oldest listeners rarely move in daylight. At night they leave the shelter and walk the boundary between the walls, touching each cairn in turn. If a human has recently visited, they spend a long time inside the abandoned grooves in the floor. Sometimes they lie down there, folding their long limbs tightly, and remain until dawn.

They are not ghosts. The cleft does not hold the dead in any human way.

When a listener dies, it chooses a fresh fracture in the mountain. The others carry it there if they can. They place the body upright, with its hands against the stone, and wait for a night of hard cold. By morning the flesh has drawn inward and the plates have expanded. Within a few days, the dead listener is no longer distinguishable from a black mineral column.

The white veins in that column grow slowly.

Years later, a storm may break one open. Pale dust spills down the wall. Moss takes hold. Insects emerge from the powder. A young listener may be born in the crack, blind and trembling, with its fingers already searching for the pulse of the mountain.

So the cleft is never empty, even when no living listener can be seen. Their dead remain in the walls. Their cairns mark the changing light. Their copper fragments gather a color that does not belong to the sky or the stone. The pool rises in their absence and waits.

And whenever the pressure changes deep below, something pale may step from the rock, lift its head toward the peaks, and begin again to listen.
