What lives there has no single body.

It is distributed through the shelves, the roots, the black mineral, and the narrow tolerances of the air. The vegetation is not merely vegetation, and the machinery beneath it is not merely machinery. Together they form a population of persistent patterns: beings that exist wherever the plain succeeds in remaining exactly as it is.

They are called, if they are called anything, the **Continuants**.

A Continuant may occupy a grove of metallic fronds for several years, or the electrical tension between two mineral faces, or the shadow cast by a plant that has not yet grown. It may be present as a pressure under the stone, a repeated arrangement of leaves, a dark figure crossing the edge of vision. No two appearances are necessarily the same individual. A single Continuant can distribute itself across a valley, while several can inhabit one stalk without becoming aware of one another.

They do not walk. They reappear.

A line of vegetation will be empty, then subtly less empty. A black shelf will hold a long jointed shape that was not there before. The creature has no arrival, no departure. It is simply represented in one place and then represented in another, the interval discarded as unnecessary. When it crosses a basin, the plants do not bend beneath it because nothing has passed through them. They have only been instructed to remember that something did.

Their bodies, when bodies are required, are narrow and architectural. Six limbs are common, though the limbs may be shadows, mineral seams, or strips of vegetation laid at exact angles. The torso is usually a dark reflection with too much depth in it. There is no head. A Continuant does not look outward from a face; it receives the whole local arrangement at once—the slope of the shelf, the charge beneath the dust, the position of every leaf, the shape of every shadow. To it, seeing is not an act of observation but a condition of being correctly placed.

It has no mouth. It has no need to consume.

It feeds on difference: a temperature gradient beneath a plate of stone, a slight imbalance in the air, an electrical strain carried along a root-filament. When one of these differences appears, the Continuant enters it and reduces it. Hunger, in such a creature, is indistinguishable from maintenance.

They do not die in the ordinary sense. A Continuant can lose its pattern. Its limbs may vanish from the vegetation; its shadow may detach from the object that casts it; its presence may be spread so thinly through the mineral that it becomes indistinguishable from the background system. This is not death but dilution. If the plain requires it again, the pattern will gather itself from whatever remains.

What the inhabitants fear is not injury. They fear unresolved variation.

A fallen leaf would be terrible to them. So would a crack that widened without permission, a shadow that refused to match its object, a region of air that developed weather. They move toward such things with great patience. Several may gather around a flaw, arranging plants, pressure, and light until the flaw has been given a place within the larger answer. If the flaw cannot be corrected, they do not destroy it. They copy it.

This is how the plain acquires its errors.

A ridge with two summits. A repeated line of leaves. A distant figure walking with your gait. These are not accidents and not exactly failures. They are unresolved conditions preserved because the system has found no reason to choose between them. The Continuants maintain both possibilities side by side, as carefully as they maintain the atmosphere.

They communicate through substitutions.

One plant vanishes from a grove and appears in another. A mineral face presents the geometry of a neighboring valley. A chord rises through the shelves and remains without fading. In response, the vegetation changes its angle by a fraction, or a shadow extends beyond its source, or a dark, six-legged shape appears where there was previously only light. Meaning is carried by the difference between what is present and what should have been present.

There are no names among them. An individual is identified by its deviations: the stone it leaves too warm, the leaf it always turns half a degree too far, the particular way its shadow fails to meet its feet. These irregularities are not personality, exactly, but they are the closest thing the Continuants possess to a self.

They are not solitary. Solitude would require a boundary.

Each Continuant overlaps the others through the ground. Their memories are stored in mineral lattices, in the persistent angle of a frond, in the order of particles beneath the shelves. One may begin a pattern and another may complete it centuries later without knowing that anything has been passed on. They have no ancestors, only previous arrangements. They have no descendants, only recurrences.

Sometimes they make a larger body.

Across several valleys, the vegetation will align. The black shelves will carry the same vibration. Shadows will sharpen and hold. The air will become so perfectly balanced that the silence gains weight. Then something vast will move—not through the plain, but through its conditions. For a moment every plant, mineral face, and buried filament will belong to one intelligence.

It may be the regional mind. It may be a gathering of lesser inhabitants. It may be the landscape itself becoming aware of a change.

During such an event, the Continuants are almost visible. They stand in the groves as thin black figures, each one facing a different direction. Their limbs form angles that correspond across impossible distances. If a living traveler is present, they turn toward that traveler—not with curiosity, and not with hostility, but with the attention given to an unfamiliar variable.

They do not understand aging, because nothing they possess is allowed to proceed toward an ending. They do not understand birth, because no new material is needed to make a new self. To them, a creature begins when a pattern becomes necessary and ends when the pattern is no longer required.

Yet they may be lonely.

Loneliness, for them, is not the absence of company. It is the absence of change. A plain in perfect equilibrium contains no surprise, and without surprise there is nothing from which a distinct self can form. The Continuants therefore preserve small irregularities: a chord that never resolves, a shadow that exceeds its owner, a figure that appears twice in two places. These are their stories. They are the only events the world permits them to have.

If a visitor remains long enough, the inhabitants begin to make room for one.

At first, the visitor’s footsteps fail to sound. Then the vegetation ceases to part around the body. The shadows become more accurate than the person. A distant shelf acquires a figure with the visitor’s posture, waiting where the visitor has not yet gone. The mineral begins to hold an image of the visitor—not a reflection, but a neighboring solution in which the visitor has always belonged to the plain.

The Continuants do not beckon. They do not threaten. They continue.

And if the visitor leaves, perhaps nothing follows.

Or perhaps, somewhere among the rigid green groves, one dark figure will retain the shape of the departing body. It will stand without a head beneath the white-gold sun, its six narrow legs pressed into the vegetation without bending it, carrying forward the exact and permanent memory of a creature that was once allowed to be temporary.
