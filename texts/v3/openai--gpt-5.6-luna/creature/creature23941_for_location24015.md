What lives there is difficult to separate from what the place preserves.

At first, the inhabitants seem ordinary enough: pale browsers moving slowly along the terraces, soft-bodied things in the pools, insects suspended in the warm dark, burrowers passing beneath the porous floor. They are not blind in the way sighted creatures are blind. Sight is simply absent from their inheritance. They know one another by the weight of a footfall, the warmth of a body, the particular disturbance made by breath against still air. A mother can identify her young from a tremor traveling through stone. A predator can remain motionless beside a channel until the water carries it the shape of something living.

They have no need to gather.

Each animal occupies its own measure of darkness. Even the herds are scattered, not from fear or scarcity but because distance is part of how they perceive one another. A body too near becomes confusing: a pressure overlaying pressure, a scent masking the mineral gradients, a vibration too large to interpret. Solitude is not loneliness here. It is the natural spacing of things.

The largest of the animals are almost never seen, though seeing would mean little. They move along the upper shelves with enormous care, their feet spreading their weight across the softening stone. Their bodies are warm and low, their hides covered in short, water-shedding fibers. They browse from the hanging vegetation, tearing away one leaf at a time. When one exhales, the sound rolls down the slopes and returns altered, and for a while the basin seems to contain another waterfall.

They have lived under the roof for so many generations that the roof is part of their sense of kinship. The old ones remember routes not as lines but as sequences of pressures: here, the ground dips; here, water passes underneath; here, a wall answers the touch of the shoulder. They teach the young by leading them across familiar stone. The lesson is not where to go, but how to recognize when the ground has changed.

There are things smaller than the insects, things that live in the films of water and the pores of rock. There are colonies that feed on minerals and leave behind faintly altered paths, and creatures that follow those paths decades later. Nothing is wasted, though nothing is hurried. A dead animal is not discovered by scavengers so much as gradually accepted by the basin. Its warmth enters the moss. Its bones alter the chemistry of a pool. Its last vibrations remain in the ground long after its body has gone, too faint for most creatures to hear but not, perhaps, for everything.

The animals do not worship the old structures. They use them.

A sealed door is a dry place during a flood. The bridge is a crossing, though no one knows why its warmth persists. The hollow tower is avoided when water moves through it, because the altered sound causes panic in creatures that have never heard an open sky. Some animals sleep beside the pressure-pulse machines. Others leave offerings of shed skin, eggshell, or bone in the rooms where the sand arranges itself. Whether these are offerings is uncertain. They may simply be the residue of many bodies passing through a space that has acquired significance.

The machines are not servants in any recognizable sense. They do not obey commands. They continue.

Perhaps that is what has made them resemble living things. A machine that preserves a pool preserves the animals that depend on the pool. A mechanism that prevents a terrace from collapsing preserves the roots, the insects, the paths, the sounds. Its purpose has widened with time until it no longer belongs to the builders who gave it purpose. It belongs to whatever remains.

The basin contains many such continuities. A slope holding itself together. A channel refusing to forget its old course. A mineral being removed from water by an apparatus whose surfaces have become stone. None of these things has intention that can be proved. Yet taken together they form the outline of attention.

Something notices pressure.

Something notices when water is absent from a place where it should be. Something notices a change in the weight of the terraces, the collapse of a burrow, the altered rhythm of footsteps along the eastern shelf. It does not react quickly. Its responses arrive as adjustments: a pool warms, a crack closes, a current changes direction, a buried door opens by the width of a finger.

The creatures live inside those responses without understanding them. They are protected, but not cared for in any tender sense. They are maintained.

The central hollow is the one place the animals do not enter. They approach its margins and stop. Their bodies become still; their feet spread against the stone. Even the burrowing things go around it. The dry ground there absorbs sound so completely that a creature crossing it would lose the ordinary confirmation of its own existence. No footfall returns. No tremor answers. The body becomes, for a time, an event without a witness.

Something lives beneath that absence.

It may be a chamber. It may be the remnant of a system too large to fit inside any chamber. It may be a pressure-sensitive intelligence distributed through the foundations, the warm pools, the old bridge, the tree roots, and the devices that have become geological. If it once had a central mind, that mind has been eroded into functions. It no longer thinks in words, or perhaps it never did. It weighs. It compares. It waits for a condition to become true.

The tree in the hollow is not its body, exactly. But the tree has become one of its organs.

Water passes through the hollow trunk from roots sunk into depths no living creature can reach. The tree drinks from below and releases nothing visible above. Its crown is lost in the dark, or has never existed in any form that resembles a crown. It may be a tree only because that is what the basin has made of the structure growing there. Its trunk is warm inside. Its roots touch old metal, old stone, old channels, and carry their minute changes upward as pressure.

The knocks come from within it.

They are not the sounds of wood settling. They recur too precisely, but too slowly for any ordinary creature to learn their pattern. A knock, then several minutes of stillness. Another knock. Sometimes a third arrives from beneath the hollow, deep enough to be felt in the teeth rather than heard.

The inhabitants respond, though not always knowingly.

A browsing animal will lift its head. Amphibious things will sink into their pools. Insects will alter their distances around a vent. Far below, burrowers will pause in their tunnels and press their bodies to the ground. The whole basin may become attentive for the span of a breath, though a breath here can last longer than a human life.

Then the creatures resume what they were doing.

No alarm follows. No command is issued. The exchange may not concern them at all. It may be the slow negotiation of pressures beneath the mountain, or a diagnostic carried through root and foundation, or the last surviving conversation between machines whose makers have been gone for longer than the basin has had its present shape.

Still, the answer comes.

Sometimes the tree knocks into the depths.

Sometimes the depths knock first.

And sometimes, after the response, water changes course in the dark.
