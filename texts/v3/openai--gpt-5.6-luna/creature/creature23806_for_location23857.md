They are called the **Hollow-born**, though none of them is born in the hollow.

Birth takes place in the lower houses, where the wind is warmest and least violent. The rooms there are small, double-opened, and lined with layers of woven stalk. A child enters the world already surrounded by sound: the continuous pressure of the air, the knocking of dry leaves, the low complaint of the city’s foundations. Its first cry is not heard by anyone. It is watched.

The mother’s hands rest against the infant’s ribs. The attendant places two fingers beneath its jaw. They feel for the rhythm of the breath, the flutter of the heart, the small tremors that travel through the body. When the child has steadied, a strip of colored cloth is tied around its wrist. The color indicates the direction of the prevailing wind on the day of its birth.

This is not a name, but it will remain with the child all its life.

Names are given later, after the child has demonstrated a nature. One is named for the way they sleep, another for the first plant they touch, another for the pattern of their footsteps on a bridge. A child who watches doors and openings may become **Between-Stones**. One who laughs whenever a wind house begins to sound may be called **Teeth-Rattle**. Names are not spoken, but shaped with the hands and face, accompanied by a pressure at the wrist or shoulder. The older names are intricate. Some require the whole body.

The Hollow-born are not silent people. They are people who have moved speech elsewhere.

They communicate through palms, elbows, brows, colored flags, chalk marks, shutters, bells, and the posture of the body in the wind. A raised hand may mean danger, but the angle of the fingers specifies what kind: falling stone, sudden pressure, animal, stranger, or failure in one of the upper vents. Two people negotiating in a market may never look at each other’s faces. They watch the hands, the shoulders, the direction of the feet.

The deafening has made them intimate with one another. A person learns to read another person by touch long before learning to trust their face.

Children are taught this as soon as they can stand. They are taken into the courtyards and placed among the plants. The teacher bends a silver leaf and releases it. The child must point toward the opening from which the current came. Later they are taught to distinguish the warning shiver of a bridge from the ordinary tremor of footsteps. They learn which walls can be leaned against, which doorways inhale, which plazas become dangerous when the upper vents open.

At seven, they are given their first pair of wrist bands: one blue for stillness, one red for pressure. At ten, they spend a season in a wind house, sleeping beside its membranes and learning the district’s tones. At fourteen, they are sent alone across the city. The journey is short. The test is not. They must return with a stone removed from their family wall and place it somewhere no one expects.

The Hollow-born are slender, though not fragile. Their bodies have grown accustomed to continual resistance. They walk with a forward inclination, knees slightly bent, feet broad and certain. The muscles of their backs and hands are heavily developed. Their fingers are long, scarred, and roughened by rope, mineral, and plant fiber. They do not wear loose clothing. Every garment is tied close to the body, with hems weighted against the wind.

Their skin is often pale from the reflected brightness, but the palms, elbows, and faces become darkened by mineral dust. Many wear thin metal plates over the ears, not to block the sound—that would make movement dangerous—but to divide it into manageable layers. Others use woven caps packed with felt and dried root. Children dislike these and tear them off whenever adults are not looking.

The Hollow-born have a particular way of standing together. They do not form circles. Circles catch and tangle the air. They arrange themselves in staggered lines, each person leaving room for the wind to pass between bodies. Even at celebrations they remain spaced apart, connected by strips of cloth or lengths of braided fiber held between their hands.

Affection is shown through contact maintained against resistance.

A lover leans their forehead to another’s and remains there while the wind tries to separate them. Friends sleep with their wrists tied together by a loose cord. Parents place one hand against the backs of their children whenever crossing a bridge. To release someone’s hand abruptly is an insult, or a declaration of fear.

There are no private silences in the city, but there are private sounds.

People cultivate small wind objects in their rooms: a reed that clicks only when the door is opened, a membrane that hums at the pressure of approaching dust, a string of mineral plates that rings when someone passes beneath the window. These are not ornaments. They are extensions of attention. A household is known by the sounds it makes.

The richest families possess rooms with many such devices. The poorest may have only one.

No one owns land in the outer sense. The city itself is too old and too interlocked for ownership to remain simple. A family claims responsibility for walls, plants, openings, and mechanisms, but not exclusive possession of them. A roof garden may belong to three households and be tended by twelve people. A staircase may be maintained by one district and used by all. A wind house belongs to no one, though its failures are remembered against particular names.

Their wealth is measured in reliable things: a dry room, a strong bridge, an unfractured lifting rope, a plant that produces useful gas, a wall whose hidden weakness has been found before it becomes a collapse. They trade in mineral powders, fiber, food, polished stone, membranes, and access to skilled hands.

The plants provide most of what they eat.

Some are scraped into pale cakes and baked against heated slabs. Some release dense, bitter nodules that are ground with salt. Others produce hollow seed chambers filled with dry flakes. The Hollow-born chew these slowly, because eating too fast causes the mouth and throat to swell. Their food has little sweetness. Sweetness, in the old stories, is associated with water.

They keep no animals except for the pale foundation creatures, which are tolerated rather than domesticated. A family may leave scraps for one beneath the floor, and in time the creature may begin to recognize the vibration of their footsteps. This is considered companionship. The creatures are never named. To name one would imply that it had agreed to remain.

The upper vent-things are treated more seriously. When one drifts down into the city, people gather beneath it with open hands. If it settles on someone, that person is thought to have been noticed by the hollow. The event may mean nothing. It may mean illness, marriage, an approaching dustfall, or death. No one tries to decide immediately.

The blind grazers from the plain are different.

When one enters through the lower seam, the Hollow-born become almost cheerful. Gates are opened. People climb to the terraces to watch its many jointed limbs negotiate the streets. Its body is usually armored in dull plates and coated with the dust of the outer world. Children are forbidden to touch it, though they do so whenever they can. Adults gather the fibers it sheds and feed it bundles of mineral grass.

The animal is never hunted. It is considered a visitor from the world beyond the seed, and therefore evidence that the world still exists.

When it leaves, the city seems diminished. For several days, people look toward the seam and stand in its warm draft. Some leave offerings there: a bell, a piece of bread, a carved stone, a child’s first wrist band.

The Hollow-born do not have priests in the usual sense. They have **Listeners**.

Listeners live near the wind houses and spend much of their lives with their hands against walls. They learn the city’s deepest vibrations: the settling of foundations, the grinding of buried stone, the distant flexing of the hollow’s shell. Their bodies become thin and bowed. Their hearing is often poor, but their palms are extraordinarily sensitive.

A Listener may place their hand on a pillar and tell a household whether it will survive another season. They may identify a new fissure before it becomes visible. They may declare that a certain street must be emptied because the air below it has changed.

No one asks how they know. Knowledge gained through the body is not considered mysterious. It is considered costly.

The dead are given to the upper niches.

There is no ceremony of mourning that can overpower the wind. Instead, the living gather near the body and touch it in turn. Each person leaves a different mark of mineral paste on the forehead: one line for gratitude, two for regret, a circle for an unanswered question. Then the body is carried upward and placed where the currents are strongest.

The dead remain visible for a while. Their clothing loosens. Their hair and fibers lift. Dust gathers in the hollows of the face. Eventually the wind takes what it can, returning bone, salt, and dried tissue to the city’s walls and plants.

The Hollow-born do not say that a person has gone away.

They say that the person has become part of the passing.

Their greatest fear is not death, nor even collapse. It is a failure of movement.

A blocked vent. A sealed room. A plant dying at its root. A bridge that no longer hums. These are signs of the same danger: the beginning of stillness. The Hollow-born believe that anything held too tightly becomes poisonous, whether a room, a wall, a grief, or a promise.

So they loosen what they can.

They remove stones from their homes. They leave doors unlatched. They cut openings into old buildings. They allow plants to break courtyards and roots to lift paving. When a person becomes too old to work, they are not shut away. They are moved to the bright terraces, where they can feel the whole city through the soles of their feet.

And sometimes, in the quietest hours—not quiet, exactly, but moments when the wind drops into a deeper register—the oldest inhabitants claim they feel something stirring beneath the streets.

They place their palms on the mineral and wait.

The children see them doing this and imitate them. Soon an entire courtyard may be kneeling, hands against the ground, faces lifted toward the hard white light.

Nothing happens.

Then a vine clicks open. A membrane begins to tremble. Somewhere above, one of the wind houses finds its note. The current catches the silver leaves, and the city rises into sound again.

The Hollow-born stand, each reaching for the nearest hand.

They return to their work, their gardens, their bridges, and their narrow rooms with two openings. They move through the brightness in small, well-spaced bodies, carrying no water and almost nothing that cannot breathe.

They have lived so long inside the hollow that they no longer know whether they inhabit the seed or serve it.

Perhaps there is no difference.

Perhaps the city is made of their walls, their plants, their dead, their gestures, and the endless weather passing through them.
