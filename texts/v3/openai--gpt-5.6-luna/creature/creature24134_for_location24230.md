What lives there is not a collection of species so much as a single appetite divided into bodies.

The smallest inhabitants are the ash-thin crawlers that comb the leaves. They have no eyes, only pressure-sensitive frills and soft mouths capable of distinguishing living vein from dead surface. They move in sheets. Wherever an old leaf begins to mineralize, they arrive, stripping it down to its useful fibers and leaving behind a pale lattice that the plants reclaim almost immediately. Their swarms drift through the air when disturbed and settle on anything warm. A traveler can emerge from a thicket wearing them like gray powder.

Beneath them, in the root mats, live the burrowers. Some are no longer than a finger, with shovel-shaped skulls and circular jaws. Others are long, blind, and thick enough to serve as tunnels themselves. They do not merely eat roots. They prune them, open them, and carry pieces of one growth into another. Their nests are chambers lined with bitter secretions, where the animals cultivate pale fungi and hatch young that already carry mineral plates beneath their skin.

The grazers are the first inhabitants a person learns to fear. They resemble long, low animals armored in overlapping shields, but their bodies are arranged around hunger rather than anatomy. A grazing beast may possess several heads, each protected by a different kind of horn, or a single wedge-shaped skull with mouths along its underside. It moves through the vegetation in a straight line, consuming whatever rises before it. Its passage creates corridors, though the corridors do not remain empty for long. Smaller creatures pour into the fresh wounds. Vines knot across the trail. New growth thrusts upward behind the animal as if pursuing it.

Some grazers carry entire landscapes on their backs. Root mats cling to their armor. Thorn bushes grow from the joints. Blooms open between the plates and feed on the scraps caught there. The beasts tolerate this colonization because the gardens conceal their heat from predators and provide warning when the surrounding vegetation changes. An old grazer may be mistaken for a moving hill until one of its flowers turns toward the observer.

The predators are stranger, because they have not been shaped by scarcity. They do not need to be efficient. They are shaped by opportunity.

There are ribbon-bodied hunters that lie flat among the mineral sheets and rise only when something crosses them. There are many-legged animals that use their forelimbs to imitate root movement. There are floating predators with transparent bodies, drifting through the warm air on pulses of internal gas. Some have no visible mouths until they strike. Others open along their entire length, becoming a row of teeth and feeding surfaces.

The largest predators are difficult to distinguish from disasters. They travel slowly, but their bodies are so massive that every movement alters the terrain. A black, jointed creature may spend days forcing its way through a forest, crushing trunks and exposing mineral shelves. Animals flee before it, but the plants do not. They bend around it, climb it, root into its wounds, and eventually turn its retreat into a new ridge. When such a creature dies, it remains part of the landscape for years. Its ribs become bridges. Its skull becomes a shrine, a nest, or a mineral basin. Its interior fills with roots until the corpse rises again as a forested mound.

The most numerous winged creatures are the dust-feeders. Their bodies are scarcely more than bundles of vibrating membranes, hooked legs, and filtering mouths. They enter the opened blooms in clouds, emerge coated in spores, and carry the material across the cavern. Their flight is never graceful. It is a constant correction against heat, pressure, falling grit, and the bodies of other fliers. When one is crushed, the swarm closes around it and carries away whatever remains useful.

Larger winged things hammer the ceiling. They are dark, broad-winged organisms with mineral weights embedded in their chests. They cling upside down to unfinished shelves, then release themselves and strike the stone with astonishing force. Their impact loosens the crust. The roots below receive the falling fragments. In this way, the creatures feed the landscape that feeds them. They are not servants of the plants, but neither are they separate from them. The distinction has no survival value.

There are also the rooted ones.

At first glance they appear to be plants that have learned to move. They have trunks, fibrous limbs, mineral bark, and crowns of rotating mouths. But they hunt. A rooted one can withdraw its anchors and drag itself a short distance across the floor, leaving behind a net of severed fibers that continues to twitch and feed. Some wait for animals to brush against them. Some imitate the chemical signal of fresh wounds. Some grow false shelters—arched walls, dry hollows, inviting shade—and close around whatever enters.

The oldest rooted ones are too large to relocate. Their bodies form groves, towers, and suspended shelves. They communicate through low pulses carried by the roots. The signals can travel for miles, passing through animal tissue, mineral columns, and the living floor. When a distant ceiling membrane tears, an entire forest may react before the sound arrives. Blooms close. Browsers turn. Burrowers vanish into the soil. Something in the grove has received the news.

The native people understand these signals, though they do not pretend to understand all of them.

They are narrow-bodied and heavily adapted, with darkened eyes, layered breathing membranes, and hands protected by calluses that have hardened into translucent plates. Their skin is usually painted or coated with mineral paste, both to mark belonging and to confuse the sense organs of local predators. They wear bark masks packed with bitter fiber. Their clothing is woven from root strands, insect silk, and the shed membranes of winged organisms. Nothing they carry is truly dead. A cord will tighten in response to heat. A knife-handle may bud thorns if left in damp—though there is no dampness in the ordinary sense. A shelter wall must be cut back every morning before it grows around its occupants.

They do not farm. Farming suggests ownership and patience, and neither is possible there. They tend passages, wounds, and useful deaths. A fallen animal may be opened and seeded with edible growths. A dangerous bloom may be fed mineral salts until it produces a tractable offspring. Root doors are encouraged to thicken in some seasons and pared down in others. The people memorize the behavior of entire groves as other cultures memorize roads.

Their villages are temporary because permanence is interpreted as surrender.

A camp may be built inside a hollow carcass, beneath a lifted slab of bark, or between the ribs of a living root column. The inhabitants suspend themselves from the floor at night, since the floor is never entirely trustworthy. They sleep in shifts. One person listens for the deep impacts that announce a forming ridge. Another watches the ceiling. A third tends the walls, cutting away the first pale threads of encroachment before they can identify the shelter as food.

They leave offerings, but not from reverence alone. A bowl of heated salts placed before a bloom may persuade it to open away from a path. A bundle of bones may attract the small scavengers that keep a tunnel clear. A painted handprint tells travelers that a certain wall has recently changed its chemistry and should not be touched. Their shrines are made from discarded organs, shed armor, and the hollow throats of dead flowers. They pray by making themselves useful.

The people have words for hunger, growth, and danger. They have fewer words for individual identity.

A person who has been colonized by too many harmless organisms is not necessarily considered ill. If the growth is fed, if the person can still hear the root pulses, if their movements remain their own, they may be regarded as fortunate. The boundary between body and environment is treated as a condition, not a principle. Children are warned not to let the plants learn their names, but an adult may deliberately cultivate a protective sheath along the spine or allow a nesting creature to inhabit an old wound.

Some eventually become rooted.

They sit in chosen hollows and bind themselves to the mineral floor. Fibers enter their limbs. Their lungs develop filtering fronds. Over years, their speech changes into clicks and subsonic pulses. They become listeners, route-keepers, and interpreters of the deep growth. When they die, they do not leave the community. Their bodies become columns, then walls, then feeding surfaces. Their descendants continue to consult them by placing their hands against the bark and waiting for the slow answer.

Yet beneath all these forms—the crawlers, grazers, predators, winged swarms, rooted ones, and people—there may be another inhabitant.

The cavern itself is learning.

Its intelligence is not housed in a skull or a central organ. It is distributed through the root networks, the mineral films, the pressure-sensitive animals, and the vast membranes stretched toward the unseen surface. A new ridge is not merely a geological event. It is a decision made through countless local reactions. A bloom opens because an animal has passed, because a vein has warmed, because a message arrived from a forest many miles away. The landscape remembers injuries as altered growth. It anticipates food. It tests paths. It closes spaces that have become dangerous and creates others where life can be harvested.

Perhaps the people live there.

Perhaps the animals live there.

Perhaps the cavern is using all of them as temporary organs, changing one into another whenever the work requires it.
