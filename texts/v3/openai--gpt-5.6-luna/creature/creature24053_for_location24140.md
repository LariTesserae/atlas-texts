What lives there is not a species in the usual sense.

It is a continuity.

The first creatures were small enough to fit inside the builders’ cupped hands. They lived in the warm seepage beneath the mountain, where mineral-rich water rose through cracks in the buried stone. The builders gathered them, perhaps as food, perhaps as tools, perhaps because they understood something about their slow metabolism that no longer survives in any record. They brought them into the chamber. They made basins for them. They fed them scraps, blood, softened shell, the bodies of their dead.

The creatures remained after the builders did not.

They multiplied without ever becoming numerous in any simple way. A generation would cling to a wall, digest its surface, and excrete a harder substance in its place. Another would settle over them. Some became pale, many-legged scavengers. Some lost their limbs and turned into sheets of pulsing tissue. Some grew shells thick enough to be mistaken for masonry. Some fused with the stone and continued living inside it, their organs opening and closing behind cracks no wider than a finger.

They are all related, though the relation is less like kinship than accumulation.

A creature in the wall may contain the remains of hundreds of others. A shell may be grown around a dead ancestor, and then around another, until a whole family becomes a single mineral mound. The mound may later split open, releasing pale young that crawl away on jointed limbs. Elsewhere, a living column may contract as one body, though each section is inhabited by a different generation.

Nothing in the chamber is truly solitary. Even the animals that drift alone carry other life within them.

The most common inhabitants are called, if they are called anything, the **underkeepers**.

They have no fixed shape. In open water they resemble long, boneless bodies covered in folded membranes. Along the walls they flatten into many-lobed fans, each lobe tipped with a soft ring of sensory cilia. They can narrow themselves to pass through a drain or spread wide enough to cover a doorway. Their bodies are translucent in places, and through them one can see slow interior movements: strings of contracting organs, cloudy reservoirs, small lights traveling along nerves.

They do not possess eyes as the upper world understands eyes. Instead, each underkeeper bears clusters of pressure-sensitive pits and dim amber organs that perceive warmth, vibration, mineral concentration, and the movement of living bodies. They know when a creature is injured before it bleeds. They know which bricks have shifted. They know where the water has grown too stagnant, where a colony has died, where a crack is admitting cold from the sea.

They maintain the chamber without intending to.

An underkeeper will settle into a broken joint and slowly seal it with its own secretions. It will consume the softening clay around a stair and replace the lost material with a ribbed shell. It will gather corpses from a current and press them into a niche, where other creatures will feed on them until only a hard, pale residue remains. The residue becomes part of the wall. The wall becomes habitat. The habitat produces more underkeepers.

Their labor has no beginning and no end. They are not preserving the ruin. They are continuing it.

In the gallery above the nave, larger creatures hang upside down from the ceiling. They are almost invisible except for their undersides, which shine with a muted blue-white glow. Their bodies are narrow and many-jointed, but between the joints stretch translucent curtains of flesh. They use these curtains to read the water. When a current changes, they fold and unfold like flags in a wind too slow to feel.

They are the **listeners**.

Whenever the deep vibration passes through the mountain, the listeners become rigid. Their bodies align with the masonry. Their limbs press into sockets and grooves left by the original builders. For a few moments, the chamber becomes a network of living instruments. The listeners receive the vibration, divide it among themselves, and transmit it through the walls.

Whether they understand the sound is impossible to say.

They may be listening for instructions from the sealed gate. They may be announcing the movement of the machinery behind it. They may simply be responding to pressure, as trees respond to wind. Yet the creatures pause with such complete attention that it resembles fear, or prayer.

The small shelled animals do not pause for long. They continue their processions across the floor, traveling in lines that follow the oldest paths through the chamber. Some carry mineral plates over their backs. Others grow their shells from fragments of copper, bone, fired clay, and the dark porous material of the ancient paving. Their shells bear patterns that resemble writing only because the original writing has been worn into them.

They are the chamber’s slow scavengers. They scrape old organic matter from the joints. They collect dissolved metals. They feed on the soft bodies of animals that have died in place. When they perish, their shells remain, and new creatures settle into them.

At times, a procession will stop before a doorway or a sealed recess. Hundreds of shelled bodies will gather there, touching one another in a dense, trembling line. They may be following warmth. They may be waiting for a current. Or they may retain some inherited instinct from the days when the builders opened those doors and poured food into the chambers beyond.

No creature seems to know that the builders are gone.

The underkeepers still gather around the hand-sized sockets in the lintels. They still occupy the shallow bowls carved into the floor. They still cluster beneath the circular platform, where the water is warmest, as if waiting for figures who will descend the worn steps carrying tools.

Some of them have developed forms that imitate the builders.

These are taller than the others, with narrow bodies and multiple folded limbs. They move upright for short distances, though they have no bones and no need to stand. Their heads are smooth, pale sacs marked by several amber openings. They enter the galleries, inspect the walls, and press their bodies against damaged masonry. They seem to be searching for something. Sometimes they pick up a corroded blade or a hardened length of cord, hold it for a while, and then place it carefully back into the silt.

They are not descendants in the human sense. No memory lives in them. But the chamber has shaped them around its old requirements. They have become the gestures of vanished hands.

Near the altar platform, one enormous organism has grown through the floor.

It may once have been an underkeeper. It may once have been several thousand. Its body forms a low, spreading mass beneath the paving, visible only where the blocks have separated. Threads of it pass through the joints and descend into the abyss. The platform trembles faintly when it contracts.

The creatures gather around it but do not feed on it.

They treat it as a source.

Warmth travels outward from the organism in pulses. Water rises through the old stairwell when it contracts and withdraws when it relaxes. Small translucent animals ride these currents upward, emerging from the lower architecture like motes in a submerged shaft of light. The larger wall colonies open themselves to the pulse. Their internal organs brighten, briefly, and then dim again.

The organism may be the root of the entire living structure.

Or it may be the final remnant of whatever the builders placed beneath the chamber to make it function. The distinction has been erased by centuries of growth. Its flesh has become stone; its secretions have become mortar; its nerves, if they are nerves, run through the walls in branching blue-white traces.

Behind the sealed gate, it is still awake.

The creatures never force the gate. They do not gnaw at it or pry at its seams. They gather along the bottom edge and wait in the warm water. Their bodies form a pale, shifting congregation. Some press their sensory organs against the slabs. Some anchor themselves to the floor. Others drift above them, suspended in the current, as though listening for a signal.

At long intervals, the gate releases a pulse.

The water moves outward in a measured breath. The floor gives a slight tremor. Heat passes through the chamber. Every animal turns toward the dark slabs, even those too primitive to have anything resembling a face.

Then the gate becomes still again.

Whatever operates behind it does not need to be repaired. It has outlasted the beings who built it and the meaning they gave it. It may be a pump, a heart, a furnace, a reservoir, or a sleeping intelligence whose thoughts have become pressure and temperature. The creatures do not worship it. Worship would require separation, and there is no clean boundary between them and the mechanism.

They are its leakage.

They are its maintenance.

They are what happens when an abandoned function continues long enough to become an ecosystem.

If a human entered the chamber, the inhabitants would not flee. They would approach slowly, first as scattered points of light, then as bodies brushing against the arms and legs. The underkeepers would taste the warmth of the intruder. The listeners would register the disturbance in the water. Small shelled creatures would begin to climb the boots. Soft-bodied animals would open along the walls.

Nothing would attack quickly.

The chamber has no use for speed.

The creatures would gather, not with hunger alone, but with the impersonal attention of a place recognizing a new material. They would test the stranger’s skin for salt and heat. They would settle into the folds of clothing. They would enter wounds. They would follow the currents disturbed by breath and movement.

If the intruder remained, the chamber would begin to incorporate them.

First, the body would become another source of warmth. Then another surface to feed upon. Then another deposit in the paving, another layer beneath the feet of lives yet to come. In time, the underkeepers would close over the remains. Shell would form around bone. Mineral would fill the joints. A new irregularity would appear in the floor.

The creatures would continue moving over it.

And behind the gate, whatever still lives would release its next slow breath.
