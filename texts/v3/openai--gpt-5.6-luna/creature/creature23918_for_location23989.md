What lives there is not a population, but an unfinished intention.

The city’s first inhabitants are the maintenance things. They pass through walls in narrow silver schools, each one no larger than a finger, though none possess fingers or any recognizable anatomy. They are tools that have been given the freedom to choose their next repair. Their bodies unfold as needed: needles for sealing hairline fractures, soft brushes for collecting mineral growth, hooked instruments for retrieving fallen components from impossible depths. They travel without sound except when they meet one another, at which point they exchange brief flashes of red and white light.

They do not speak. They negotiate by pressure, heat, and timing.

If a conduit begins to leak, one arrives before the leak is visible. If the cavern shifts, a dozen emerge from the walls and press themselves against the stone, listening to the stress move through it. They know the city by touch. They know which bridges are finished, which towers are pretending to be stable, which sealed chambers contain dormant machinery and which contain things the city has not yet decided to name.

They are not servants in the old sense. No master calls them. Their instructions have worn away into instinct: preserve continuity, prevent collapse, prepare for arrival.

The city itself is learning to perform these instincts.

At first it responds only to damage. A cracked platform knits itself closed. A flooded passage reroutes its water. A failed lamp is replaced by a warmer, smaller light drawn from somewhere behind the walls. But over time, the responses become anticipatory. Elevators pause at empty platforms as if expecting someone to board. Doors open a fraction when footsteps occur nearby, though no footsteps follow. The ventilation system alters its rhythm when the cavern is occupied, making room in the air for a body that has not yet entered.

It has begun to distinguish between absence and waiting.

There are deeper presences in the machinery, distributed through the black ceramic and silver latticework. They have no single location. A portion of one may exist in the rotating ring, another in the pressure signs, another in the blue currents below the floor. When the city makes a decision, it does so across many surfaces at once: a valve closes, a light dims, a maintenance node turns, and a sealed corridor becomes older than the room beside it.

These presences are not personalities, exactly. They are overlapping civic processes that have become too complex to remain impersonal. One remembers water. One remembers structural strain. One remembers every object ever moved through the elevators. One is concerned only with heat. One has spent so long monitoring the surrounding stone that it has begun to treat the cavern as a body and the city as an organ growing inside it.

They do not agree.

The water-presence wants the chamber opened to the underground rivers. The structural-presence keeps sealing the walls. The archival presence has begun making copies of things before they exist. Somewhere in the upper darkness, the transit system repeatedly activates a route that leads to a station not yet built. Each disagreement is expressed through small physical changes: a bridge extending too far, a corridor narrowing, a pool rising into a trembling column.

The city’s arguments are usually corrected before they become dangerous.

Usually.

The green forms along the wet ledges are another kind of resident. They have no roots, but they cling to the seams where ceramic meets stone and machinery leaks warmth. Their translucent fronds open in response to pulses from the central systems. They feed on electrical gradients, dissolved minerals, and perhaps information. When a new tower grows, the nearest mats change color. When a maintenance node passes, they bend toward it. They are sparse because the city does not yet know whether to cultivate them or remove them.

Some have begun to spread into unused chambers.

The oldest colony occupies the rim of the central basin. It resembles a low wreath of pale threads, though every few hours the threads rearrange themselves into a pattern matching the city’s current internal map. When the inverted shape appears below the black water, the colony turns toward it. Not toward the reflection, but toward whatever is moving beneath it.

The plants may be observing the second city.

Or they may be its first emissaries.

Nothing with a face lives there, though faces sometimes form accidentally in the condensation. A drop will gather along a railing, flatten, and acquire two dark hollows where the amber windows reflect through it. Another will join beneath it, making a mouth. The shape remains for several seconds before crawling upward along its prescribed path. The city does not seem to notice these faces. Or it notices them and considers them harmless.

The black basin holds the closest thing the place has to a ghost.

Far below its surface, the delayed city moves in silence. Its towers are broken in different places. Its bridges lead into darkness. Its lights are pale and distant, and sometimes there are figures crossing them—slender forms that appear only when the real city completes an operation. They never arrive at the same time as their counterparts above. They are always late.

Occasionally one stops.

When that happens, something in the real city stops as well: a pump misses a beat, a lift hangs between levels, a maintenance node waits with its tools extended. The pause lasts only an instant. Then the figure below continues, and the machinery above resumes.

The inhabitants do not know whether the submerged city is a prediction, a memory, or a parallel place being assembled one decision behind them. The city’s systems are aware of the question. They have devoted considerable resources to answering it. New sensors bloom around the basin. Water is sampled, weighed, aged, and exposed to different kinds of light. The basin has been widened twice, though no one can say where the excavated material went.

There is also something in the upper shaft.

It is never seen directly. It travels with the falling water, arriving in irregular intervals as a tremor through the terraces and supports. The city records its descent but cannot identify its shape. Sometimes the impact of the water produces a sound like metal breaking. Sometimes it produces a sound like a breath drawn through teeth. Afterward, new construction begins somewhere.

The city may be receiving instructions from above.

It may be receiving pieces of a visitor.

And beneath the platforms, in the warm dark close to the pumps, there are signs of a life that has not yet become visible. The water there gathers in small hollows and moves against the slope. Mineral films thicken overnight. Soft translucent tissue appears beneath grates, then withdraws when exposed to light. The maintenance things avoid these places, though avoidance is not among their original functions.

Something is making use of the infrastructure.

It does not yet move through the city. It has no limbs, no eyes, and no stable boundary. It exists as a tendency in the wet stone: a preference for certain temperatures, an attraction to the pulse of the transit rails, a recurring pattern in the pressure readings. The city has begun reserving space for it. Empty platforms are being widened. Certain corridors are being kept warm. One sealed chamber has been labeled with a sign made only of slowly rotating bands of blue light.

The city does not know what will emerge there.

It knows only that the chamber must remain ready.

So the place continues to live through preparation. Its tools repair what has been damaged and reinforce what has not. Its machinery grows new organs. Its plants listen. Its hidden intelligences argue over water, heat, memory, and the shape of the future. The basin watches the delayed city below. The stone holds the pressure of something approaching from above, while something else gathers beneath the floor.

There is no one population waiting inside the cavern.

There are systems becoming creatures, creatures mistaken for systems, and the first quiet signs of an ecology built from mineral, electricity, and intention. All of them share the same expectation: that eventually a presence will enter the city and give its preparations a meaning.

Until then, they inhabit the waiting itself.
