What lives there is difficult to separate from what grows there.

The oldest inhabitants are not animals, and perhaps never were. They are the great rooted bodies beneath the luminous forest: organisms spread through the basin in such breadth that a single grove may be only one exposed portion of something extending for kilometers. A trunk is not an individual trunk but an opening, a breathing organ, a lifted finger of a buried body. A carpet of fleshy plates may belong to the same life as the hanging curtains overhead, the roots under the stone, and the pale leaves casting light across the basin.

They do not walk. They occupy.

Their awareness, if it can be called awareness, travels through pressure, moisture, and the slow exchange of fluids. A contraction deep in one root is answered by a tightening in another. The muted thumps beneath the ground are messages passed through living architecture. Information moves more slowly than speech and more deeply than sound, carried through the connected tissues of the country. A disturbance at the edge of the basin may be felt long before it arrives. A new seam in the rock is registered. A pool filling beneath a ceiling crack is known throughout the surrounding growth.

The plants are not passive in their stillness. They are watching in the manner of plants: by receiving. Their leaves sense warmth, mineral content, vibration, and the presence of passing bodies. Their translucent surfaces turn slightly toward sources of heat too subtle for a visitor to notice. Their roots test the newborn stone, entering some fractures and avoiding others. When the earth swells, they loosen their grip. When it cools, they enter it more deeply.

There are no hunters because hunger has been distributed through the system. Nutrients arrive in water, in dissolved stone, in the slow breakdown of organisms that do not visibly decay. Small translucent creatures cling to the leaves and drink from the pores. They may be young, or parasites, or custodians. They have nearly no color and no apparent urgency. Some carry grains of mineral beneath their bodies, placing them in shallow depressions where the plants can absorb them. Others disappear into the root mats and return changed, their limbs rearranged into finer and more numerous forms.

The creatures have no need to flee. The rooted life does not pursue them. It opens.

A leaf may lower itself over a small animal, not to trap it but to shelter it from an unseen alteration in the air. A warm cavity may form beneath a mat of growth. A seam in the stone may widen by a finger’s breadth. Protection here is not an act of kindness so much as a function of the world, like condensation or light. The basin preserves what belongs to it.

There may also be beings that resemble people, though “resemble” is the more certain part.

In the root-built chambers, one sometimes finds upright forms waiting in alcoves or beside the shallow basins. They are narrow and pale, with surfaces like polished bark or cooled clay. Their faces, if they possess faces, are almost featureless: a slight arrangement of planes, a pair of dark recesses, a mouth sealed into a line. They do not breathe visibly. Thin roots emerge from their feet and disappear into the floor.

Some are statues. Some are dormant inhabitants. Some are bodies being grown for a purpose that has not yet arrived.

They stand for very long intervals without moving. Then, when no one is watching—or when the entire forest has decided that watching is unnecessary—one may turn its head toward a new sound in the rock. It may carry a full basin beneath a ceiling seam. It may place a stone upright along a path. It may enter a chamber and become part of the wall, leaving only the faint outline of shoulders beneath the living surface.

These figures do not seem to command the plants. They are more like specialized organs of the larger organism: hands that remember how to arrange stone, eyes that can look across open ground, feet capable of carrying the forest’s attention from one root system to another. The old platforms and marked paths may have been made by them, or by earlier forms of the same intelligence. Their work has not been abandoned. It has been absorbed.

If they have language, it is written in placement.

A filled basin means that water is available, or that someone has passed through and wished to leave it ready. Three upright stones may indicate a safe route. A wall of leaves parted at one height may mark a boundary intended for bodies of a particular size. A root chamber with one opening may be a room; one with two may be a passage; one with no opening at all may be a sealed memory.

The plants preserve these meanings by continuing to grow around them. They do not erase the old signs. They thicken them.

There are larger inhabitants deeper in the basin. Their presence is felt as changes in the arrangement of the vegetation: a corridor opened overnight, a canopy raised, a field of luminous pads flattened into exact concentric bands. They may be animals too slow to see, or mobile portions of the root network traveling beneath the surface. Occasionally the ground lifts in a broad line and settles again. A curtain of hanging stems draws aside without wind. Something has passed through, but no body is revealed.

These beings do not leave tracks because the ground closes behind them.

They may be the gardeners of the country, though gardening suggests a separation between the gardener and the garden that does not exist here. They redirect water. They coax stone into walls. They encourage certain plants into arches and suppress others around the old platforms. They maintain the basin’s stable brightness. They keep the world from becoming too dense, not by cutting anything down but by persuading growth to harden elsewhere.

Their patience is not passive. It is geological.

The country may contain memories that are not stored in objects but in living arrangements. A particular root crossing a particular stone can retain the pressure of a hand from centuries earlier. A flower opening in a chamber may reproduce the chemical pattern of a vanished visitor. A pale stalk may hold the echo of a voice as a change in the way its internal fluid moves. The inhabitants do not remember by looking backward. They make the past present in the same way they make light: continuously, without display.

No one there seems lonely. Solitude is only a temporary distance between connected parts.

A small creature beneath a leaf belongs to the leaf. The leaf belongs to the grove. The grove belongs to the basin. The basin may belong to the deep body beneath the stone, whose limits no resident has ever reached. Even the upright figures, if they are persons, are not fully separate. Their bodies are temporary concentrations of a shared substance. When they stand motionless, they are listening. When they enter the walls, they are not dying. They are returning to a larger form.

And yet individuality has not vanished entirely.

A particular root may be more cautious than its neighbors. One translucent creature may favor the warmest leaves. One of the pale standing figures may continue to visit the same basin, filling it even when no one appears to need water. A flowering body may hold its petals open longer than the others, as though waiting for a name. There are preferences here, perhaps even attachments, but they are expressed through growth, placement, and persistence rather than gesture.

The inhabitants are not building a civilization inside the earth.

They are becoming one.
