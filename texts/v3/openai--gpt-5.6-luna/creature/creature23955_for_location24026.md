What lives in the city is not one species, and no longer knows where one species ends.

There are still people.

They live in the upper floors of the apartment blocks, in rooms kept dry by constant breathing: walls that perspire moisture into bowls, curtains that close when the wind changes, furniture grown from pale wood and repaired by allowing it to root through the floor. They are narrow-shouldered and long-fingered, with skin gone gray from the cold and from generations spent beneath filtered light. Their hair is usually cut short—not for appearance, but because anything left loose begins to catch spores.

They call themselves residents, though the word has become less a declaration of citizenship than a statement of continued presence.

Most of them wear harnesses.

The harnesses are made from braided vine, treated fiber, and strips of old industrial rubber. They attach to doors, railings, power lines, and sometimes to one another. A person traveling across the city without a tether is not considered brave. They are considered unfinished. The plateau has too many ways of misplacing the unattached.

Residents leave their doors marked with small knots. One knot means sleeping. Two means feeding. Three means that the room has changed shape and visitors should knock from the inside. Some doors are covered in hundreds of knots, though no one remembers the people who tied them.

The city’s people do not farm in any ordinary sense. They negotiate with growth.

Every district has tenders: individuals who listen to the foundations, inspect the root pressure under streets, and decide which walls may be allowed to thicken. They spend their lives underground or inside the walls of buildings, moving through warm green passages with lamps strapped to their chests. They carry knives, pruning hooks, and tuning forks. The knives are rarely used. A plant that has been cut remembers the wound and tends to grow around the person responsible.

Tenders learn to distinguish hunger from curiosity.

A hungry root will lift floorboards and seek out the nearest body heat. A curious one will open a door and wait. A grieving one will grow small white leaves along the walls. There are roots that have been known to follow people home for years, pressing gently against their foundations each night as if asking to be let in.

The tenders do not always refuse.

Children grow in the city, but childhood is brief and carefully observed. A child’s first years are spent learning which colors indicate edible tissue, which sounds mean that a wall is breathing, and how to remain still when the ground begins to move. They are taught to recognize the names of the major trees before they learn the names of their own ancestors.

At some point—usually around the age when they begin to dream in places they have never visited—a child is taken to the highest square.

They stand beside the basin while an elder places a hand in the condensation. The water is cold enough to hurt. The child is asked what they can hear.

There is no correct answer. Some hear machinery. Some hear rain falling upward. Some hear a voice beneath the plateau. A few hear the sound of their own bones growing.

Those children are not sent back to their families immediately.

They are taken below the factories and taught to work with the old systems. They learn how to enter machinery without being crushed, how to follow electrical current through living vines, how to sleep while roots move around them. They become listeners, and eventually the people who operate the city’s oldest engines.

The oldest residents are difficult to recognize as human.

They live in the industrial quarter, where the warm spores collect beneath the ceilings and the floors remain permanently damp. Their bodies have adapted to the city’s abundance. Pale filaments grow beneath their skin, most visibly around the throat and wrists. Their lungs are broad and dark. When they speak, a second voice sometimes answers half a moment later from somewhere in the surrounding vegetation.

They are the machine-keepers.

They maintain presses that have become partially alive, turbines whose blades are made from layered root tissue, and conveyor belts that remember the destinations of materials better than any map does. A machine-keeper may spend a week inside a factory wall, listening to the current pass through it. They emerge covered in dust, leaves, and small translucent organisms that must be gently persuaded to leave.

No one asks how old they are.

Some have been alive for more than a century. Some may be the descendants of people who died long ago but continued to operate the machines through their nervous systems. In the northern quarter, a large number of workers share a single shift schedule, though only a few bodies appear at the controls. The rest are distributed through the factory: a hand inside a housing, an eye set into a valve, a mouth speaking from a ventilation shaft.

The city considers this normal.

Other things live there that were never human.

The black grazers move in herds of three or four through the open meadows. They are larger than cattle and lower to the ground, their bark-armored backs furrowed with moss. Their joints are jointed like those of insects, but their movement is slow and deliberate. When they feed, their mouths unfold into layered petals lined with grinding plates.

They graze not on leaves, but on excess intention.

A field that has begun trying to become a road will attract them. So will a wall growing too quickly, or a bridge that has developed ambitions of reaching another district. The grazers strip away these unwanted possibilities, leaving behind ordinary vegetation and a faint smell of wet stone.

People avoid them during migration. The grazers are peaceful, but they do not perceive bodies as separate from the landscape. If one stops beside a person, it may begin to feed on the person’s shadow.

In the drainage canals lives something called the below-water, though it has never been properly seen. It is said to be long enough to pass beneath several districts at once. Its presence is known by the line of green glass that forms behind it and by the sudden flowering of submerged plants. Children throw objects into the canals to attract it. Adults forbid this, not because the below-water is dangerous, but because it has a habit of returning things altered.

A key may come back as a seed.

A shoe may come back with a childhood memory inside it.

A dead relative’s ring may return warm and wet, with the impression of teeth along its edge.

The pale birds in the factory vents are not truly birds, either. They resemble folded scraps of wax or old paper until they open. They have no visible eyes. They navigate by pressure changes and by the sound of human thoughts, which they detect through the walls. When a person is about to make a decision, the birds gather nearby.

Some people regard them as omens. Others believe the birds are simply waiting for decisions to become edible.

There are more secret inhabitants in the sealed buildings.

In rooms that should not fit inside their structures, families of small green figures maintain gardens under artificial skies. They wear masks made from seed husks and communicate by arranging objects in circles. They have never seen the open plateau, yet they know when condensation forms on the outer leaves. They call the city above them the ceiling.

There are also rooms occupied by people who have forgotten to remain singular.

They move in groups, though each group may possess one memory, one appetite, or one name. A dozen bodies might emerge from an elevator carrying a single conversation between them. They sleep in piles beneath tree roots. When they wake, they divide the day’s work among themselves without speaking.

No one can say whether they are sick, blessed, or merely further along.

The city itself has inhabitants of another order.

Every building contains a remnant of its original purpose, but these purposes have become temperaments. The old hospital is anxious and keeps opening its doors during storms. The railway station is vain; it lengthens its platforms whenever people praise it. The power station is territorial and sends small blue shocks through anyone who wanders too close without announcing themselves.

The factories are less like buildings than sleeping animals. They become alert when fed the proper materials. They sulk when neglected. They have learned certain human habits—impatience, secrecy, spite—but not the human habit of ending things. A factory may continue producing a component for a machine that disappeared generations ago, laying the parts carefully in a room no one can locate.

The trees are older than the city and regard the residents with a patience that is not quite benevolent.

Their hollows contain archives: scraps of clothing, tools, bones, metal plaques, preserved voices. The largest trees know the names of people who have not yet been born. They communicate by altering the pressure of their roots, causing foundations to creak, pipes to knock, or entire districts to lean very slightly toward one another.

When they shed leaves, the people collect them and read their undersides.

Sometimes the veins form maps.

Sometimes they form warnings.

Sometimes they spell the name of someone standing nearby.

And beneath everything—the roots, the buildings, the machinery, the roads, the people—there is the pulse.

It is not a heartbeat, though the residents call it one. It does not belong to a single organism. It passes through the plateau as a signal, a pressure, a thought too large to fit inside any living body.

When the basin trembles, the inhabitants stop whatever they are doing.

The grazers lower their petaled mouths. Birds flatten themselves against the vents. Machine-keepers place their palms against the factory walls. People step out onto balconies and causeways, tethered to the city by living ropes.

For a few moments, everything that lives there becomes aware of everything else.

The roots feel the people.

The people feel the engines.

The engines feel the water below.

The water feels the old mountains, and the mountains feel the clouds passing beneath them.

Then the pulse recedes, and separation returns.

The people resume their work. The city continues to operate. The plants go on becoming walls, the walls go on becoming soil, and somewhere below the highest square, something ancient turns in its sleep and begins, once again, to dream the plateau into a different shape.
