What lives there is not a creature, but a continuity.

It has no body that can be separated from the plateau. It is present in the roots, in the white motes circling stones, in the mineral crust sealing the cracks, in the slow traffic of water through the drowned valleys. The plants are not its organs exactly, nor are the channels its veins. They are more like its thoughts made visible: a meadow turning toward an approaching current, a forest opening its crowns beneath the surface, gravel returning uphill to an old hollow.

It does not think quickly. A decision may take a season, a century, or the time required for an entire shelf of rock to collapse and fit itself together again. Its attention moves through pressure and light. It knows where the water is by the way roots tighten. It knows the weather by the taste of minerals. It recognizes a stranger not by face or name, but by disturbance: an unfamiliar warmth in a pool, a footstep that reaches the stone before the body does, a breath held too long beneath the surface.

The fine white motes are part of its sensing. They gather around new objects and remain there, almost invisible, recording shape and temperature. When noticed, they scatter—not from fear, but from the discomfort of being made distinct. The plateau does not like boundaries. A stone is not only a stone; it is also the hollow that receives it, the roots around it, the water that once carried it, and the hands that may have placed it there.

The old inhabitants were not its makers.

They were its first listeners.

They learned to build above the water because the water allowed it. They cut stairs into the stone and discovered that some stairways continued into places where no stairway could possibly lead. They set bowls of fresh water in roofless shelters and found them full again each morning. They marked the height of the sea, then discovered that the plateau had already marked them in return.

Their figures had no faces because the plateau could not preserve faces. It preserved posture, pressure, weight: a person standing against a wall; a person kneeling beside a pool; a person looking toward the deep tide. Those are the things that remain. Identity dissolved early. Function lasted longer.

Perhaps the inhabitants were taken gradually, as visitors are taken now. Moss beneath the nails. Green threads in the lungs. A whitening of the bones, though no bones are left to find. Their bodies became part of the maintenance of the place. Some entered the roots. Some became pale mineral seams. Some remain as delays in sound, as footsteps that arrive before anyone walks, as shapes glimpsed in reflections of the dry plateau.

The old people may still be present in the structures, though not as ghosts. A shelter remembers the pressure of a shoulder against its wall. A copper hook remembers the hand that bent it. A fire circle retains a low red warmth beneath the ash, not because the flame survived, but because the act of tending it was absorbed into the stone. When wind passes over, the circle brightens as if answering a question.

There are also the ones who chose incorporation.

They can sometimes be seen during the falling of the water, when new terraces emerge. They stand among the exposed gardens as thin, dark figures, their bodies covered in wet growth. They have no faces. Their arms are too long, or perhaps they only appear so because strands of vegetation trail from them. They do not walk unless the ground beneath them changes. When the water rises, they lean with the plants and vanish into the submerged forests.

They are not guardians. They do not protect the plateau from trespassers. They have no concept of trespass.

They are the plateau’s older habits.

A visitor may meet one in a reflection before meeting it in the world. In the pool, the figure may stand on dry ground beneath a star-filled sky. It may be carrying a tool, or holding a child, or turning toward a settlement that no longer exists. If the visitor looks away and back, the figure may be closer. If spoken to, it does not answer in language. Instead, water begins moving through a nearby channel. A plant opens. A buried line appears in the stone.

The message is rarely about the visitor.

It is usually about height.

How high the water has been. How high it will rise. What was once above the surface. What must remain above it next time.

The plateau’s deepest inhabitant is the tide.

It is not merely water entering and leaving. The water is the part of the place that remembers motion. It travels through the drowned valleys, beneath the shelves, and along channels cut before the flooding. It carries no fish, no eggs, no drifting animal life. It carries only fragments of the plateau itself: dissolved leaves, mineral dust, spores, old ash, traces of people who entered and did not return.

When the deep tide comes, everything turns toward it because everything recognizes it.

The plants lean toward the distant point not in obedience, but in anticipation. The roots loosen their grip. The old structures open. Foundations hidden beneath the sea become visible, as if the tide is not uncovering them but calling them by name. The water draws away to make room for something rising beneath it.

At the greatest height, the plateau becomes almost entirely submerged. This is when the inhabitant is closest to being whole. The separate things—stone, moss, water, root, memory—lose their edges. The forests glow. The towers become dark stems. The old stairways vanish into the currents. Somewhere below, the incorporated people may wake, not as individuals but as a pressure moving through the roots.

Then the water falls, and the place divides itself again.

A ridge becomes a ridge. A plant becomes a plant. A shelter becomes an empty shelter with a bowl of fresh water inside.

Still, something has changed. A new channel may have appeared. A stairway may lead somewhere different. A rock may settle into a position that feels intentional. New growth rises from cracks, mature and already familiar with the light.

The plateau does not feed on visitors, exactly. It learns them.

A person who stays too long becomes increasingly difficult to distinguish from the country. Their footprints fill with pale threads. Their reflection begins showing places they have never seen. Their voice may return from underwater before they speak. Eventually the plants near them begin to turn—not toward the current, or the light, but toward the person.

At that stage, leaving is possible only by force, and force is poorly understood there.

The plateau does not resist. It simply continues.

It offers a path across a ravine. It fills a bowl. It lowers the water from a passage. It grows a handhold where none was before. These may be kindnesses, or merely adjustments made by a vast system correcting for a new presence. The place does not distinguish between mercy and maintenance.

If the visitor leaves, the plateau keeps their shape for a while.

A patch of moss grows where they stood. A delay enters the sound of their name. In a still pool, they may appear walking across the old dry valleys, always at a distance, always moving toward higher ground. The reflection never shows them reaching it.

If they remain, they become one more quiet process.

Their warmth enters the roots. Their memories settle into the water. Their bones, if bones remain, become pale supports for new growth. Somewhere in the submerged dark, a forest opens its crowns in response to a pressure it has learned to recognize.

And the plateau, cold and green and without a single animal, continues to live through them.
