They are a people of thresholds, ledges, and interruptions.

No one among them stands quite as one stands aboveground. The body is trained from infancy to negotiate uneven floors, low vaults, open channels, hanging ropes, wet steps, and sudden darkness. Children learn to walk by gripping cords stretched along the walls. They learn to sleep through the knocking of water and wake at the absence of it. They can cross a crowded walkway carrying a jar on one shoulder, a lamp in one hand, and a newborn tied against the chest without touching another person or spilling a drop.

Their bodies bear the city’s proportions.

They are mostly compact, though not frail. Their shoulders are strong from hauling baskets and clearing grates. Their hands are broad-palmed, the fingers thickened and shiny at the tips from stone dust, rope, resin, and constant wetness. Knees and shins are marked by pale scars. Many have one shoulder slightly higher than the other, shaped by years of carrying water vessels along sloping passages. Their hair is kept short or bound tightly, not for fashion but because loose hair catches on hooks, snags in machinery, and drinks up smoke.

The people do not smell alike, though they all carry some part of the city upon them. A potter smells of damp clay and ash. A channel keeper smells of copper, waste water, and the bitter herbs rubbed into the skin before entering the lower drains. Those who tend the fungus beds smell sweetly rotten, like wet wood split open. Children smell of smoke, goat milk, and the mineral damp of the stone floors where they play.

Their clothing is layered, repaired, and made to be hung.

The outer garments are sleeveless or close-fitting, allowing the arms to pass through narrow openings without catching. Reed fiber, softened hide, woven hair, and cloth salvaged from the upper world are stitched together with waxed thread. The hems are weighted so that they do not float into channels. Pockets are everywhere: in belts, cuffs, collars, and the backs of aprons. A person may carry a scraper, a length of cord, a flint, a folded reed marker, a spoon, a plug of resin, and three different kinds of seed without appearing burdened.

Wet clay marks their intentions.

A thumbprint on a door means that passage must remain open. A line around a channel grate means the grate is under repair. Three small dabs beside a stair warn of a loosened tread. Marks are placed at the height of the people who need to read them: low for children, high for those carrying loads, near the floor for those who work bent over in the drains. The marks are temporary, but never casual. A person who smears another’s warning is not merely vandalizing a wall. They are altering the city’s memory.

They are not a numerous people, but they occupy one another completely.

Families are difficult to distinguish from workshops, workshops from shrines, and shrines from storage rooms. A sleeping platform may be shared by three generations, two goats, and a row of jars. A person’s closest companions are often not those who eat beside them, but those whose routes meet theirs: the woman who clears the same drain, the boy who tends the same fungus trays, the old man who opens the same floodgate every season.

Privacy exists as a temporary arrangement of fabric and sound. A reed curtain, a lowered lamp, a blanket hung across a passage: these are enough. Everyone knows when not to look. Everyone also knows that no secret can survive entirely in a place where water, smoke, rats, and footsteps travel through walls.

They speak in short practical phrases when moving through the crowded levels. Names are often followed by location or duty.

“Sev of the western grates.”

“Dara beneath the warm vault.”

“Little Oru, who feeds the black channel.”

A person may have several names over a lifetime. One is given at birth, another after surviving a dangerous flood, another when taking up a permanent craft. Some names refer to sounds: Bell-Water, Three Knocks, Hush-Step. Some refer to repairs: Resin-Hand, Keeper of the Split Arch, She Who Bound the Seventh Stair. A person whose name refers to a structural failure is not mocked by it. They have made the failure part of their usefulness.

Their infants are carried in slings suspended from the body, leaving both hands free. Newborns are introduced to the city through sound before sight. They are held near a running channel, a turning wheel, a goat pen, a work hammer, and a quiet wall. The elders observe what makes them startle and what makes them settle. A child who sleeps through water but wakes at distant knocking may one day be sent into the maintenance galleries. One who follows echoes may become a caller, trained to direct people through confusing chambers. One who dislikes open grates is not forced toward the channels; the city has other needs.

The people are listeners before they are builders.

They place their palms against walls. They press their cheeks to paving slabs. They crouch beside channels and distinguish, by rhythm alone, water that is clean from water that carries sand, fish, waste, or a body. They know the ordinary sounds of the inhabited stone and are disturbed by small deviations: a drip that repeats too evenly, a drain that breathes between surges, a stair that gives back a hollow answer.

Children play at identifying hidden workers by their footsteps. Adults do the same thing more seriously.

There is a class of people who spend much of their lives below the ordinary floor level. They are narrow, patient, and scarred around the wrists and ankles. They enter the drains through removable grates and disappear with hooked poles, clay lamps, and baskets. When they return, their faces are washed but never entirely clean. They are called by names associated with descent, but not with death. Their work is considered intimate rather than lowly. Everyone depends on what they remove, and everyone knows that a blocked channel can kill more surely than a blade.

The channel keepers have their own customs. They never step over a flowing line, even if it is only a finger’s width. They do not eat while standing above waste water. Before entering a lower gallery, they place a pinch of grain at the edge of the opening—not as an offering to a god, exactly, but as acknowledgment that whatever lives below is also feeding. When they return, they knock twice on the nearest pillar so that those inside know the passage has not taken them.

The people do have gods, perhaps, though their gods resemble functions more than figures.

There is the Thirst, which is not worshiped but feared. There is the Weight, which must be respected in every roof and buttress. There is the Returning Water, patient and dangerous. There are remembered makers whose names have lost their sounds and become titles: First Cutter, First Gate-Opener, Mother of the Seven Basins. Their statues, where any remain, are rubbed smooth by hands. Offerings collect in the hollow feet of carved animals: grain, fish scales, strips of dyed fiber, a child’s first tooth, a worn tool too precious to throw away.

But the most sacred act is still repair.

A cracked bowl is mended before a new one is made. A loose stone is reset. A leaking joint is cleaned, packed, sealed, and tested. To leave a defect uncorrected is a moral failure because every defect invites another. Their oldest stories are not about conquerors or lovers, but about people who noticed something in time.

A girl hears a different note in a ceiling drip and saves a sleeping tier from collapse.

A widower keeps a waste channel open through a fever season.

Three sisters remain awake for six nights, passing buckets through smoke, until a flood is made to obey an old route.

These are the stories told to children, often while their hands are occupied. The children sort beetle shells by size, twist cord, scrape fungus trays, or remove grit from a basin while listening. A tale that cannot be used is considered incomplete.

They are affectionate in ways suited to crowdedness.

A hand at the back of the neck guides someone beneath a low beam. A heel nudges a sleeping child away from a grate. A person passing through a workshop silently lifts another’s burden for three steps, then returns it. Lovers share lamps, tools, and sleeping platforms, but also routes: one learns the other’s breathing in the dark, the slight pause before a cough, the way their foot hesitates where stone has become slick.

They quarrel loudly because the city carries anger well. Arguments rise through the vaults and return magnified, acquiring the voices of the people in neighboring chambers. Yet reconciliation is often quiet and practical. Someone clears someone else’s blocked channel. Someone leaves a cup of warm grain beneath a curtain. A damaged tool is repaired without comment.

Death is not an absence they understand easily.

The dead are washed with collected condensation and wrapped in cloth that has already been used once. Their bodies are placed in dry chambers above the highest active water line, where the air moves slowly and the stone is warm. Tools are set beside them, though never food; food belongs to the living channels. The name of the dead is scratched into a wall only after the family has determined where the sound of it should be spoken. Some names are forbidden near drains. Some are used only in the upper vaults. A few are never spoken again, because the city seems to answer.

The people believe that no one leaves entirely.

Not in a ghostly sense. The dead persist as altered routes, worn thresholds, repaired cracks, habits of inspection, songs whose words have been forgotten but whose rhythm remains useful. A grandmother is present in the way a child checks a jar lid. A long-dead mason is present in the curve of a buttress. A drowned worker survives in the rule that no one enters the eastern drain alone.

Even their bodies return to circulation. Bone becomes pigment or tool handle. Ash is mixed into plaster. Hair binds fiber. Fat feeds lamps. Nothing is discarded, because abandonment is a form of amnesia, and amnesia is dangerous underground.

They are wary of the upper world, though not all of them have seen it.

Those who go above return blinking, sunburned, and temporarily unable to judge distance. They describe trees as impossible structures, clouds as moving ceilings, and wind as a force with no walls to guide it. Children ask whether the sky makes a sound. The travelers struggle to answer. They bring back feathers, unfamiliar seeds, bright beetles, lengths of grass, and stories that change each time they are told.

The surface people, if there are any, are regarded with curiosity rather than envy. The underground inhabitants understand that the upper world has too much light, too much uncontained water, too many directions in which a person can become lost. They admire it as one admires a dangerous animal.

Still, some children dream of climbing.

Others dream of finding a sealed chamber beneath the sealed chamber, a place where the first builders still sit at their work, waiting for someone to bring them water and ask what the city was meant to become.

The old inhabitants would tell them that the city is not meant to become anything. It is meant to continue.

So they continue it.

They wake to the channel bell, or to the lack of one. They test the walls. They feed the animals. They turn the wheel. They scrape grit from the joints and carry it to the basins. They open the floodgates when the storm speaks overhead. They lift silver fish from the dark water and thank the unseen reservoir. They mend what can be mended and mark what cannot yet be mended.

At night, beneath the low vaults, they lie close together while the city moves around them.

Water passes under the floor.

Bats breathe in the ribs of the ceiling.

Somewhere behind a wall, a sealed doorway exhales the smell of rain.

Everyone hears it.

No one panics. They rise, one by one, and listen.
