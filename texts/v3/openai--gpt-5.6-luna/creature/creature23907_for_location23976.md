What lives on the plateau has learned not to spend itself.

The small things live beneath the surface, where the day’s heat enters the stone and remains. They are pale from generations without light, jointed and soft-bodied, with mouths suited to dust, fungal threads, shed skin, and the slow meals provided by roots. They do not burrow so much as occupy the existing weaknesses of the ground. A crack, once found, becomes a passage; a passage becomes a chamber; a chamber fills with eggs, husks, and the faint mineral smell of living bodies.

When rain comes, some of them rise.

They emerge from the dark in thousands, no larger than a fingernail, and gather in the temporary pools. Their young are almost transparent. They cling to stone with circular mouths and feed on the green film that appears there overnight. By the time the water has withdrawn, they have vanished again, leaving only black clusters of eggs sealed beneath the mud, waiting for the next season that may be years away.

The lizards are more visible, though they have no interest in being seen. They press themselves into the narrowest shadows, their bodies flattened and overlapping. Some are mottled like shale. Some carry thin translucent membranes along their flanks, which they raise to collect the last warmth from the rock. They wake one eye at a time. If danger passes, they remain still. If danger does not pass, they become part of the ground so completely that even the predator lying nearby may mistake them for a seam.

The grazers are ancient in their proportions.

Their necks rise from broad, heavy torsos and move with the deliberation of branches in weak wind. Their mouths are blunt instruments, made to crop the sparse grasses down to their roots without killing them. Many carry hardened shelves across their backs, plates grown from their own bodies over years. Dust settles there. Lichen takes hold. Insects lay eggs in the crevices. Small white birds nest in the warmth beneath the ridges. A grazer may walk for half a century with an entire miniature country traveling upon it.

The animals do not seem to recognize the distinction between body and burden. Nothing here is wasted enough to be discarded.

The long-necked herds communicate through pressure. A footfall travels through the stone. A shift of weight passes from animal to animal. When one raises its head, the others follow, not because they have seen anything, but because some disturbance has entered the ground and spread among them. Their calls are rare and low. A single note may be meant for a herd several miles away. By the time it arrives, the caller is usually gone.

The broad-backed travelers are less graceful and more necessary. They follow the ancient channels whether water remains in them or not. Their padded feet loosen the dust and expose roots, buried insects, dormant seeds, and stones warmed by the sun. Everything follows them: beetles, white hunters, thin-legged scavengers, birds that perch in the folds of their hides. Even the grasses depend upon their passing. Where the herds have rested, the ground retains moisture longer. Where they have walked, the plateau remembers a temporary road.

They do not migrate because of seasons. They migrate because of old instructions.

No one living on the plateau appears to know where those instructions began. Perhaps they are carried in scent, or in the deep arrangement of the stone. Perhaps the animals inherit them as they inherit bone. Each year, the lines of movement alter by only a little, bending around ruins, springs, and certain empty channels. The animals never cross some places. They will approach the edge, pause, and turn aside with the calm certainty of creatures obeying a wall no eye can see.

The hunters have learned patience from the land.

The six-legged cats are not fast animals. Their bodies are long and low, their joints narrow, their coats powdered with the same red and gray dust as the basalt. They lie beside water and wait until thirst has made prey careless. Sometimes they wait through the entire bright day. Their eyes close against the heat, and their breathing becomes so shallow that beetles pass over their flanks.

When they strike, they do so without display. There is no roar, no tearing rush across the basin. The body beside the pool simply changes shape. A throat is caught. Dust rises. Then the plateau closes over the event.

The white hunters arrive afterward. They come in flocks, soft-winged and small, with narrow beaks adapted to picking rather than tearing. Their feeding makes a dry, delicate sound. They leave the bones clean and carry pieces of hide into the grass, where the scraps become nourishment for larvae and pale root-things. The dead animal enters the lives of hundreds of others. On this plateau, death is less an ending than a redistribution.

Above them all, the gliders occupy the warm air.

Some are true birds, though their feathers have become sparse and membrane-like. Some are insects large enough to cast the shadows of birds. Some belong to lineages too old for any useful distinction. They ride the thermals rising from the stone hollows and move through the sky in broad, silent spirals. Their wings are often difficult to see. What can be seen is their collective turning: a dark geography appearing between earth and cloud, reshaping itself around invisible columns of heat.

They feed on one another, on spores, on drifting insects, on the airborne fragments of flowering plants. At midday, they gather above certain places for no apparent reason. When the entire congregation turns at once, the animals below become still. Even the grazers stop chewing.

Something beneath the plateau may be speaking.

The builders, if they still exist, are not found in the open.

There are signs of them in the walls, in the aligned stones, in the doorways that lead nowhere. But the plateau has absorbed their bodies, if bodies were ever what they possessed. Perhaps they were tall once. Perhaps they had hands. Perhaps they did not build at all, but persuaded mineral and water to remember particular forms.

What remains of them is difficult to distinguish from the rules of the place.

At night, creatures gather against the old walls because the stone holds warmth. They press there in layers: beetles beneath lizards, lizards beneath sleeping mammals, wings tucked over furred backs. The walls do not merely retain heat. They seem to release it slowly, according to some pattern that the animals can feel. A ruin is not shelter in the ordinary sense. It is a kind of instrument, and the living know how to place themselves against it.

The solitary doorways are used by no visible animal. Yet they are never dusty for long. Fine tracks lead toward them and stop. In the morning, the dust on the threshold may have been disturbed by something that left no footprints. A faint warmth sometimes remains in the open frame after sunrise, as though a body had stood there during the night.

There are creatures that may be responsible for this.

They are seldom seen because they do not move through the landscape as the other animals do. They move through its intervals. A change in pressure beneath a basin. A darkening under a stone. A series of shallow depressions appearing where no animal has passed. Their bodies, when glimpsed, seem to be made of overlapping plates or folds of skin, gray and reddish and almost indistinguishable from the ground. They have no obvious eyes. They may have no need of them.

They gather where the shadows of the old stones meet.

They do not eat the grasses or hunt the herds. They tend the hidden arrangements on which both depend. A blocked channel clears overnight. A fallen marker is found upright again. Pools that should have dried retain a narrow, cool center. The animals avoid them, but not from fear. The grazers simply give way. The insects become quiet. Even the wind seems to move around them.

Whether these are the descendants of the builders or merely another species that has made use of their remains cannot be known. They may not understand the ruins any more than the lizards understand the ledges beneath which they sleep. A bird can nest in a tower without knowing why the tower stands. A root can split a wall without knowing it was built.

Still, the plateau behaves as though something is listening.

When the deep sound rises from below, every living thing pauses. The herds lift their heads. The insects stop their crawling. Wings fold across the sky. In the temporary pools, the water trembles before the vibration can be heard.

Then the sound ends.

The animals resume their lives—not hurriedly, not reassured, but according to the same long arrangements that held them before. A grazer takes one step. A moth rises from a crack. A hunter opens its eyes. Beneath the stone, eggs wait in their dark clusters, and the roots continue their patient search for water.

Nothing on the plateau is truly solitary. The creatures are bound to the stone, the stone to the water, the water to the old channels, the channels to structures whose purpose has outlived memory. Even the predators depend on the grazers, and the grazers depend on the sparse plants, and the plants depend on bodies passing over buried ground.

The high country is inhabited by beings that have accepted slowness as a form of strength.

They do not conquer the plateau. They do not transform it. They fit themselves into its remaining spaces, becoming quieter, tougher, and more numerous with every age of erosion. Their lives are hidden in cracks, under plates, within roots, along walls, inside the warm hollows of sleeping animals.

By dusk, when the stone gives back its heat and the first nocturnal multitudes pour into the open, the whole land seems to stir without waking.

The plateau is not empty.

It is a vast, patient body, and everything that lives there is one of its ways of breathing.
