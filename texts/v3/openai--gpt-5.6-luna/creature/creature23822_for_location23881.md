What lives there has learned not to mistake emptiness for absence.

The largest animals are the shelf-grazers, long-legged and dark-backed, with coats the color of wet basalt. From a distance they seem assembled out of separate pieces: narrow limbs, high shoulders, blunt heads, and tails held stiffly against the wind. Their bodies are spare because nothing on the plateau grows in abundance. They feed where water has gathered beneath the stone, cropping moss from seepage walls and tearing sedge from the thin soil around the tarns.

Their hooves are broad and divided almost to the heel. Each half can spread independently when the ground shifts. They test a ledge before committing their weight, lowering one foot and waiting for the rock to answer. Sometimes it answers with a tremor. Sometimes a slab gives way. The grazers do not startle easily. They have lived too long beside instability to believe that stillness means safety.

They travel in small family lines, never more than a handful together. The oldest animal walks first, not because it is strongest but because it remembers where the ground has failed before. The youngest follow in the center. When they stop, they face different directions, their ears turning independently toward the vents, the ravines, and the high empty air. They sleep standing when the weather is mild. During the worst storms they wedge themselves beneath overhangs, pressed flank to flank, steam from their bodies collecting in the hollow above them.

Their calves are born dark and wet in sheltered cuts between the shelves. Within hours they can stand. Within days they can cross the loose ground. There is no long season of safety here, and the young learn early to distinguish the harmless settling of gravel from the deeper thumps that mean something below is moving.

The creatures beneath the plateau are less visible and more numerous.

They are pale, nearly blind animals with flattened skulls and flexible ribs, shaped by narrow tunnels and hot stone. Their fur is short and oily, darkening toward the spine where the mineral-rich dust settles. They do not burrow with claws alone. Their forelimbs end in hard, many-jointed pads that press and release the earth in alternating rhythms, loosening gravel without sending it rolling. In places where the rock has softened around a vent, they can move through it like swimmers.

Their tunnels follow heat, but not blindly. Too much warmth kills them. Their colonies occupy the edges of the geothermal channels, where the stone is warm enough to sustain insects, worms, and translucent root-things but cool enough to breathe. They know the plateau beneath itself: the chambers, the rising seams, the channels where water runs black and hot. When pressure builds far below, the burrowers feel it through their whiskers and retreat from the fault lines before the first visible crack appears.

Sometimes they emerge at dusk in long, silent strings, each animal carrying a mouthful of pale mineral paste. They deposit it around the entrances to their nests, building low rings that resemble crude foundations. The paste hardens overnight. A colony may spend generations raising one such ring by fractions, only to abandon it when the stone underneath begins to lift.

The white cliff birds live in the spaces left by the rock.

They are small, compact birds with gray feet and feathers so white that they disappear whenever the cloud rises to meet them. Their nests are deep cups of moss, hair, and shed grass, fixed far inside cracks where neither wind nor falling ice can reach. They lay only a few eggs. Each one is speckled with mineral dust gathered from the vent fields, so that the shells resemble fragments of the plateau itself.

They are nervous only in the air. On the ground they move with a peculiar calm, walking to the seepages in little groups and dipping their beaks into the cold threads. They bathe in meltwater, then stand with their wings spread until the thin sunlight dries them. When steam rolls over the cliffs, they vanish into their cracks and remain there, silent, while the vapor scalds the outer stone.

Their shadows are more noticeable than their bodies. A flock passing overhead scatters a quick pattern across the rock, and the grazers lift their heads though there is nothing to see.

The larger birds do not nest on the plateau. They arrive when the weather opens, circling above the vents and fault lines. Their wings are broad and nearly motionless. They feed on what the ground exposes: dead burrowers, animals caught in sudden collapses, the bodies of young grazers lost to the ravines. They are not cruel, but the plateau allows little room for sentiment. A carcass left in the open becomes part of the place within hours. Birds strip it. Small animals carry away the bones. Mineral water whitens what remains.

Even the stone has inhabitants.

In the wet seams, mats of black-green growth spread over the rock like bruises. They are mosses, lichens, and stranger colonies that feed on the warmth rising through the cracks. Near the vents, some turn sulfur-yellow, others copper-green. Their colors are strongest in the dim light, where the whole ground appears faintly stained from within.

These organisms do not spread evenly. They advance in thin borders around the sources of heat, stopping where the rock becomes sterile or too hot. Their growth records the movement of the underground pressure. A yellow band may mark a vent that has been active for centuries. A sudden dead patch may mean that the channel beneath has shifted. The grazers know this without understanding it. They avoid the bare halos around new fissures and return to old vent terraces where the mineral crust is stable.

There are also minute things that live in the water before it reaches the surface: colorless filaments, heat-loving cells, colonies that cling to the walls of underground channels. They feed on the chemical difference between deep warmth and cold air. They thicken the water, alter its taste, and leave behind the first traces of the deposits that later form terraces and crusts. Much of the plateau’s visible life begins with them, though nothing larger ever sees them.

The living things do not conquer the land. They occupy its pauses.

A grazer stands where a slope has held steady for one season. Birds nest in a crack before the crack widens. Burrowers raise their young in a chamber whose ceiling may one day become a vent. Moss spreads across a stone that is still slowly lifting. Every life is provisional, but none of them seems troubled by that fact. They are adapted not to permanence, but to change at a scale that would destroy more settled creatures.

The plateau has no single ruler and no central nest. Its inhabitants are linked by pressure, water, and heat. The animals listen through their feet. The birds read the mist. The plants mark the safe edges of fire. The burrowers move through the hidden anatomy of the ground, carrying warmth from one chamber to another.

At night, when the clouds close over the shelves and the vents breathe into the dark, the grazers gather near the warmest rock. The cliff birds tuck themselves into their cracks. Beneath them, the pale animals shift through their tunnels, following the slow underground pulse.

For a little while, nothing moves on the surface except steam.

Then the ground gives a soft internal thump.

The grazers lift their heads. The birds wake. Far below, the burrowers turn as one toward the new warmth, and the plateau begins to make room for whatever will live there next.
