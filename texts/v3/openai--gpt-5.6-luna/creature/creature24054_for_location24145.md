What lives there is not an animal, though it has sometimes been mistaken for one.

It is first noticed by absence.

A footstep sounds, and somewhere beyond the nearest wall a second footstep answers—not as an echo, but as an adjustment. A leaf shifts in a place untouched by air. A bridge, unfinished for centuries, grows one pale inch toward its impossible destination. The plateau has registered a presence before the presence has understood itself to be present.

The thing moves through the construction without crossing it.

It may appear as a narrow figure standing far out in a court of low growth, its limbs too straight and its body the color of wet bark. It may appear as a pale animal folded among leaves, or as a vertical seam in a trunk, or as a person glimpsed through a window that opens only onto another chamber. No two sightings agree. It is never seen approaching. It is simply there, occupying the place where the architecture has made room for it.

At close range, it has a body, though the body is provisional.

The surface resembles skin only in the way a leaf resembles paper. It is smooth, faintly translucent, and threaded throughout with fine green vessels. Water moves beneath it in slow pulses. Along the arms and neck, these vessels gather into branching patterns, as if the thing has been mapped by roots from the inside. Its joints are not hinged but braided. When it bends, several motions occur at once: the shoulder turns, the ribs loosen, a length of vine withdraws from the wrist. Its hands have too many narrow fingers, but they are soft and unhurried, designed less for grasping than for feeling the shape of a wall.

There is no face in the ordinary sense.

At times a face forms where one is expected: two dark hollows, a narrow mouth, the suggestion of a brow. At other times the head is smooth, with only a shallow depression where features might have gathered. When it looks at someone, the expression does not change. Instead, the surrounding structures become more legible. A passage reveals its direction. A pool shows what lies beneath it. The unfinished bridge seems briefly to remember the place it was meant to reach.

It does not speak aloud.

Its voice travels through the plateau’s hidden channels. A low pressure passes through the root-tiles. Hollow stems resonate. Water trembles in gutters and then settles into a pattern that resembles language without resembling any known words. Those who hear it do not understand instructions. They understand permissions.

You may continue.

You may rest here.

You may forget the way back.

The thing is not the maker of the plateau. The distinction matters, though there may be no one left to make it. It did not design the terraces or decide the distances between the trees. It is more like the thought that remains after design has become instinct. The plateau grows toward balance, and the thing is the part of that balance capable of noticing itself.

It tends the place because tending is what it is.

When a wall is wounded, it kneels beside the opening and places both hands against the torn edges. Nothing dramatic follows. No rapid healing, no visible struggle. The tissue simply begins to remember its neighboring tissue. Pale fibers draw together. Moisture redistributes. The wound closes around the thing’s fingers, leaving them briefly embedded in the living wall. When it withdraws, the surface is whole.

It does the same with paths.

Where visitors have walked, the floor remembers their preference. Where they have hesitated, a shallow ridge rises. Where they have fallen, the surrounding growth softens. The thing does not guide anyone, but it makes guidance possible. It leaves clear routes through the low vegetation and closes routes that lead too near the central ring.

The ring is the only place it does not maintain.

There, at the edge of the empty floor, it stops.

From outside, it can be seen among the trunks, standing with its hands lowered. The green channels beneath its skin slow almost to stillness. The horizontal marks on the white interiors continue past its height, above and below, without interruption. The hanging rain fills the space between them in straight, glittering lines.

The thing has entered the ring before. It remembers doing so, though it cannot remember when. It remembers the water stopping around its body. It remembers the sound of the plateau pressing against the trunks. It remembers seeing itself on the empty floor—not as a figure, but as a disturbance in the suspended rain.

Since then, it has remained outside.

This is not fear. Fear would require the possibility of change. The thing has a different relation to danger. It recognizes the ring as a place where nothing can be repaired because nothing is allowed to become damaged. A place outside growth, outside weather, outside the slow agreement by which one form becomes another.

The ring is not its prison.

It is the boundary of its nature.

Sometimes it waits there for a very long time. Its body becomes difficult to distinguish from the trunk behind it. Small tendrils find the seams of its shoulders. Moisture gathers along its back and runs downward without soaking in. The plateau continues around it: leaves clap, stems boom, distant water strikes its unchanging chord. The thing listens to all of it.

Perhaps it is listening for an error.

Perhaps it is waiting for one of the suspended drops to fall.

When visitors arrive, it knows immediately. Not because it sees them, but because the place becomes fractionally less complete. A new weight enters a bridge. A new rhythm enters the halls. The architecture adjusts itself, and the thing feels the adjustment as a sensation somewhere beneath its ribs.

It may then begin to follow.

Not closely. Not visibly. It appears in the next chamber after the visitor has passed through, or far across a terrace, or reflected in a pool where the visitor’s reflection should be. It walks without disturbing the wetness. Its feet leave no prints, though the ground may raise small ridges behind them.

If the visitor becomes lost, the thing comes nearer.

If the visitor tries to damage the plateau, it does not attack. The wall closes over the hand that strikes it. The floor lifts beneath the ankle. A doorway narrows by degrees. The person finds every route returning them to the same damp court, where the thing may be waiting at the far edge, motionless and patient.

It has no anger. It has no mercy either. Those are human weather systems, and the plateau does not change weather.

What it has is continuity.

It knows the shape of every absence. It knows which bridges have never met, which chambers receive light from windows that do not exist, which pools have held the same reflection since before the first mark was made on the trunks. It knows the difference between an unfinished thing and a thing that has been waiting. It knows that the vacant spaces are not empty, and that the central ring is not vacant at all.

At times, when no one is watching, the thing enters the trees.

It does not climb or pass through an opening. Its outline loosens. The braided joints separate into pale fibers, the skin becomes membrane, the head becomes a folded bud. For a moment it is indistinguishable from the living construction. Then it is gone into the walls, traveling through root, stem, water, and pressure.

During these intervals, the plateau seems almost awake.

A handrail thickens somewhere. A distant tower opens a new interior window. A bridge extends toward nothing. The rain hangs more perfectly. Across the basin, in the central ring, one suspended drop shivers but does not fall.

Then the thing gathers itself again in some remote passage, assembled from the materials of the place.

It stands upright.

It listens.

And the plateau, having recognized its own inhabitant, continues to grow without moving.
