The people of the basin are difficult to separate from the place that raised them.

They are lean, sure-footed people, accustomed to moving across surfaces that shift, bend, leak, or disappear. Their feet are broad and toughened, their toes strong from a childhood spent on roots and wet planks. They walk with their weight low, testing each step before committing to it. Even the oldest among them cross narrow bridges without looking down. Their hands are scarred by bark, stone, fiber, and fish spines, and their palms carry the dark permanent staining of clay, leaf juice, and smoke.

They dress in layers that can be removed, dried, patched, and put back on again. Woven plant fiber forms the inner garments; animal hide, bark cloth, and treated leaves provide outer protection. Nothing is left loose where it might catch on a vine or drag into a channel. Hoods and shoulder coverings are common, as are belts crowded with small tools: a knife, a bone pick, cord, a fire tube, a folded scrap of waterproof material. Their clothing is rarely bright. It is the color of wet wood, moss, old yellow leaves, and river stone, though individuals mark themselves with dyed thread, polished seeds, feathers, or strips of red fungus cloth.

Children are everywhere, and they are rarely still.

They crawl through root chambers, climb the living handrails, fish in drainage channels, and vanish into the green tunnels between houses. They learn the settlement by touch and sound long before they learn its names. A particular knock beneath a bridge means that a support has loosened. Three birdcalls in quick succession may indicate a snake. The change in a stream’s tone can tell an adult that the upper basin has received rain. Children are expected to listen, to notice, and to return when called—but not necessarily to remain clean or dry.

They are given small responsibilities early. A child may be sent to check a trap, gather fallen branches, collect medicinal leaves, or watch the level of a channel. Older children carry messages across the settlement, help tend animals, and learn which roofs can bear their weight. Their games imitate the labor of adults: bridge-building contests, races along the raised paths, mock negotiations with animals, and elaborate attempts to dam water without angering the people who live downstream.

The adults work in a constant exchange with the basin.

Some tend the living structures, guiding roots around walls, cutting away growth that threatens a walkway, and feeding the trees whose trunks hold the oldest rooms. Some maintain the watercourses, clearing silt, opening blocked channels, and moving bridges when the mountain changes its mind. Others trap fish, cultivate useful plants, weave mats, smoke meat, gather insects, repair tools, or watch the animals. No occupation is entirely separate from the others. A person who harvests reeds must know where water will rise. A potter must understand roots. A hunter must recognize which creatures are tolerated, which are dangerous, and which are dangerous only when cornered.

Their houses are not private in the manner of dryland houses. A room may belong to a family, but the roots around it belong to everyone. Walls are shared with insects, birds, and small animals. Smoke passes through neighboring spaces. Voices travel freely. Privacy is achieved by timing, by curtains, by climbing higher into the branches, or by withdrawing into one of the quiet chambers beneath the roots.

Families are large and porous. Grandparents sleep near children; cousins pass between households; widows and widowers are absorbed into other rooms without ceremony. A person’s place is defined less by a doorway than by the tasks they know, the paths they maintain, the animals that recognize them, and the people who will answer when they call across the basin.

They have names for kinds of rain that outsiders would consider identical. They distinguish water falling through leaves from water falling on open ground, the first runoff from a roof, the clean seepage of stone, and the brown surge that comes after an upper slope has collapsed. They know which pools belong to frogs, which to fish, and which only appear for a few hours before sinking back into the earth. To them, water is not scenery or weather. It is traffic, inheritance, warning, and food.

Their stories are full of movement.

They tell of ancestors who followed roots downward into the first hollow, of trees that agreed to hold rooms in their branches, of animals that taught people where salt could be found. Floods are remembered as persons with tempers, and mountains as old bodies whose joints sometimes shift. A fallen tree may be mourned, thanked, or adopted into the settlement. A new stream is given a name before it is given a bridge.

They do not imagine themselves as masters of the basin. Such an idea would be absurd to anyone who has watched a walkway vanish beneath a night’s rain or seen a wall become a garden in a single season. Instead, they think in terms of keeping relationships in working order. Water must be allowed through. Roots must be guided rather than defeated. Animals must be discouraged, fed, trapped, or endured according to circumstance. Fire must be respected because it is both tool and threat.

Their rituals are practical enough to resemble maintenance.

At the beginning of the wet season, every family clears the channels near its rooms and replaces the cords that mark dangerous ground. At the coldest part of the year, fires are carried from house to house so that no hearth goes dark. When a new child is born, an elder takes a bowl of basin water and pours it over the threshold—not to bless the child, exactly, but to introduce the household to another person who will depend on it. When someone dies, their tools are cleaned and placed somewhere useful: a knife in a communal shed, a basket beside a fish channel, a weaving frame under a roof that needs repair. The dead remain present through the work they made possible.

They are not a silent people. They call to one another constantly, using names, whistles, birdlike clicks, and sharp warnings. A person crossing the basin announces themselves when approaching a blind turn. A worker on a roof calls before dropping a branch. Children shout to animals as though speaking to unruly neighbors. The settlement’s voices overlap with water and birds until individual conversations become part of a larger, continuous sound.

At night, they gather where the ground is driest.

Families sit around guarded fires, steaming their clothes, roasting roots, cleaning fish, and repairing whatever has been damaged during the day. Stories rise and fall with the smoke. Someone sings while working, and others join without abandoning their tasks. Dogs or doglike animals sleep in the warmth beneath the platforms. Beyond the firelight, the basin continues to move: frogs call from the channels, wings beat under leaves, and unseen bodies disturb the vegetation.

The people listen without always looking up.

They know that nothing in the darkness is truly empty. Something is drinking, hunting, nesting, growing, or making its way through the wet undergrowth. The settlement survives not because it has shut the wild world out, but because its inhabitants have learned to recognize when they are being watched—and when they are simply sharing the night.
