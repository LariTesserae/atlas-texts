What lives there are the **Unfinished**.

They have no fixed shape, though each begins with one. Some hatch as translucent shrimp, some as blind threads, some as many-legged knots, some as small plated beings with hands concealed under their faces. Their bodies are provisional. The hot water tests them constantly. A limb may become a tendril, a shell may soften into skin, an eye may open where a mouth was. They do not resist these changes. Resistance, in the hollow, would require believing that one form was more true than another.

They are born from the water-beads.

When a drop gathers large enough to hold its own reflection, something stirs inside it. The new creature slips free without breaking the surface. For a while it remains nearly transparent, with the image of some distant place suspended in its body: a forest, a storm, a field of ash, a face that has never belonged to anything in the pocket. As the creature moves among the others, the image fades. It is replaced by whatever it has touched.

The Unfinished have no names in the ordinary sense. A name would imply a boundary, and their boundaries are unreliable. They identify one another by changes: the one whose left side remembers cold; the one carrying a red horizon beneath its skin; the one that was bitten yesterday and still bears the wound in the wall; the one whose reflection has begun to look back before it moves.

They feed on distinctions.

A small creature will approach a larger one and drink the difference between its body and the stone beneath it. Predators do not consume flesh so much as certainty. When one catches another, the prey does not die. It becomes less separate. Its legs continue walking for a while on the far side of the basin. Its eyes appear in the water. Its hunger passes into the predator, which grows briefly crowded with another will.

The larger four-limbed beings are the nearest thing the hollow has to a people. They are not the oldest, but they are the most persistent. Their plated faces conceal organs that may be mouths, ears, or memories. They work in small groups at the upper ledges, cutting condensation from the walls and prying heat from the basalt. They know how to hold a water-thread without letting it choose them. They know which mineral flakes will remain solid for three breaths, and which will turn into fingers when warmed.

Their tools are never kept. A tool that lasts too long begins to develop an intention of its own.

The four-limbed ones harvest water for the young. They press cool beads into the mouths of newly formed creatures, then hold them still while the bead decides what the creature will become. Sometimes the result is useful: a climber, a wall-drinker, a watcher with many eyes. Sometimes it is a creature that can exist only in the reflection of the pool. These are not discarded. Nothing here is discarded. The impossible things are released into the cracks, where they become weather, or predation, or another kind of light.

They have no shelter because the stone is not outside them. Their bodies and the basin share a history too intimate for walls to matter. They sleep by sinking partly into the basalt. In sleep, their limbs continue to work. They cut, gather, feed, and climb while dreaming. Often they wake with a tool in hand and no memory of making it.

They do not speak, but they communicate through interruption.

A creature stops moving, and those nearest it stop as well. A wall darkens, and an entire layer of animals turns toward the change. One of the plated ones may place a claw in the pool, causing a particular reflection to vanish. This is warning, instruction, mourning, or command depending on what disappears.

They have learned that something exists beyond the pocket. They do not call it sky, mountain, world, or outside. Those words would be too small. They know it as the **Height**, though the Height is sometimes beneath them, sometimes inside the pool, sometimes looking through their own eyes.

Some of them desire it.

These are the climbers: membrane-winged creatures, pale hooked things, the long hot-bodied animals with cracks moving beneath their skins. They ascend toward the place where the walls nearly meet. They climb through the black ceiling and return from the floor, altered each time. One returns carrying a field of snow under its tongue. Another comes back with a second shadow. A third is gone for so long that the others begin to feed on the space where it had been, until it falls upward into the basin as a single drop of warm water.

The Unfinished do not mourn the dead. They gather around the absence and wait to see what it becomes.

The oldest among them are those that have been eaten many times and never completely vanished. They move slowly through the crowd, assembled from too many bodies: a jointed creature with a bird’s membrane, a shell of smoked glass, three unrelated eyes, and a mouth that opens only in the walls. The others make way for them. Not out of reverence, exactly, but because the oldest ones carry too many directions at once.

When the pocket shifts, they remain calm.

They have lived through the floor becoming a wall. They have watched the pool rise into the ceiling and spill downward into itself. They know that gravity is only one of the hollow’s temporary habits. During these upheavals, the smallest creatures cling to the larger ones, the predators continue hunting, and the four-limbed beings grip their mineral tools with all four hands.

Only the newborns panic.

They are the only ones who believe the world has changed.

The others understand that the world is always changing. It is the creatures who keep moving through it. Their hunger is the closest thing they possess to faith. Their tools are prayers made from stone. Their bodies are experiments conducted by heat, water, and pressure. Their lives are brief, but nothing in the hollow is ever entirely lost. A limb becomes a path. A wound becomes a spring. A vanished animal becomes an opening in the rock through which another landscape looks back.

And when the rain begins, all of them gather.

The blind ones come first, guided by the taste of new possibilities. Then the wall-drinkers. Then the plated workers with their chipped tools. Predators follow the prey, and the prey follow the hollows opening inside the rain. They crowd around each new pocket, peering into its green places, its cold seas, its enormous watching eyes.

Some enter.

Some are pulled in.

Some remain at the edge, holding their companions by the limbs while the world multiplies beneath them.

They do not know whether they live inside the mountain, inside the hand, inside the water, or inside the thought of something much larger. They know only that there is stone beneath them, heat around them, and one another everywhere—pressing close, changing shape, vanishing and returning, continuing the great silent work of becoming.
