What lives there is not a people in the ordinary sense, though there are beings that gather, feed, shelter, and seem to choose.

The true inhabitant is the chamber itself.

It is a slow, blind creature spread through stone, membrane, water, shell, and colony. Its body has no boundary. The pale films on the walls are its skin; the mineral ribs are its growing bones; the gill-curtains are its lungs. The worms are its digestion. The blue bacterial lights are perhaps its nerves, or perhaps only the visible consequence of nerves too diffuse to recognize. Every animal in the hollow contributes something to its shape. Some are prey, some builders, some parasites, some organs that have learned to move independently.

It does not think in a head. It thinks in pressures.

A wall softens where the current has weakened it. A bridge tightens when weight passes over it. A mineral bloom spreads toward the warmth of a nesting body. A gate closes around an egg, or opens for a familiar chemical trace. The chamber remembers not by storing images, but by becoming easier to pass through in the places where passage has been useful before.

The tall pale beings are its most deliberate portions.

They move slowly because haste has little value in a world where everything is pressed close and every gesture alters the architecture. Their bodies are thin and flexible, made for slipping between ribs and under shelves of stone. Their faces are hidden behind delicate fans that read the water: salinity, temperature, blood, decay, mating secretions, the distant mineral taste of an opening seabed. They do not see one another clearly. They know one another by the currents they disturb.

When they gather around a new growth, they are not building a house. They are tending a decision.

They lean against the soft stone and alter the flow around it. One presses with its shoulder. Another braces the base with its webbed hands. A third releases a cloud of living cells that will harden into a pale inner lining. They encourage a ceiling to bend rather than collapse, a passage to remain narrow, a pocket to deepen around the eggs of some harmless creature. Their work is intimate and patient. They do not impose shapes upon the chamber. They negotiate with its appetite.

Perhaps they are the closest thing it has to architects, though they would not understand the word. They are more like fingers that have become aware they belong to a hand.

They have no names. Individuality would be difficult here, where every body is touched by water carrying traces of every other body. Still, they distinguish one another. A familiar being brings a known rhythm of pulses through the water, a certain warmth, a certain pattern of hesitation before entering a passage. They meet by placing their hands against the same wall and feeling one another through it.

They are not peaceful. Peace suggests an agreement that could be broken. Their lives are governed by need, and need is constant.

The white fish strip the walls and one another. The flattened hunters wait beneath ledges, their limbs folded into the mineral roughness until they seem like cracks. The transparent eels occupy channels too narrow for anything else and lash out at whatever brushes their sides. The shelled creatures comb eggs from the current with almost ceremonial delicacy, consuming some and carrying others into protected hollows. The great many-mouthed predator folds through the upper city like a moving absence, tasting every street.

When it comes, the inhabitants do not sound an alarm. There is nowhere to flee that is truly elsewhere. The fish vanish. The gates contract. The pale beings flatten themselves into recesses and become part of the walls. Blue colonies extinguish their light. The whole settlement enters a moment of suspended breathing.

Then the predator passes.

The fish return first, pouring through the corridors in white clouds. The blue nodules kindle behind them. The worms emerge from the sediment. A damaged bridge flexes and begins to repair itself. One of the tall beings places its hand against the stone, feeling for what has changed.

The dead are not carried away. They become material.

A fallen fish is taken by the worm colonies and reduced to chalk. A broken shell becomes part of a threshold. A drowned predator is buried in the lower basin, where its bones are slowly threaded with living mineral. Even memory has a physical form: a ridge where many bodies sheltered, a widened channel worn by generations of passage, a crust that contains the chemistry of old blood.

The inhabitants understand loss as rearrangement.

They may grieve, if grief is the lingering impulse to return to a place where another body once rested. They may mourn by maintaining a cavity after its occupant is gone, leaving a gate open longer than necessary, allowing the current to pass through an empty chamber. But eventually something enters. Eggs drift in. A worm colony takes hold. A wall grows across the entrance. The hollow becomes useful again.

Nothing is permitted to remain merely vacant.

The tall beings do not dream of escape. The open sea is too vast, too bright, too wasteful. Out there, space would separate them from everything that sustains them. Here, the walls press close enough to touch, and every current carries information. The city is dangerous, but danger is legible. A change in pressure. A vibration through the ribs. A chemical warning in the water. A sudden extinguishing of blue light.

They live by listening with their entire bodies.

Sometimes one of them finds the seam above the chamber, where the weak gray light enters. It may rise through the stacked architecture, passing beneath living bridges and between the pale fish. At the uppermost level it pauses, feathery organs extended toward the unfamiliar brightness.

It does not know the sky.

It knows only that the light is thin, cold, and distant, and that nothing in the chamber grows toward it for long. The being returns to the lower levels, to the pressure of stone and the warmth of clustered bodies. Behind it, the current closes over the passage.

In the deepest pocket, the new growth continues to harden.

The beings remain gathered around it, shaping its entrance with their hands. A small shelled animal crosses one of their backs. Fish pass through the unfinished arch. Mineral dust settles over their shoulders like pale ash. Somewhere above, the predator tastes the water and turns.

The chamber contracts.

The walls answer.

And the inhabitants, who are also the city, continue becoming—feeding, sheltering, repairing, consuming, and growing around one another in the dark.
