What lives there is difficult to separate from what the factory does.

At first it appears to be a pale animal moving along the central trench. It rises with the blind colony when the floor is disturbed, longer than the others and narrower, its body jointed in places where no fish would have joints. It has no eyes. Its head is a blunt arrangement of feelers, pressure pits, and soft plates that open and close against the current. Sediment clings to it. Small white animals travel across its back without being noticed.

It does not swim so much as accept the movement of the water. When the turbines turn, it is carried upward through the room. When the pressure gates open, it vanishes into the trench and remains there until the current has finished changing its mind.

The smaller creatures do not flee from it.

They gather around it.

Fish shelter in the folds beneath its body. Eels thread themselves through the channels between its joints. The transparent shrimp pick organisms from its skin. Even the lampreys, which attach themselves to anything slow enough to feed upon, seem to recognize some limit in it. They approach, touch its pale surface, and withdraw.

It is not harmless. Nothing in the chamber is harmless. But it is not a hunter in the ordinary sense. It does not chase. It does not ambush. It waits for failures.

A loose cable trembles above the floor, its insulation swollen and splitting. The creature turns toward it. The change is so slight that at first it seems to have been caused by the water. Then one of its feelers extends.

The cable has been vibrating for decades. The creature has known its vibration for nearly as long. It follows the signal through the dark, moving beneath the catwalks and between the pillars. Where the cable has pulled free from its bracket, the creature presses its body against it. Its skin opens along a seam. Fibrous material emerges—gray, wet, and threaded with thin mineral filaments. The fibers wrap around the cable and hold it against the wall.

The cable stops knocking.

The creature leaves.

This is how it lives. It attends to motion.

It has no nest, because the whole factory is its nest. It has no territory, because its territory is defined by sound, pressure, current, and the slow failure of materials. It knows every machine not by sight but by the particular disturbances each one sends through the water. The turbines speak in a broad, repeated concussion. The old piston produces a shallow vertical tremor. The conveyor belt gives a dry sequence of knocks, one every several minutes, though there is nothing left upon it to carry.

The creature knows when one sound is missing.

A bearing begins to seize somewhere beyond the haze. The turbines continue turning, but one of them has acquired a roughness beneath its ordinary bellow. The creature detects the change before the mesh barrier begins to shake. It leaves the trench and rises through the open water.

As it passes, the animals make room.

It enters the turbine chamber through a gap in the barrier. Its body contracts, flattening into a ribbon. The blades are turning slowly enough that each one passes with the force of a moving wall. The creature does not touch them. It moves between the rotations, guided by the pressure wake, and reaches the bearing housing.

There is a crack in the casing.

Inside, warmth has gathered. The warm place is crowded with life: small fish, black-green fans, translucent worms, and a dense film of bacteria that glows faintly when disturbed. The creature settles against the housing and begins to feed. Not on the organisms. On the metal.

Its mouth is hidden beneath its head, surrounded by layers of hard, rasping plates. It scrapes rust from the crack and consumes the loosened scale. It takes in flakes of iron, mineral, shell, and old grease. In its body, these become something else. A dark paste passes through its length and emerges from pores along its sides as a sealant.

The crack closes by degrees.

The bearing remains imperfect. It will never become new. The creature does not make anything new. It reduces the speed of failure. It rounds the damaged edge. It gives the moving part another century.

This is the nearest thing it has to purpose.

Whether it was born there or changed into existence there cannot be known. Its oldest tissues contain traces of things that once occupied the factory: wire insulation, pressure-door rubber, fish bone, human hair, powdered concrete. Some of the mineral plates along its spine carry faded paint. A yellow stripe runs down one side, interrupted by scars and growths. Along its underside are small red patches that brighten whenever the warning lamps above the control cabinets pulse.

Sometimes, when the lamps remember electricity, the creature rises to meet them.

It moves through the open chamber and turns beneath the cracked glass faces of the indicators. The needles twitch. Orange filaments flare. For a moment, the creature’s body reflects the weak light in a sequence of dull bands, as though the machine is reading it.

Then the current fades, and both return to darkness.

The creature is most active during the pressure reversals. When the gate opens, the water pulls through the factory with enough force to strip silt from the floor and shake loose the dead. The creature does not resist the surge. It anchors itself at the central trench, extending its body through several openings at once. Its limbs—or roots, or cables—catch in the grating. The blind white animals rise around it in a cloud.

During these intervals, it listens to the entire room.

Every pipe is speaking. Every chain, plate, valve, and door is being tested by the moving water. The creature sorts the sounds. It remembers them. It detects the new break hidden beneath the old noise: a bracket beginning to shear, a pressure door settling crookedly, a section of concrete hollowed behind its skin.

It cannot mend everything.

Some failures are too large. Some have already become part of the structure’s stable shape. The creature does not try to restore the factory to what it was. It has no memory of dry floors or human hands. A collapsed stair is as natural to it as a reef arch. A sealed doorway is a nursery. A dead motor is a warm hill covered in algae.

It maintains only what still moves.

When the pressure gate closes, the creature releases the trench. It drifts into the settling water, pale and nearly invisible. Fish return to the spaces around it. The blind animals sink back into the sediment. A thin cloud of rust falls from its body and disappears between the slabs.

For a while, it lies against the floor.

Then the conveyor belt knocks once.

The creature lifts its head.

The sound comes again several minutes later: one link advancing, then stopping. The belt is carrying nothing. Its motion is pointless, but it is motion nonetheless. Beneath the silt, one of the rollers has begun to grind.

The creature turns toward it.

Behind the creature, in the dimness of the drowned machine room, the factory continues to operate. Its turbines bellow. Its cables answer. Water falls from the ceiling in heavy curtains. Small fish flash in the green dark and vanish.

The thing that lives there moves toward the next worn place, carrying the whole habitat with it—not in its body exactly, but in its attention.

Wherever something still turns, it is listening.
