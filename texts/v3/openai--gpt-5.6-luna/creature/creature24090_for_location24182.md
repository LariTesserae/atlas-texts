The people of the basin are built for keeping what little they have.

They are shorter than surface-dwelling humans, broad through the torso, with heavy joints and compact limbs. Their skin is gray-brown or pale green, usually dull rather than reflective, and covered in a fine, close growth that holds a thin layer of oil against the body. Along the backs of their hands, necks, and faces the skin is thickened into small overlapping plates. These plates crack and shed with age, but do not bleed easily. Their mouths are narrow, their lips strong, and their noses can be sealed almost completely against dust.

They do not sweat in the ordinary way. Heat leaves them slowly through the palms, soles, and the thin membranes behind the ears. When they must travel far, they wrap themselves in layered fiber and resin, not to keep warm but to prevent the body’s moisture from escaping. A healthy person carries water in the tissues of the neck, belly, and upper arms. Hunger makes them thin; thirst makes them rigid and quiet. The oldest among them resemble bundles of bark drawn tightly over bone.

Their eyes are large, but not bright. They are adapted to the even grayness of the basin and are easily hurt by sudden light. In the deeper districts, many people wear shallow stone or shell shades over their brows. Their ears are small and folded close to the head, though the hearing beneath them is extraordinarily fine. They can distinguish the movement of a familiar person from the movement of an animal by the quality of the footfall alone. They know which wall is being used by which colony of burrowers. They can hear a distant roof settle and tell whether the damage is structural or merely mineral.

The city’s deafening center has changed the way they speak.

They use voices in close quarters, but ordinary speech is low, breath-saving, and clipped. Public conversation is conducted through the hands, shoulders, and face. A finger placed against the wrist can mean *wait*. Two taps beneath the jaw can mean *danger above*. A palm pressed briefly to the sternum is a greeting, but also an acknowledgment: *I have felt you arrive.*

Families and work groups develop private vocabularies of touch. Some signs are so subtle that an outsider cannot see them—a change in the angle of a knuckle, a pause before closing the hand, a thumb drawn once along the side of the palm. Children learn these gestures before they can form words. They are taught to watch the whole body, not just the face. A person who cannot read the hesitation in another’s shoulders is considered dangerously inattentive.

They are not silent people. In the quieter avenues, they sing.

Their songs are low and repetitive, built from tones that carry along the floor and through the stone rather than through the air. Workers sing while scraping mineral crust from a threshold or binding a damaged wall. Mothers sing into the chambers where infants sleep. The dead are not mourned with wailing, which wastes breath and cannot be heard clearly in the central districts, but with a long descending pulse repeated by everyone who knew them. The sound travels through the paving slabs. Sometimes the entire neighborhood joins.

Their clothing is made from plant fiber, animal membrane, and the dried skins of broad leaves. It is wrapped close to the body and sealed at the seams with resin. Garments are repaired until no original material remains visible. A cloak may contain fibers taken from a hundred generations of clothing. Wealth is measured partly in stored food, but more importantly in the quality of one’s seals, the age of one’s tools, and the number of people who will remember how to mend what one has made.

They have no profession that can be separated entirely from maintenance.

Some tend the mineral gardens, kneeling for hours beside cracks in the floor and placing seed dust into seams with a bone stylus. Some cultivate the resin-bearing plants whose leaves are cut only once every several years. Others manage the herds, guiding the long-legged grazers between the green bands of the plain. There are people who specialize in ceiling roosts, climbing old ladders and crawling along the low upper rock to remove diseased growth or relocate a colony before it overwhelms a settlement.

Wall-workers spend their lives inside the city’s passages. They know the hidden routes beneath courtyards and the older foundations below the streets. Their bodies become narrow and flexible from generations spent moving through tight spaces. They mark safe tunnels with shallow grooves that can be read by fingertips. A passage with three horizontal cuts is stable. A passage with one interrupted line is occupied. A passage marked by a circle should not be entered until the next mineral shift.

The city is governed by those who remember its weaknesses.

They are called, in translation, the Keepers of Continuance, though they are neither rulers nor priests. Their authority comes from knowing what has failed before. They maintain records in carved tablets, layered walls, and patterns of planted growth. A district’s history may be encoded in the order of its courtyards: old foundation, repair, collapse, enclosure, regrowth. The oldest records are no longer legible as writing, but the Keepers can still tell that a certain wall was rebuilt after a roof pressure event, or that a particular grove was planted to draw salts away from a residential chamber.

There is little argument over ownership. Stone belongs to whoever can keep it standing. A house is not inherited as a fixed object; it is inherited as a responsibility. If a family dies out, their rooms are opened, inspected, and usually converted into storage or garden space. Abandoned chambers are not left empty for long. Animals enter them. Roots cross the thresholds. Another household eventually moves in and becomes part of the building’s long succession.

The people do not fear the dead, but they do not keep them close.

Bodies are stripped of useful materials and placed in mineral beds beyond the inhabited streets. There they dry, split, and gradually disappear into the roots. Personal objects are rarely buried with them. A knife may be given to a child. A garment may be unraveled. A carved bowl may be set in a public chamber where anyone can use it. To retain an object without purpose is considered a form of neglect.

Children are born small and dark-eyed, with soft skin that hardens during their first years. They are carried in sealed slings against the parent’s body. Infants learn the city through vibration before they learn it through sight: the pulse of nearby feet, the scratching of wall colonies, the heavy tremor of distant herds. They sleep through noises that would terrify a stranger. When they wake, they often place their hands against the floor before opening their eyes.

They are not wholly separate from the other life around them. They eat the dense fruits of the mineral plants, the inner tissues of certain animals, and pale fungal growths harvested from old walls. They breed some creatures for fiber, some for meat, and some simply because their movements loosen useful dust from the paving seams. They know which winged colonies are safe to approach and which become violent during their breeding season. They leave portions of planted beds untouched for animals that cannot be domesticated. A city that consumed everything around it would not survive long enough to become old.

Their greatest virtue is patience, though patience is not always kindness.

They can wait years for a wall to dry before sealing it. They can plant a grove whose full value will not be seen by their descendants. They can spend an entire lifetime preserving a passage that may eventually be closed by the basin’s slow pressure. But they are suspicious of novelty, especially novelty that requires speed. A new tool must prove itself over generations. A new settlement must first be built beside the old one, never in place of it. Even personal grief is expected to settle gradually, like dust in a sealed chamber.

When they travel beyond the city, they move in groups and remain close to the marked routes. Each traveler carries food, resin, a scraping blade, and a small vessel of living plant tissue. They do not carry water, because water is too valuable to transport in exposed form. Instead they carry bodies that can be opened and eaten when needed.

At a distance, among the pale plants and low stone walls, they are difficult to distinguish from the basin’s other inhabitants. Their backs are bent, their movements economical, their garments gray with dust. They cross the open ground in deliberate lines, pausing often to touch a pillar or inspect a seam in the floor.

They do not seem like visitors to the buried world.

They seem like one of its long-established habits: another form of life that has learned to live without waste, another patient colony occupying the space between stone and root, speaking softly where it can and touching the walls to learn what has changed.
