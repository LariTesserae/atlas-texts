What lives there is not a single creature, nor even a collection of creatures, but a kind of distributed inhabitant: the city itself, made animate by everything that has learned to use it.

Its body is spread through the basin.

The roots are its buried nerves. The old channels are its veins. Water passes through it in sudden pulses, vanishing beneath stone and reappearing far below. The cisterns are its organs, dark and receptive, storing what arrives from the sky. Its skin is masonry warmed by the sun, cooled by rain, and continually broken and healed by moss, claws, wings, and roots.

No one animal understands the whole of it. The bats know the bridges. The frogs know the reservoirs. The snakes know the gardens and the drains. The birds know the towers, the open courts, and every place from which a flock can rise without striking stone. Small mammals know the walls from within, moving through cavities that were rooms before they became passages. Each species possesses only a portion of the ancient design, yet together they occupy it more completely than its builders ever did.

At dusk, the inhabitants begin to change places.

Lizards leave the hot parapets and descend into cracks. Bats pour out from beneath the galleries. Insects rise from the vegetation in a fine, shifting cloud. Frogs call from the water with a confidence that seems almost architectural, as though each reservoir has been assigned its own voice. Birds settle into the higher masonry, folding themselves into ledges and broken windows until they resemble knots in the stone.

Then the small ground-dwellers emerge.

They are pale where they live in darkness, brown and rust-colored where they travel through the open courts. Their bodies are narrow and flexible, built for stairs, gutters, and the gaps between courses of masonry. They have broad, sensitive feet that feel water beneath paving slabs and detect the faint vibrations of approaching wings. Their eyes are large but not bright; they rely as much on scent, touch, and remembered routes as on sight.

They live in families, though the families overlap until they become difficult to distinguish. A litter may sleep in a collapsed pantry, feed in a courtyard, drink from three different cisterns, and disappear for days into the walls. They carry seeds, fallen fruit, eggshells, feathers, and pieces of soft bark into hidden chambers. Their dens are never entirely dry. They prefer the warmth held by old stone after sunset, and they gather in quiet heaps where the walls are thickest.

They do not rebuild the city. They maintain it by passing through it.

Their feet clear certain stairs. Their bodies press soil into particular corners. Their droppings feed the gardens. They carry seeds into courtyards and spread the growth that later cracks the walls. They open paths for others and close them again. A channel blocked by roots may be exposed by their scratching; a loose stone may be dislodged by a nest, a burrow, or the slow traffic of many small bodies. What appears to be neglect is partly their method of inhabitation. They preserve the old order without intending to preserve anything.

When rain comes, all distinctions become less certain.

The hidden animals leave their refuges. Snakes cross the glistening pavements. Frogs climb out of the cisterns and cover the terraces. Birds crowd the drainage mouths, stabbing at insects driven from the wet vegetation. The small mammals move along the walls in frantic lines, seeking higher ground and newly uncovered food. For a few hours the entire basin behaves like one creature startled awake.

Water runs through its passages. Wings lift from its roofs. Roots drink. Stone sweats. Every living thing moves according to the same ancient arrangement, drawn toward the reservoirs and away from the flooding channels. The city’s design appears not as architecture but as instinct.

Afterward, when the rain has passed, the basin settles again. The animals return to their territories. The birds resume their separate calls. The frogs withdraw to the black mouths of the cisterns. The little wall-dwellers vanish into the masonry, leaving only a few wet tracks and the faint disturbance of leaves.

But the inhabitation continues.

Something is always crossing a bridge, drinking from a hidden basin, nesting in a broken tower, or carrying a seed into a room where no human foot has stepped for centuries. The ruins are not occupied in the old sense. They are being digested—slowly, communally, without hunger or intention—by a vast succession of lives.

What lives there is the habit of life itself: water remembered by stone, shelter remembered by animals, and an entire mountain city inhabited so thoroughly that even its emptiness has become a form of presence.
