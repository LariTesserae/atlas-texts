They are called the **Listeners**, though this is only the nearest translation.

They are not tall. The basin has taught them to keep their bodies close to the ground, where the stone is warmer and the changes can be felt first. Their limbs are compact and strong, their hands broad-palmed and heavily jointed. The skin of their faces is darkened by mineral dust and cold, but beneath it runs a pale, almost luminous undertone, as if they too carry some reflection of the snowfields. Their hair is worn short or bound tightly against the wind. In the oldest people, it grows white and fine as the cliff plants, and often lies permanently damp against the skull.

They dress in layered wraps of felt, hide, woven grass, and the fibrous sheets harvested from the high plants. Nothing is dyed brightly. Their colors are slate, bone, moss, rust, and the deep green of wet stone. Clothing is made to loosen. Sleeves unfasten. Belts release. Boots can be opened at the ankle. A person who becomes caught in a sudden shift must be able to leave a garment behind without being dragged with it.

Every Listener carries a length of cord.

Some wear it looped around the wrist; some braid it into their hair; some fasten it to a belt of carved bone. The cord is not merely for climbing or securing furniture. It is used to measure the town’s alterations. Each morning, household members tie one end to a fixed point and mark where the other end falls. If the distance has changed, they do not ask why. They record it.

The people keep records in many forms. Their oldest histories are carved into walls, but not as sentences. A circle means a settled year. A descending line means a movement of stone. Three close cuts indicate water that arrived from below. Certain marks are touched rather than read, because the fingertips can distinguish a warning from an account long after rain and lichen have blurred the surface. Children learn the wall-signs before they learn letters. They are taught that a house is not where it stands, but where it has stood and where it may stand next.

Their speech is soft and difficult for outsiders to hear. A Listener does not waste breath on volume when a tap will do. Messages travel through doorframes, cistern rims, bridge rails, and the packed walls of houses. There are household rhythms, work rhythms, mourning rhythms, and the quick three-beat warning that means: **the stone beneath you is awake**.

They also speak with the animals.

Not in the manner of charming them or commanding them. The animals are not possessions, and no Listener would call one a pet. Instead, people have learned the meanings of proximity. A cluster of roof-creatures facing east may indicate a pressure change in the cliffs. The eyeless birds turning their dark faces toward an empty stair may be watching something move beneath it. White bodies gathering in a drain mean that the water there is safe to drink—or that something farther upstream is not.

Children are given animals to observe before they are trusted with tools. They spend long hours lying on roofs, motionless among the shaggy mountain beasts, learning which creatures sleep heavily and which only pretend. They learn never to block an animal’s route, never to touch a thing that has come out of a wall, and never to assume that a creature is small merely because it is close.

The settlement has families, but not quite in the way outsiders understand them. A household is formed by shared thresholds rather than blood. People who sleep behind the same movable door belong to one another, though they may have been born in different districts or even found as infants in the reeds beside a channel. Children are raised by the whole tier. A child’s first word is often not a name but a direction: *under*, *away*, *listen*, *water*.

Names change.

A newborn receives a temporary name, usually drawn from some harmless feature of the day: Quiet Rain, Red Crack, Third Bell, Warm Wall. When the child survives its first major basin-change, it is given a truer name, one associated with the manner in which it endured. Someone who remained calm while a house shifted might be called **Held-Through**. Someone who noticed a new flow beneath the paving might become **First-Water**. The names are not always flattering. A person who repeatedly steps where the ground is thinking may be known as **Almost-Fallen** for the rest of their life.

The most respected inhabitants are not rulers. They are those who can tell when the mountain is preparing to alter itself.

They are called **Hearers**, though their work involves the palms, the tongue, the soles of the feet, and sometimes the entire body. A Hearer presses against a wall and waits. They taste water from a fresh seam. They sleep on bare floors. They place bowls on the paving and study the tremor of the reflected light. Some can identify a coming collapse by the way dust settles in a gutter. Others stand for hours at the reservoir, watching the sheets of overflow, listening for a second sound beneath the first.

A Hearer may advise a household to move its beds, remove a lintel, open a roof, or abandon an entire room. Their instructions are obeyed without argument, but not because they are thought infallible. The people understand that certainty is dangerous. A warning is usually phrased as a question.

*Would it be wise for this wall to remain here?*

The answer is shown by what happens next.

There are also the **Menders**, who rebuild after a change without trying to restore what was lost. They do not straighten leaning houses or return streets to their previous courses. They fit new stone into whatever shape the old stone has become. Their work is patient and almost tender. A Mender may spend a month joining a collapsed stair to a new channel, making a bridge from the damage rather than over it.

The youngest Menders are taught to leave gaps. No wall is considered finished if it cannot breathe, drain, flex, or permit the passage of a small animal. A perfectly sealed building is regarded with suspicion. It is likely to crack in a single, violent thought.

There are **Water-Keepers**, who tend the reservoir and remember the routes beneath the town. They know which cisterns are fed by snowmelt, which by underground springs, which by old channels that no longer appear on any wall. They inspect the water with bone spoons and silverless mirrors. During disturbances, they walk waist-deep through the streets, opening and closing gates while the rest of the settlement watches from raised planks.

Their greatest taboo is stillness imposed by force.

To dam a living channel, wedge a shifting door, nail a roof beam to a wall that wants to move—these acts are not merely foolish. They are forms of violence. A person who commits them is not punished with imprisonment, since no cell in the basin can be trusted to remain a cell. Instead, they are made to accompany the Menders until they have learned how to release what they once tried to hold.

The dead are not buried deeply.

Deep ground belongs to water, and water does not keep what it is given. Bodies are placed in shallow stone beds along the upper cliffs, where wind and cold preserve them for a time. Their possessions are divided according to usefulness. Their names are carved near the nearest active seam. When the mountain eventually takes the body, the people alter the inscription to mark the direction of its departure.

They do not speak of the dead as gone. They say that a person has **entered the lower roads**.

Some believe the dead become the animals beneath the channels. Some believe they remain in the walls, listening through the mortar. Some believe the mountain remembers every person and will one day return them in another shape. No doctrine has been settled, and the people are wary of doctrines that do not leave room for revision.

They have priests, though the priests would deny the title.

These are the **Open-Handed**, keepers of the wall at the bright cleft. They maintain the layered handprints and oversee the renewal of the marks after each great alteration. Their own hands are scarred from pressing wet pigment, clay, blood, ash, and mineral paste into the stone. They do not ask who made the marks before them. Human, animal, stranger, or mountain, all impressions are given the same care.

At certain times, the Open-Handed lead processions to the cleft. The people go without lamps. They carry bowls of water and leave them beneath the suspended beads in the air. If the bowls fill, the basin is considered willing to receive travelers. If they remain empty, the passage is closed. If the water in them turns warm, the people wait in silence until something moves beyond the light.

No one agrees about what the settlement is waiting for.

Some say it waits for the mountain to finish unfolding. Some say it is a refuge built by people who fled a world beneath the clouds. Some say the basin is an animal itself, and the town is a colony of living things that have mistaken its breathing for weather.

The inhabitants do not settle the matter.

They are accustomed to living beside mysteries that can be touched but not understood. Their wisdom is not knowledge of what will happen. It is knowledge of how to remain present while happening changes shape.

So they go about their quiet lives.

They wash in warm seepwater. They mend harnesses. They trade dried meat, mineral salt, eggs, rendered fat, red leaves, and the translucent skins shed by the roof-creatures. They teach children the safe edges of cracks. They sleep with ropes beside their beds. They wake whenever the reservoir changes its voice.

And when a wall gives a soft internal knock, the nearest person places a palm against it.

Others gather.

For a while no one speaks. The whole household listens together, trying to distinguish whether the sound is water, settling stone, a buried animal, or the first hollow note of a passage opening somewhere beneath the floor.

Then, very carefully, they begin to move the furniture.
