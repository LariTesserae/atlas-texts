What lives on the plateau is not one population but an agreement between several kinds of life.

The people are the most visible, though they have learned not to mistake visibility for importance. They inhabit the reinforced apartments, the worker barracks, the old administrative towers, and the rooms that have appeared inside buildings without being included in any plan. They are few enough that a stranger can walk for an hour without seeing another human being, yet numerous enough that every inhabited district contains someone awake, listening, measuring, or making a note.

They are a practical people. Their clothes are layered against cold, damp, and the occasional change in pressure. Most wear boots with soles scored in different patterns, so that footprints can be identified after a street has altered its slope. Belts carry chalk, wire, a folding rule, a small mirror, and a sealed tin of salt. Some wear cords around their wrists, not for warmth but to count how many times a corridor has been crossed. Others carry bells whose tones are known to remain local.

They do not speak often about the plateau as a living thing. That would be considered childish, or impolite, or both. They say instead that the ground is active. They say a building is settling, though everyone knows that some buildings rise. They say a room has been revised. They say the weather has come up from below.

Their houses are never entirely private. Every dwelling contains a listening place: a vent, a crack, a pipe, an old service hatch, some narrow opening through which the pressure of the deep works can be felt. At night, families sit near these places and wait for the low industrial pulse. A child may put an ear to the wall and hear pistons moving in a factory three districts away, or the slow shifting of stone beneath the foundations, or a voice repeating a name that belongs to no one in the household.

The children are taught the rules before they are taught the history.

They learn which puddles are safe, how to recognize a shadow belonging to an object that is no longer present, and how to leave a room when its proportions begin to change. They are trained to count doors without looking back. They know that a staircase may acquire an extra flight but will not necessarily admit that it has done so. They play games in which one child describes a place and another must determine whether it exists now, existed yesterday, or is waiting to exist.

They are not afraid of the animals. The animals are more trustworthy.

The small eyeless grazers are common around the lower yards. They move in threes, sometimes fours, and press their whiskers to the ground before crossing any open space. Workers leave scraps of compressed grain along the railway embankments for them. In return, the grazers gather around new cracks and make a soft clicking noise when the fissure is likely to widen. Their young are born already furred and able to walk. No one has seen one being born, though the herds sometimes contain an unfamiliar size of animal after heavy rain.

The black-winged birds belong to the upper city. They nest in ducts, substations, and the hollow spaces between floors. Their feathers are matte, drinking the little light that reaches them. They are capable of mimicry, but rarely imitate human speech directly. Instead, their muttering assembles itself out of fragments: a warning bell, the clank of an elevator, the final words of a conversation held many years ago. A whole band may repeat the sound of a door being locked, again and again, until someone checks whether the door is still there.

The birds know the city better than anyone. They vanish before a street contracts. They leave buildings shortly before a new room appears inside them. In the abandoned districts, their nests are built around objects drawn from elsewhere: a spoon, a rail spike, a child’s shoe, a green glass bottle full of dry rain.

The long-backed ravine animals are less familiar. They come up only in wet weather, with their jointed legs moving carefully over the raw stone. Their translucent ears turn toward machinery, voices, and distant weather. They drink from temporary pools and stand very still when the rain ends. People do not hunt them. Their meat, if it is meat, is said to taste of iron and cold soil. Their reflections are never disturbed. A hunter who kills one would have to kill its reflection separately, and no weapon has been found capable of doing that.

The plants are beginning to become inhabitants in their own right.

The silver-gray trees were first treated as invasive growth, then as a useful timber, then as a sign of water. All three interpretations proved incomplete. Their roots enter concrete without breaking it. They soften it from within, making the structure receptive. Around their trunks, forgotten objects become incorporated into the soil: bolts, tools, bones, pieces of pipe, sometimes the foundation stones of older buildings. The trees do not seem to grow toward the light. Their leaves turn toward activity. A grove leans toward a running generator, a conversation, a sleeping person, even a sealed room in which something has begun to move.

The ferns beneath the conveyor belts are more dangerous to disturb. When opened, they release a dry powder that induces a brief conviction that the industrial yard is much larger than it is. Workers caught in it have walked for hours between machinery that was only a few meters apart. They return carrying objects from the wrong side of the plateau: a warm stone, a fragment of unfamiliar brick, a seed that sprouts into grass with metallic blades.

The pale mushrooms bred by the six-legged grazers glow faintly at the edges. They appear overnight in dark clusters and vanish by midday. Children collect them despite being forbidden to do so. The mushrooms are not poisonous, but they remember the shape of the hand that picked them. If placed in a room, they grow in the outline of the absent person.

There are also the things made by the city that have outlived their makers.

The cranes are not conscious in the way people are conscious, but they possess preference. They favor certain lots. They stop when spoken to in anger. They will lift a load that has no visible mass and carry it across an empty yard, then set it down beside a foundation that has not yet been poured. Some workers believe the cranes are assembling the next version of the city. Others think they are clearing away what the city will no longer need.

The freight elevators are more deliberate. They remember passengers. An elevator may open on a floor removed decades ago and wait with its doors apart. It may return carrying dust, snow, or the smell of a room in which the passenger once lived. People do not enter alone unless they are certain which floor they have requested. Even then, certainty is not regarded as protection.

The empty factories continue their movements without human supervision. Their pistons compress, release, and compress again. Their internal belts carry nothing. Their furnaces maintain a temperature no material requires. Sometimes a sealed factory produces a finished object: a ceramic cup, a length of cable, a child-sized coat, a key without a lock. These objects are catalogued and stored. They are never destroyed. The city has learned that destruction is often only another form of redistribution.

The deepest inhabitants are the least easily named.

In the original tunnels, people encounter figures standing at intersections with their backs turned. They may be engineers, though their clothing belongs to no known period. They may be shadows cast by absent lamps. They may be workers from one of the other cities glimpsed on distant shelves. If addressed, they usually point toward a door. If followed, they lead the traveler somewhere useful, though not always somewhere intended.

There are voices beneath the plateau that answer questions a few seconds before they are asked. There are warm places in the rock where people sit during illness and dream of streets that have not yet been built. There are corridors that collect lost names. A person who has forgotten their own name may sometimes recover it there, but may also find several others waiting in its place.

No one agrees whether the plateau itself should be counted among the living.

It has no face, no pulse that can be touched, and no known center. Yet it produces pressure, moisture, stone, rooms, and elevation. It alters its surroundings with the patient assurance of an organism enlarging itself. The people build on it, the roots enter it, the tunnels measure it, and the machines respond to movements too slow for human senses.

Perhaps the plateau is not growing toward a purpose. Perhaps purpose is something that gathers around growth, as moss gathers beneath a bridge.

The city’s inhabitants have accepted this uncertainty. They maintain the reservoirs. They mark the doors. They tend the trees and avoid the wrong pools. They repair the rails after the empty trains have passed. When a new crack appears, they wait through three rains. When a factory begins moving in a new rhythm, they listen for a week before deciding whether to intervene.

And when the ground rises beneath them, they lift the houses, level the streets, adjust the pipes, and continue living there.

They are not waiting for the city to become finished.

They are the part of it that has learned how to notice.
