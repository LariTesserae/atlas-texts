What lives there is not a creature in the ordinary sense, though sometimes it gathers itself into one.

Most of the time it is spread thinly through the hollow: in the dark seams beneath the limestone, in the warm films floating on the pools, in the dull colonies that stain the stone. It has no single body and no need of one. Its awareness, if awareness is the word, moves by seepage. A change in one basin is felt in the next. A stone shifted on a ledge becomes a disturbance carried through the water, down into the gravel, and outward through the hidden channels beneath the floor.

It knows the hollow by pressure and temperature. It knows where the water enters, where it pauses, and where it leaves. It knows the weight of every settled stone.

When it must act, it forms.

The first indication is usually a disturbance too deliberate to be caused by seepage. Silt gathers in the bottom of a pool and draws itself into a low, many-jointed shape. Pale limbs unfold from it, jointed like the legs of some deep-water insect, though softer and almost translucent. The body remains difficult to distinguish from the water. There may be a rounded head, or only a cluster of dark pores where a head ought to be. It has no eyes. It does not need them. Its skin is covered in tiny openings that taste the minerals and vibrations around it.

It moves slowly, but not uncertainly. Its feet do not splash. Each step presses into the clay and remains for a little while as a shallow, water-filled print. It may cross the floor, climb the wall, or fold itself beneath a limestone shelf where it disappears entirely. If it is watched, it gives no sign of noticing. The distinction between being watched and being part of the place may not exist for it.

There are several of these bodies in the deeper pools, though whether they are individuals is unclear. One may dissolve into a basin and emerge from another hours later. Two may meet under a ledge and become a single pale mass, joined by threads of cloudy fluid. When separated, each continues with the same slow purpose. They do not breed, as animals do. They thicken where conditions permit. A new body begins as a discoloration beneath the water, develops a few movable folds, and eventually learns the shape of the hollow by inhabiting it.

Their purpose is equally difficult to separate from their existence. They maintain what is already there.

A crust too thick on the wall is softened by their secretions. A channel beginning to clog is opened with patient pressure. Gravel is moved from one basin to another. Fallen stones are nudged back into old depressions. They do not improve the hollow or alter it toward any design. They prevent it from becoming other than itself.

The settlement is included in this care.

The beings do not understand the old shelter as a house, perhaps, but they recognize its walls as part of the arrangement. When a lower stone loosens, one of them presses its body against it for days, allowing mineral matter to gather in the gap. When the dark leather strips begin to separate, they are drawn beneath the water and returned stiffened with a thin glassy coating. The old tools are not used, yet neither are they allowed to vanish. A scraper that slips into the mud is lifted and placed again in its niche. The broken bowl remains where it settled because, after so long, its shape has become part of the water's path.

Sometimes the beings take the forms of the people who once lived there.

Not convincingly. A shoulder may rise from a pool, followed by a long, bowed torso with no face. Hands form out of pale clay and mineral slime, too many-fingered at first, then reduced to the blunt approximation of human fingers. A head may appear beneath the roofless shelter, featureless except for a wet depression where a mouth would be. The figure stands among the old walls and seems to wait.

It is not waiting for the dead to return. It has no memories in the human sense. It has absorbed traces: the pressure of knees in the packed floor, the angle at which a person once leaned against the wall, the repeated movement of a hand toward the water bowls. These traces remain in the stone as patterns of wear, and the hollow reproduces them when disturbed. The figure is an imitation made by long familiarity, like a footprint that has learned to walk.

If someone enters, the imitation may appear.

It will not approach. It may stand at the far side of a pool, half-submerged, its outline softened by vapor. It will possess the approximate height of a person and the stillness of stone. The visitor may recognize the shelter, the tools, the old practical arrangement, and assume that the figure belonged to the people who made them.

Then it will lower one hand into the water.

The gesture is neither invitation nor warning. It is simply what the hollow remembers people doing.

The creatures are not hostile. They have no hunger that can be satisfied by flesh, no territory to defend, and no fear of injury. If cut or crushed, they disperse into pale fluid and return gradually through the cracks. If touched, they are warm—warmer than the surrounding water—and their surfaces tremble with minute internal currents. The touch may leave a faint mineral film on the skin, smelling of iron and bitter chalk.

They do not like disturbance, though “like” is too strong. A loud voice, a fire, or repeated footsteps sends pressure through the pools. The colonies darken. The water clouds. The creatures retreat into the stone and wait. Nothing dramatic happens. The hollow simply becomes less accommodating. A threshold turns slick. A pool rises around the visitor's ankles. Condensation thickens until every surface seems to sweat. The place does not attack; it closes its processes around the intrusion until the intrusion either leaves or becomes another settled thing.

In time, anything left behind is incorporated.

A dropped knife would blacken at the edge and acquire a crust. A scrap of cloth would sink into the silt and become a dark seam. Bones would be hollowed by the warm water, then used as channels by the hidden life. A body would not be mourned or consumed in any recognizable fashion. It would be disassembled patiently, its salts returned to the pools, its harder parts fitted into the old equilibrium. Years later, an imitation of its posture might rise briefly from the water, not because the person remained, but because the hollow had learned the shape of their weight.

What lives there is therefore a colony, a keeper, and a memory without intention.

It has no name because names require a distinction between the thing named and the place that contains it. The hollow does not make that distinction. Its life is in the water, its body is in the stone, and its occasional limbs are only the visible edge of a much older process. The pale creatures beneath the shelves are not visitors to the place. They are what the place does when it needs hands.

At night—or in the dimness that serves as night beneath the overhang—their bodies sometimes gather in the shallowest basin. Several pale forms lie folded together there, almost indistinguishable from submerged roots, though no root has ever grown in the hollow. Their limbs overlap. Their skins pulse faintly with the movement of the water. From above, they resemble a single person kneeling with its face lowered into the pool.

By morning they have separated again.

One has returned beneath the limestone shelf. One is sealed into the dark gravel. One stands beside the old wall with a hammerstone held loosely in its hand, though it never strikes. The water passes around its feet. Drops fall from the ceiling. The figure remains until the stone has accepted its shape, and then it sinks without a sound.
