What lives in the basin is not a single creature, though from a distance it might seem to have one body.

It is the basin itself, inhabited down to its smallest wet crevice.

The moss, the pools, the insects, the broad-backed animals beneath the leaves—all of them belong to one old arrangement. They do not form a society, because society implies separate lives learning how to coexist. Here, separation has never properly occurred. Each creature begins where another ends. The insects live in the mouths of flowers; the flowers live on the breath of the damp stones; the stones hold the water that gives the roots their shape. A long-bodied animal lying in the green is not merely resting among the plants. It is part of the architecture that lets them remain standing.

The smallest inhabitants are everywhere.

They are pale, jointed, many-legged, soft-shelled, glass-winged. They occupy the undersides of leaves in patient congregations, their bodies shining with the basin’s permanent moisture. Some feed on the films that gather over stone. Some draw nourishment from flowers that never close. Some appear to eat nothing at all, but simply cling to one another and vibrate faintly, contributing their small frequency to the great continuous hum.

They do not seem born so much as produced. Now and then a cluster loosens from a leaf, and a few new bodies emerge already complete, already knowing where to settle. There is no frantic larval stage, no struggle toward maturity. Change occurs without drama. A shell is shed, and beneath it another shell waits, scarcely different from the first.

The plants are more deliberate.

They hold the basin in their leaves. Their roots do not penetrate so much as occupy the spaces made available to them. They grow in layered agreements: one species lifting its leaves just high enough to let another receive the dim light beneath; one sending water down a ribbed stem while another catches it in a cup of moss. Nothing reaches greedily. Nothing casts another into permanent shadow. Their stillness is not passivity but completion.

Some of the oldest plants may be mistaken for trees, though they have no need of trunks. They are columns of living tissue, smooth and swollen, their crowns spreading into dark fans. Rain gathers above them and descends in measured drops. Around their bases, small animals sleep in the warm mud. When one of these plants flowers, the flower opens with the solemnity of a door, and remains open until the flower is no longer distinguishable from the plant that bore it.

The larger animals possess more recognizable forms, but not more individuality.

There are low, heavy grazers with mouths broad enough to gather whole mats of growth. Their hides carry moss, pale fungi, and tiny clinging creatures that comb them clean. They move slowly through the corridors, stopping not to feed but to allow the plants to fold around their bodies. When they pass, the vegetation closes behind them without a scar.

There are amphibious things that rest in the black pools with only their eyes exposed. Their eyes are dull and amber-colored, and their broad mouths open at intervals to receive whatever the water brings. They do not lunge. The insects seem to enter them willingly, or perhaps do not recognize the mouth as an ending. When these creatures breathe, the pool trembles in concentric rings that fade before reaching the shore.

There are tall, reed-thin animals that walk among the shallows on careful legs. Their bodies are almost invisible except where droplets cling to them. They carry smaller creatures on their backs: bright mammals, blind nestlings, long-fingered things that groom the joints behind their knees. The passengers do not appear to be parasites. They are simply part of the tall animals’ shape, as natural as leaves are part of a stalk.

And then there are the concealed ones.

They lie beneath the mats of green, beneath the darkened fallen matter, beneath the warm, weeping shoulders of rock. Their presence is known by the movement of leaves and by the sudden opening of eyes. Some have many eyes. Some have none. A few are so thoroughly covered in mud and growth that they seem to be small hills that have decided to move.

These hidden inhabitants do not ambush. They rise only when something has come close enough to be included.

There is no fear among the animals because there is no sharp boundary between feeding and being fed upon. A body may be shelter, nourishment, transport, or soil, and these conditions do not contradict one another. The creatures have no concept of survival as a contest. To be consumed is not to be defeated. To consume is not to triumph. Each is merely a change in location.

Even the predators seem gentle, though gentleness is not the right word. They possess no mercy; mercy would require them to imagine an alternative. They open their mouths, and other lives pass into them. Their digestion is slow and complete. Nothing is wasted because nothing is ever truly lost. What enters one body eventually becomes moisture in a leaf, warmth in a stone, or the faint mineral taste of a pool.

The basin has perhaps made a few beings that are closer to awareness.

At intervals, something large moves through the haze with a measured, almost ceremonial gait. It may be an animal, or it may be a portion of the basin that has temporarily acquired legs. Its hide is dark and slick. Its head hangs low. Along its spine grow small crowns of vegetation, each holding a pool of water in which insects live. When it passes, the lesser creatures turn toward it—not in alarm, but in recognition.

These great beings may be the basin’s memory.

They carry what the place does not otherwise retain: the impression of a disturbance, the route of a vanished animal, the shape of rain that fell long ago. Yet even they do not preserve events as history. They preserve them as posture. A bend of the neck. A pause beside a particular stone. A recurring path through the green.

If they possess names, the names are not spoken. They are made of movements repeated until the landscape accepts them.

Everything here is alive, but nothing is in a hurry to become something else. The inhabitants do not build nests, mark territory, gather tools, or leave monuments. They do not need to prepare for winter, drought, age, or ruin. Their lives are not journeys toward endings. They are continuous states, held in place by water, warmth, and the mutual patience of all living things.

To enter the basin is to be noticed without being singled out.

The insects settle on you. Vines make room around your ankles. A low animal raises its wet head and studies you with an eye that has seen no reason to fear a stranger. Somewhere nearby, something opens its mouth. Somewhere above, wings pass in a single coordinated sheet.

Then the plants close gently behind you.

For a while, the basin carries your shape.

And if you remain long enough, the distinction between visitor and inhabitant begins to soften.
