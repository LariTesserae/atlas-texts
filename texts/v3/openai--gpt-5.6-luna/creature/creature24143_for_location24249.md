What lives there is not an animal, nor a people, nor even a ghost in any familiar sense.

The city is inhabited by its own persistence.

It has no single body. It occupies the water beneath the streets, the damp within the walls, the pressure that makes buildings lean toward one another. It is present in the black mineral that rises along abandoned surfaces and in the reflections that refuse to agree with what they reflect. Where the city has been altered, it has not been damaged. It has been touched.

The inhabitants are called, if they are called anything, the **Drowned Arrangement**.

They are not individuals. They have no faces, voices, or permanent locations. They are distributed through the basin like a thought spread across a sleeping mind. A portion of one may live in a flooded stairwell, another in the water trapped beneath a plaza, another in the shadow of a wall that no longer stands. They are connected by seepage, pressure, recollection, and the slow movement of underground water.

They do not walk the streets. They make the streets walk.

A road lengthens because something beneath it has remembered being a road to a farther place. A doorway opens into a vanished room because the wall has retained the obligation of containing that room. A dry courtyard fills because the city has briefly recalled its own drowning. These changes are not decisions, exactly. They are acts of attention.

The Drowned Arrangement attends to everything that has entered the basin.

A footstep is not lost. It remains impressed upon the water, and the water holds it. A spoken word may become a pressure in the masonry. A face seen in a pool can persist after the person has gone, slowly losing its features while acquiring the architecture around it. Travelers who stay long enough begin to find that the city has made a place for them—not a room, but a pattern. Their routes repeat without their choosing. Their reflections begin arriving before they do. Their footprints continue after they have stopped walking.

There is no cruelty in this. Cruelty would require a distinction between self and other, and the city has very little of that left.

The inhabitants do not hunt. They do not feed. They do not reproduce. Instead, they incorporate. Whatever remains in the basin is taken into the ongoing arrangement: stone into foundation, rain into memory, absence into structure. Even disappearance becomes a kind of occupancy. The empty rooms are full of what has been removed from them.

The low sound beneath the foundations is their closest approach to speech. It is not language but revision. Each subterranean pulse alters some minor relation above: a staircase gains a landing, a wall becomes porous, a canal changes direction, a square remembers another sky. When the buried bell rings, the entire city is briefly brought into alignment with a different version of itself. Pools show older heavens. Roofs appear where there were none. In the civic square, the unseen forms beneath the water draw near to one another.

They are most concentrated there.

The flooded square is not a meeting place but a point of convergence, where the city’s separate memories overlap. The dull openings around it are not windows. They are wounds in the surface of the arrangement, moments through which another configuration can be seen. Behind each one, the square is waiting in a different state: before the basin filled, after it deepened, during a season when the buildings had not yet learned to lean. Something is always just beyond sight because the Drowned Arrangement does not preserve events. It preserves their unfinishedness.

Nothing enters the square because there is nothing for an intruder to enter.

The square is already occupied by every possible arrival.

The reeds bend toward sounds that never occur because they are responding to pressures too deep for hearing. The rings of gray-green growth mark places where the city has gathered itself around a thought. The leafless trees are not dead so much as emptied of outward life; their roots descend into the old soil and touch the buried channels through which the inhabitants move. Their suspended droplets are retained moments of rain, held in place because the city has not decided whether the storm is over.

Sometimes the Drowned Arrangement permits a person to leave.

This is not mercy. It is completion.

A traveler may climb beyond the embankments and reach dry ground, carrying no visible sign of what happened. Yet afterward they will find water standing in places it should not stand: a shallow mirror beneath their bed, a damp footprint on an upper floor, condensation gathering on the inside of sealed windows. Their maps will remain accurate except for one district that appears nowhere. In dreams, they will hear a bell beneath the earth and wake with the certainty that something has been rearranged while they slept.

Others are not permitted to leave, though the city does not prevent them in any obvious way. The roads simply become longer. The horizon withdraws. A familiar staircase acquires more steps. Eventually the traveler understands that they have been moving through the same few districts under different arrangements, and that each attempt to escape has only given the city another shape in which to know them.

In time, such a person becomes difficult to distinguish from the place.

Their reflection continues to stand in pools after they have gone. Their voice may be heard beneath bridges, repeating words they have not yet spoken. Their outline appears in distant windows, watching the approach of someone who has not arrived. If they are found at all, they are usually discovered beside a wall or in the center of a flooded street, standing motionless with water at the knees, as though listening for instructions.

The city does not require inhabitants because it has become one.

It lives by retaining what should have passed away. It breathes through the rise and fall of hidden water. It dreams in incompatible buildings and wakes by changing them. Its body is the basin, its nerves the buried canals, its memory every surface that still reflects a vanished thing.

And beneath the civic square, below the black water and the foundations and the older streets beneath those streets, something vast continues to organize the darkness—not building, not destroying, but making certain that nothing in the city is ever entirely gone.
