The people of the basin are difficult to distinguish from the systems they maintain.

They live in narrow spaces left between reservoirs, foundations, pressure mains, and migration routes. Their homes are not built so much as sealed into whatever structures already exist. A room may occupy the hollow inside an old buttress, a disused pump chamber, the upper level of a settling tower, or a bridge widened over generations with sheet metal and poured aggregate. Most dwellings have sloped floors and drains. Nothing is left level if it can be made to carry water somewhere.

They are a compact, patient population. Bodies are wrapped in layered work-cloth, rubberized aprons, treated hide, and flexible plates that shed condensation. Their clothing is less for warmth than for managing the endless wet: collars that close against falling water, boots with broad gripping soles, gloves reinforced at the knuckles, hoods fitted with small transparent shields. Everyone carries a lamp, a tool, or a valve key. Many carry all three.

The people do not hurry unless a pressure alarm sounds.

They have learned that haste on wet stone is usually another form of labor. A fall can send someone beneath a moving load, into a drainage throat, or onto a migration route at the wrong moment. They move with the deliberate caution of creatures that know the ground is never fully solid. Even children walk carefully. They are taught to listen through the soles of their feet before crossing a bridge, to test railings for slickness, to recognize the difference between ordinary vibration and the deep irregular shudder that means something has shifted below.

Their faces are often hidden. Masks filter metallic dust, organic vapor, and the fine mineral spray that rises from the works. The masks give them a uniform, unfinished appearance, as if the city has produced a population of pale-eyed workers and has not yet decided what to do with their mouths. In private, they have many expressions. In public, much is conveyed by the angle of a hood, a hand against a pipe, a lamp turned briefly toward another person.

They know one another by sound.

A worker’s footfall on a catwalk, the pattern of a tool striking a housing, the cadence of someone calling through vapor—these are forms of identity. In dense weather, names become unreliable. The city swallows them. A person may be recognized instead by the pause before they speak, the rhythm with which they descend a ladder, or the particular way they close a valve.

Most people belong to a workhouse, a district, and a route. The workhouse may operate pumps, furnaces, filters, rail systems, reservoirs, or the gates that regulate animal passage. The district determines where they sleep and where they receive water, heat, and food. The route is more personal: the sequence of bridges, stairs, service tunnels, and platforms that carries them through the city each day. People are suspicious of anyone who has no established route. Such a person is either lost, recently arrived, or avoiding something.

The oldest families are not defined by blood as much as by inherited maintenance. A family may have tended the same pressure tower for two hundred years, though no member remembers when the tower ceased to be new. They know its moods: the hours when its inner walls sweat, the season when a particular bearing begins to complain, the depth at which the mineral crust changes color. They can tell, by touch, whether water has come from the upper shelves or the deep stone.

These skills are spoken of as practical knowledge, but they resemble kinship.

A pump is not alive, exactly. Neither is a reservoir. Yet people mourn when one is dismantled. They leave small marks on the casings of machines that have served for generations. Before restarting an old engine, they place a palm against its housing and wait for the vibration to settle into a recognizable rhythm. Children are sometimes given names taken from components: Gate, Siphon, Third Bearing, Low Valve. Such names are not considered humorous.

The city’s food comes from many sources. There are dense cakes made from filtered residues, pale fungi cultivated in warm wall cavities, mineral-fed cultures grown in sealed tanks, and oils rendered from small aquatic organisms gathered in the lower channels. Some larger animals are harvested after natural deaths or controlled culls, though their flesh must be processed carefully. Much of what people eat is soft, dark, salted, and warm. Meals are designed to be consumed near pipes.

There is little distinction between kitchen, laboratory, and water station. The same building may house vats of fermentation, a family sleeping chamber, and a bank of gauges. Children grow up surrounded by the sounds of pressure release and the smell of wet metal. They play in abandoned conduits, build miniature gates from scrap, and learn the migration schedules before they learn the names of distant plateaus.

The animals are not pets, though some are companions.

Young workers sometimes raise small plated scavengers in wall boxes, feeding them scraps and mineral flakes. The creatures cling to the warm surfaces and tremble when strangers approach. Larger animals may be known individually if they frequent a particular district. A broad-backed feeder that rests beneath a tram bridge for thirty years acquires a name. People repaint the warning signs around its resting place. They bring offerings of residue to keep it from entering the filter channels. When it finally moves on, leaving the bridge open, the absence is felt like the departure of a neighbor.

The city’s children are taught not to fear the animals but to respect their dimensions.

They know that a long segmented thing climbing a wall is harmless if its underside is pale, and dangerous if its plates have begun to shine. They know which species can be startled by light, which will follow warmth, which can be diverted by a stream of mineral slurry. They know never to stand between a migration and the smell of water. Their games imitate the work of adults: one child becomes the gate, another the pressure, several others the advancing mass. The rules are elaborate and change according to local custom.

During a migration, the city’s people become still.

Gates descend. Conveyors halt. Walkways fill with observers. Those who work above the route lean over the rails, watching the animals pass beneath them in thousands. No one speaks loudly. The migration is not a spectacle, precisely. It is an obligation to witness. The city survives by accommodating these bodies, and the people understand that survival is not the same as ownership.

Some inhabitants follow the animals beyond the basin.

They are surveyors, route-keepers, collectors, and those who have grown tired of enclosed streets. They travel across the terraces in sealed clothing, carrying water, lamps, and instruments to measure the slow changes in the land. They return with samples of pale crust, records of altered channels, and stories of herds that have shifted their routes by the width of a city block over a generation. A few do not return. Their names are entered into maintenance registers, then gradually attached to the places where they disappeared.

The people have no strong belief in permanence, though they live among permanent things.

They know that a wall can stand for five hundred years and fail in an afternoon. They know that a migration path can remain unchanged longer than a language, then bend around a new leak. They know the ground beneath a factory may be hollow, warm, and full of movement. Their records therefore concern flows more than events: how much water passed through a district, how long a pressure remained unstable, how far a species traveled, when a foundation began to sweat.

Their calendars are based on recurrence.

There is the first return of a certain herd, the yearly clearing of a vapor basin, the interval between deep rains, the long maintenance of a furnace. Birthdays matter, but not as much as the date a family inherited a station or the day a reservoir was first filled. People celebrate completed cycles by opening sluices, lighting bridge lamps, and sharing food beside the warm tanks.

Death is handled with the same practicality and reverence as everything else. Bodies are dried, sealed, and placed in stone chambers above the flood lines, or reduced in furnaces whose heat is carefully recovered. Some families keep mineral casts of the hands of their dead. Others dissolve ashes into the upper reservoirs, allowing them to enter the circulation of the city. No one is said to leave entirely. A person becomes sediment, heat, trace metal, memory, or a story told beside a failing pump.

There are outsiders, though the city makes them look temporary.

Trade crews arrive along the raised rails from the outer shelves, bringing condensed minerals, machine parts, preserved food, and news from distant settlements. They are recognized by the cleanliness of their equipment and by the way they distrust the vapor. They sleep in designated houses near the rail embankments, where the floors are kept artificially dry. After a few weeks, most have learned to walk the city’s routes. After a few years, some stop speaking of leaving.

No one entirely belongs to the basin by choice. Belonging comes gradually, through accumulated knowledge. A person belongs when they can tell which dripping pipe is safe to pass beneath. When they know where to stand during a migration. When they can identify a coming storm by the taste of the condensation. When the vibration beneath their bed changes and they wake before the alarm.

At night, when the lamps turn the vapor amber and the machinery settles into its deeper rhythms, people gather on platforms above the streets. They watch the animals move through the illuminated channels. The smallest forms tremble around the lights. Broad bodies pass under bridges, carrying water and mud on their plated backs. The factories breathe behind them.

From above, the inhabitants seem scattered and insignificant: figures crossing gantries, tending valves, carrying lamps through wet corridors. But they are everywhere the city has a pulse. They occupy its warmth, interpret its pressure, clear its channels, and make room for the ancient lives that preceded them.

They do not rule the plateau.

They persist within it—another adapted population, another layer of traffic and residue, living between stone and water while the immense world continues slowly beneath their hands.
