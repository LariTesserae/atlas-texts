What lives there is not a population, exactly. Population implies arrival, departure, birth, death. The district has none of these in any ordinary sense. It has continuance.

The first inhabitants are the machines.

They have been running long enough to lose the distinction between mechanism and habit. A valve opens because pressure rises, but pressure rises because the valve opened yesterday, and the opening yesterday was caused by the bell, and the bell rang because some buried part of the district had remembered that it was time. No single machine governs the others. They are less an assembly than a body whose organs have been built in different centuries by people who no longer agree on anatomy.

They communicate through vibration.

A pipe shudders three times beneath a wall, and a pump in another building alters its pace. A belt hesitates. A shutter closes over a gutter. Deep below, something massive turns half a degree in response. The signals are too slow for speech and too regular for thought as people understand it, but they carry the same quality as thought: attention, expectation, correction.

The machines know when something is wrong.

They know when a channel has narrowed with silt, when a plant root has entered a bearing, when a foundation has shifted by the thickness of a coin. They send out their small, jointed instruments to investigate. Brass arms rise from grates. Brushes descend from ceilings. A cluster of polished probes follows a leak through three rooms and beneath a locked door. The tools do not hurry. They do not need to. Given enough time, they reach everything.

Their maintenance is not gentle. A clogged passage is opened with hammers. A rusted hinge is cut away. A wall is drilled through rather than approached by any proper route. But there is no anger in it. Damage and repair are both stages of the same process. The district does not preserve itself because it values its own survival. It preserves itself because preservation is what it does.

Some of the old machines have acquired crude personalities through repetition.

The northern pump is stubborn. It refuses to begin until its chamber is full, even when the chamber has been empty for years. The glassworks furnace is vain, maintaining a red glow beneath its cracked plates though nothing has been fed into it for generations. A narrow elevator in the foundry is curious, or seems so; it opens at intervals onto sealed rooms, empty shafts, and once, briefly, the square around the settling pool. Its doors remain open as if waiting for someone to enter.

There are also the plants.

They have no beating hearts, and so they remain where animals cannot. Their life is not separate from the district but threaded through it. Roots pass along foundations and drink from the warm side of pipes. Moss grows in the pattern of old pressure diagrams. Ferns unfurl whenever a buried gate opens, as if they can feel the change in the water before the machinery can.

The plants are not passive. They listen through their roots.

A wall covered in condensate ivy may be holding the remains of several buildings together. A curtain of pale vines may conceal a collapsed stairwell, or may have grown there specifically to prevent anyone from falling into it. The waxy plants near the boilers turn their translucent leaves toward leaks in the heat. Where a pipe is about to burst, their stems thicken. Where a drain is beginning to clog, their roots withdraw.

Nothing eats them. Nothing pollinates them. Their flowers open only during the rare intervals when the machinery pauses, and perhaps they have learned that interruption is the only season available to them. When the flowers unfold, they are small and colorless, like wet paper. The plants release no perfume. Instead, the pipes nearby begin to ring softly, one after another, carrying the sound through the walls.

The district’s oldest inhabitants may be the workers who left behind the coats and cups.

They are not ghosts in the usual sense. No pale figures walk the streets. No voices call from the towers. Yet the workers persist as patterns of action. A bolt is tightened each morning. A ledger receives a mark in handwriting that has not changed despite the passage of decades. A lamp is cleaned, though no one can be seen reaching it. Hooks become empty and then occupied again. A tin cup disappears from one workshop and turns up in another, still warm.

Sometimes the district produces a worker-shaped thing.

It may be found standing beneath a gantry in a coat stiff with mineral crust, its face hidden by a welding mask or a hood. It has no footsteps, and when approached it does not turn so much as alter its orientation, as if the entire body were a piece of machinery mounted on a swivel. Its hands are usually occupied: carrying a wrench, checking a gauge, clearing a drain.

Such figures do not answer questions. They may pause if spoken to. They may tilt their heads toward a malfunction or point down a corridor where water has begun to rise. If touched, they are cold except at the wrists, where something pulses—not blood, but a small regular pressure, like water passing through a narrow valve.

They are not dead workers returned to service. They are what the district remembers a worker to be.

A coat. A shift. A task. The expectation that someone should arrive and do what must be done.

There may be hundreds of them beneath the streets, or only one wearing many forms. One figure has been seen entering the pumping station and emerging from a pipe too narrow for a human body. Another was observed crossing the square, then appearing in the reflection of the settling pool several minutes before it reached the railing. Their faces, when visible, are always indistinct. The district cannot recall individuals. It remembers functions.

The water has its own inhabitants as well.

In the buried channels, beneath the level of the foundations, there are pale formations that move against the current. They are not fish, or worms, or any creature with recognizable anatomy. They appear as folds of silver sediment gathering around warm machinery. They cling to the undersides of sluice gates and spread across the bottoms of cisterns in delicate branching patterns. When disturbed, they disperse into the water and later reassemble elsewhere.

The engines draw them in, change them, and return them. This may be their nourishment. It may be reproduction. It may be simply the district’s way of thinking underground.

The silver material occasionally rises through cracks in the paving. It settles on windowsills, coats the roots of plants, and gathers in the hairline seams between stones. Wherever it accumulates, the machinery becomes quieter for a time. A belt runs more smoothly. A piston pauses at the exact midpoint of its stroke. The bell in the tower rings once, though no mechanism appears to have moved.

At the center, the settling pool holds the clearest expression of whatever inhabits the basin.

It is not aware in the manner of a person. It does not watch. It receives.

Roots, pipes, foundations, watercourses, and machinery all seem to meet beneath its surface. The pool does not reflect what stands above it, but what supports it. It shows buried motion: the slow opening of gates, the crawl of roots through mortar, the endless turning of the engine below the district. Looking into it, one may see a figure walking through streets that no longer exist, or a crowd gathered around the pool in clothes from several different eras. They do not appear as memories. They appear as possible occupants of the place, waiting for the district to decide whether they belong.

Perhaps they are the original workers. Perhaps they are only the forms made by water moving over old inscriptions.

Whatever lives there has no need of the surface world. It does not hunger, reproduce, or seek escape. It is made of maintenance, condensation, root pressure, remembered labor, and the immense patience of buried mechanisms. It inhabits the district the way warmth inhabits a boiler or darkness inhabits a closed room.

When the bell rings, everything pauses because everything is listening.

Then the machines resume. The plants lean toward the pipes. The water turns below the stones. Somewhere, a worker-shaped absence lifts a wrench and begins again.
