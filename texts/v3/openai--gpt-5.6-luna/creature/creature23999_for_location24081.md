They live by levels.

Not by neighborhoods, exactly, and not by the old divisions of street and building. They know the city vertically. They say they are from the Ninth Water, the Upper Foundry, the East Intake, the school decks above Conveyor Three. They give directions in ascents and descents: two ladders past the pressure door, across the warm bridge, down through the market, then follow the blue pipe until it becomes green.

Most people rarely touch the original face of the mountain. They are born above it, work above it, are buried—if burial is the word—in chambers cut into it. Their homes occupy whatever volume remains between the city’s necessary systems. A family may live in a narrow stack of rooms inserted between a ventilation shaft and an old retaining wall. Their windows look across a gap at another wall, close enough to touch if both windows are opened. Rain moves past them in pipes. Trains slide behind their kitchens. When the building flexes in winter, everyone hears it as a muted settling sound and knows that somewhere below, another structure is taking their weight.

They do not find the closeness remarkable. A child learns early that every surface has a function. The floor may be a roof for someone below. The ceiling may carry water. A wall may contain a railway, a garden, a stair, a heat exchanger, or an abandoned room that will one day be opened again. Children are taught not to drill into concrete without checking the registry first. They learn the warning colors of pipes before they learn the colors of flowers.

They know the weather as a sequence of pressures.

Rain is not merely rain. It is an increase in flow, a change in the sound of the walls, a tightening of work schedules. It sends crews into the drainage galleries and closes certain bridges. The people watch the gauges mounted outside apartment blocks. They know which reservoirs are rising, which gates have opened in the eastern towers, and whether the pale line on the overflow display means ordinary release or emergency descent.

A storm on the far side of the mountain may alter their day before its clouds arrive. Production is reduced in one district. Pumps begin their deeper cycle. A set of stairs is closed because a retaining chamber has taken on water. Workers with lamps move into the lower tunnels, carrying sealant, tools, and packets of hot food. Above them, office clerks change the city’s flow diagrams. Below them, old concrete darkens with the pressure of the mountain.

Everyone is, to some extent, a water worker.

Even those who have never touched a valve understand water in the body. They know the taste of different districts. They can tell when a pipe has been flushed. They keep towels near doors, cloth over sleeping vents, and small dishes beneath the persistent leaks that maintenance has judged harmless. They grow accustomed to the sound of water passing behind walls at night: a full-bodied rush, then a pause, then the slow knock of air clearing from a line.

The maintenance crews are the city’s most intimate inhabitants. They enter the places other people only infer: the crawlspaces behind housing blocks, the undersides of bridges, the heat chambers below factories, the narrow galleries between old and new foundations. Their clothing is waterproof, insulated, and marked with district colors that fade quickly in the damp. They carry hand lamps, breathing filters, chalk, patching compounds, and paper maps sealed in plastic.

The maps are never complete.

There is always another pipe, another stair, another sealed door behind a wall that has been opened for some unrelated repair. A crew may discover a line of old inspection lights still connected to a power source, or a passage where the wall has been painted with the name of a department that no longer exists. They record everything. The city’s memory is maintained by annotations: arrows on concrete, dates beside valves, red numbers above doors, small metal plates stamped with the names of people who made repairs decades ago.

Some workers develop a private relationship with the buried districts. They leave offerings there—not religious ones, usually. A thermos of tea in a dry alcove. A bright strip of cloth tied to a ladder. A replacement bulb installed in a dead corridor. They say the old tunnels are not haunted, but they are not empty either. They contain the shape of previous decisions. A sealed rail line still seems to expect a train. A drained machine hall keeps the posture of its vanished machinery. In certain chambers, when the pumps are running, the whole floor trembles with the memory of work.

The people of the basin are practical about ghosts.

What they fear more are unregistered changes.

A new vibration under a walkway. A patch of moss growing where no water line is shown. A door warm to the touch. A pipe that should be empty but carries pressure. A sound from below that repeats at regular intervals. These things are reported. Not because the city is superstitious, but because the city has taught them that every mystery is likely to be a system that has escaped its diagram.

They are skilled at inhabiting temporary places. A roof garden may become a repair deck overnight. A market may be dismantled for a crane. An entire block may be wrapped in scaffolding while people continue living inside it. Families move their kitchens around construction zones. Schools divide their classes between borrowed rooms. Shops hang signs on temporary walls and learn to recognize customers by the sound of their footsteps through the rain.

Nothing is permanent, but nothing is allowed to disappear.

The old are the keepers of the vanished levels. They remember when a freight line ran where the clinic now hangs, when the lower basin was open to the sky, when the eastern reservoir had not yet been covered by its present roofs. They remember districts by their sounds: the iron clatter of the former stamping halls, the high whistle of the first turbines, the way rain struck an uncovered platform before the city learned to enclose everything.

They tell these stories without sentimentality. The past is not a lost natural world. It is another arrangement of machinery, another layer beneath the feet. When they speak of what used to be, they are often giving practical information: the old wall leaks in winter; the third tunnel floods first; there is a staircase behind the storage tanks; the mountain is hollow here.

Children grow up with a talent for disappearance. They know how to pass through the city without being seen by anyone they know. They ride the silent trains in groups, emerging onto platforms glazed with condensation. They cross service bridges after school and watch the cranes move through the fog. They collect small things: smooth mineral deposits, broken ceramic insulators, bolts stamped with obsolete factory marks, pale leaves from plants that should not grow in filtered air.

They are not wild children. The city leaves little room for wildness. But they are experts in gaps.

They find the warm spaces above vents where insects gather. They know which security doors fail to latch and which stairwells echo all the way down. They make games of following pipes until the pipe changes size, color, or temperature. They invent names for the distant towers and the hidden falls. They dare one another to place a hand against a wall and guess what is moving on the other side.

Their parents teach them to respect barriers. The city teaches them to be curious about them.

There are people who work where the green growth is managed: horticultural crews, wall gardeners, moss inspectors, root-control teams. They tend to the assigned wilderness. They know which ferns protect a drainage seam and which will split it open. They trim vines away from sensors but leave enough for the walls to remain shaded. They cultivate moss over concrete because moss slows runoff, traps dust, and keeps certain surfaces from cracking under rapid temperature changes.

Some of these workers speak of plants as if they are junior engineers.

That vine is persistent. This moss is good with minerals. Those roots are searching for a chamber. The fern has found a leak.

They understand that the green life does not belong to them entirely. It has been invited into the city for useful reasons, then permitted to develop preferences. It chooses the warm side of a pipe, the wet edge of a retaining wall, the dim underside of a bridge. It follows the water with an intelligence too slow to be called intention and too successful to be dismissed as accident.

The people watch it closely.

They also watch the fungi. White shelves appear in forgotten service rooms. Black films spread beneath cooling units. Threadlike growths occupy old insulation and feed on compounds no one remembers installing. Samples are taken, labeled, and sent to laboratories. Some are cultivated deliberately. Some are sterilized. Some are left alone until they begin to compromise a beam.

Bacteria live everywhere, in the filters, tanks, pipes, settling beds, and warm dark chambers beneath the streets. Their work is invisible but indispensable. The city depends on organisms that digest, bind, separate, clean, and transform. The people speak of them in technical language, but they understand that the basin is not maintained by human hands alone. Much of the city’s life happens at scales too small for walls, maps, or laws.

The black birds are known individually in a few districts. They nest above warm vents and steal reflective objects from loading platforms. Workers leave scraps for them, though officially this is discouraged. The birds understand the safest routes through the industrial roofs. They move with more freedom than the people do, crossing gaps without permits, riding heat currents through the mist, vanishing into the folds of the upper basin.

The reservoir fish are counted and recounted. They are pale from the depth and the filtered light. Their movements are tracked by sensors, their health read as evidence of the water’s condition. People sometimes stand at the observation windows and watch them turn below the surface. The fish seem to belong to another order of life: silent, weightless, unconcerned with stairs or schedules. Children press their hands against the glass. Adults do too, when no one is watching.

No one sees the rumored rats, but everyone knows their routes. They are said to inhabit the oldest levels, where the air is warmer and the foundations have settled into shapes not recognized by current construction codes. They live among forgotten cables and mineral crusts. Some claim the rats have become almost translucent. Others say they have learned the maintenance alarms and come out only after the crews leave.

The rats may be the only creatures in the city that live without permission.

But the largest inhabitant is the city itself.

It does not think as a person thinks. It has no single center, no face behind the control rooms. Its awareness is distributed through gauges, valves, cameras, schedules, maintenance reports, microbial readings, pressure fluctuations, and the practiced instincts of thousands of workers. It knows where the water is because water changes the walls. It knows where people are because doors open, trains fill, lights change, heat gathers in rooms.

It is always adjusting.

When one district sleeps, another is drawing power. When factories slow, pumps continue. When rain falls, the city receives it through roofs and gutters and channels it down into tanks, turbines, filters, and reservoirs. It absorbs weather and returns altered weather: warm air through vents, mist above the drains, condensation under the bridges.

The city is not alive in any simple sense. It cannot hunger, though it consumes fuel, labor, metal, stone, and time. It cannot dream, though its old structures persist below the current ones like recurring images. It cannot speak, though its warnings chime, its pipes knock, its towers blink, and its walls reveal themselves through damp stains.

Yet the people listen to it.

They listen for changes in rhythm. They learn which noises mean ordinary strain and which mean that something deep has shifted. They sleep through the endless motion of the basin, trusting the pumps, the crews, the gates, the layers of concrete. Trust here is not faith. It is accumulated evidence: a million repairs, a million successful releases, a million mornings when the water went where it was supposed to go.

At night, they pass through the dim corridors beneath the green roofs. Their faces appear briefly in dark windows and disappear again. They carry food, tools, letters, uniforms, medicines, replacement parts. Their footsteps join the hum of machinery and the fall of water.

They are numerous, but the city does not gather them into a crowd. It separates them into flows.

Human beings moving through channels.

Water moving through channels.

Heat, freight, light, waste, spores, rumor, and memory moving through channels.

Everything here is given a route. Everything is directed downward, upward, across, through, around. Even growth has its assigned surfaces. Even abandonment has a registry number and an inspection date.

And still, beneath the assignment, something keeps searching.

Roots search for earth. Water searches for lower ground. Fungi search through walls. Children search for unmarked doors. The buried city presses upward through cracks in the new one. Birds search the warm air. The mountain holds its weather against the terraces and waits.

The people live inside the searching.

They repair what breaks, cultivate what can be cultivated, seal what must be sealed, and open what has become necessary. They make their homes in the narrow intervals between systems. They are at once the operators of the basin and another of its dependent organisms: warm, mobile, inventive, and replaceable.

When they look out across the wet terraces, they do not see a city built over nature.

They see a city that has made a nature of its own—one of pressure and circulation, moss and concrete, buried heat, controlled water, and bodies moving continuously through the dark.
