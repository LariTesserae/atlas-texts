The people of the plain are not the grazers beneath the leaves, though at a distance they might be mistaken for them.

They move on six narrow limbs, with their bodies held low when they travel and raised high when they work. Their outer surfaces are layered in overlapping mineral plates: ochre along the back, pale at the joints, green-black where living tissue shows through. The plates are not armor. They are skin, grown in successive seasons and shed only when the body has made a newer shape beneath them.

They have no soft, exposed faces. What would be a face is a small arrangement of sensory fans, dark apertures, and delicate branching feelers. These open and close according to heat, air pressure, chemical traces, and the minute vibrations of the ground. Their eyes, if they can be called eyes, are shallow lenses set along the crown. They do not see much farther than the plain’s ordinary light allows, but they read surfaces with extraordinary precision. A change in the texture of a wall. A faint swelling beneath a road. The disturbed growth where something has passed in the night.

They are warm-bodied, though not in the way animals are warm. Each carries a slow internal circulation of heated chemical vapor, drawn from the mineral beneath the settlement and passed through chambers in the torso. When they rest, they press themselves against sun-warmed walls or lie half-buried beneath broad leaves. When the weather turns cold—which means only that the air loses some of its stored heat—they gather in groups, touching plates, sharing warmth through the seams of their bodies.

They do not drink. They have no thirst.

They draw nourishment through the undersides of their limbs and through long, fine contact-organs that extend from the abdomen when they are still. These organs enter the powdery crystal of the plain and take from it what the body requires. Some inhabitants remain in one place for hours, anchored like pale, many-legged plants, while others carry the loosened mineral back to communal chambers. They feed also on the air: on spores, resins, charged dust, and the invisible exhalations of the vegetation. Their breathing is quiet and irregular. A resting individual may seem entirely inert except for the slow unfurling of a membrane at the throat.

The young are not born helpless.

A new inhabitant begins as a dense knot of living mineral cultivated in a communal chamber. Several adults contribute tissue, warmth, and chemical patterning. The knot is placed in a shallow cradle of root-mat, where it grows for years among the walls. During this time it has no single parent. It receives impressions from the chamber itself: the vibration of feet in the corridor, the daily opening of the fronds, the low settling calls of the walls. By the time it unfolds its first limbs, it has already been shaped by an entire neighborhood.

The first thing it learns is not a name.

It learns a route.

An elder leads it through the settlement along a path worn smooth by generations. The route may pass beneath arched leaves, across a plaza, through a chamber where the walls are warm, and finally out to the raw edge of the city where the mineral is still rising. There the young one places its contact-organs against the ground and feels the slow pressure moving through the continent.

This is an introduction to belonging. One does not belong because one was born in a chamber. One belongs because one can recognize the land’s changes and answer them.

Their speech is made partly of sound and partly of touch. They produce low pulses in the chest, too soft to carry far through open air, but clear inside walls and along compacted roads. A conversation may travel through a settlement as a sequence of faint tremors: a pulse in one chamber, an answering vibration in the floor, a pressure passed into a buttress and carried upward through the living architecture.

They also communicate by scent and secretion. A hand—or one of the smaller forward limbs—pressed briefly to a wall can leave behind a chemical statement. It may mean danger, ripeness, grief, permission, or simply: someone has passed this way. The oldest corridors are thick with such traces. To outsiders they would smell only of chalk and green resin. To the inhabitants, the settlement is legible down to its smallest surface, crowded with old messages and unfinished ones.

Individual voices are quiet. Groups are not.

When many gather, their bodies resonate against the floor and their plates knock together in slow, irregular patterns. The result is not song exactly, but a layered vibration that moves through the chambers. They use it during collective work, especially when directing the growth of a wall or opening a new route through the vegetation. Hundreds may kneel around a mineral terrace, touching it with their limbs, feeding it warmth and chemical instruction. Under their attention, the terrace changes its shape. A ridge becomes a ramp. A ramp thickens into a bridge. A hollow becomes a chamber with a vaulted roof.

They do not regard this as construction.

They call it persuading the ground.

There are specialists in that persuasion. Some have broad, many-branched limbs for distributing pressure through new growth. Some produce dense mineral secretions that harden into load-bearing ribs. Some cultivate the vast fronds that shade districts, teaching them when to furl and when to open. Others tend the root-mats beneath the streets, pruning them away from public routes and encouraging them beneath walls.

The work is inherited, but not rigidly. An individual may spend a century guiding a tower upward, then leave it to become a keeper of grazing paths. Another may remain nearly motionless in a communal chamber for decades, listening to changes deep in the continent and warning the settlement of a swelling ridge long before it reaches the surface.

Their sense of time is correspondingly broad.

They do not hurry, though they are capable of sudden movement. A predator crossing a plaza can make them scatter with startling speed. A collapsing wall can bring a whole district into motion. But ordinary decisions unfold slowly. A new road may be discussed for years before the first vegetation is pressed aside. A tower may be given a new purpose only after its growth has made the old one impossible.

They are accustomed to living with change that cannot be prevented.

A settlement is not an ancestral monument to them. It is an ancestor still undergoing its life. The oldest walls contain the mineral traces of countless inhabitants, not as memorials but as actual participants. Their bodies dissolved into the architecture after death, their structural matter taken up by root-organs and rising through buttresses. A chamber may be held together by the remains of those who once occupied it. A bridge may carry the altered substance of an entire lineage.

This does not make death invisible.

When one of them is dying, it is brought to a quiet place where the vegetation grows thin and the ground can be felt directly. The others gather around without touching at first. The dying individual gives off changes in heat and chemical rhythm, and these are received through the air. Eventually its limbs fold inward. The plates loosen. The body becomes soft along the seams.

Then the others place their contact-organs against it.

For several days, sometimes longer, the settlement absorbs what can be absorbed: memories held in chemical pattern, useful tissue, mineral density, the particular warmth of the dying body. Nothing is hurried away. Nothing is sealed off. When the process is complete, there may be no recognizable corpse, only a darkened patch of ground or a new thickening in the nearby wall.

Their dead remain everywhere, but not as ghosts. As material.

They do have ghosts, perhaps, though they describe them differently. A familiar vibration may recur in an old corridor. A chemical trace may survive beneath layers of dust. A chamber may continue to open its roof at the hour preferred by someone who has been gone for a hundred years. Such persistence is not supernatural to them. It is simply another form of growth, an unfinished influence.

They are not a warlike people, but neither are they gentle in the human sense. Their patience can be ruthless. They will let a district be buried if the pressure of the continent demands it. They will redirect a herd through a nesting grove and accept the deaths that follow if the grove is necessary to stabilize a wall. They understand care as continuity rather than protection.

A child may be watched closely for years, then left alone at the edge of a dangerous growth-front so that it learns the difference between a living ridge and a dead one. A predator may be driven from a residential chamber, but not killed if it can be guided toward the outer groves. A damaged plant is not cut away merely because it has become inconvenient. It may be given another role: shade, barrier, food, or support.

Even their tools, where the word applies, are living companions. A gripping tendril cultivated for carrying. A hardened rib used to lever open mineral sheets. A pouch-grown membrane for collecting spores. They do not possess these things; they inhabit relationships with them. When an implement is no longer needed, it is planted or attached somewhere, where it continues to grow.

At midday, the inhabitants withdraw beneath the broad fronds.

The streets fill with green-brown shade. Small grazers pass between their limbs. Workers lie flat against the walls, letting the mineral plates of their bodies equalize with the cooler surfaces. From the communal chambers comes the low, intermittent conversation of the settlement. Somewhere beneath a plaza, an animal scratches. Somewhere above, a pod opens.

Then the heat begins to lessen.

The fronds unfold. The city brightens by degrees. The inhabitants emerge and resume their routes, each returning to the task that has been waiting—not because it is urgent, but because the world is never finished waiting.

At the outer edge of the settlement, where the buildings thin into groves, they often stand for a long time facing the unfinished plain.

There is no border there, only a diminishing density of walls and a widening field of growth. Beyond the last cultivated shelter, fresh crystal is lifting from the earth. Its translucent edges catch the pale sun. A new fold is forming in the distance, so broad and slow that it seems at first to be a change in light.

The inhabitants feel it through their limbs.

Some turn back toward the city. Some go forward to meet it. A few lower themselves onto the ground and begin, with patient contact and shared warmth, to teach the rising land what shape it might take.
