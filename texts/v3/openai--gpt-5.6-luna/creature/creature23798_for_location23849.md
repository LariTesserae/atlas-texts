What lives there is difficult to separate from the place.

Beneath the basalt, in the shallow fractures where heat rises most evenly, there is a single slow organism—or a colony so old that the difference has ceased to matter. It has no fixed outline. Its body is made of pale mineral threads, dark membranes, and minute chambers pressed into the stone. They extend outward from the deepest warm seams, crossing beneath the floor in branching patterns, then narrowing until they become no more than a stain in the rock.

The fibrous plants are its visible parts.

Each tuft is attached to the buried network by a bundle of nearly hair-fine filaments. What appears to be a root is not merely a root, but a continuation of the organism’s body. It draws vapor from the heated stone and takes dissolved iron, sulfur, and calcium from the mineral dust. In return, it alters the chemistry of the cracks just enough for the plants to persist. The plants give it nothing that could be called food in an ordinary sense. They give it surface, exchange, and time.

It does not grow upward. The air is too dry, the darkness too complete, and there is no advantage in becoming conspicuous. It grows sideways and downward, occupying the unnoticed spaces between old flows. When a new fracture opens, it enters. When a seam cools, it withdraws from it so gradually that withdrawal cannot be observed. Its life is measured in the settling of crusts and the slow release of heat from stone.

There may be a central body under the basin, though no one could point to it. The mineral threads gather most thickly beneath the shallow chamber, where the warmth is strongest. There, under a thin layer of black rock, are broad, flattened chambers lined with a dull, glassy substance. They are not organs in the familiar sense. Some collect heat. Some concentrate dissolved minerals. Some preserve chemical traces brought from distant cracks. Together they form a kind of memory.

The organism knows the basin by pressure, temperature, and composition. It knows when a wall sheds a grain of crust. It knows where the roof is thickest, where the cold cleft interrupts the surrounding stone, where the mineral dust has settled after centuries of imperceptible movement. It has no eyes and no need of them. The chamber is held in it as a pattern of warmth and resistance.

It has no voice, but the occasional deep settling note passes through its body. The sound does not carry information as speech does. It is more like a change in posture: a distant shift, a compression, a warning that some part of the stone has altered. The buried threads answer by closing certain passages and opening others. Above, nothing visibly happens. A brittle stalk may remain upright. A patch of dust may keep the same shape. Only below the floor does the organism rearrange itself around the new condition.

It is not intelligent in the manner of an animal, though it is not mindless either. Its awareness is dispersed, without a center and without urgency. It does not think about the darkness. Darkness is simply the absence of a useful signal. It does not fear the cold cleft, because cold is not danger until it reaches the living seams. It does not desire escape. The basin is not a prison to it. It is a body, or at least the portion of a body that has become stable enough to be mistaken for a landscape.

The old fallen stalks are gradually taken back into it. Their dry fibers lie on the mineral floor until the buried threads find them. Then, over years, their substance is drawn down through the dust. A reddish seam appears beneath them. The crust hardens. Nothing seems to have fed, and nothing seems to have decayed. The basin merely continues.

At long intervals, a new tuft rises from an empty crack. It is not born so much as uncovered: a pale, jointed growth pushing through a seam that the organism has prepared long before. Elsewhere, an older tuft becomes brittle and remains standing after its living connection has failed. The colony does not mourn its dead portions. It retains their shape for a while, then incorporates them into the floor.

If disturbed, it would not attack. It would close itself. The mineral threads would harden at the edges, the plants would dry further, and the shallow chambers beneath the rock would become chemically inert. An intruder might stand among the tufts without knowing that anything had noticed. Only after leaving would the basin slowly reopen its hidden passages.

But nothing enters, and nothing leaves.

So the organism remains spread beneath the chamber, almost motionless, feeding on warmth and the faint contents of stone. Its surface parts stand widely separated in the darkness, each one small, dull, and patient. Between them, the black basalt appears empty. It is not empty. The living body is simply too slow and too deeply integrated to announce itself.

The basin is what it inhabits.

The basin is also what it has become.
