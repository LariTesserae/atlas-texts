What lives in the Long Hollow is called, by those few who have survived meeting it, the **Kept One**.

This is not its name. The basin has no names for things. The word is only an approximation, chosen because the creature seems less to inhabit the Hollow than to preserve it.

At first it appears to be a person standing far off among the slate plates: tall, narrow, wrapped in something pale and stiff. It is always seen at the edge of recognition. The eye finds a shoulder, a head, the suggestion of arms held close to the body. Then the shape becomes a broken wall, or a seam of shadow beneath a ledge, or one of the dark figures on the distant slopes.

It does not walk in any ordinary manner. When observed directly, it remains still. When attention shifts—even for the briefest instant—it is somewhere else. Not necessarily closer. It may be standing beside a pool that was empty a moment before, or inside one of the roofless shelters, or at the far end of the basin with its back turned. Sometimes its arrival is announced by a change in the ground’s vibration. Sometimes the vibration stops altogether.

The Kept One is made of the basin’s materials, though not in any stable proportion. Its body may be covered in gray skin as thin as wet paper, with slate showing beneath it in long plates. In other sightings it is translucent, like a figure frozen inside clear water. Its joints seem to have been assembled incorrectly. The elbows bend too far; the knees do not bend at all. Its head is smooth and featureless, except for a shallow vertical depression where a face might have been.

No one has seen it eat.

It drinks from the small pools, but not by lowering its mouth to the surface. When it bends over the water, the pool rises to meet it. A column of black liquid climbs slowly into the hollow of its face and vanishes. The water level is unchanged afterward. In the same way, it sometimes places one hand against a frost-covered stone and draws out a thin white filament. The rime comes away like hair. Beneath it, the stone is neither warmer nor colder.

The few plants of the Hollow grow more densely near places where the Kept One has stood. Closed flowers appear in its footprints, though the marks themselves are rarely shaped like feet. More often they are round depressions, or long narrow cracks, or impressions of something with too many toes. The black moss follows it through the basin in slow stains. Silver lichen forms rings around its resting places.

It tends the ruins.

This is the creature’s clearest purpose. It goes from structure to structure, touching the walls, standing in doorways, lowering its head over the shallow pools. If a slate block has shifted, it returns the block to its former position. If a crack has opened in a wall, it presses its palm against either side until the two edges face each other again. It does not repair the damage. It restores the arrangement.

The work may take no time, or it may take centuries.

A visitor who watches the Kept One at the central house will find that it never enters by the door. Instead, its outline gradually appears inside the darkness, beginning with the feet and rising upward. Once complete, it stands with one hand against the inner wall. During this contact, all sound around the house becomes intimate and small. The deep vibration fades. The far-off rushing disappears. A person may hear their own heart stumble, or the click of their teeth, or a second breathing in the room that is not their own.

The Kept One listens to the body.

It listens especially to the thoughts that a person does not speak.

Those who encounter it report hearing voices in the surrounding stone. The voices do not say words at first. They repeat scraps of sound: a childhood nickname, the rhythm of a mother’s cough, the phrase used by someone long dead. The sounds seem to come from beneath the ground, but they are often heard before the visitor has remembered them. If the visitor answers, the Kept One turns its head.

This turning is the only movement it makes that resembles attention.

Afterward, the basin begins to rearrange itself around the visitor. A pool appears where the visitor slept. Their tracks lead away in directions they did not take. Their shadow may be found several yards from their body, lying flat and sharp upon the slate. If they speak again, their words return from the mountains before they leave their mouth.

The Kept One is not malicious. Malice would require a preference, and preference is foreign to it. It does not hunt, though it can be found beside the remains of the hollow animals. It does not attack, though a person who reaches for it may discover that their hand has become cold, transparent, and full of moving water.

It does not want visitors to leave.

Nor does it want them to stay.

It wants them to occupy the correct place.

This is why travelers sometimes find themselves standing in a ruin they had never seen, looking through a doorway at their own camp. This is why footprints continue through the house and emerge elsewhere. The Kept One is not moving bodies through space. It is correcting the relation between a body and the basin, placing each thing where it has always been meant to stand.

The creature itself may have been a visitor once.

There are signs of this in the oldest structures. One wall bears a sequence of shallow marks, each approximately the width of a human hand. They begin at ground level and climb upward, then descend again on the opposite side. Another ruin contains a stone shaped like a seated figure, though the shape may be accidental. Around it, the frost is always disturbed in a circle. In the central house, beneath the white rime, the inner wall is warm in the form of a human silhouette.

Perhaps the builders made the Kept One. Perhaps they were the first beings it kept. Perhaps the Hollow is the creature’s body, and the ruins are bones arranged according to a forgotten anatomy.

There are times when the dark figures on the slopes descend.

They come in threes or fours, keeping far apart, their shadows pointing into the stone. They approach the basin’s center only when the sound beneath the ground changes. Then the Kept One rises from wherever it has been hidden and stands among them. For a long while, nothing moves. The figures face inward. The pools become perfectly clear. The thin plants cast no shadows at all.

At the end of this gathering, one of the figures is usually missing.

Afterward, the basin is restored. A new crack appears in the slate. A new hollow fills with water. Somewhere, a ruin has acquired another wall.

On rare occasions, the Kept One comes close enough to be seen without distance disguising it.

It is taller than a person, but not by much. Its body is starved and broad at the chest, as if shaped for breathing an atmosphere too thin for lungs. Frost coats it from head to foot. Under the frost, things move slowly through its frame: dark water, pale roots, mineral threads. There are no eyes. Instead, the front of its head is covered in a thin film that reflects the observer—not as they are, but as they will be after remaining in the Hollow too long.

The reflection is always standing still.

The Kept One raises one hand. Its fingers are long, jointed, and almost delicate. The nails are clear disks of ice. It does not beckon. It does not warn.

It places the hand against the air between them.

The gesture creates a sound like a crack traveling through distant stone.

Then the visitor remembers something that has not happened yet: walking alone beneath the gray sky, finding a pool with a clear disk of ice at its center, looking down, and seeing no reflection—only the Kept One standing far below the water, waiting for the correct arrangement to return.

After that, the creature is gone.

But the basin has acquired one more fixed detail.

A new shadow falls from an empty place.

And somewhere beneath the slate, the Kept One resumes its patient work.
