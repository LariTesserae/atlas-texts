The people of the high country are difficult to distinguish from what shelters them.

They are tall, narrow-bodied beings, though not from delicacy. Their limbs are long and heavily jointed, built for crossing shelves of stone and gripping slopes where ordinary feet would slide. Their hands and feet have broad, flexible pads beneath the toes and fingers. In wet weather these swell slightly, giving them purchase on the living floor; in dry weather they harden and become almost callused stone. They can climb the lower trunks of the great plant-colonies without tools, moving slowly and steadily, their bodies close to the layered stalks.

Their skin is dark in the exposed places, ranging from slate to blue-black, but along the shoulders, spine, and outer limbs it grows in overlapping plates of keratin. These plates resemble the scales of the mat vegetation. Dust settles into them. Fine lichens sometimes take hold in the shallow seams. A person standing motionless beside an escarpment may disappear completely—not through magic, but because the country has taught their bodies the same colors and textures it has taught the animals.

They have large, pale eyes protected by a second transparent lid against the wind. Their hearing is more important than their sight. They can tell, from the character of a vibration through the ground, whether a herd is approaching, whether a distant tower has split under its own weight, or whether a predator is moving beneath the root-mat. Their speech is low and resonant. Ordinary conversation carries poorly across the open shelves, so they use tones that are felt as much as heard: a pulse in the chest, a note held against the teeth, a descending call that can travel from one tower to another through the wind.

They do not have villages in the usual sense. They have intervals.

A family may live for part of the year beneath one arch, then abandon it when the condensation patterns change or a herd begins using the nearby shelf. Another group will occupy the shelter later. The old inhabitants understood that a place could not be owned without becoming useless. To claim a water basin permanently was to foul it; to cut too deeply into a tower was to weaken the living architecture that made the basin possible. Their homes are therefore temporary in appearance but ancient in repetition. A fire-blackened hollow may have been occupied every season for a thousand years, each generation repairing the same low wall, clearing the same drainage groove, and leaving the rest to grow over.

They sleep in shallow recesses above the ground, where the cold cannot gather so heavily. Their sleeping platforms are woven from the tough upper fronds of the plant-mat and suspended between living roots. Children are wrapped in broad leaves coated with a wax that holds the night’s condensation. At dawn, adults collect the water from these coverings and pour it into stone cups. No one drinks first. The first water goes to the roots.

Food is taken rather than harvested. The people know which parts of the vegetation can be stripped without killing the colony, which seed pods will burst into insects, and which gray-blue cushions hold enough stored moisture to sustain a traveler. They carry shallow scraping tools made from horn and stone, but most gathering is done with the hands. They eat the tender interiors of tower shoots, the fatty bodies of burrowing animals, the eggs of cliff-nesting creatures, and the small bright insects that emerge after rain. During the great herd crossings they take only the injured, the very young animals abandoned by necessity, and those that die naturally. To kill a healthy grazer is considered a form of waste so profound that the word for it is also used for lying.

They are not peaceful because they are gentle. They are peaceful because the plateau makes waste visible.

A spilled water vessel can mean a failed journey. A broken root bridge can close a route for generations. A frightened herd can strip an entire shelf and leave the nesting grounds exposed to wind. Every action enters a chain too large for any one person to see. The people therefore speak constantly of consequences that will not occur within a lifetime. Their elders are not valued for authority but for the number of storms, migrations, collapses, and flowerings they remember.

The most respected among them are the interval-keepers: people who memorize the timing of the country. They know when the black mats will open their yellow flowers, when the high birds begin circling a dead animal, when the first clouds below the rim will rise high enough to signal rain. They carry no written calendars. Instead, they mark their bodies with narrow bands of mineral pigment, one for each major event in a cycle. A person’s arms and throat may show decades of weather in layered rust, bone-yellow, and blue-gray.

They travel in small groups, but they are never alone. The plateau is too open for secrecy and too loud for silence. A lone traveler announces themselves with a repeating call, not to ask permission but to prevent panic. Other travelers answer from far away. The exchange may continue for hours, their voices passing between the towers and cliffs until everyone within range knows where everyone else is.

When danger comes, they do not flee in a straight line. They enter the vegetation.

The plant-mat parts for them. Their hands find channels invisible to strangers, and their bodies slip beneath the surface into the root-floor, where cool tunnels run between stone and living fiber. These passages are not built, though generations of passage have shaped them. The people maintain them by removing dead growth and guiding young roots around the narrowest places. Predators can smell them there, and sometimes reach them, but most cannot follow the sudden turns and vertical drops. The tunnels connect shelters, pools, tower interiors, and hidden ledges across distances that seem impossible from above.

The dead are placed in those tunnels.

A body is wrapped in a woven sheath and lowered into a root chamber where condensation gathers. No stone is set over it. No name is carved. Instead, the family plants a cutting or places a seed pod against the body and leaves. In time, roots enter the wrapping. The bones become pale obstructions in the dark soil. Years later, a new stalk may rise from the chamber, or a flowering wall may spread over it. The dead are not believed to become plants exactly. That distinction is too narrow for the high country. They become available.

The people have stories about the old inhabitants who tried to remain separate. In these stories, there were once houses with straight walls, roads that crossed the plateau without bending for roots, and towers cut down to make room for fields. The result was not punishment but exposure. Without the living architecture, the wind took the soil. Without the root channels, water fled over the shelves. Without the herds, the predators changed their paths and the flowering plants failed. The builders survived for a while in their clean, bright settlements, then disappeared one by one into the country they had refused to join.

Whether the stories are true is not important. Every abandoned structure confirms them.

The current people leave almost nothing that can be found after a few generations. Their tools are made to return to the ground. Their clothing is woven from fronds, tendon, and the long fibers shed by the towers. Their shelters are designed to collapse inward without killing the plants around them. Even their fires are small and smoky, fed by dead stalks gathered from places where the vegetation is already closing over.

They adorn themselves with objects that cannot last: beetle wings, shed claws, the translucent skins of gliding animals, strips of copper-colored bark. During ceremonies they paint their bodies with wet mineral dust and stand beneath the largest towers, where the wind turns their voices into a deep communal vibration. The tower answers through its hollow interior. Water falls somewhere in the dark. The people listen for changes in the rhythm.

They are not rulers of the plateau, nor its caretakers in the sentimental sense. The country does not need tending as a garden needs tending. It needs witnesses, participants, bodies that carry pollen, disturb the soil, frighten certain predators away from nesting grounds, and die where roots can reach them.

When the herds cross, the people climb to the shelves and watch them pass. They stand in small groups among the copper grasses, almost indistinguishable from the stone. The animals move around them like a dark river. Birds circle above. The mat bends and rises behind each body. Across the immense distances, calls answer calls, and the old towers release their organ-like roar.

The people lift their faces to it.

They know that the sound is not a voice, and yet they live as though the plateau is speaking.
