What lives in the High Garden Below has no separate word for dwelling.

The plants do not merely occupy the terraces. They are the terraces, and the terraces are their accumulated bodies. A wall may be alive at one end, dead at another, and neither condition matters to the wall. Root-fibers continue to draw nourishment through mineralized tissue. New growth pushes from chambers whose first cells were born before any creature now living had acquired its present shape. The architecture is therefore not a shelter for life but a life that has become large enough to shelter other lives.

The oldest inhabitants are the rooted masses.

They stand in forests, lie in mats, descend in curtains, and hang over the open water in enormous pale folds. Some are slow enough to appear geological. Their growth is measured not in height but in the alteration of pressure around them: a bridge thickens, a rib closes, a ceiling lowers by the width of a hand over a thousand years. They sense the movement of water through their tissues and respond to the continual weight of the ocean. Where the current presses hardest, they become denser. Where it slackens, they open.

They do not think as animals think. There is no center in them, no hidden face turned toward the world. Their awareness is distributed through roots, membranes, pores, and the faint electrical weather passing from one layer of tissue to the next. A living wall knows that something has approached it. A forest knows that a distant branch has broken. A column knows the changing weight of the water above it. Knowledge travels through the Garden not as a message but as a change in growth.

The plants remember by becoming.

A scar becomes a groove in a pillar. A dead root becomes a vein of dark mineral. An ancient nest is sealed beneath translucent tissue and later opened again by a passing animal. Nothing is stored away. Everything remains exposed in the body of the place.

The animals are more temporary, though some of them are older than civilizations that once existed above the sea.

The smallest move through the Garden in clouds, threads, and brief black constellations. They graze on the films coating the old architecture, gather around opened flowers, and sift through the soft sediment trapped in root-fiber. Individually they are almost nothing. Together they alter the world. A passage worn by their repeated bodies becomes a path. A path becomes a shallow channel. The plants recognize the channel and reinforce its edges. In time, an avenue has formed, though no animal intended to make one.

The black herds are among the most purposeful creatures. They travel in scattered formations along the grown roads, keeping precise distances from one another. They possess no visible leader and show no sign of panic. When the water changes around them, they alter their spacing. When one disappears into a living wall, the others do not wait. The wall has opened for it; the wall will know when to close.

Their migrations are not journeys toward elsewhere. They are circuits. Each herd returns to the same terraces, the same chambers, the same regions of dimly luminous growth. Their bodies carry spores and microscopic eggs from district to district. Their excrement feeds the roots. Their passage keeps the plant-built routes open. They are perhaps less like travelers than like circulation made visible.

The long translucent feeders hang beneath the canopies with the patience of suspended instruments. Internal chambers hold them against the current. Their mouths widen and close. They do not pursue food. They wait for the Garden to bring it to them. Small bodies vanish into the pale darkness of their throats; particles settle along their filtering membranes; waste drifts away to nourish something rooted below.

Some have lived so long in one place that the plants have grown around them. Their bodies are threaded through with algae and fine roots. Young animals rest against their flanks, mistaking them for warm architecture, though nothing in the High Garden is warm. When one finally dies, it does not fall. The surrounding growth opens, receives it, and begins to incorporate its skeleton. In a few centuries the animal is a chamber. In a few thousand years it may be a stairway.

The flat creatures are more difficult to distinguish from the world they inhabit. They cling to walls beneath the pale leaves, their jointed fins folded like layers of bark. They move only when necessary, and then with a slow, deliberate unfolding. Some graze on mineral films; others feed on the tiny organisms drawn to the faint light in the plant tissues. They leave behind clean patches in the dark growth, pale shapes like erased writing.

The eel-like animals live inside the architecture. They pass through pores and narrow channels, emerging from the walls to feed and withdrawing again when the water around them becomes too open. Generations of them may inhabit a single pillar. They know its internal routes as a burrowing animal knows a tunnel system, though the pillar itself may continue growing and alter those routes over centuries. When a passage closes, they do not struggle against it. They find another way through.

Farther from the terraces live the great animals.

Their presence is first felt in the sound they make. A call arrives through the water, travels along the ribs of the plant-built world, and reaches a distant chamber long before the body that produced it. By the time the creature appears, the walls have already begun to tremble in recognition.

The broad-headed grazers move through the open water with hundreds of delicate fins. Their mouths gather immense veils of suspended life. They are not swift. To watch one cross the basin is to watch a change in the darkness, a gradual rearrangement of leaves and glows around an advancing body. Scavengers follow behind in drifting swarms. Small animals settle temporarily upon its back. Spores adhere to its skin and are carried into regions the rooted plants could never reach.

There are larger things still.

Sometimes a pale underside glides across the upper darkness, too distant and extensive to understand as a body. Sometimes a line of eyes appears among the leaves, each one opening and closing with the slow regularity of a chamber filling with water. Sometimes a pressure travels through several districts at once, and all the smaller creatures alter their positions before anything visible has arrived.

These great inhabitants do not rule the Garden. They pass through it. Their size grants them no dominion, only a longer relationship with distance.

Predation is present, but it has lost the violence familiar elsewhere. A hunter settles over its prey and allows its body to unfold around it. A colony of filter-feeders closes by degrees, as naturally as a room becoming dark. The prey may continue moving for a long time within the enclosing structure. There is no chase, no struggle that can be separated from the movements of water and tissue. Consumption is an architectural act. The body is taken apart, absorbed, and eventually returned as food, shelter, or shape.

The creatures of the Still Rooms are stranger.

They enter through the dark openings and settle facing the same direction. Some remain for days; some for centuries. They do not feed. They do not mate. They do not appear to sleep. Their bodies become still enough that the pale markings on the walls seem to continue through them.

Perhaps the Still Rooms are places of refuge. Perhaps they are nurseries, though no young have ever been observed there. Perhaps the animals come because the old root-marks beneath the walls exert a pressure on their nervous systems, drawing them into a posture older than their species. Whatever occurs inside, it is not an event in the ordinary sense. It is a suspension. The creatures enter as living things and wait until some unrecorded condition has passed through them.

The basin at the summit gathers nearly all forms of life, but not into a community that could be called society. There are no councils, territories, tools, or voices raised in command. The inhabitants are arranged by depth, pressure, nourishment, and memory. Small bodies move near the floor. Long bodies hang in the middle water. The largest pass above them, their silhouettes interrupted by leaves and shafts of blue-green light.

They do not gather to witness the white-and-green column at the basin’s center. They gather because the currents meet there, because the plants release spores there, because old migration paths cross beneath its shadow, because the column’s tissues contain food and minerals carried up from the buried mountain. Yet their arrangement resembles attendance. From a distance, the animals seem to have come for something.

Perhaps they have.

The Garden may be the only thing that can be said to live there completely. It possesses no single body, but every body belongs to it for a time. The plants make the ground, the animals make the paths, the water moves through both, and the dead return as structure. Its intelligence is not an intention but an equilibrium maintained across immense spans of time.

Nothing in it seeks to escape the darkness.

The darkness is not an absence to the creatures below. It is the medium in which they are visible to one another: a blackness interrupted by leaves, bodies, spores, eyes, and the slow opening of flowers. They do not need to see the mountain beneath them. They feel it through the pressure of the roots. They do not need the sea’s surface. The surface has no meaning at their depth.

They know only the current, the cold, the old living walls, and the paths that return.

They live by being carried, by attaching themselves, by filtering, grazing, opening, closing, growing around what has died. Their lives are quiet because nothing here must hurry. Every creature is part of a process that began before its lineage had a name and will continue after its body has become indistinguishable from a bridge, a pillar, a basin, or a pale seam beneath the floor.

The High Garden Below is inhabited by organisms that have forgotten where life ends and landscape begins.

Or perhaps they have never known.
