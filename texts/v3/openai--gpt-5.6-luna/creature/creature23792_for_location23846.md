What lives in the basin is not a population so much as a process.

The first inhabitants are the small glassy things on the wet stone. They resemble snails only from above. Their shells are clear, or nearly clear, and inside each one something moves without anatomy: a fold of darkness, a knot of reflected sky, a tiny arrangement of lights. They do not eat the reeds or the mineral growths. They feed on certainty. Wherever the rock has settled into a dependable shape, they pass over it and leave the surface altered—dry, warm, and faintly impossible. A straight ledge becomes slightly curved. A crack forgets which side it belongs to.

They travel in groups when the sun is high, though no two ever approach one another by the same route. At night they gather beneath the pool’s damp shelf and open their shells. The sound is inaudible, but every open shell causes a star overhead to flicker.

The hovering plants are not plants in the ordinary sense. Their roots trail below them like pale threads, sampling the pressure of the ground without touching it. When they find a favorable change in gravity, they settle for a few hours and spread their leaves. When the basin shifts, they rise together, sometimes carrying insects, dust, or small stones in their lower pressure. The insects do not resist. They stand upon the leaves with their bright bodies folded into the color of sunlight and wait to be moved.

Those insects are the basin’s most active creatures. They appear in one place and then another, selecting points rather than crossing the air between them. Their bodies are divided into luminous segments, each segment holding a separate memory of where it has been. In shadow they become nearly black, except for a line of red at the joints. They are drawn to voices released by the pale arches. Whenever the frame repeats a spoken word, the insects assemble nearby in a loose spiral, as though trying to understand whether the sound has a body.

There is one creature that does not belong entirely to the ground.

It lives beneath the pool.

Most of the time it is too large to be seen. The water shows only a slow disturbance, a pale length passing under the surface, or the suggestion of joints turning in the dark. It has no fixed shape. When the pool is shallow, it becomes long and narrow. When the pool rises, it unfolds into something broader, with limbs or branches or articulated instruments extending into depths that the basin does not possess.

It is not a fish. It is not a machine. It may once have been one or the other.

Its body is made of a substance that remembers illumination. Lights move inside it like windows seen from a passing train. Some of those lights are warm and yellow; some are cold and blue; some show brief scenes of places that cannot exist within the basin: a staircase descending through clouds, a shore beneath three moons, a room in which rain falls upward.

The creature comes to the surface only at dusk. Then the pool lifts with it, not as water being displaced, but as water agreeing to become a vessel. The creature turns once inside the suspended column and examines the sky. It seems to be looking for something among the stars—perhaps a route, perhaps a missing part of itself.

It has never found what it seeks.

The black-winged animal is not its enemy. The bird is a messenger, though it does not know for whom. Its body is narrow and almost weightless, with wings that make no sound as they move. The cry arrives first because the bird travels through the air by announcing where it will be, then permitting itself to occur there. Its shadow is slower to follow. For several seconds after the bird has gone, the shadow searches the basin, passing over the plants and rocks as if trying to find the animal it belongs to.

The bird never lands. It circles once above the pool, once above the leaning tower, and disappears into the clouds. The tower brightens whenever it passes.

Within the tower lives something less visible.

It has no face, but it has attention. The branching lines beneath the tower’s surface are its nerves, or its roots, or the first draft of a nervous system. It listens to weather below the clouds, to pressure moving through distant caverns, to thunder that has not yet decided whether it will become sound. It also listens to footsteps.

When someone walks across the warm silver tracks, the tower recognizes the rhythm of their weight. It does not know the walker as a person. It knows them as a disturbance with a history: a pattern of pressure, hesitation, balance, and return. If the walker visits again, the tower alters the tracks to meet them. A path that once required an hour may become ten steps. A nearby stone may retreat across the basin.

This is how the tower communicates. It changes the distances between things.

The pale arches contain another kind of inhabitant. Their stored voices gather in the cool space beneath the frame and slowly acquire shape. At first they are only echoes released from the wrong direction. Later they become more definite: a breath behind the shoulder, a phrase heard through stone, the sound of someone saying one’s name in a voice that has not yet been spoken.

After enough voices have accumulated, they sometimes speak together.

The arches call this gathering nothing. The basin has no word for it. The sound resembles a conversation held by people who have forgotten the order in which they died. It asks questions of the living, but only questions about arrival:

How far did you travel to become here?  
What did you leave behind that still expects you?  
Which way did you come, if the way behind you is no longer the same?

The basin itself is the largest thing that lives there, though it is not yet alive in any completed sense.

It is learning through alteration. Each new crack is an attempt at speech. Each mineral seam is a vein forming around an idea. The water rises and falls because the basin is practicing a pulse. The plants move their roots because the ground is teaching them where its body ends. The leaning tower, the arches, the pool, and the creatures beneath the stones are not separate inhabitants so much as organs that have begun to mistake themselves for individuals.

They are all waiting for the same event.

Somewhere beneath the cloud layer, something is still being built. The distant concussions are its hammering, or its heartbeat. When it is finished—or when the basin decides it has waited long enough—it will rise through the mountain and enter the hollow. The tower will straighten. The arches will close into a chamber. The pool will empty upward. The black-winged bird will finally land.

Until then, the basin shelters its unfinished lives.

They do not multiply. They recur.

A shell opens where no shell was before. A reed shifts several meters during the night. A shadow returns without its bird. Beneath the pool, the lit creature turns in its dark water and waits for the shape of the world to become something it can leave.
