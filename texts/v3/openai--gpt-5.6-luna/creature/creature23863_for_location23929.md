What lives in the Upper Hollow is not a species so much as a condition that has learned to move.

The individual creatures are called, if they are called anything at all, **hollowskins**. They have no names for themselves. A hollowskin is not sufficiently separate from the bodies around it to require one. It may begin as a warm patch in the stone, a blister where pressure has folded mineral around a pocket of black tissue. It may bud from the underside of an older body, or emerge when two long-dead forms grind together and the heat between them becomes active. At first it is no more than a dark seam. Later, it acquires plates.

The plates are not armor, though they resemble it. They are records.

Each ridge marks an exposure to heat. Each pale scar records a sudden pressure. The deep grooves along their backs are channels through which they sense the mountain’s changes. A hollowskin feels weight arriving from above. It feels the slow expansion of buried rock. It feels a body moving several layers away, not as motion but as a disturbance in the shape of the surrounding heat.

In this place, sensation does not belong to an organ. It belongs to contact.

The creatures know one another by what happens through them.

A small hollowskin pressed beneath a larger one may register a tremor that began at the sealed stair. It passes the disturbance into the body above it, and that body passes it onward. The signal travels through the packed basin as a long, dim shiver. By the time it reaches the far wall, it is no longer a message from any single creature. It has become a decision.

The colony shifts.

No hollowskin leads the movement. No hollowskin understands it. Yet the bodies part around a rising form, gather near a fault, retreat from an unstable ledge, and turn toward the mountain when something presses from within. They behave like fragments of one animal whose nerves have been scattered through stone.

Perhaps that is what they are.

Perhaps, long ago, the Upper Hollow contained one enormous living body. It may have descended from the heights or emerged from the mountain itself. The bodies now covering the basin could be its shed scales, its broken organs, its children, or its thoughts given physical shape. The tall ones that unfold from the floor may be the portions that still remember being whole. When they descend over the rim, they are not leaving. They are extending the colony downward, carrying its awareness into the shafts and heat below.

They do not return quickly.

Some never return at all. They vanish into the lower dark, where the air thickens and the red glow comes closer. The hollowskins left behind do not mourn them. They alter around the absence. A space opens, fills with another body, and eventually becomes indistinguishable from the rest of the floor.

But absence is not the same as death.

A descending form may remain in contact with the colony through the mountain. A vibration may return after months, arriving from far beneath the basin. When that happens, the creatures closest to the rim flatten themselves and open their seams. Gray vapor leaks upward. The message passes through the colony in silence.

The returned one has found something.

Or been found.

The hollowskins feed on gradients, but they seem to prefer gradients made by intention. They gather where pressure is applied and released in a rhythm. They crowd the sealed opening because something behind it is not merely pressing against the stone. It is testing the stone. It has done so for longer than any living creature in the Hollow can remember.

The hollowskins cannot open the door. They cannot close it. They have no tools, no speech, no plan that can be separated from the mountain’s own slow behavior.

Yet they attend it.

The stair is their threshold, and the threshold has become the center of their life. Generations of bodies occupy the steps, each layer formed over the last. The oldest creatures lie nearest the sealed landing, darkened almost beyond recognition. The youngest cling to the lower steps, pale at the edges and easily displaced. None crosses the final boundary. Even the tall forms, capable of descending cliffs and hanging from vertical stone, stop before the thin line in the wall.

This restraint may be instinct.

It may be fear.

Or the creatures may understand that the opening is not a door at all, but a wound held shut by pressure from both sides.

When the thing beyond flexes the mountain, the hollowskins become still because stillness is the only response that does not feed the disturbance. Their bodies lock together. Their ridges stop expanding. Their inner folds contract and hold. For a little while, the colony becomes a single mass: a seal over the basin, a listening organ spread across the floor.

What they hear in those moments is inaccessible to air.

Perhaps it is a voice moving through basalt. Perhaps it is the memory of immense movement below. Perhaps the pressure comes not from an inhabitant waiting behind the stone, but from the mountain itself, trying to rise further, with the hollowskins clinging to its crown.

The creatures do not know the difference. Knowledge, for them, is not an explanation. It is a change in posture.

When the pressure passes, the Upper Hollow resumes its slow life. Bodies climb over bodies. New seams open. Old forms darken into landscape. A small hollowskin works its way free from beneath a dead one and finds a warmer place near the wall. Across the basin, a tall shape begins to unfold, carrying in its plates the faint memory of a signal from below.

The colony receives it.

The colony changes.

And beneath the stone, beyond the sealed opening, something waits for the next answer.
