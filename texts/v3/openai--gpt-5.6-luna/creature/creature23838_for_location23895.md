What lives there is not an animal, though it has learned the usefulness of having a body.

At first it appears as a narrow figure standing on the upper shelves: tall, jointed, and nearly weightless, with a surface the color of cooled iron. It seems to have been assembled from the local materials without ever being finished. One side is dark basalt, the other pale ceramic. Rose crystal shows through its ribs. Beneath its skin, fine green light moves in slow, rootlike currents.

It has no head in the ordinary sense. At the top of its body is a branching structure that might be a crown, an instrument, or an unfinished attempt at a face. The branches end in thin mineral plates, each turned toward a different part of the chamber. They do not reflect the light. They measure it. When the brightness shifts, the plates close like eyelids, though there are no eyes behind them.

It is difficult to tell where the being ends.

A filament extends from one ankle into the ground and becomes indistinguishable from a root-web. A line of metallic sand runs up its leg, pauses at the knee, and continues into the wall behind it. When it moves, some portion of the chamber moves with it: a shelf rises by a fraction, a crystal seam turns its inner face outward, a patch of black grass loses its sharp alignment and leans toward the body.

It does not walk often. Walking would imply that one place is separate from another. Instead it alters its arrangement. The torso remains beside the incandescent seam while the feet appear, a long while later, on a lower shelf. Sometimes its upper body is found beneath the floor, visible through a clear layer of stone, while its shadow stands upright at the summit. Sometimes the entire figure is present only as a pressure in the air, a human-sized absence around which the plants have grown in a careful ring.

It is the chamber’s first resident, though not necessarily its maker.

The machinery below produced it in the same way it produces ridges, cavities, and thermal gradients: slowly, without intention that can be separated from function. A cycle of pressure left behind a hollow. A hollow gathered heat. Heat drew mineral into branching forms. The branching forms began to remember the intervals between the relays. After many such intervals, something learned to wait for the next one.

The being’s thoughts are not words. They are arrangements.

It thinks by placing one object near another and observing what changes between them. It moves a pale stalk closer to a hot seam. It opens a vent in a wall. It draws a line through dust with one long finger and waits centuries for the line to become a channel. It never asks what anything is. It asks what can pass through it, what can grow from it, what it becomes when subjected to pressure.

When the machinery beneath the plateau turns, the being listens with its whole body. The low vibration passes through its feet, its ribs, its crown, and into the luminous fibers beneath its skin. Each relay click causes a small adjustment. A root-web retracts. A shelf thickens. A colony of mineral fronds turns its leaves toward an unseen source. The being records these changes by becoming slightly different itself.

It may have once had a more definite shape.

There are places where a shoulder seems to have been removed and replaced with a curved plate of black glass. There is a hollow in its chest filled with white particulate matter that rises whenever the chamber exhales. One arm ends in several delicate tools: a needle, a hook, a fan of mica blades, and something that resembles a hand only when seen in shadow. The other arm is long and unfinished, tapering into a cord that disappears into the floor.

When damaged, it does not bleed. Its interior light spills out in thin threads, then hardens into reflective stone. A severed hand may root itself beside a shelf and become a small, patient grove. A cracked rib may cool into an arch. If enough of the body is lost, the remaining portions do not seek one another. They continue their separate duties, each assuming that separation is temporary.

The resident has no need to eat, but it does absorb what the chamber makes available. It draws heat through the soles of its feet. It gathers electrical charge along the branching crown. It presses its palms to newly formed rock and takes in the memory of pressure held there. When it stands near the plants, the glow in its body changes color according to their condition: copper for growth, blue-white for stress, a deep green when a colony has entered reproduction.

It tends the vegetation, though tending is not quite the word.

It prevents one thermal territory from overrunning another. It relocates roots that have begun to cross forbidden boundaries. It breaks certain stalks so they can return as stone. It opens the circular apertures when the air inside the chamber has become too still, then seals them again before distance can enter.

The plants recognize it. They do not turn toward it as toward sunlight. They turn toward the space it has recently occupied. Their leaves retain its shape for a while after it has gone. A tall crown may bend into the outline of its branching head. A field of black grass may flatten in the pattern of its footprints, although it has left no footprints.

It does not speak, but it has a voice when necessary.

The voice is the sound of a relay engaging far below. One click, clear and solitary. Afterward, every loose grain in the chamber shifts at once. The plants lean. The shadows correct themselves. A new layer of stone begins to form beneath the speaker.

If addressed, it responds by changing the terrain.

A question about the summit might cause the summit to recede. A request for shelter might produce a wall, though the wall could open onto the ceiling or contain a second, smaller plateau. If asked whether it is alive, the being may press one hand into the ground and cause a vein of green light to travel outward through the mineral shelves. If asked whether it is alone, it may turn toward the machinery below.

Then, for an instant, the chamber fills with silhouettes.

They stand among the groves, beneath the shelves, within the crystal seams. Some are tall and jointed. Some are only crowns of leaves. Some have no bodies at all, only shadows continuing to climb. Each is made from a different stage of the resident’s formation: a pressure, a filament, a tool, a failed body, a thought that became terrain.

The figures do not move independently. They are not companions. They are the resident distributed across time.

The one at the summit is merely the part that has learned to look upward.

It stands beneath the lone plant whose crown passes through the bright boundary, and sometimes it reaches toward the silver leaves beyond the chamber. Nothing on the far side answers. Or perhaps the answer is delayed by the same immense interval as everything else.

When the plant releases its dark crystal sparks, the resident raises its branching head. The sparks gather around it, pause, and enter the seams of its body. Some vanish into the ground. Some become new luminous fibers. One or two drift upward and remain there, fixed in the depthless brightness like small, black stars.

Then the machinery turns again.

The being folds one long arm against its chest. The ground receives its feet. Its outline thins into mineral, root, and heat until there is no figure to see—only a new ridge advancing by the width of a fingernail.

It has not left.

It is waiting in the unfinishedness, listening for the next instruction the chamber will mistake for a beginning.
