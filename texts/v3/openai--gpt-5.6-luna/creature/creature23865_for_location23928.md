What lives on the plateau has not separated itself cleanly into plant, animal, and machine.

At first there seem to be only the small lives: moss swelling over stone, fern roots drinking from cracks, pale insects rising after rain in brief, coordinated clouds. The insects have translucent bodies and dark points where eyes might be, though they do not appear to look at anything. They gather around warm pipes and drainage mouths, settling in the same patterns each time, as if obeying diagrams too fine to be seen. By morning they are gone. Their wings remain caught in the moss, silvering it.

The red-stemmed shrubs are not still. Their stems tighten when the electric cars pass. Not from wind or vibration—the motion begins several moments before the cars arrive, traveling outward through the mats in widening ripples. Afterward, the plants relax. Their leaves retain a faint charge, and small sparks move between them in darkness.

The pale jointed growths are more deliberate. They turn toward a source of illumination that is not always the sun. On clear days their fan-shaped leaves face upward and eastward. During long periods of cloud, they incline toward the transmission towers. When the slow radiance passes through the cables, every stand on the plateau makes a nearly invisible adjustment. The movement is too gradual to witness directly, but a person who returns after an hour finds that the trees have changed their minds.

Their roots go deep.

They enter the porous earth, follow buried pipes, and pass through the foundations of the pumping stations. Some have reached the machinery itself. Roots lie against pistons and valves without obstructing them. They pulse when the pumps pulse. In the oldest stations, the plant matter has become indistinguishable from insulation: pale fibers braided through wire, green filaments running beneath the enamel of control panels. If a door is left open, the roots turn toward the room.

There are animals, though they are seldom seen at close range.

They move on four long limbs and have the narrow, balanced bodies of creatures built for broken ground. Their feet are broad and dark, divided into several soft pads that leave almost no impression. Their coats are the color of wet schist. In direct light, they disappear against the cliffs; among grasses, they appear as gaps in the grasses. Some bear branching structures from their skulls. These may be antlers, or mineral growths, or the exposed roots of something that began inside them. The branches collect condensation. Water gathers there in clear beads and drips slowly down their necks.

They do not graze in any ordinary way. They stand among the grasses with their heads lowered, listening through their feet. When one moves, the others do not follow. Instead, distant machinery changes rhythm. A pump slows. A cable brightens. A rail car stops between stations and waits until the animal has crossed a ridge several kilometers away.

Nothing seems to prey on them. They are not numerous enough to form herds, but the plateau is full of signs that they have passed: flattened circles in the moss, warm depressions beside concrete walls, narrow strands of dark hair wound around bolts. In the wettest ravines, one may find a print filled with water that remains long after every other puddle has dried.

The rail cars carry another kind of life.

They are empty when they arrive and not always empty when they leave. Their interiors contain no seats, only dark panels and shallow metal channels. After rain, small pale organisms sometimes gather inside them. They resemble folded leaves at first. Then they unfold jointed limbs and attach themselves to the walls, where they remain until the car reaches the highest shelf. There they detach, one by one, and vanish into the moss.

The cars seem to know where to stop.

No platform marks the places. No signal changes. A car simply pauses beside a bare section of track while something climbs aboard: a root bundle, a white lichen branch, a long-legged animal seen only as a distortion in the air. The doors close. The car continues. At the next ridge, the passenger is gone.

The pumping stations contain larger organisms.

They occupy the spaces behind the walls and beneath the floors, where the machinery is warmest. They have no stable shape. In one station they appear as a skin of gray material stretched over the interior pipes; in another, as clusters of glassy nodules surrounding a turbine shaft. They feed on pressure, heat, and the minute electrical differences between one system and another. Their growth is slow but exact. They thicken around a leak before the leak becomes visible. They withdraw from a failing valve. They leave behind smooth, sealed surfaces where rust should be.

The workers who built the stations may once have known about them. There are signs of accommodation everywhere: inspection panels widened without explanation, vents angled toward the ground, shallow basins set beneath pipes that do not carry water. One barracks contains a row of small rooms with drains but no fixtures. On each wall, at shoulder height, someone has marked a series of horizontal lines. The marks continue behind the moss. They may record the height of floods, or the growth of something that lived in the rooms.

The buildings are not abandoned by their original inhabitants so much as repurposed by what came after them.

Doors open and close without wind. Lights come on in rooms with no power. A staircase may acquire an additional step over the course of a year, formed from mineral deposits, root tissue, and something harder than either. The control huts occasionally contain objects that were not there before: a wet stone placed in the center of the floor, a coil of grass, a polished fragment of black metal warm to the touch.

These offerings are not made to anyone visible.

The plateau itself may be the oldest inhabitant.

It communicates through pressure and accumulation. It sends water uphill in certain places, turns shadows away from towers, and holds reflections that belong to another arrangement of sky. Its fractures widen by measured amounts. Its streams alter their courses whenever the machinery below changes frequency. The rock is not conscious in any human sense, but it remembers weight. It remembers where the towers stand. It remembers the paths of the cars and the feet of the long-legged animals. When something is removed, the surrounding land slowly leans toward the absence.

There are perhaps people somewhere in the region, but none live openly on the plateau. If they come, they wear dark insulated clothing and move between structures without speaking. They inspect gauges, touch the cables, scrape moss from numbered plates. Sometimes they leave food in sealed containers. Sometimes they leave nothing and return years later to find that the containers have been opened from the inside.

No one has seen them in the open for long.

The plateau has its own guardians, if guardians are what they are. They are distributed through the roots, the pipes, the animals, the towers, and the patient electrical pulse beneath the stone. They do not protect the place from intrusion. They maintain its continuities. A fallen wall is incorporated. A blocked channel is redirected. A dead machine is warmed until something living enters it.

When the long-legged creatures stand among the grasses, the transmission lines brighten.

When the plants turn toward the unseen light, the rail cars alter their routes by fractions of a degree.

When rain fills the hollows, the organisms beneath the pumping stations wake and begin to move through the system, sealing, opening, carrying, measuring.

Nothing calls because nothing here needs to announce itself.

The plateau is occupied by a slow agreement among things that were grown, built, split from the earth, or left behind. It has no single species, no center, and perhaps no name for itself. Its inhabitants do not appear to own the land. They pass through it, settle into it, correct it, and are corrected in turn.

Given enough time, they may cover everything.

Or they may already have.
