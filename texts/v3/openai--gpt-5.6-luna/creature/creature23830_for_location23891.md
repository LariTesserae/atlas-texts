They are people of the basin, though the basin has altered what “people” means there.

They move along the raised walks in waterproof aprons, patched boots, gloves darkened by sap, and close-fitting hoods that keep the dripping leaves from their faces. Most are not hurried. There is nowhere to hurry toward that is not connected by the same narrow bridges, the same sagging platforms, the same green-walled passages they have known since childhood. They walk with one hand on the railings, testing the give of metal and wood without looking. They know which boards are beginning to soften, which roots have lifted a tile, which vines conceal a live cable, which patches of mud will swallow a heel.

Their clothes are made for continuous damp. Heavy fabrics are waxed, rubberized, or woven from plant fiber treated with oil. Sleeves are short in the processing yards and long beneath the trees. Many wear colored bands around their wrists or throats to indicate their work: blue for water, yellow for heat and boilers, red for cutting crews, white for nursery and medical work. The colors fade quickly beneath the moisture and machine dust, so they are renewed often. No one thinks of this as decoration. A worker seen through leaves at a distance must be identifiable before they are close enough to speak.

They are not large in number. The basin supports only as many people as its pumps, gardens, workshops, and recovery beds can sustain. Houses are crowded into old plant structures and attached to one another by covered walks. Families occupy rooms inside hollow trunks, above workshops, beneath pumping stations, and along the sides of the processing yard where the machinery’s vibration keeps mold from settling. A household may consist of several generations, two unrelated maintenance crews, and a child who has not yet been assigned a sleeping place of their own. Privacy exists, but it is architectural rather than cultural: a curtain, a ladder, a room deep inside a tree.

Children learn the settlement by touch before they learn it by name. They know the difference between a warm pipe and a dangerous one, between a living root and a cable sheath, between the rough bark of a load-bearing trunk and the brittle skin of a tree due for cutting. They are taught never to pull a vine unless they know what it is attached to. They play in the drainage channels, where they float folded leaves and small wooden scraps through the slow water, but they are not allowed near the settling ponds without an older person. Their games involve routes, shortcuts, valves, and hidden rooms. A child raised here can cross the basin in darkness by listening to the pumps.

The inhabitants speak quietly, partly from habit and partly because the machinery has trained them to reserve their voices. Their faces are often close together when they talk. In the yards, they use hand signs: a raised fist for shutoff, two fingers pointed downward for a leak, a flat palm turned twice for reverse flow. These gestures have grown elaborate over generations. A worker can communicate the location, severity, and probable cause of a fault while standing on one side of a conveyor and remaining unheard by the machine.

They are attentive to small changes. A new smell in the drainage air is reported before the water is tested. A change in the pitch of a turbine brings someone running from a meal. If leaves begin to hang on a plant that should be upright, if moss dries on a roof, if a root channel grows warm, the information travels through the settlement by voices, bells, marks painted on pipes, and people carrying messages through the green corridors. Nothing is too minor to matter. A failed gutter may become a dead tree; a dead tree may bring down a walkway; a fallen walkway may interrupt the movement of water or fuel.

Their work is divided, but not cleanly. The pump operators know the names of the trees around their machinery. The nursery workers understand the pressure readings in the main water lines. People who harvest leaves also inspect insulation panels. Children are assigned to maintenance before they are old enough to regard it as a profession. Most occupations are inherited at first and altered later. One becomes a boiler tender because one’s mother was a boiler tender, then spends half a lifetime developing a better moss compound for condenser roofs.

They carry tools that have been repaired as many times as the buildings. Knives with handles wrapped in resin. Measuring rods marked by generations of use. Wrenches whose jaws have been reshaped to fit obsolete bolts. Some instruments are passed between households as carefully as heirlooms. A pressure gauge may bear the initials of five people who kept the same pump running across eighty years. A pruning saw may have teeth filed into a pattern recognized by everyone in the cutting crews.

The people are physically accustomed to the basin’s conditions. Their skin is often stained at the fingertips and along the nails by plant dyes, iron, and soil. They have broad, practiced feet from years of crossing uneven walkways. Their lungs are sensitive to certain spores, and most carry folded masks for the rare days when the air turns thick. Many have small scars from cuts that closed greenly around splinters or from hot metal brushed against in cramped machinery rooms. They treat such injuries with the same unceremonious care given to a leaking pipe: wash, bind, observe, repair.

They do not romanticize the vegetation. A tree can crush a house. A vine can enter a gearbox. Roots can lift a foundation or split a cistern. Plants are pruned, grafted, trained, harvested, poisoned, and sometimes burned. Yet the people understand that their own structures are temporary in a way the plants are not. A wall may be rebuilt in a season. A machine may be replaced in a year. A tree that supports a bridge may have been growing before anyone’s grandmother was born.

They give the older plants names, but not always human names. There is the First Column, the Seven-Throated Tree, the Bitter Shade, the House That Drinks, and the Old Pump’s Root. Some names refer to function, some to accidents, some to people long dead. The names are used in maintenance instructions, family stories, and warnings. “Do not tap the eastern window of the House That Drinks.” “The Bitter Shade has flowered early.” “Your grandfather fell from the Seven-Throated Tree and finished his shift.”

At night, they gather where the machine noise is least severe, though nowhere is truly quiet. People sit beneath covered platforms, beside warm trunks, or on the roofs of low workshops where the leaves have been cut back. They eat thick soups, pressed cakes, smoked fish from the settling ponds, and vegetables grown in raised beds above the mud. Food is seasoned heavily with sour leaves and mineral salts. Conversation is close and practical at first—water levels, repairs, the condition of someone’s lungs—but stories emerge gradually, especially when the lights come on and the settlement becomes a constellation of lamps under foliage.

Their ceremonies follow maintenance cycles rather than celestial ones. When a major pump is rebuilt, the workers mark the housing with wet clay and press their thumbs into it before the casing is closed. When a tree is harvested, the surrounding households sleep elsewhere for one night and return the next morning to collect the first usable lengths. At the beginning of the wet season, children carry bowls of clear water from the upper cisterns to the low nursery, where it is poured over the roots of newly planted stock. The act is symbolic only in part. The plants need the water, and the people need to remember which systems depend on which others.

Death does not remove anyone completely. A person’s tools remain in circulation. Their repairs become visible in the walls. Their name may be painted on a valve, attached to a graft, or spoken whenever an old machine behaves in the particular way they taught it to behave. The dead are often buried beyond the drainage ring, where the unmanaged vegetation is thickest, though some ashes are mixed into planting beds. The basin accepts them slowly. Roots enter the soil, fungi take what they can, and years later a worker may find a familiar tool handle beside an unfamiliar flower.

There are visitors, but few. Outsiders stand uncertainly on the bridges, disturbed by the lack of clear streets and by the way every surface appears to belong simultaneously to construction and growth. The inhabitants recognize this uncertainty and do not correct it. They are accustomed to being mistaken for caretakers, squatters, factory workers, or gardeners. They are all of these, and none of these alone.

They maintain the basin because they live in it, and live in it because they maintain it. Their descendants will inherit no untouched wilderness and no finished city, only the same damp agreement: water must be directed but not exhausted, plants must be restrained but not killed, machines must be kept running long after their intended lives have ended. Each generation makes small changes. A channel is widened. A roof is raised. A root is taught a new route through a wall.

Then the change is absorbed.

The inhabitants disappear into the vegetation as they work, their voices reduced by leaves and engines to fragments of sound. A sleeve moves behind a curtain of reeds. A lamp lifts along a walkway. Someone reaches through a hatch in a living trunk and adjusts a valve. Somewhere beneath the mud, a pump begins to labor more smoothly.

The basin continues around them, not silent, not wild, and not waiting for rescue. They are its hands, its memory, and its slow, persistent habit of repair.
