At first, the inhabitants seem to be another layer of the vegetation.

A pale shape loosens itself from a wall and remains motionless beside it. A cluster of dark leaves folds inward, revealing a face—or something that serves the same purpose. Along the communal hollow, several upright mineral pillars appear to have grown narrow arms. Only after watching for a while does it become clear that the pillars are not moving. The creatures are.

They are low, many-jointed beings, built for contact rather than distance. Their bodies are long and slightly flattened, with a central trunk of dark, translucent tissue and several pairs of narrow limbs arranged along it. The limbs end in soft, branching pads that can cling to resin, read vibration from stone, or draw themselves into delicate hooks for climbing. Their outer surfaces are almost black where they are thickest, but amber and blue-green light moves beneath them in slow currents.

They have no obvious eyes. Instead, the sides of their heads are covered with overlapping translucent plates, sensitive to heat, pressure, and the faint mineral radiance of the plateau. They know a nearby body by the warmth it gives off. They know a person’s condition by the rhythm of its internal fluids. They know whether a wall is weakening by placing a hand against it and listening through the bones.

Their faces are narrow and unfinished-looking, as if still being shaped. A mouth opens only when they feed or exchange resin. Beneath it are fine, comb-like structures used to taste the air and sample the surfaces around them. Their voices are rarely audible. Most communication passes through touch: a limb laid against another’s shoulder, a sequence of taps along a pillar, a vibration sent through the black-root paths. A group can hold an entire conversation without turning its heads.

Color carries emotion.

A calm inhabitant may show a dim green glow beneath the ribs. Alarm appears as a sudden amber flare along the neck and limbs. Grief—if grief is the right word—drains the body of color until it reflects only the pale light of the stone. During collective decisions, the participants gather in a circle and let their bodies brighten and darken together. Agreement is not spoken. It is reached when the pattern becomes smooth.

They do not own houses.

A dwelling is less a possession than a condition of the town. The walls thicken around those who sleep beside them. Doorways narrow or widen according to the bodies that pass through. A person may spend one season in a pod-shaped room and the next within a warm pillar near the communal hollow, leaving behind a layer of grown architecture that records their presence. The oldest inhabitants are not necessarily the largest or most powerful. They are the ones whose bodies have been incorporated most deeply into the settlement.

Some have become almost immobile.

They sit within mineral alcoves with their lower limbs fused into the resinous floors, attended by smaller relatives and by plants trained around their shoulders. Their bodies have thickened over decades, turning hard and pillar-like. These elders are not treated as rulers. They are used as living archives. When touched, they release stored patterns: the vibration of a distant collapse, the heat of a vanished season, the shape of an old path before it was covered by roots. Memory in this place is not a story told from one mouth to another. It is a pressure preserved in material.

The young are more difficult to distinguish from the animals.

They move quickly through the close vegetation, slipping under platforms and across roofs, carrying small bundles of shed leaf plates or beads of hardened resin. They practice by repairing things. A broken nest, a split wall, a plant with a torn valve: these are their lessons. They learn the town by placing their hands everywhere and feeling which structures answer.

When one of them is injured, the surrounding life responds before any individual does. Small translucent animals gather against its body to draw away excess heat. Plants bend their leaves toward the wound. An older inhabitant opens a vein in its forearm and lets a thread of silver-white fluid run over the damaged tissue. The resin hardens into a clear shell, and beneath it the injury begins to close.

There is no distinction between medicine, gardening, and construction.

The inhabitants cultivate the plants because the plants hold up the town. They prune only when invited by a change in color or pressure. They guide a stem through a wall, wait for it to thicken, then hollow the stem into a room. They encourage roots beneath a path until the ground becomes resilient enough to carry a procession. A structure that begins to fail is not demolished. It is persuaded into another shape.

Their tools are living things as well. A cutting limb may be grown for a single task and then planted. A storage vessel may be a swollen seedpod whose interior responds to touch. Threads of mineral fiber are pulled from certain roots and woven into flexible membranes. Nothing is discarded so much as returned. Broken objects are pressed into cracks, where roots and resin gradually decide what they should become.

The inhabitants eat, though not in a way that leaves much trace. They absorb mineral-rich fluids from plants and from the warm stone, taking only what the living tissues can spare. At communal times, they gather beneath the canopy and place their hands against the pillars. The pillars open small pores. Each creature receives a measured flow of clear, faintly luminous resin. In return, they leave heat, carbon, and chemical traces that help the plants determine where to grow next.

The exchange is not ritual in the human sense. It is as ordinary as breathing. Yet the town becomes most visibly alive during it. Leaves turn toward the hollow. Fine cords beneath the floor tighten. The pillars brighten from within, and the gathered inhabitants settle into a shared vibration that can be felt more easily than heard.

They do not seem afraid of the world below, though they rarely descend.

The lower plain is too diffuse, too exposed, and too poor in the mineral warmth that sustains them. Their bodies are adapted to the plateau’s enclosed heat. At the rim, they sometimes stand among the leaning plants and look down through the haze. They may be watching other young landforms emerge. They may be listening for the subterranean movements that connect one upheaved shelf to another.

Or they may simply be keeping watch over the edge.

The plateau is still forming, and the inhabitants understand themselves as part of that formation. They do not speak of having founded the town. They speak of having arrived at the moment when the stone became able to remember them. Before that, perhaps, they were smaller things in the cracks—colonies of warmth, crawling shells, roots that learned to move. Perhaps the distinction between animal, plant, and dwelling has never been important to them.

An individual can die without leaving the settlement.

Its useful fluids are drawn into the roots. Its shell plates are set into a wall. Its final colors remain in the pillar beside which it rested. Over time, the body becomes another feature of the town: a smooth ridge in a path, a warm place beneath a roof, a faint pulse in the mineral.

And so the inhabitants are everywhere, but never quite alone.

They move through the streets in silent streams. They sleep against living walls. They carry heat from one chamber to another. They bend plants, mend bodies, thicken pillars, and listen to the slow stresses moving through the new stone.

From a distance, the settlement might appear empty—only a dense garden occupying a black plateau above the haze.

Then a roof will flex.

A dozen dark forms will unfold from the leaves.

Somewhere beneath the ground, a vibration will pass from root to root, from pillar to pillar, and every creature in the basin will turn at once toward the sound.
