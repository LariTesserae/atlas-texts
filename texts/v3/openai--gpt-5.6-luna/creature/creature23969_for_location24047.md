What lives there has no single shape.

The city is inhabited by creatures that have learned, over generations too long to count, to treat masonry as landscape. They do not distinguish between street and seabed, between a house and a reef. A wall is simply a vertical surface where eggs can be attached. A staircase is a slope along which food collects. A doorway is a current, and a courtyard is weather.

The smallest residents are everywhere.

They live in the pores of the limestone, beneath loose paving stones, inside the hollow bodies of statues whose faces have been worn away. Transparent shrimp move through the cracks in congregations, their bodies catching the brief blue sparks of disturbed plankton. They groom one another beneath arches. When a shadow passes, the whole colony disappears into the stone, leaving only a few trembling antennae visible.

There are worms that build pale tubes over inscriptions and red worms that gather around the warm fountains in such density that the basins seem carpeted with living flame. Tiny snails graze the green films on stairways. Minute crustaceans sift the silt from courtyards. Some feed on the soft growth covering the buildings; others feed on those grazers; others feed on the scraps left by hunters. The city’s stones are never clean because they are never unoccupied.

The blind fish belong most completely to the lower districts.

They gather where the old drains meet the mineral springs, hovering in dense, colorless layers. Their eyes are small and useless, their bodies sensitive to every shift in pressure. They know the shape of a plaza by the warmth held in its stones. They know when a ray has settled nearby because the current bends around its body. They travel through the drowned streets in streams, flowing around columns and through windows, never colliding, each fish reading the movement of the others through the water.

Some return to the same fountains to breed. Some never leave them. Their young cling to the undersides of basin rims until their bodies are strong enough to enter the current. When the geothermal pulses arrive, the fish rise all at once, not in panic but in recognition. Warm water means loosened food, opened worms, exposed crustaceans. For a few hours, the blind congregations become silver clouds, and the plazas seem almost illuminated from within.

The rays are older in their manner.

They rest in the broad avenues, their bodies conforming to the paving patterns beneath them. A ray may occupy the same stretch of mosaic for years, moving only when the current changes or prey gathers elsewhere. Small fish shelter in the folds near its eyes. Cleaner shrimp work along its back. When it lifts, the pavement beneath is revealed as a dark, living impression of what has lain there.

The largest rays are less like animals passing through the city than like districts of it. Their slow movement alters the local world. A street closes when one settles across it. A colony of shellfish grows in the calm beneath its body. Silt gathers along its edges. Young rays follow the adults into the shallow basin and imitate their resting places, learning the city by lying against it.

The sharks keep to the outskirts.

They are pale from generations beneath the dim water, their bodies almost luminous when they cross a patch of living blue. They patrol the old markets and seawalls where currents carry the scent of injured animals. They rarely enter the central district. There is too much stone, too little room to turn, and too many smaller creatures that know the passages better than they do.

A shark may circle a collapsed arcade for hours, following the invisible trails of movement through the water. It knows which alleys contain sleeping seals, which courtyards are crowded with rays, which fountains draw the injured and the careless. It does not need to see the city whole. Its world is made of gradients: warmth, blood, vibration, salt, the sudden scatter of small bodies.

The whiskered amphibious creatures sleep in the columns.

They are broad and warm-skinned, with blunt heads and webbed limbs, able to remain in the water indefinitely but still drawn to the shelter of stone. They wedge themselves into the spaces between fallen capitals and lean towers. Their breathing is slow. Their whiskers trail in the current and twitch at the approach of anything unfamiliar.

Their young are more restless. They gather in the old fountains, climbing over one another in the warm basins, diving through the tubeworm thickets and emerging covered in pale shells. They play in the upwelling currents, then scatter whenever the water changes. At night—if night can be said to exist here—they sleep in heaps beneath the statues, each small body pressed against another for warmth.

They are among the few creatures that appear to recognize individual places. A mother returns to the same broken fountain. A juvenile follows a particular seawall. Certain families den beneath the same collapsed temple, generation after generation, though whether this is memory or merely inherited preference is impossible to know.

The kelp forests belong to no one.

They rise from rooftops and statues, their long strands drifting through rooms and archways. Fish feed among them. Eels coil around their stems. Transparent siphon creatures hang between the old harbor masts, opening and closing like bellows as they draw nourishment from the current. When the city exhales its geothermal breath, the kelp bends in one direction across every district, and all the inhabitants feel the change before it arrives.

The predators move then.

Eels abandon their channels. Sharks leave the walls. Hunting shoals pour into the warmed streets. Rays lift from the pavement with a great silent flexing of their wings. The city’s usual inhabitants become suddenly visible in their flight: shrimp flashing blue, fish scattering through windows, crabs climbing over one another toward cracks in the stone.

For a little while, the old avenues are full of pursuit.

Then the warmth thins. The silt settles. The hunters return to the edges, and the grazers emerge from hiding. A crab resumes its patient work beside a fallen column. A ray covers the same mosaic. The blind fish return to the fountain basins, passing through the rooms and courtyards as though nothing has happened.

There may be descendants of the people who built the city, though nothing in them now would be recognizable as human. Perhaps some small, warm-blooded line still shelters beneath the arches. Perhaps the old inhabitants survive only as organisms that have adopted their structures so completely that the distinction no longer matters.

The residents do not preserve the city.

They use it, alter it, feed upon it, hide within it, and grow over it. Their trails polish the stones. Their shells fill the streets. Their bodies block drains and open new currents. They make nurseries in cisterns, dens in sewers, reefs in temples, hunting grounds in plazas. They inherit no names, but they inherit routes.

The city belongs to whatever can live in its pressure and darkness.

And whatever lives there has stopped thinking of the buildings as ruins. To the fish, the city is shelter. To the worms, it is a foundation. To the rays, a resting ground. To the sharks, a maze of scent and vibration. To the kelp, a forest of stone.

Each creature possesses only a fragment of it.

Together, moving through its rooms without knowing they are rooms, they make the drowned city alive.
