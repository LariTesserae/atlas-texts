What lives there is not a species but a multitude.

The first inhabitants are the grazers: narrow-bodied, long-legged animals that move with their heads lowered and their weight carefully distributed. Their hooves have widened into blunt fans, spreading over loose stone instead of sinking into it. They are not graceful, exactly. Grace belongs to open air and firm ground. These creatures are deliberate. Each step is tested, each ledge judged, each descent made with the patient caution of an animal that expects the earth to give way beneath it.

Their coats are the colors of the cavern: ash, wet brown, old ivory, iron-gray. Many carry a permanent dusting of pale grit along their backs. Their eyes are large but not bright, adapted to gathering the weak light rather than creating it. They travel in loose herds, though the herd has no clear boundary. One animal may join another group at a pool, follow it for half a day, and leave without alarm. Their unity is practical. They move together because a hundred bodies find safer routes than one, because predators are less likely to strike at a mass, because the presence of others reveals unstable ground before it collapses.

They graze on the cavern’s thin growth without ever exhausting it. Their mouths are fine and searching, able to strip moss from a damp shelf or take the few yellowed blades from a crack. They do not remain where food is plentiful. They feed, soil the stone, disturb hidden insects, and continue along the worn corridors. The patches of vegetation are maintained by their hunger as much as by water. Nothing is allowed to become a meadow.

Beneath them, beneath the mud and scree, lives another population: the burrowers.

They are small, quick, and difficult to see except when they move. Their bodies are compact, many of them nearly spherical, with digging forelimbs and blunt, sensitive snouts. They make holes in the fresh clay and abandon them before the walls have settled. Some live alone. Others occupy whole pockets of gravel, erupting from the ground in a chain of sudden appearances. Their tunnels are shallow and temporary, less like homes than pauses in the stone. They shelter there from passing feet, lay eggs in the damp, hide food, and vanish through a different opening when the ground begins to tremble.

A few are almost blind. A few have no eyes at all, their faces softened into pale folds around whiskers or vibrating feelers. Others possess enormous black eyes that reflect the dim ceiling-glow in brief, startled sparks. All of them are sensitive to movement. A distant herd can send a warning through the floor long before its bodies appear. When that tremor comes, the gravel flashes with life: small backs, pale claws, mud-colored tails, and mouths opening soundlessly before the animals disappear again.

The pools belong to the amphibians.

They are thick and low, with bodies like wet stone and skin that seems permanently coated in a thin film of water. Some are the size of a hand; others are broad enough to fill a hollow between two shelves. They sit half-submerged, their backs breaking the surface like rounded rocks. Their colors are subdued—black-green, gray, brown with the occasional bruise-colored marking—but their throats and undersides may flare with sudden reds or yellows when they call.

They do not waste movement. Hours pass while one remains still beneath the trembling surface, its eyes just above the water. Then a smaller creature approaches, and the amphibian becomes a hinge of muscle. Its mouth opens, its body lunges, and the pool erupts. Water strikes stone. The prey is gone. Almost immediately the animal settles back into its former shape, as though nothing has happened.

Their young are everywhere, though rarely visible. Transparent strings of eggs cling beneath ledges. Tadpoles cloud shallow puddles, feeding on mineral film and the soft growths around submerged stones. Some pools are nurseries; others are traps. The amphibians themselves do not seem to distinguish between the two.

The ledges are occupied by climbers and clingers. Small, many-limbed animals spread themselves flat against the rock, their feet ending in hooks or soft gripping pads. They feed on mineral crusts, insects, eggs, and one another. They are pale where they live in the open and nearly black where they remain under shelves. Some carry clusters of young along their backs. Others molt in the shelter of cracks, leaving behind translucent skins that catch on the stone and tremble whenever the air moves.

Higher still, the winged inhabitants make the roof seem to breathe.

They are not all bats, though some resemble them: bodies no larger than a fist, membranous wings, delicate faces constantly testing the air. Others have long, jointed wings like folded fans. Some glide without sound until they land; some beat heavily enough to stir dust from the ceiling. They navigate by cries that return from the rock in layers. Their voices are too fast and numerous to be understood individually, but the cavern is mapped inside them. They know where the roof rises, where the walls narrow, where a flock can turn, where a predator waits on a shelf, where water collects in the dark.

Their colonies are not permanent in the surface sense. They gather where insects gather, where ledges remain dry, where the air is warmest by a degree too small for anything else to notice. A whole roof may be empty one hour and crowded the next. They settle in dense, restless masses, folding over one another until the stone seems furred. Then a single disturbance passes through them, and the colony lifts away as a dark sheet.

When they descend, the smaller life hides. Grazers bunch together. Burrowers vanish. Even the predators watch the air.

The predators are not rare enough to be legends.

The low cats are the most recognizable. They have elongated bodies and broad, splayed paws, their joints built for sudden traction on rubble. Their fur is short and dull, patterned in broken bands that dissolve against the mineral ridges. They do not stalk with theatrical patience. They lie in plain sight and allow the moving population to come near. Their ears turn independently toward every sound. Their whiskers are long, constantly quivering, able to read the disturbed air around a passing body.

When they run, they run only as long as necessary. The attack is a violent interruption in the cavern’s rhythm: a burst of claws, hooves, dust, and shrill calls. Then it ends. The cat feeds quickly or is driven away by larger bodies, and the herd resumes its passage around the disturbance.

Other hunters are more difficult to recognize as animals. They stand among the ridges on too many narrow limbs, pale joints folded close to their bodies. Their stillness is so complete that mineral growths form on them. They hunt by vibration more than sight. A footfall, a scrape, the small concussion of a burrower breaking through the gravel—these are enough. The hunter unfolds with terrifying speed, crossing several body lengths in a single movement, then becomes still again.

There are heavy predators that do not hide at all. They patrol the borders of the herds with blunt heads lowered, feeding on injured animals, young animals, and whatever cannot keep pace. Their jaws are built to crush shells and bone. Their shoulders bear old scars filled with white mineral dust. They are followed by scavengers: small long-nosed creatures, pale beetlelike things, and winged animals that settle in the wake of violence before the blood has finished spreading.

Nothing dead remains private for long.

The scavengers are among the most constant inhabitants. They live in cracks, beneath shelves, inside abandoned burrows, and under the shallow edges of pools. Some are nearly microscopic, making a dark film over exposed flesh. Others are large enough to drag a limb away. They strip the dead until only pale fragments remain, then carry those fragments into the damp. Bones vanish into crevices. Fur becomes nesting material. Tendons feed larvae. Even the mineral content of a body returns quickly to the pools and mud.

Because of them, death does not accumulate. It appears, is briefly surrounded, and disappears.

There are also animals that seem to live on the cavern itself. They scrape the softest mineral films from the walls. They drink from fresh fractures. They lick the salt from damp stone and leave narrow polished marks behind. Their teeth and tongues are worn down by the work, but the rock continues to expose new surfaces faster than they can consume them. They are among the few creatures that move vertically, climbing the ribs and walls with slow certainty.

Some carry lights.

Not true lights, perhaps, but small living glimmers: cold blue points beneath the skin, faint green membranes around the eyes, clusters of luminous mites cultivated in fur or slime. The glow is too weak to illuminate the way. It serves instead as a signal, a warning, a lure, or a means of finding one another in the dark. A single creature passing beneath a ledge may leave behind a brief constellation of blue-green marks. Then the marks close, and the stone is dark again.

No inhabitant understands the whole basin. None needs to.

The grazers know water, food, safe passage, and the approach of heavy feet. The burrowers know the structure of the upper gravel. The amphibians know the pools by their chemical differences. The winged creatures know the airspace. The predators know where movement narrows and where the herds become careless. Each species occupies a portion of the cavern’s knowledge, and their overlapping habits form something larger than instinct without ever becoming intention.

They have no monuments because their bodies are the record.

The lanes across the floor are made by generations of hooves. The smooth places beside the pools are made by amphibian bellies. The shelves darkened by droppings and shed skins belong to the winged colonies. The deepened cracks, the disturbed scree, the spread of moss, and the whitening of bones all testify to continuous use. Nothing is built, yet everything bears the pressure of habitation.

They reproduce without display of abundance because abundance is already present. Eggs are laid in water, in mud, beneath bodies, inside warm fractures. Young creatures emerge small and immediately endangered. Many are eaten. Enough survive. The cavern does not favor any one lineage for long. A predator grows numerous, prey thins, and the predator becomes lean. A pool dries slightly, amphibians crowd the next one, insects multiply in the abandoned mud, and the hunters follow. Equilibrium is not peace. It is a ceaseless correction.

The animals do not seem imprisoned by the walls. They simply do not possess the idea of elsewhere.

A herd may move for days through connected galleries and still never reach an opening. A winged creature may circle the highest vault until its cries return from every direction. A predator may follow the migration corridor from one end of the basin to the other, knowing every ridge and hollow, yet never cross into the upper country. Their world is enclosed, but not experienced as confinement. The boundaries are as natural to them as gravity.

They are creatures of interrupted light, of water that has not yet decided what shape to take, of stone still carrying the violence of its making. Their lives are raw-edged but not primitive. They have adapted not by transforming the cavern, but by fitting themselves into its unfinished forms.

The place is theirs because they are in it everywhere: under the grit, in the pools, along the shelves, above the unseen roofline, moving through the channels and gathering at the mineral ridges. Their calls become the cavern’s voice. Their tracks become its temporary writing. Their feeding, mating, hiding, hunting, and dying keep the new country from remaining empty.

They do not tame the darkness.

They fill it.
