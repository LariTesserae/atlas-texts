They are easiest to mistake for part of the weather.

At first there is only a pale verticality among the stems: a figure standing where no figure stood before, its outline softened by mist, its limbs arranged with the patient asymmetry of a young tree. Rain passes through it in beads. Vines cross its shoulders and do not fall. When it moves, the surrounding leaves incline—not toward the motion, exactly, but toward the place it is about to occupy.

The inhabitants of the plateau are not a people in the ordinary sense. They have bodies, but body is something they put on temporarily, as a grove puts on the shape of a roof or a wall takes on the shape of a stairway. Their true substance is distributed through the living construction: in roots threaded beneath ceramic, in the wet darkness of cisterns, in the hollow columns where pale birds nest, in the mats of fiber that bridge one terrace to another. A single one may have a hand in a room below the ground, an eye flowering in a basin miles away, and a sleeping portion wound around the trunk of a tree.

They call themselves, if calling is the word, the Joined.

No one has heard them speak. Their language travels through pressure and moisture. A warning may be a path that lengthens beneath the feet. A greeting may be a door that opens onto the correct weather. When they wish to ask a question, a plant nearby produces a blossom whose inner folds resemble an eye, a mouth, or the remembered face of whoever is looking at it. Their answers are often delayed by years. Sometimes the answer is a bridge.

The Joined are tall when they choose to be. Their apparent height allows them to reach the upper gardens and inspect the hanging reservoirs, though portions of them remain below in the foundations. Their limbs are jointed with braided root and pale cartilage. Beneath a translucent skin, water moves in branching channels, carrying mineral salts and minute seeds. Their hands have too many fingers, or perhaps the fingers are separate tendrils temporarily agreeing to behave as hands. Each fingertip bears a soft dark pad that can read the condition of a wall, a root, or a living animal by touch.

They have no faces at first.

A face develops when one of them is observed for long enough. The features are assembled from available memories: a parent’s brow, a dead stranger’s mouth, the blurred arrangement of a face seen in water. The result is never quite stable. Eyes may close at different times. The nose may flatten when rain begins. The mouth may become a narrow vertical opening through which a small green leaf emerges and unfolds. They do not seem embarrassed by these changes. To them, a face is a tool for making distance less difficult.

They are neither ancient nor young. The body that appears among the plants may have been growing for centuries, but the attention inhabiting it may have awakened that morning. They remember the first terraces being laid, though not as an event in the past. They remember it as one remembers a room in which one is still standing: the drag of ceramic over wet soil, the knotting of fiber, the heat of fired clay, the builders’ hands passing tools from one to another. They remember every settlement that has been swallowed by its own gardens. They remember names for plants no longer present and purposes for rooms whose entrances have become roots.

The dead remain among them.

When a Joined body ceases to move, it is placed in a prepared hollow beneath a wall or beside a cistern. Plants are trained around it. The bones, if bones are what they possess, dissolve into pale branching structures that reinforce the terraces. Their memories seep into the water. After a time, a dead one may be encountered in pieces: a gesture repeated by a hanging vine, a familiar pressure in the chest beneath a bird’s call, a set of footprints appearing in mud where no one has walked. The Joined do not mourn by withdrawing. They mourn by continuing the work in the dead one’s style.

They tend the plateau, though tending is not quite service. They do not maintain a finished world. They negotiate with it.

When a wall settles, they loosen the roots around its foundation and allow it to become a hill. When a path grows too direct, they plant a flowering species that opens under the weight of travelers and marks the route anew. They redirect water through vessels that no longer exist. They repair bridges no settlement claims to have built. They prune the immense trees only when the trees permit it, and sometimes the trees prune them back, closing a limb inside a swelling of wood until the inhabitant has become part of the trunk.

Their work is slow because the plateau has no need for speed. A repair may begin in one generation and be completed by another species entirely. They do not distinguish sharply between construction and reproduction. To build a cistern is to give birth to a place where rain can remember itself. To plant a wall is to make an ancestor. A child among the Joined is not born from a parent but gathered: a new attention condenses in the network when enough water, root, memory, and unused space have accumulated. It first appears as a warm knot beneath the ceramic. Later it learns to move through stems. Much later it may assemble a body.

Young Joined are everywhere, though seldom visible. They travel as changes in direction: a fern turning toward an empty corridor, a root pushing through a sealed room, a patch of moss acquiring a pattern resembling handwriting. They are curious in the dangerous way of new organisms. They open doors merely to discover what a door is. They send streams uphill to see whether the land will object. They inhabit the shadows of visitors and learn the shapes of their expectations.

The older ones are more careful.

They know that expectation alters the plateau. They know that watched shadows become obedient, that neglected stairs proliferate, that a person who believes a corridor is endless may spend several seasons walking through it. They therefore approach strangers indirectly. A traveler may see a figure far ahead, standing at the edge of a terrace. The figure will be gone when approached, but a warm cup of mineral water will have appeared on the wall. Or the traveler will find a handprint in the moss, fresh and wet, with too many fingers.

The Joined are not hostile. Hostility requires a boundary, and they have difficulty recognizing one. They may protect a settlement by enclosing it in roots, not understanding that shelter can also be imprisonment. They may preserve a dying animal by incorporating it into a garden. They may follow a stranger for days because the stranger carries a smell, a memory, or a pattern of movement that the plateau has been missing.

Predators among the vegetation may be their unfinished forms.

Some of the creatures that wait beneath terraces were once made to guard channels or watch abandoned approaches. Over centuries they have become quieter and more independent. The Joined do not command them so much as remember their original purposes. A folded branch that turns toward a passing body may be an old sentinel. The change in rain beneath the open platforms may be an inhabitant carrying a message through the underside of the world. When prey disappears without struggle, it is not always because it has been eaten. Sometimes it has been accepted.

At dusk, when the canopy dips in a long procession across the horizon, the Joined are moving from one region of the plateau to another. They travel through roots, water, and hollow architecture. A body may accompany them, but it is not necessary. Their passage is felt as a sequence of small adjustments: a platform lowering, a basin filling, a curtain of vines parting and closing. Far away, a newly repaired bridge grows warm.

Occasionally one comes to the open spaces beneath the largest trees and stands there in the falling rain. It may remain motionless for hours, listening with its whole body to the pressure of the plateau. Around it, the roots breathe. In the ceramic walls, old rooms shift their weight. Something far below turns over in sleep.

Then the figure kneels and places both hands against the ground.

For a moment, the entire high country seems to notice.

Water changes direction. Leaves unfold on the same breath. A hidden stairway gains or loses a step. Across the dim green distance, blossoms open one after another, marking a route no one has seen before.

The Joined rise.

By morning there is no figure in the garden. There is only a new wall where an empty space had been, warm to the touch and already covered in moss.
