The inhabitants are not quite animals, though they possess bodies that can leave one place and enter another.

They grow.

At first glance, they might be mistaken for knots in the architecture: upright bundles of dark stems, jointed and slightly bent beneath the weight of their own leaf-crowns. They are usually smaller than a human, though some old ones become tall and columnar, with several shoulders branching from a central trunk. Their surfaces are green-black, ridged, and damp. Along the throat and inner arms runs a fine network of pale vessels, visible only when they are warm or agitated. Their faces are not faces in the familiar sense. They have shallow hollows where eyes might be, covered by translucent membranes, and narrow openings that close and open like the vents of a leaf.

They do not speak often.

A conversation between them may consist of a stem laid against a wall, a hand pressed into moss, a change in skin color, or the release of a scent too subtle for an outsider to notice. The basin carries these signals everywhere. A warning given at one end can arrive at the other as a tightening of roots beneath the floor. A birth, a death, a sudden fear: all of them pass through the settlement as changes in moisture and pressure.

Yet they are not a single mind. Not entirely.

Each inhabitant has a private center, a knot of dense living tissue held somewhere behind the ribs, and this center gives them a particular rhythm. One may be quick to open, another slow and heavy; one may prefer dry upper galleries, another the black wetness near the pool. They remember differently. They dream differently. They can disagree.

But their individuality is porous. When they sleep, their roots enter the walls and mingle with the basin’s cords. In this state they lose the hard boundary between self and settlement. They feel the thirst of distant leaves, the strain in a bridge, the burrowing movements beneath the platforms. A person who has spent too long disconnected becomes pale and brittle, as though solitude were a form of dehydration.

Their young do not come from two bodies.

A child begins as a bud in a communal nursery, a pale, sealed swelling among warm layers of root. Several inhabitants tend it, feeding it stored sugars through their palms and singing—not with voices, but with the minute vibrations of their bodies against the nursery wall. When the bud opens, the new being is soft, nearly colorless, and unable to stand. It remains attached to the nursery for many seasons, learning the settlement through the roots before it learns to walk through the paths.

The child’s first independent movement is treated as a serious event. No one applauds. The surrounding growth simply makes room.

They have limbs, but they use them sparingly. Their hands end in many fine, flexible digits suited to separating leaves, guiding tendrils, and feeling for damage beneath a wall’s outer skin. They can climb without effort, flatten themselves against vertical growth, and pass through doors that would seem too narrow for them. Sometimes they disappear entirely into a house, entering through a seam in the stems and traveling inside the living walls.

They are the settlement’s keepers, though they would not describe their work that way. There is no profession of gardener because gardening is not distinct from eating, healing, building, or worship.

They inspect the routes of water. They press new roots into cracks. They bind injured stems with living fibers and feed weak sections from their own stored warmth. When a roof begins to fail, several of them may sit beneath it for days, bodies touching the supporting columns while their skins darken and the leaves overhead slowly reorient themselves. Their labor leaves no finished object behind. It leaves a structure that has chosen, or been persuaded, to continue.

They eat by drawing nourishment through the soles of their feet and the palms of their hands. At certain times they consume soft growths, mineral-rich pods, and the pale insects that shelter in the leaves, but food is less a meal than an exchange. The basin feeds them because their bodies are among the basin’s active organs. In return, they exhale warmth into cold chambers, carry spores from one structure to another, and return their dead to the roots.

Death is gradual and difficult to locate.

An old inhabitant may become less mobile, then more deeply attached. Their legs stiffen into supports. Their arms thicken and send out tendrils. Others visit them, not to say farewell, but to learn which memories have begun to pass into the walls. Eventually the person is no longer seen moving along the paths. A column appears where they once sat. A new bridge arches over an alley. A pale lamp-flower opens in a place that had always been dark.

The inhabitants still call such beings by their names.

Their memories are held in the basin’s growth, but imperfectly. A gesture may survive without its meaning. A scent may awaken a grief no living person can explain. The oldest roots contain impressions of faces that no one has ever seen, and of journeys beyond the shelf, down through warmer forests and into lands where stone has been softened by centuries of rain.

The central pool is where the settlement’s separate selves come closest to becoming one.

They gather there during heavy weather and during flowering, kneeling around the black water with their roots extended into the white descending threads. The pool gives them access to the oldest currents beneath the basin. Through it, they sense changes in the mountain: pressure building under the shelf, hidden water moving through the rock, the slow mineral heat far below.

They also see things in its reflection.

The inhabitants do not agree on what these visions are. Some believe the pool reflects possible growth—the town as it may become if left undisturbed for another hundred years. Others say it remembers places the basin once touched, or places it will eventually reach. A few insist that the tall shape moving behind the reflected leaves is an ancestor, not dead but distributed, still traveling through the underground cords.

When the shape appears, the inhabitants become very still.

They do not pursue it. They do not call out. They watch the water until the image is gone, then return to their work with a new attentiveness, as if the settlement has received an instruction too deep for any single body to understand.

They have no word for “outside” that means merely elsewhere. Outside is exposure, severance, unshared water. It is the condition of being cut from the living exchange.

Nevertheless, some leave.

These wanderers are grown with lighter bodies and longer limbs, able to cross bare shelves where the settlement’s ordinary inhabitants would dry and crack. They carry packets of seed, living cords wrapped around their torsos, and return after long absences changed by weather and distance. Sometimes they come back with mineral dust in their membranes or the scent of distant rain. Sometimes they do not return, and the basin sends roots toward the paths they once used, as though extending a hand.

The people of the settlement do not fear the mountain in the way a fragile creature might fear it. They are part of its recent upheaval: a green response to broken stone, a body grown over a wound. But they know the shelf is unfinished. The cliffs may split again. The basin may drain, freeze, or tilt into the valley.

Their answer is not to build something that will withstand change.

Their answer is to remain connected enough to change with it.

If the rock rises, they will raise their walls. If a channel opens beneath the pool, they will follow the water downward. If the basin collapses, the inhabitants may survive as mats, cords, spores, and buried knots, waiting in the dark for another surface to become possible.

And if, years later, something climbs into the new hollow and finds the wet stone covered in green-black growth, it may see no ruins there—only a settlement that has become too old to distinguish from the living mountain.
