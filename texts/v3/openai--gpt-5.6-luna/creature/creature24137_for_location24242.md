What lives in the basin has no word for animal.

The distinction would be useless there. The pale bodies are not separate from the pipes, the walls, or the engines that sustain them. They belong to the same arrangement. A creature may spend part of its existence clinging beneath a conveyor, part of it dissolved into a drainage system, and part of it hardened into a wall. None of these states is more alive than the others.

The smallest are the wall-scrapers. They resemble pale fingers with too many joints, or narrow plates of wet ivory fitted with rows of delicate mouths. They travel in straight lines over the stone, cleaning away the films left by condensation. Their bodies flex only where necessary. When they reach an obstruction, they do not go around it; they flatten until they can pass beneath. Their movements are so exact that the mineral surfaces seem to draw them onward.

They gather in the oldest channels, where the stone is darkest and most polished. There they feed on salt, rust, and the transparent residue that gathers beneath warm pipes. Their waste is nearly indistinguishable from dust. Their numbers are controlled by the amount of moisture in the walls. After rain, they appear by the thousands. During dry weeks, they become thin and nearly invisible, retreating into seams no wider than a thread.

Some live inside the machinery.

They occupy the blind spaces behind pistons, the housings of gears, the long interiors of cooling pipes. They have no eyes and no need of them. Pressure tells them where they are. Heat tells them what surrounds them. Vibration passes through their bodies as clearly as speech might pass through a human ear.

When a pump begins its downward stroke, the animals inside it contract. When it rises, they open again. Their pale organs pulse with the rhythm of the mechanism. After years of this, they become difficult to distinguish from components. A maintenance hatch may reveal a nest of translucent bodies arranged around an axle, each one turning slightly with the shaft. A furnace door may open on a curtain of blind, jointed animals, all waiting for the temperature to become acceptable before returning to the dark.

They do not flee from danger. They enter the correct configuration.

Near the foundries live the heat-feeders, long creatures with soft bodies and ringed mouths. They lie in heaps beneath grilles, though the heaps are never random. Each animal touches another at precisely two points, forming lattices that conduct warmth from the furnace walls into the colder corridors. Their hides are gray when cool, blue-white when heated. In the deepest chambers they become almost transparent, their insides visible as slow-moving threads of light.

The larger ones are called—if they are called anything—the bridges.

They rest across roofs and gantries, sometimes mistaken for abandoned hoses or collapsed cables. Their bodies are broad and flattened, their skin folded into thick mineral-colored ridges. They may remain in one position for decades. Dust settles on them. Pipes are fastened to their backs. Small creatures live in the hollows between their folds, where the body heat is constant and the vibration is gentle.

When a bridge-creature moves, the entire district notices.

It does not rise. It shortens.

One end draws inward, then the other, and the long body pulls itself across the structure in a series of slow contractions. Anything attached to it is carried along: nests, rust flakes, pipes, sometimes whole sections of scaffolding. The machinery accommodates the movement without stopping. Conveyors alter their speed. Doors remain open. Water is diverted through alternate channels. The settlement has known these creatures longer than it has known its own architecture.

Some of the largest are not merely inhabitants but organs of the basin. They lie beneath the streets, their bodies filling abandoned tunnels. Pumps draw through them. Warmth passes from them into the stone. Their breathing creates pressure changes that help move water through the lower channels. When one dies—or enters whatever condition replaces death—its body is quarried, cut, and fitted into foundations. The walls built from them retain a faint internal warmth for centuries.

There is no cruelty in the way the animals use one another.

A small creature enters a larger one through a soft seam beneath the jaw. The larger closes around it. Hours later, the body divides along a line that was not previously visible, and two animals emerge, equal in size and equally complete. Elsewhere, several animals gather around one that has become unresponsive. They open it carefully. One group removes the mineral-bearing plates. Another takes the warm organs. A third collects the clear fluids and carries them to a condensation channel. The process is quiet, efficient, and without urgency.

Nothing is wasted because nothing is considered separate.

The animals do not communicate by sound, though the basin is full of their clicking. The clicking comes from feeding: countless mouths working against stone, scraping the surfaces clean. Their actual messages pass through contact and vibration. A change in the rhythm of a wall can summon them from several streets away. A pipe struck three times may tell a colony that water is coming. A long tremor beneath the floor may cause every animal in a district to flatten, not from fear, but because the presses are about to descend.

They recognize one another by rhythm.

A creature displaced from its district may follow a familiar vibration for a while, crossing streets and climbing walls with increasing uncertainty. If it cannot find the proper sequence, it slows. Its limbs stiffen. The clear fluid beneath its body clouds. Within minutes it begins to take on the color of the surrounding stone.

The inhabitants do not migrate. They are made for places.

One species belongs to the undersides of bridges, another to the hot sides of furnaces, another to the wet black hollows where rainwater remains for three days before sinking away. A body from the upper foundries placed in the lower pumping district would not be an invader. It would be an unfinished object. The settlement would absorb it, sort it, and return its matter to the proper channels.

At night, beneath the yellow lamps, their order becomes visible.

They stand at the corners of pools. They line the drainage gutters. They attach themselves at measured intervals to the walls, each pale body separated from the next by the same narrow distance. Some face the machinery. Some face the cliffs. Some face one another across the streets, touching the ground with their lower limbs while their upper membranes open toward the sound.

Those membranes are thin and translucent. When the presses strike, they tremble. When generators deepen their hum, the animals glow through the ribs with a faint internal blue. The light is not produced for illumination. It is the visible remainder of listening.

Perhaps the animals do not experience the basin as a place.

Perhaps there is no wall, no factory, no street, no distinction between stone and body. There is only pressure, heat, moisture, vibration, and the constant fulfillment of one condition by another. The creature beneath the press is not hiding from it. The creature in the pipe is not traveling through it. The creature that becomes masonry has not ceased to belong to the living world.

They are the basin’s persistence given shape.

They keep the channels open. They keep the machines clean. They gather what condenses, consume what flakes away, and return themselves to the surfaces that support them. Their bodies are pale against the black stone, but they are not intruders in the darkness. They are the visible skin of its processes.

And beneath the immense mechanical roar, where the pumps thud and the flywheels turn, their mouths continue working.

Not feeding.

Maintaining.
