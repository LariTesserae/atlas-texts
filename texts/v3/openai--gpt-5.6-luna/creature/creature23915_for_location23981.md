What lives there has learned not to oppose the water.

The smallest inhabitants are almost never seen. They live inside the root mats and under the moss, pale and soft-bodied, with no clear distinction between limb, gill, and sensory organ. They do not burrow so much as persuade the vegetation to open. A passage forms before them and closes behind them. When they feed, they remove single leaves from a living carpet, never enough to make a mark. Their nests are circles of shells, polished stones, and shed membranes arranged in patterns that may be maps, offerings, or simply the remains of an order they have forgotten how to explain.

They have no voices. They communicate by pressure.

A touch to the root sends a small change through the surrounding growth. A tremor crosses a wall. A hollow fills or empties. For them, distance is not measured in meters but in how long it takes a disturbance to travel. A message may pass through a forest over the course of a day, reaching bodies that have been waiting for it beneath the stone. By then, the sender may have dissolved into the same soft darkness that carried the signal.

There are larger things in the valleys.

They resemble long, jointless animals only from a distance, and even then their boundaries are uncertain. Their bodies are veiled in drifting tissue, transparent in some places and clouded in others, so that one sees the sediment inside them as clearly as the organs. They move without swimming. The currents take them, and they alter the currents slightly as they pass. Some are grazers, though what they consume is not vegetation but mineral bloom: amber shelves, green crusts, the slow flowers that emerge from warm fissures. Others carry colonies of smaller life in their folds. They are less individuals than traveling habitats.

When one crosses above a ridge, the forest bends beneath it.

The inhabitants of the upper terraces are more difficult to classify. They are shaped enough like people to have hands, faces, and a way of standing, but none of these features have remained fixed. Their bodies have adapted to the unstable boundary between water and vapor. Skin, where they possess it, is thin and darkly translucent. Their ribs open and close like the slats of a bellows. Hair grows as fine rootlets and anchors them during strong currents. Some have eyes. Some have only pale patches that brighten when another creature approaches. A few carry their senses outside themselves in trailing filaments, tasting changes in pressure and mineral content long before their bodies enter a place.

They are not descended from the builders in any simple way.

Perhaps the builders changed themselves to survive the deepening water. Perhaps the water changed them. Perhaps the builders died, and these beings are what remained when their houses, tools, memories, and bodies were all taken up by the same patient ecology.

They inhabit the ruins without occupying them.

A doorway is useful because currents behave differently near it. A tower may be inhabited by a colony of bodies that never meet one another, each living in a separate room and sensing the others through the stone. The low walls serve as instruments. Residents lay themselves against the fitted blocks and listen to pressure moving through the foundations. A bridge, even an incomplete one, may be treated as a sleeping place if its absence creates a stable eddy. The houses around the moss-covered square are not homes in the old sense. They are chambers for slowing down.

The people of the terraces spend much of their lives motionless.

They attach themselves to rock or root and allow water to pass through their bodies. During these long stillnesses they absorb dissolved minerals, exchange spores, and remember. Memory is not stored in words. It accumulates as changes in texture: a groove worn into a wall, a band of different-colored moss, a deliberate arrangement of shells beneath a ledge. To remember a person is to maintain the place where that person once rested. To forget is not to lose an image but to allow the current to take the shape away.

They may spend centuries learning one another.

A greeting can last longer than a human lifetime. Two beings meet at the edge of a ridge and settle opposite each other, their sensory filaments touching the rock. Each sends a minute pulse through the stone. The other receives it, alters its own pressure, and waits. Between one response and the next, entire forests may flower and collapse. A child may grow into a mature form without ever leaving the conversation.

Their young are not born.

They begin as concentrations of living material in sheltered hollows: a soft knot of root, membrane, and mineral dust. The parents—or whatever relation serves in their place—feed the hollow with warmth and patterned vibration. Over many years the knot takes on a direction. It grows toward one current, then another, developing a body by choosing what to resist. Some young emerge with recognizable forms. Others remain attached to the rock and become part of the local landscape, conscious perhaps, but without any wish to separate from it.

There is no shame in becoming terrain.

Indeed, the oldest inhabitants are difficult to distinguish from the oldest plants. They are wide, dark, and deeply rooted, with a few openings in their surfaces where they sense the passing water. Their thoughts move slowly through fungal threads and mineral channels. A single one may occupy an entire terrace. It may remember the construction of a wall, the first arrival of the warm currents, the gradual drowning of the lowlands. Its memories do not appear as scenes. They surface as changes in growth, as a sudden flowering beneath an arch, as a region where no spores will settle.

The forests defer to these beings.

Not consciously, perhaps. A current divides around them. Root mats grow in rings. Animals avoid certain patches of sediment. Ruins become covered and uncovered according to their sleep. When one of the great rooted inhabitants dies, the event may last a thousand years. Its body becomes a hill. Its hill becomes a valley. Its valley fills with pale reeds. At the end, only a particular absence remains: a place where the water circulates differently, where shadows gather in the wrong direction.

The largest creatures are nearly indistinguishable from weather.

They move through the drowned valleys in groups so sparse that a generation may see only one. Their bodies are immense, transparent, and difficult to hold in the eye. They carry entire communities of symbiotic life beneath their skins. Light passes through their fins and reveals slow galaxies of spores. They feed on the warm mineral plumes rising from the deep, opening themselves above the fissures until the amber water has passed through them.

When they migrate, valleys change.

Their pressure wakes dormant blooms. Their shadows alter the growth of forests. The sudden displacement of water may push a ridge upward by a few centimeters or cause a suspended lake to spill sideways. The inhabitants of the terraces do not hunt them. They mark their passage with fresh arrangements of stone and then wait for the resulting changes. To these people, the great creatures are not gods, exactly. They are occasions.

Nothing here seems to worship in the old manner.

There are no congregations, no fixed doctrines, no sacred objects that remain separate from the world. Instead, there are practices of attention. A body lies beneath a roof where the water is still. A hand is placed against a wall. A chorus of pressures is sent into a root system and allowed to return altered. The participants do not ask for rescue, prosperity, or an afterlife. They listen for the deep tone that passes through stone and living tissue alike.

When it comes, everything pauses.

The small animals stop feeding. The vast things in the valleys become still. Fronds cease their slow breathing. The people of the ruins flatten themselves against the ground, opening every sensory organ they possess.

The tone is not a command. It contains no language.

It is the sound—or perhaps the memory—of the world changing beneath them.

Afterward, they rise carefully and inspect what has moved. They find a stairway leading somewhere new. A familiar current has reversed. A dead ancestor has begun to flower. A distant ruin is suddenly visible through water that was opaque the day before. They do not ask whether the alteration was good. Good and bad are distinctions belonging to stable landscapes.

They ask only what must be inhabited now.

And then they begin again: rooting themselves in the changed stone, tending the living walls, following the slow messages through the moss, and becoming, little by little, part of the place that is still becoming them.
