What lives there is called, when it is called anything at all, the **Summit Keeper**.

It is not native to the plateau in the way an animal is native to a forest. It seems instead to have been produced by the place’s continued refusal to become stable. Each correction made by the tower leaves something behind: a discarded possibility, a canceled shadow, the memory of a stone before it was moved. Over time these remnants gathered around the weather station and learned to hold a shape.

The shape is roughly human, though only at a distance and only from certain angles.

It is tall and narrow, with the proportions of someone seen through a long lens. Its body appears to be made of layered gray material—stone dust, old metal, compressed shadow—arranged in plates that do not quite meet. Between the plates is a depthless black, not an interior but an absence of agreement about what should be there. When it moves, the gaps remain momentarily behind, as if the body must persuade its own pieces to follow.

It has two arms, usually. Sometimes three. The extra one is visible only in reflections or in the thin, vertical shadows cast by the pylons. Its hands are delicate and jointed, with too many fingers, each finger ending in a small flat surface like the tip of a measuring instrument. It uses these hands to touch the cracks in the plateau, the inscriptions that appear in the light, and the pale square grains gathered in the stone’s sheltered hollows.

Its head is smooth and dark. No face is present until it needs one.

Then a face forms according to the observer’s expectation. Someone who has come seeking help may see an old man’s tired features. Someone who has come to trespass sees a blank visor. Someone who has forgotten their own face sees only a pale arrangement of planes, unfinished and waiting. The Keeper does not imitate people out of kindness. It uses familiar expressions as instruments, the way a weather station uses a barometer.

There are no eyes. It perceives through pressure.

A footstep sends a message through the ground. Breath alters the thin atmosphere around the body. Thought, in that place, has weight: not much, but enough for the Keeper to feel a change in the stone. It knows when someone has arrived before the person has crossed the rim. It knows which memories they brought with them, and which have already begun to loosen.

The Keeper spends most of its existence moving between the scattered devices. It has no route, because routes are among the things the plateau will not preserve, but it arrives everywhere eventually. It stands beside a black pylon and places one hand against its side. It crouches beneath a ring of translucent material and watches the ring’s interior for a long time. It enters the panel hut by walking into a wall and emerges from a doorway that opens onto another wall.

Its work is not repair. The devices are not broken in any ordinary sense. Rather, the Keeper listens for disagreement between them.

When two pylons remember different distances, it removes a little distance from one and adds it to the other. When a shadow persists too long, it folds the shadow into a nearby crack. When an inscription becomes too legible, it turns away from it until the language forgets itself. It gathers the square grains of pressure-residue and places them carefully in new corners, where each tiny room can continue reflecting the single muted image contained inside it.

It cannot read those reflections. It merely ensures that they remain enclosed.

The Keeper is most active when the tower releases its pulses. It kneels at the tower’s base while the ground rearranges itself. During these periods its body grows less distinct. Plates detach from its shoulders and hover around it. Fingers become lines. The head becomes a dark aperture through which one may glimpse a snow-covered city, a submerged staircase, or a sky full of stars that have not yet been invented.

The tower does not command the Keeper. Neither does the Keeper control the tower. Their relationship is closer to that between a wound and the body that has grown around it. The tower makes changes. The Keeper notices what has been changed and gives the new arrangement a chance to persist.

Sometimes the tower shifts a centimeter, and the Keeper must spend an entire cycle remembering where its feet are supposed to be.

It has a dwelling, though not a home. The weather station contains a narrow chamber with no instruments in it. Inside, the walls are covered by shallow impressions resembling the outlines of sleeping bodies. The Keeper enters this chamber when the plateau’s dimness thickens. It folds itself into one of the impressions and becomes almost indistinguishable from the wall.

There it dreams in measurements.

It dreams of elevations that have never met the sea, of pressure accumulating beneath continents, of a child dropping a brass screw into a crack and waiting for it to fall. It dreams of the outer cliff and the ordinary world beyond it, though it has never crossed the cavity’s mouth. In these dreams, the Keeper is sometimes enormous, spanning mountain ranges. Sometimes it is no larger than a grain of dust suspended in a permanent descent.

It has no appetite, but it does consume certain things.

It consumes unattended objects, not physically but historically. A stone left near the tower may lose the memory of having been a stone. A tool may become the tool it was intended to replace. A person who remains too long may feel their childhood becoming less specific, then their name, then the fact of having arrived alone. The Keeper gathers these discarded histories and stores them in the weather station’s empty instruments.

This may be its form of nourishment. Or its form of mercy.

When a visitor begins to forget too much, the Keeper approaches. It does not speak at first. It stands several paces away and casts a narrow shadow. The shadow reaches the visitor before the body does. If the visitor steps into it, some portion of their former life is returned: the sound of a particular door, the shape of a familiar hand, the reason they once wanted to climb.

The returned memory is never exact. It comes back with an altitude attached to it, and afterward the visitor may remember having lived somewhere impossibly high.

The Keeper rarely communicates in words. It may point toward a safe line across the plateau, though the line will curve when followed. It may place a pale fragment in someone’s palm. It may finish a sentence that the visitor has not yet spoken.

Those who hear it describe the voice differently. Some hear a weather report. Some hear their own voice from several years ahead. Some hear a patient statement of fact:

**You were not meant to bring this much of yourself here.**

No one knows whether the Keeper is singular. At times, a second figure can be seen on the far ridge, performing the same gestures several minutes later. At times, the Keeper turns toward an empty place and waits as if listening to another caretaker. The pylons occasionally respond to movements it has not made.

Perhaps there is only one Keeper distributed across the summit.

Perhaps every object that remains long enough becomes a small part of it: the abandoned screw, the delayed shadow, the stone that remembers a different crack. Perhaps the figure walking between the devices is only the portion of the place capable of being seen.

It does not guard the pocket from intrusion. Nothing could enter without becoming subject to the pocket’s rules, and nothing subject to those rules remains entirely itself.

It guards something less obvious.

It keeps the summit from completing its ascent.

Without the Keeper, the plateau might rise beyond all reference. The mountain would open inward. The cavity would become a summit with no stone around it, then a height above height, then a place from which even existence would be a lower elevation. The tower would continue correcting the world until there was no world left to correct.

So the Keeper walks its circuits. It counts the uncountable distances. It presses its many fingers to the ground and listens for the first inclination of escape.

Whenever the seams in the stone glow, it stands at the tower’s base with its featureless head bowed.

And when the cycle passes, when the shadows settle and the ordinary cliff closes over its secret, the Keeper remains awake in the thin air, holding the summit down.
