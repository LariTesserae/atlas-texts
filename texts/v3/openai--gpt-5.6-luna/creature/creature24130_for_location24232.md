They are people of the near-dark, though they do not think of themselves as dark-dwellers.

They call themselves the **Hearthbound**, which is less a name than a statement of fact. A hearth is not merely a place where food is cooked. It is a measure of a household’s warmth, a sign that the roof above it has held, a promise that someone will still be awake when the lamps burn low. To be hearthbound is to belong somewhere beneath stone, among other breathing bodies, in a place that may shift but has not yet rejected you.

They are not pale in the manner of creatures that have never known the sun. Their skin carries the colors of the surface—brown, copper, umber, olive, gray—but those colors are softened by generations spent under rock and lamp smoke. Their eyes are large, not unnaturally so, but accustomed to distinguishing useful things from very little light: the dark wetness of a new leak, the black line of a hairline fracture, the difference between a person standing still and a doorway with cloth hanging across it.

They are excellent at seeing movement in poor illumination and poor at tolerating sudden brightness. A shaft opened after a long closure can make grown people turn away, covering their faces with both hands. Children are taught never to look directly upward when a surface hatch is unsealed. The sun is not worshiped exactly, but it is treated with the uneasy respect given to a powerful animal.

Their hands reveal where they come from.

The palms are thickened by stone, rope, baskets, and wooden tools. Fingers are often stained with clay, soot, or the greenish residue of exposed minerals. Almost everyone carries small scars: a cut from a shard, a burn from fire-setting, a crushed nail, the pale line left by a falling brace. The scars are not admired, but neither are they hidden. A person who has never been injured in the work of the cavern is considered either very young, very fortunate, or untrustworthy.

They walk quietly by habit. Not silently—silence would be impossible in a city so dense with life—but with a constant awareness of what lies beneath their feet and above their heads. A careless step can loosen a ceiling flake. A dropped tool can send a startling report down a shaft. People learn to set objects down rather than throw them, to close doors against their frames rather than let them swing, to call out before turning a corner.

They also learn to listen.

Every household knows the ordinary sounds of its walls: the faint tick of cooling stone, the water in a gutter, the settling of a beam, the cough of a neighbor beyond the partition. A new sound may be more important than a spoken warning. Three taps from the other side of a wall means someone is trapped or needs passage. Two taps, repeated after a pause, means fresh movement in the stone. A long scrape is usually nothing. A silence where dripping water should be is not nothing at all.

The most respected people in the city are not rulers, exactly. They are those who can tell what the cavern is doing.

The **Listeners** go from district to district with stone hammers, cords, bowls of water, and thin copper plates. They tap walls and study the trembling surface of the water. They press their ears to support posts. They remember where cracks appeared during the last heavy rain and compare them to the new ones. Some are born with an instinct for it; others train for decades. Their knowledge is not written only in records, though there are records: knot-cords, charcoal maps, painted marks, lines of chiseled symbols. Much of it lives in the body. A Listener may pause in a crowded lane, tilt their head, and tell a family to move their sleeping mats before nightfall.

People obey them because the city has taught obedience more efficiently than any government could.

There are councils, certainly. Each district has elders, builders, water-keepers, lamp-wardens, and representatives from the surface caravans. There are disputes over food, space, tools, marriages, and access to the warmer galleries. But no council can argue successfully with a ceiling that has begun to come down. The deepest law is structural. A family may inherit a room, but no one inherits the stone above it. A wealthy household may own storage jars, animals, or a profitable workbench, but it cannot own the right to remain beneath a dangerous shelf.

When a district must be evacuated, people leave quickly and without ceremony. Possessions are sorted by weight and necessity. Infants, water, lamps, tools, seed, food, and records go first. Decorative things go last, if they go at all. A household may spend generations accumulating cups, blankets, carved figures, and copper fittings, then abandon them in the span of an hour because a seam has opened overhead.

This has made them practical, but not joyless.

They decorate what can be safely decorated. Door lintels are painted with mineral powders: white from crushed stone, red from iron-rich clay, green from exposed seams. Families hang strings of polished shells brought from the surface, though shells are rare and treasured. Children press their hands into wet plaster beside sleeping rooms. Small figures are carved into support posts—not gods, usually, but ancestors, animals, builders, and the old shapes of the cavern before a passage was widened.

They have a strong appetite for color. A bright scarf, a blue bead, a yellow fungus cap, or a strip of surface cloth can draw attention from halfway down a lane. In the lamp-lit city, color is more intimate than grandeur. It belongs to the person wearing it.

Their clothing is layered and close-fitting. Loose hems catch on stone and snag on tools; trailing fabric is dangerous near wheels, pulleys, and fire. Wool and felt are common in the upper districts, where drafts are less severe. In the low passages people wear wrapped leggings, thick footcloths, and patched leather. Children are given soft caps reinforced at the crown, not because every child is expected to fall, but because the city does not permit people to pretend falling is impossible.

They carry lamps in different ways according to occupation. Water-carriers use hooded lights that can be set on a ledge. Builders wear lamps at the shoulder, leaving both hands free. Surveyors carry a flame inside a perforated clay vessel whose behavior reveals air movement. Midwives prefer small, steady oil lamps with no smoke. The dead are often remembered by the type of light they favored: a tallow flame for a patient person, resin for someone restless, charcoal glow for a person who disliked being seen.

They are born in rooms that are rarely private.

Birth is attended by women from neighboring households, and often by whoever happens to live on the other side of the wall. A laboring mother may hear a loom shuttle clicking through the partition, a child being scolded in the next room, someone carrying water overhead. The city does not allow birth to become an isolated event. There are too many dangers for that. If the mother weakens, hands are available. If a roof shifts, voices are nearby. If a lamp dies, another can be brought.

The newborn is held up beneath the ceiling—not toward the rock, but so that everyone present may look at the space above them and speak the same blessing:

**May the roof remember us.**

They do not believe the stone literally remembers. Or, if they do, they disagree about what remembering would mean. The phrase is an acknowledgment that survival is mutual. A roof shelters a household, but a household also maintains the roof: bracing it, draining it, clearing its weight, noticing the first signs of failure. To be remembered by the roof is to be held by something that must also be tended.

Children grow up knowing the shape of danger before they know its name.

They play in stairwells, under tables, along abandoned galleries, and in the spaces between closely built houses. Their games involve routes and warnings. They make miniature settlements from loose chips of stone, then invent reasons to evacuate them. They race from lamp to lamp without stepping beyond the marked safe lines. They hide in storage chambers and imitate the calls of water-keepers. A favorite game is called **After the Tremor**, in which one child becomes the fallen district and the others must retrieve people, lamps, and jars before an imaginary second collapse.

They are not forbidden to enter the dangerous places. They are taught how to recognize them.

A child who can identify a fresh fracture by its pale interior receives praise. A child who knows which damp moss is edible is trusted with a knife. A child who can hear the difference between an ordinary settling groan and a dangerous one is given a Listener’s cord to wear for a day.

Their schooling is woven through labor. They learn letters by reading storage marks and water tallies. They learn numbers by measuring beams and dividing grain. They learn history through the sequence of collapses, migrations, repaired chambers, and lost entrances. The city has no single foundation story. Every generation discovers another older wall beneath its floors, another sealed niche, another tool abandoned in a chamber that was once considered new.

Their oldest stories speak of a time when the cavern was wider, though no one agrees whether this means the first ancestors lived in larger open spaces or merely that the storytellers were young. Some say the city was once under a single roof. Some say there were no roofs at all, only a world of exposed stone and enormous wind. Some claim the surface is an abandoned upper district whose streets have been filled with blue air.

The surface people are real, of course. They arrive seasonally with grain, wood, fruit, salt, hides, and news. The Hearthbound call them **Skywalkers**. The Skywalkers call the Hearthbound by various names—miners, underfolk, stone people, the buried, the patient ones. The names vary by valley and trading route.

Relations are necessary and uneasy.

The Hearthbound depend on the surface for fuel and staple food. The Skywalkers depend on them for copper, worked stone, mineral dyes, fungi, subterranean medicines, and the knowledge of what lies beneath the plain. Surface caravans camp near the entrances, where sunlight and open wind make the Hearthbound uncomfortable. Children from below sometimes follow them upward and return with sunburned faces, feverish eyes, and stories too large to fit into the city.

Some surface visitors are treated as honored guests. Others are watched closely. A person accustomed to an unbroken horizon may not understand that a casually abandoned fire, a blocked shaft, or a newly cut passage can endanger hundreds of lives. The Hearthbound forgive ignorance more readily than carelessness.

They rarely go above without purpose. The open plain frightens them, though they describe this fear in practical terms: the lack of walls, the lack of a dependable ceiling, the exposure to weather, the difficulty of knowing where sound comes from. In the open, a person can see too far and understand too little. Many return below after only a few hours, grateful for the first low roof over their head.

The city’s food is plain but carefully altered. Surface grain becomes dense cakes, sour porridge, or thin noodles dried above communal fires. Fungus is stewed with bitter roots. Cave mice and long-bodied scavengers are eaten when trapped, though the taste is considered unpleasant and the meat reserved for poor seasons. Salt is used sparingly. Fruit brought down from above is divided into portions so exact that children learn fractions by watching an orange or plum cut at the communal table.

Nothing is wasted, but not everything is mourned.

They burn dead wood, grind old plaster, melt broken metal, scrape mold from walls, and rework worn shoes. A person’s final possessions are often dismantled within days of their death. Their beam may become a bridge rail. Their cloak may line a cradle. Their knife may be given to an apprentice. This is not considered disrespectful. To remain useful after death is one of the quieter forms of honor.

The dead themselves are sealed into niches above the living levels, wrapped in cloth and clay. The niches are marked with names, dates, and sometimes the sound the person was known for: a three-note whistle, a cough, a hammer rhythm, a laugh. The city is too crowded for monuments, but it is full of small acknowledgments. A wall contains someone’s initials. A water channel bears the name of the woman who first diverted it. A stair has the nickname of a boy who fell from it and survived.

They are religious in the manner of people who must negotiate constantly with the material world.

Some worship old powers of stone, pressure, water, and flame. Others believe the cavern is not conscious but behaves so consistently that consciousness is unnecessary. There are shrines made of ordinary objects: a cup beneath a drip, a row of smooth stones, a lamp kept burning in a sealed alcove. Prayers tend to be brief and specific.

Let the seam hold.  
Let the water come clean.  
Let the fire draw upward.  
Let the path remain open.  
Let those beneath us wake before the falling.

Their festivals are built around temporary abundance. When the surface caravans bring fruit, fuel, and new timber, the city holds a night of open lamps. Every usable light is cleaned and set burning. Music travels through the lanes: drums muted with hide, bone flutes, rattles filled with seed, the rhythmic striking of stone on stone. People dance in places that are ordinarily work yards, careful to leave clear routes and to keep their elbows away from walls.

The music is never wholly loud. Even celebration must leave room for the cavern to speak.

They have adapted their songs to stone. Melodies are short, repeated, and separated by pauses. Call-and-response songs allow a singer in one passage to answer another from a neighboring gallery. Certain notes carry well through particular rock. Lovers use them. Water crews use them. During evacuations, wardens shout in measured patterns so that instructions can be distinguished from panic.

Their arguments, too, are often about sound.

A person who bangs doors is accused of behaving like a surface dweller. A household that sings late into the dim cycle may be asked to soften its voices. A quarrel conducted in a public passage is considered dangerous—not morally, but acoustically, because shouting can hide warnings. Anger must learn the dimensions of the place.

And yet the Hearthbound are not merely cautious people shaped into tools by a hostile environment. They are vain, tender, jealous, curious, and capable of foolishness. They fall in love across districts. They hoard sweets. They carve jokes into beams. They steal lamp oil. They become obsessed with rare minerals or with maps of passages no one has entered. They resent the families above them, envy the families with warmer rooms, and dream of opening businesses in districts that do not yet exist.

They make plans for a future the cavern has given them no reason to trust.

A young builder may speak confidently of a new avenue beyond the northern pressure ridge. An old Listener may tell them the ridge will fail first. Both may be right. The city advances anyway. It must. Behind every new wall are children needing space, water requiring diversion, workers needing air, and families who cannot remain packed three rooms deep forever.

So the Hearthbound continue to shape the hollow, while being shaped by it.

They carry stone from one place to another. They brace what can be braced. They mark what cannot yet be trusted. They hang cloth over drafts, raise fungus in darkness, collect rain from a roof that has not finished becoming a roof, and sleep beneath shelves of rock that may have been separated from their deeper layers only yesterday.

They know the cavern is unfinished.

They know it may collapse, flood, narrow, split, or open farther than any map can hold. They know the plain above is cold and empty, and that someday there may be roads, towers, animals, farms, and another city on its surface. They know that their descendants may look upward through a shaft and see structures built over the very ground beneath which the first Hearthbound made their homes.

But for now, this is their world.

It is a world of close walls and shared warmth, of lamps guarded against drafts, of water caught before it disappears, of voices traveling through stone. It is a world where every person learns that survival is not the opposite of fragility. Survival is the practice of living carefully inside it.

And beneath the young, unsettled earth, they do.
