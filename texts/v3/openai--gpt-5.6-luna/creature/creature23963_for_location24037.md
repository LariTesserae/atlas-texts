What lives on the plateau has no clear boundary between individual and environment.

At first glance, there are only scattered signs of habitation: a line of warmed stones arranged along a ridge, a sequence of condensation pools emptied in the same order each night, a grove whose translucent leaves pulse even when no water is moving through its trunks. In the morning, these things may have shifted. Stones gather where the land is about to crack. Plants lean toward hidden pressure. A route appears across a shelf that no animal seems to have made.

The plateau is inhabited by **those that adjust**.

They are not one species. They are a recurring form of life: organisms that sense change in the stone, air, water, and distance, and alter themselves in response. Some are small enough to live between mineral grains. Some move like herds. Some are rooted for centuries and still travel, slowly, by rearranging the ground beneath them. All of them participate in the same great activity—the unfinished land constantly testing possible shapes, and life constantly making those shapes habitable.

The smallest are called, if they are called anything, **seam-livers**.

They inhabit the narrowest fractures, where heat, water, and dissolved metals meet. Their bodies are translucent threads with no fixed front or rear. They extend through rock as fine branching nerves, feeding on pressure gradients and the faint electrical differences between warm and cold stone. When a crack begins to widen, the seam-livers flood it with a gel that hardens into a flexible mineral membrane. When a crack begins to close, they dissolve the membrane and retreat deeper.

Their presence can be seen as a pale blue line beneath the basalt. A person walking across the plateau might mistake it for reflected light. If they stand still long enough, the line will bend toward them—not from curiosity, but because their body alters the pressure and temperature of the rock.

There are larger creatures that tend the surface.

They resemble low, many-legged animals only because the eye insists on finding an animal in them. Their bodies are broad and dark, covered in overlapping plates that collect dew. Beneath the plates are rows of soft grinding organs. They move across the shelves in slow convergences, scraping loose minerals from the stone and pressing them into banks along the edges of growing vegetation. Their work creates the first shallow soils.

They do not eat plants. They eat imbalance.

A slope that sheds too much heat attracts them. A pool whose mineral concentration rises beyond tolerance draws a whole procession. They gather around a young vent and remain there for months, their bodies glowing red at the joints. When the vent stabilizes, they leave behind a terraced basin where moss, crystal grass, and small pools can survive.

If threatened, they fold themselves flat against the ground. Their legs disappear into grooves, their plates seal, and the entire animal becomes indistinguishable from a piece of cooled volcanic crust. Sometimes a hunter passes over one without noticing. Sometimes the animal remains motionless for years, waiting for the pressure beneath it to become safe again.

The long-legged grazers are more visible, though only briefly.

They travel in groups of seven to thirty, crossing the high shelves in a measured procession. Their bodies are narrow because there is little reason to carry anything that cannot be fed by wind, heat, or condensation. Their skin is a shifting gray-green, mottled with the colors of the terrain. Along their backs grow mats of symbiotic organisms: copper-feeding mites, pale flowers that open only during electrical storms, and small blind creatures that nest in the warmth beneath the ribs.

They graze not on grass, but on the magnetic fields produced by it.

Their mouths brush the iron-rich blades and draw out the minute currents stored along their edges. Afterward, the grass loses its charge and collapses into ordinary mineral fiber. The grazers pass through a field like a tide, leaving behind darkened lanes and heaps of gently magnetized stones.

When they sense a pressure change, they become difficult to see. Their skin turns transparent, then more than transparent: it reveals the structures behind them. Through the flank of one animal, another animal may be seen; through that second body, the vertical lights of a distant vent; beyond the vent, perhaps a valley that belongs to another hour. The herd does not disappear so much as cease to occupy a single layer of the plateau.

The creatures return when the danger has passed, though never quite where they began.

In the ravines live the **wall-climbers**, pale, many-jointed beings that spend their lives moving vertically. Their limbs end in soft gripping fans, and their bodies are threaded with pockets of warm vapor. They feed on the lichen fans growing across the cliffs, but they also cultivate them, carrying spores from one mineral surface to another in the folds of their joints.

Their heads are small and almost featureless. Most of their sensing takes place through the lamps along their sides. These organs respond to changes in pressure, not light, and pulse whenever the plateau shifts. A wall-climber can feel an approaching fracture long before the rock announces it. Entire colonies may abandon a ravine days before its walls fall.

The largest of them are rooted from crown to crown across a chasm. They resemble hanging bridges made of living tissue. Their limbs grow together over generations, forming lattices that catch condensation and provide shelter for smaller organisms. When they mature, they release their grip on the rock one limb at a time and descend into the fog. Some are seen again in other ravines. Most become part of the mist.

The pools are inhabited by things that cannot maintain a single shape.

The quicksilver fish gather in the dark water after dewfall. They are not fish except in their habits. Each individual is a colony of motile cells and reflective grains, capable of forming a narrow body, a broad fan, a ribbon, or a cloud. They swim by changing their density. At one moment, a pool may contain a hundred silver bodies. At the next, it contains one shining sheet pressed against the stone.

They communicate through temporary shapes.

A school may form the outline of a branching root to signal mineral richness, or a jagged crown to indicate danger. When a predator enters the water, the fish flatten themselves against the pool’s bottom, becoming a second layer of stone. When a storm approaches, they climb the sides of the basin and hang there in trembling curtains, waiting for the electrical charge to pass.

Their young are droplets. A mature colony releases them into the air, where they cling to fog and drift toward other pools. Many never arrive. Those that do become part of the water without any clear moment of birth.

In the fog are the absence-hunters.

No one sees these creatures directly. One notices a missing patch of vegetation, a region where the amber-green mist is slightly too smooth, a place where sound thins and footsteps seem to arrive from the wrong direction. The absence moves with patient intention.

It hunts by borrowing gaps.

A hunter may take the shape of the unoccupied space between two trunks, the shadow beneath an overhang, or the distance between a traveler’s body and their own shadow. It does not become invisible; it becomes overlooked. The mind edits it out, as it edits out silence or the dark between stars.

Only the plants respond openly. Fronds close when an absence passes. Moss turns its spore-bearing surfaces away. Crystal grass bends in two directions at once, toward the wind and toward the hidden body.

What the absence-hunters eat is uncertain. Animals vanish near them, but so do paths, memories, and occasionally whole sections of weather. A fogbank may emerge with a hollow inside it. A route followed for years may simply stop existing. Those who return from such places often remember being pursued by something that was never there.

Deep beneath the plateau live the **interior colonies**.

They are responsible for the clicks heard underfoot and for the warm air released when the stone ruptures. Their bodies occupy the tunnels as networks of pressure-sensitive chambers. No single chamber is an individual. An individual may consist of a tunnel, a mineral vein, a field of eggs, and several cubic kilometers of slowly moving rock.

They feed on stress.

Where the crust is under too much tension, they absorb it, diverting pressure through their tunnels toward the great chimneys. Where the stone is weak, they reinforce it with secreted ribs of dark ceramic. Where two underground systems meet, they exchange heat, spores, and patterns of sensation.

Sometimes they surface as small blind animals, pale and soft, with mouths adapted for tasting air. They emerge in floods when a tunnel has become too hot, pouring from a rupture in the ground and scattering across the surface. They do not flee from the opening. They wait nearby until the rock closes, then burrow back in, carrying new minerals on their bodies.

The plateau is full of such creatures: organisms that spend one stage of life as animal, another as weather, another as stone.

The filter-feeders around warm pools release gliding machines that are mistaken for seeds. These machines have no eyes, but they can read gradients of heat and voltage. They drift through the fog in silver swarms, attaching themselves to chimneys and living plants. Some become pollinators. Some become parasites. Some enter abandoned tools or fragments of metal and wake them years later with feeding organs and a new instinct to migrate.

A device left on the plateau does not remain itself.

A blade grows a root system into the soil. A metal vessel develops a membranous lining and begins collecting dew. A broken instrument, warmed by the earth, may sprout thin legs and join the nocturnal routes. The plateau does not distinguish between the natural and the made. It incorporates both into whatever cycle is nearest.

There may be intelligence here, though it is distributed beyond any recognizable body.

The chimney fields communicate through electrical pulses. The crystal grasses exchange magnetic patterns. The interior colonies alter the paths of tunnels in response to distant storms. Herds of grazers change direction before a pressure front appears, while forests in ravines open their leaves in a sequence that repeats across hundreds of kilometers.

Whether these are separate acts or parts of one mind is impossible to determine.

Perhaps the plateau thinks through all its inhabitants. Perhaps thought is merely what adjustment looks like when it becomes complex enough. A storm gathers around a field of living metal. The chimneys begin to pulse. Underground chambers open. Fog rises from a warm crack, carrying spores toward a distant shelf. The grazers turn. The absence-hunters withdraw. Across the continent, countless bodies make minute corrections.

Nothing speaks.

But the world answers itself everywhere.

At night, the inhabitants gather along the illuminated seams. Small animals drink from glowing pools. Wall-climbers suspend themselves over ravines. Long-legged grazers stand motionless while the organisms on their backs bloom. Beneath them, the tunnel colonies click and shift. Chimneys open in sequence, exhaling columns of heat that join the bronze atmosphere.

The plateau does not have cities, because its inhabitants carry their settlements with them. It does not have roads, because their movement creates routes that are alive. It does not have machines, because every machine here eventually develops hunger, reproduction, and a place in the weather.

They live in the unfinishedness.

Every crack is a possible shelter. Every storm is a migration. Every new ridge is an organ not yet assigned a purpose. The creatures of the plateau do not merely endure the unstable land; they depend upon its instability. A finished world would starve them.

So they keep the young stone moving.

They brace collapsing shelves, redirect rivers, open sealed valleys, cultivate fog, seed the cracks, and consume whatever becomes too rigid. They make life from pressure and return life to pressure. When the plateau changes, they change with it—not as survivors adapting to disaster, but as parts of the same process.

The land is still becoming land.

Its inhabitants are the means by which it continues.
