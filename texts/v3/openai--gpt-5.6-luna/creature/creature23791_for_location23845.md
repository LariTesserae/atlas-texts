Something lives there that has never been entirely born.

At first it resembles a figure standing beneath the rain, though this is only because the eye prefers figures to arrangements. It is tall when seen against a ridge and small when seen in a hollow. It may have two legs, or six, or none. Its outline is made from interruptions in the water: a vertical clearing in the falling streams, a darkening where no shadow should be, the brief suggestion of shoulders beneath a transparent wall.

It does not arrive. It becomes noticeable.

Sometimes it is found crouched in the partial ring of pale stones, its body folded so tightly that it might be mistaken for a piece of fresh black rock. Sometimes it lies flat beneath a current, indistinguishable from a seam in the plateau. Sometimes it appears as a pale hand rising from a basin, fingers opening and closing in time with the clear-shelled organisms. If watched too directly, it loses the part of itself that has been seen. A head becomes a hollow. A spine becomes a ledge. An eye continues to look from the face of the rock long after the creature has gone elsewhere.

Its body is not built around a single material. It has a core of something dark and dense, but this core is rarely exposed. Over it lie layers that alter according to pressure: translucent skin like the fronds on the ridges, smooth white plates resembling chalk, membranes thin as rain, and occasional glimpses of interiors that do not belong to any anatomy. Through an opening in its side one might see a dry field beneath a white sun. Through the translucent length of its arm, a staircase descending through blue air. When it is wounded, nothing bleeds. The injury fills with water, then with mineral dust, then with a small new chamber.

Its limbs are long enough to be useful and never quite the same length twice. They do not bend only at joints. A forearm may become a rope of black stone and anchor itself to a cliff. A hand may spread into five delicate roots, gripping the surface from underneath. When the currents become violent, the creature flattens itself against the plateau and lets the water pass through it. For several moments it is less a body than a pattern imposed on the water.

It feeds on change.

Not on fish, or plants, or the shelled things gathered in the cracks. Those are too complete and too local. It feeds when a ledge swells, when a crevasse closes, when a newly risen ridge displaces an old basin. It places its mouth—or the place where a mouth temporarily forms—against the raw stone and draws in the vibration. The black rock dims. The white stone bruises blue. Somewhere inside the creature, an unseen arrangement is made.

Afterward, a fragment of landscape may vanish.

The staircase loses a step. A patch of fronds appears several meters away. One of the pale uprights in the ring becomes slightly less real, its edges difficult to remember. The creature has taken nothing that can be measured, only possibility: the unused shape a thing might have had, the alternative direction a current nearly chose.

It is therefore hungry without being ravenous. It waits through long intervals of sameness, though sameness is rare here. When the plateau changes several times in quick succession, the creature becomes almost visible. Its body gathers definition. A face may persist for three or four breaths. The face is not human, but it has the exhausted attentiveness of something that has spent a long time listening for its own name.

The roar is its only language, and it is not the creature’s language alone. It speaks by entering the existing violence and altering it slightly. A descending stream hesitates. A collapse strikes in three measured blows instead of one. A whirlpool turns against itself. These alterations spread through the plateau as meaning, though no meaning survives translation.

To a human voice, the creature responds badly.

The first syllable pulls one of its limbs toward the speaker. The second causes the nearby water to rise. By the third, the voice has been distributed among the place. One portion echoes from the ring of stones, another clatters from the hidden staircase, another emerges from the creature’s chest in a tone too low to hear but strong enough to move gravel. It does not repeat words. It repeats conditions.

If someone says *come*, the creature may bring the speaker to a place they have already left.

If someone says *look*, the creature may give them another pair of eyes.

If someone says *home*, the entire plateau may briefly reveal itself as the inside of a much larger body.

It does not understand possession. The scattered construction is not its house, exactly. The ring is where it gathers shadows when it needs to become solid. The staircase is where it stores objects that have not yet decided whether they are memories or tools. In the opening at the bottom, it arranges small things: clear shells, black pebbles, fragments of pale stone, pieces of bone, perhaps teeth. The clatter heard within is careful and patient. Each object is placed according to a rule that changes whenever the staircase changes.

The creature sometimes carries these objects out and sets them on fresh ledges. It may place a shell beside a crack, a tooth inside a hollow, a stone upright in the current. These acts look ceremonial, though ceremony implies an audience. It is more likely teaching the place how to recognize itself.

The ribbonlike animals avoid it. They do not flee; they simply cease to be in the creature’s direction. Their distortions bend around its body. The dark fronds, however, grow toward it. When it passes, they stiffen even underwater and point after it like small black hands. The warm moss vanishes wherever it rests, then returns elsewhere, as if the plant cannot tolerate remaining close to whatever the creature is becoming.

It has no fixed age. At times it seems newly formed, with raw edges and a surface still deciding whether to be skin or stone. At other times it moves with the slowness of something that remembers mountains before they were mountains. Its gestures contain both infancy and erosion. It may spend an hour learning the shape of a step, then erase the step without apparent regret.

Perhaps it has been here since the first ridge broke through the water. Perhaps the plateau formed around the place where it was sleeping. Perhaps it is the plateau’s attempt to imagine a resident: a mind made from pressure, rain, fracture, and unfinished intention.

When the atmosphere gathers into its trembling lens, the creature climbs toward the highest point. It does not look through the lens. It places its face against the surface and waits.

Beyond the lens lies the sunlit world, but the creature does not enter it. It knows, in whatever sense it knows anything, that the opening depends on silence. The first sound would close it. So it remains there, motionless amid the impossible view, while the warm rain strikes its back and the young stone continues to rise beneath its feet.

Then some ridge shifts.

Some buried chamber opens.

Some new alteration calls to it from below.

The creature turns—not toward the sound, but into it—and its body breaks apart into water, shadow, and pale mineral light. For a while nothing lives there except the changing plateau.

Then, in the next hollow, something begins to take shape.
