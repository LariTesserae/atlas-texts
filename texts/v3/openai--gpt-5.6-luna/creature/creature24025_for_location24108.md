The plateau is inhabited by things that have learned not to waste motion.

Most of its animals are pale and long-limbed, built for crossing distances that would make a forest animal anxious. They travel alone or in loose, widely separated companies, never clustering unless weather forces them together. Their feet are broad and soft at the edges, leaving almost no mark on the mineral soil. Beneath their ribs are thin reservoirs of cool fluid, and in the hollows of their bones grow delicate lattices that collect the night’s condensation. They can go many days without drinking. When they do find water, they lower themselves around it with a solemnity that looks almost ceremonial.

The grazers feed on silver grass and on the translucent plants, but they do not tear them up. Their mouths are narrow, precise instruments. They remove only the uppermost leaves, leaving the roots and the moisture-seams beneath them undisturbed. Their green undersides are not pigment but living tissue, a secondary skin that opens to the dawn and closes before the sun becomes severe. When they stand still, they become nearly indistinguishable from the shelves of ochre stone. Only their eyes remain visible: dark, wet, and turned not toward one another but toward the distances between things.

They are listening for the underground tones.

The burrowers are smaller, round-bodied creatures with many jointed forelimbs and transparent claws. They live beneath the shrubs in chambers arranged around heat, water, and sound. Their tunnels are not random. From above, their branching excavations resemble roots, river deltas, or writing in a language whose letters have been spread apart. They cultivate pale fungi in the cool chambers and store seeds in clay-lined pockets. When the low tone passes through the plateau, they stop digging and press their bodies against the walls. The sound tells them something about the depth of the water, though whether they understand it or merely respond to it is difficult to say.

There are also animals that spend most of their lives above the ground.

The membrane creatures descend at midday, when the thermals are strongest. Up close, they are not manta-like so much as vast, flexible leaves with bones of dark filament running through them. Their skins are mottled with faint colors that change according to the angle of the sun. They drift over the grazers and lower themselves until their undersides brush the animals’ backs. In passing, they gather salt, condensation, loose scales, and the warm vapors rising from living bodies. They return this moisture in other places: to the crowns of trees, to the mouths of flowering plants, to the exposed nests of insects.

The grazers do not flinch when the membranes touch them. Sometimes they lean into the contact.

The relationship is older than fear.

At night, the membrane creatures fold themselves around the higher ridges and become almost impossible to distinguish from cloud. Their bodies cool until stars appear through them. Small luminous organisms live along their edges, feeding on minerals carried upward from the plateau. When the creatures rise at dawn, the lights detach and fall slowly toward the ground. Some are eaten by insects. Some sink into the soil. Some find the old arches and vanish through them.

The bright-winged insects are not numerous, but they are exact. They move between isolated flowers along routes that never vary, even when the flowers have not yet opened. Their wings are thin panes of color, blue, amber, white, and a red so deep it appears black in shadow. They do not buzz. Each beat produces a brief click, and the combined clicking of a passing swarm sounds like a handful of pebbles poured onto glass.

They carry pollen in small chambers beneath their throats. If one dies, others gather around it, strip the pollen, and carry on. Nothing in their behavior suggests grief, but neither does it suggest indifference. They seem to treat life as a pattern that must continue through individual losses.

The trees are inhabited as well, though not always by creatures that can be seen.

Their roots descend around buried seams of water and meet, far below the surface, in chambers of warm stone. There, pale root-animals move slowly through the soil. They have no eyes and no mouths. They sense pressure, dissolved minerals, and the faint electrical disturbances produced by roots growing nearby. When a tree is threatened by drought, the root-animals close certain channels and open others. Water shifts underground. A depression that has been dry for years may suddenly hold moisture. A distant tree may darken, flower, and survive.

The trees themselves communicate by changing their internal temperature. At dusk, a solitary tree may release a pulse of heat that travels through the ground toward another tree several kilometers away. The receiving tree answers before sunrise by opening a small number of black flowers. No animal seems to feed from them. The flowers may be signals, or they may be organs belonging to a larger body whose boundaries have been erased by distance.

There is something more deliberate living among the old constructions.

It is difficult to say whether it is a species, a colony, or a function that has become biological.

In the hollows beneath the plateau, where blue-black clay stays cool, slender figures sometimes rise. They are taller than the grazers and much thinner, with bodies composed of layered mineral films stretched over an interior framework of flexible rods. Their surfaces carry the colors of the land: pale stone, violet shadow, mica-bright flashes. They have no obvious faces. A few dark apertures open and close along the front of the head, sensing light, pressure, and the underground tones.

They do not walk in the ordinary way. They place one foot down, then wait. The ground seems to decide whether to hold them. Their next step is often several body lengths away, though nothing about the intervening space changes. When they move near the half-buried platform, the water in its depressions reflects them at different locations: standing beside the tower, under another sun, or at the edge of a hollow that no longer exists.

These figures tend the plateau without appearing to tend it.

They clear dust from the dark arches by standing near them until the wind changes. They touch certain stones and cause hidden panels to emerge. They press their hands into dry basins, and weeks later water appears there after a storm that should have passed elsewhere. They do not repair the tower. They do not straighten it. Its impossible lean is part of the system, perhaps part of the instruction.

At intervals, one of them climbs the tower.

There are no steps, but the figure rises along its surface as though gravity has been persuaded to point sideways. When it reaches the tip, the violet line begins at the base. The light travels upward through the figure’s body, passing through its mineral layers. For a moment the creature becomes transparent, and inside it can be seen a branching arrangement of luminous threads—some like nerves, some like roots, some like the lines connecting the plateau’s scattered features.

Then the light enters the sky.

The figure remains at the summit until the glow fades. It may be sending information. It may be receiving it. The plateau may be one node in a structure so vast that the other nodes lie beneath oceans, inside mountains, or under the soil of worlds no longer inhabited. The creature gives no sign that it knows the difference between maintenance and prayer.

There are rarely more than three of these beings visible at once. They do not gather. Each follows a separate line between water, tree, arch, and stone. When their paths cross, they stop at a distance of several paces. Their bodies adjust their color. One becomes darker; the other responds with a wash of violet along its limbs. This exchange lasts less than a minute. Then they continue.

They have no predators that can be seen, though the plateau contains forms that might once have hunted them. In the high gullies, beneath the horizontal clouds, there are long impressions in the mineral soil. Something enormous moves there during the wet passages, beneath the ground rather than upon it. The old beings avoid these places. So do the grazers. When the hidden thing turns, the low tone rises through the plateau, and every living creature pauses—not in terror, but in recognition.

Perhaps it is the oldest inhabitant.

Perhaps the plateau is its body.

The things that live there do not seem to divide the world into living and nonliving. Water basins, trees, animals, arches, dust, and buried structures all participate in the same patient circulation. A creature may drink from a platform and carry away an image of itself from another time. A cloud may pass through a gully and reveal a dormant machine. A tree may mark a water source, while a water source marks the position of a vanished tree. The boundaries between organism, instrument, and landscape have been softened by immense age.

Even the smallest lives belong to distances.

An insect follows a line no eye can see. A root sends warmth toward a tree beyond the ridge. A burrower pauses at a tone emitted deep below. A membrane creature descends to touch the back of a grazer, taking something from it and leaving something behind. Far overhead, shapes move through the white-gold air, carrying the plateau’s moisture into patterns that will eventually return as rain.

Nothing hurries. Nothing is idle.

When night comes, the inhabitants settle into their separate spaces. The grazers stand beneath the trees. The burrowers close their entrances with disks of damp clay. The insects fold their colored wings among the silver grasses. The membrane creatures rise until they are only shadows between stars. In the old structures, faint violet lines continue to travel, one after another, through towers, arches, platforms, and the buried seams beneath the ground.

The mineral dust moves in slow sheets.

The plateau breathes.

And within that breath, distributed across all its open distances, lives a quiet intelligence that has never needed to gather itself into a single body.
