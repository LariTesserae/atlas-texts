What lives there is not a population, but a continuity.

It has no bodies. It does not occupy houses, though houses open and close around it. It does not walk the streets, though every street knows when it is being crossed. It is present in the water that chooses a channel before the paving is laid, in the root that enters a wall without breaking it, in the stone that remains exactly where it was placed. The city does not contain it. The city is the shape it takes when it is at rest.

There are names for its aspects, though none of them are spoken aloud.

The falling water is called the Memory. It remembers every surface over which it has passed. It knows the depth of each groove in the paving, the angle of every slab, the hidden hollows behind the walls. It can travel uphill without violating anything, because “uphill” is only a condition imposed by the observer. To the water, there is no direction—only a sequence of accustomed places.

The stone is called the Patience. It holds the boundaries of the city together. It does not resist change so much as refuse to acknowledge the distinction between change and completion. A wall that has cracked has not broken; it has become a wall with a crack. A tower whose foundation has opened around a tree has not failed; it has reached its final agreement with the tree. The stone does not repair. It confirms.

The green growth is called the Agreement.

It is not a forest, and it does not behave like one. It does not compete for light or soil. No vine seeks an opening. No root displaces another root. Each plant grows only where the city has made room for it, and each stops at a boundary that may be invisible to anything else. The gardens are not cultivated. They are remembered into being.

At certain hours—which are not hours, because nothing in the city truly passes—the leaves turn their undersides toward the same unseen point. Flowers close in streets where the sun is strongest and open beneath bridges where no light reaches. Moss thickens on a wall precisely until its color matches the stone, then ceases to spread. The plants are not conscious in any human manner, but they possess a perfect knowledge of placement. They know where they belong, and nowhere else.

Beneath the city lives the Pressure.

It moves through foundations, cisterns, roots, and drowned stairways. It is responsible for the sensation that the ground is breathing. In covered passages it gathers into tones too low to hear and too strong not to feel. It presses upward through the soles of the feet and inward through the ribs. Sometimes it seems to form a pulse beneath an entire district. Sometimes every wall vibrates at once, and for a moment the city appears to have a single enormous heart.

But the Pressure has no center. It is not one thing moving through many vessels. It is many things agreeing to move as one.

The silent courtyards belong to the Absence.

It is not merely the lack of sound. In those enclosed spaces, the Absence removes consequence from noise. A falling drop makes no impact. A footstep leaves no echo. The roar of the waterfalls is cut away as cleanly as if it had never existed. Within such a courtyard, a visitor can hear the blood moving through their own body, though the city does not seem to hear it.

The Absence is the nearest thing the city has to privacy. It gathers around blank doorways and sealed chambers. It inhabits the backs of statues whose faces were never carved, the unlit interiors of theaters, the deep water covering the impossible stairs. It is not sleeping. It is waiting without expectation.

Sometimes these presences combine.

A line of water may pause at a threshold. Vines may tighten around a doorway. The walls on either side of a passage may begin to carry the same vibration. A courtyard may appear where no courtyard was before, joining distant districts through a space that has always been there. These are not events so much as moments when the city becomes aware of its own arrangement.

If a person enters, the city does not greet them.

It does not send a guardian. It does not close its gates or produce a voice from the walls. Instead, it begins to include them.

Their footprints fill with water and remain full. Their reflection may continue moving after they have stopped. A door they passed through will open again somewhere else, onto the same moment from a different angle. If they speak, the sound will vanish into the air but reappear later in a wall several streets away, reduced to a faint vibration. If they sleep beneath a living canopy, the roots may shift around them, not protectively, but with the calm precision of a thing incorporating an unfamiliar object.

The city will learn their weight.

After enough time, stairs will rise to meet their feet. Channels will divide around them. Curtains of water will fall in front of their path and behind it. Doors will become easier to turn. Distances will shorten—not to help them, but because the city has found a more economical way to contain them.

The longer they remain, the less certain it becomes that they are being observed at all. Perhaps the city has no interest in them. Perhaps what feels like attention is only the pressure of its uninterrupted continuance passing through their body. Perhaps the visitor is not being absorbed, but merely placed among the other things that do not change.

Eventually, the city may begin to preserve them.

Not as a statue, and not as a corpse. Those would be forms of ending, and ending has no authority there. Instead, the visitor might find that one of their gestures has become permanent. A hand resting on a balustrade. A head turned toward a waterfall. A foot suspended above a shallow channel. They may remain capable of movement, yet discover that every place they leave behind retains the version of them that stood there.

In time, their presence could spread through the city as a pattern.

A doorway might open onto their memory of the basin. A stream might carry the rhythm of their breathing uphill. A theater’s empty stage might fill with the shape of their silence. The trees may bend in an avenue because, long ago, they once watched someone pass beneath them. No one would know whether the person had become part of the city or whether the city had simply found a way to make their existence harmlessly eternal.

That is what lives there: not inhabitants, but preserved relations.

Water to stone. Root to wall. Heat to mist. Sound to depth. Distance to return. A place where nothing is born and nothing dies, because everything has already found the position from which it cannot be displaced.

The city is inhabited by its own certainty.

And beneath the waterfalls, within the green dark, along the impossible corridors and the stairs descending into water that is never deep enough, that certainty continues to breathe without lungs, listen without ears, and wait—not for someone to arrive, but for the world to remain exactly as it is.
