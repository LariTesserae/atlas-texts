What lives there is not a species, exactly.

It is a population only because the chamber requires one. The animals are numerous enough to suggest abundance, but their abundance has no generosity in it. They are packed into the room as insulation is packed into a wall, as blood is packed into an organ. Each body occupies a prescribed pressure, a necessary warmth, a measurable obstruction. The small ones keep the drains from running too quickly. The wall-clingers regulate the thin films of water by covering and uncovering the ceramic. The suspended winged things stir the refrigerated air. The larger bodies below the floor maintain the chamber’s tremor, their slow contractions mistaken for the pulse of pumps.

They do not feed on one another. They feed on the conditions of the room.

A blind, many-legged creature may draw its mouth along a valve and ingest the metallic taste of its operation. A pale finned animal may press itself to a seam and drink the heat leaking through it. The winged creatures consume nothing visible; they live on the updraft, on vibration, on the minute difference between air before and after it passes over a compressor. Their green points brighten when the machinery cycles, though the cycle never seems to begin or end.

Somewhere among them are mouths that produce the water.

They are not easy to find. A slit opens beneath a grille, or a fleshy aperture contracts around a pipe, and a stream emerges with the exact force required. The creatures responsible are never seen entire. Their bodies are folded into conduits and pressure chambers, their organs threaded through the architecture. What appears to be plumbing may be a throat. What appears to be a pump may be a heart with a steel casing grown around it. What appears to be a drain may lead into the digestive tract of something vast and patient.

The animals do not seem to know that they are animals.

They know contact. They know pressure, temperature, flow. They know the approach of a mechanical arm by the change it makes in the water. They know when one of their number has been removed, though they do not mourn it; the surrounding bodies close over the absence before absence can become an event. Their awareness is spread thinly through the chamber, like condensation. No single creature contains the whole of it. The room thinks through them in fragments.

At intervals, a tremor passes through the walls.

Then every animal becomes still.

The motion does not stop because they have been startled. It stops because stillness has been requested. The drainage creatures flatten themselves into the channels. The wall-clingers seal their mouths. The suspended wings hold open in the updraft. Beneath the floor, the great segmented body—or the many bodies arranged as one—ceases its movement.

For several seconds, the entire chamber appears dead.

Then the pumps resume, and the animals resume with them. Water falls. Fins fold and unfold. The small pale bodies rearrange themselves by fractions of a millimeter. The chamber has not recovered from an interruption; it has merely completed one of its permitted shapes.

There may be a keeper among them.

At first it seems to be one of the larger animals, a long pale thing glimpsed behind the pressure hatches. It has no head that can be identified, only a sequence of openings and sealed eyes passing through the dark. Whenever a valve hesitates, some part of it arrives. Whenever frost grows beyond its assigned line, a wet limb presses against the wall and the frost withdraws. Whenever the animals crowd too densely around a drain, the creature shifts below them, and the pressure redistributes.

But the keeper may be the chamber itself.

The steel ribs remember the acceptable amount of water. The lamps remember the correct degree of darkness. The pipes remember which bodies belong in them. The walls contract and release with the slow, indifferent authority of muscle. Even the creatures that appear to repair the system may simply be its reflexes made visible.

If there is a mind, it is not located behind a face. It is distributed through the wet surfaces and the buried engines, through the organs of the animals and the counting mechanisms in the valves. It has no thoughts that can be translated into words. It does not ask what the mountain is, or why it was built, or what lies beyond the black ice. It has only one continuous perception:

the arrangement must hold.

This is why the inhabitants do not escape when a hatch opens. They do not flee toward the summit air, though some of them possess wings and some possess limbs made for climbing. The outside is not freedom to them. It is an error in pressure, a region without channels, without measured return. A creature placed there would become singular, exposed, and therefore unstable.

So they remain pressed together.

The smallest live between the teeth of the grating. The largest turn slowly in the buried dark. The winged ones hover forever beneath the ceiling, shedding no feathers, casting no shadows. Their lives are not journeys but continuities. They do not grow toward a form. They maintain the form they have been given.

Occasionally, one animal appears to look outward.

An eye opens in the black water beneath the floor. A translucent face lifts from a drain. A pale head emerges between two pipes, its features almost human in their arrangement: two eyes, a mouth, a narrow vertical nose. It regards the inspection lamp, or perhaps the trembling hand of someone standing nearby. There is no curiosity in it. The gaze does not ask to be understood.

It is checking whether the observer has altered anything.

If the answer is no, the face withdraws. The water closes over it. The surrounding animals press together and erase the place where it had been.

And somewhere deep in the mountain, something counts the pressure.

It counts the falling water, the beating wings, the bodies against the walls, the frost along the bolts. It counts the first grooves of erosion and confirms that they are still too shallow to matter. It counts every living thing, though it has no number for life. It calls them valves, ballast, filters, insulation, flow.

The inhabitants have no names for themselves.

They are the wetness in the machinery. They are the machinery’s patience. They are the crowded, pale persistence that keeps the chamber from becoming a ruin.

They live there because living is the function assigned to them.

And if the chamber were ever opened, if the mountain finally decided to become a mountain and the cap of black ice split beneath the ordinary sky, the inhabitants would not emerge as a swarm. They would remain where they were, holding the water in its channels, holding one another in place, holding the room against change until the last pump stopped and the last creature became indistinguishable from the metal it had spent its existence maintaining.
