They are creatures of the unfinished stone.

No single species owns the chamber. Ownership is a concept too spacious for it. The inhabitants occupy one another’s vacancies, feed on one another’s cast-off armor, and inherit the heat left behind by bodies that have died in the cracks. Their lives overlap so completely that the colony is less a population than a moving layer of the mountain—a skin that crawls, tightens, loosens, and grows wherever the rock permits it.

The smallest are pale scraping things, no larger than a grain of ash. They have translucent plates along their backs and mouths like nested chisels. They work constantly at the fresh crust, shaving away mineral films before they have fully cooled. Their bodies are nearly colorless where the chamber is darkest, but near the seams they become stained with red, blue-black, or yellow compounds. In motion they resemble sparks trapped under glass. They are born in the warm dust and die in it, leaving behind shells so thin that the larger feeders grind them into the surfaces they eat.

Above them move the jointed grazers: low, many-legged animals with wedge-shaped heads and armored throats. They travel in dense files along the ridges, each following the chemical trace left by the one before. They do not see in any ordinary sense. Their forward feelers test heat, pressure, and the faint electrical disturbances that pass through the stone before it shifts. A warming seam sends them away. A cooling vent draws them in. They are forever arriving at the place where the mountain has just made something available.

Their bodies are not built for speed. Nothing here rewards speed for long. A fast animal runs into a wall, a collapse, or another body. The successful ones are patient and heavily jointed, capable of holding themselves against a surface while the chamber moves around them. Some cling to vertical rock for centuries, their limbs sinking gradually into mineral deposits until they become half animal and half fixture. Their offspring emerge from grooves in their sides and disperse without ceremony, already coated in the dust of the parent.

The predators are rarer and harder to distinguish from the terrain. They are long, plated forms with flattened heads and mouths hidden beneath overlapping shields. They do not chase. They insert themselves into the traffic of the colony and wait for pressure to make the decision. When a crack opens, the smaller animals flee along established paths. The predators occupy those paths. Their bodies fold and unfold with the slow precision of machinery, gathering whatever is swept toward them. They consume shell, flesh, and the mineral crust that binds the two together. Their digestion leaves behind a fine black residue that other creatures collect for its salts.

The largest inhabitants hardly seem alive.

They lie across the floor with their backs submerged in cooling stone. Their ribs rise in parallel ridges, each one thick enough to serve as a bridge. Their sides expand at intervals, drawing hot gas through porous organs beneath the shell. Then they contract and release it through vents along the spine. The exhalation may take minutes. Its sound passes through the chamber like a buried thunderclap.

Smaller animals live inside these giants.

They enter through warm openings between the ribs and pass through chambers lined with membranes that collect condensed compounds. Some feed there. Some breed there. Some emerge carrying dust from one side of the chamber to another, as if the great bodies are not animals at all but transit systems, refineries, and nurseries wearing armor. The giants tolerate them because the traffic keeps their internal passages clear. When one finally dies, the colony does not mourn. It dismantles the body over generations. First the soft channels vanish, then the plates, then the embedded ridges. Long after the animal has ceased to exist, its mineral remains support new life.

There are also the sealed ones.

These creatures have no visible legs and almost no exposed body. They press themselves into cracks and wait for the mountain to close around them. Their shells are layered with materials drawn from successive vents, so that each individual records the chemistry of the chamber in bands of color. They may remain dormant through many pressure cycles, waking only when the stone opens enough to expose a new surface. When active, they extend narrow feeding structures into the air and draw in vapor, dust, and the heat of nearby bodies. They are less like animals moving through a landscape than like roots belonging to the rock itself.

Yet even they are not permanent.

The chamber is too young for permanence. Every creature lives beneath the knowledge—if knowledge is the word—that its shelter may become a wall, its feeding ground a sealed pocket, its path a vertical face. Their instincts are therefore not directed toward territory. They do not defend places. They respond to conditions. Heat means retreat or gathering, depending on its pattern. Pressure means stillness. Fresh mineral means feeding. The first vibration of a collapse means movement toward whatever space will exist after it.

Their bodies are made for interruption.

They can fold their legs beneath themselves and become inert. They can lock their jaws shut against dust. They can seal breathing pores with plates grown for that purpose. Some can survive being buried for years, their metabolism reduced to the faintest chemical exchange. Others abandon their bodies entirely during a crisis, leaving behind a hollow shell while the living core contracts into a protected chamber inside it. When the pressure passes, the creature rebuilds itself from within.

This is why the colony seems calm even when the mountain convulses. It does not resist change. It has evolved to become briefly indistinguishable from whatever change produces.

The animals communicate through contact and vibration. A claw tapping twice against a shelf may be warning, invitation, or simply the residue of movement in a crowded passage. The largest bodies transmit slower signals through the floor: pulses that travel farther than sound. Entire sections of the colony can turn, withdraw, or converge without any visible leader. A wave passes from shell to shell. Bodies rearrange. A feeding front forms around a fresh seam. The chamber behaves as though it has one nervous system, though no creature contains it.

Perhaps the nervous system is the chamber itself.

The animals know the mountain through their undersides. They feel the pressure accumulating beneath them. They feel the difference between a crack that will open and one that will merely cool. They feel the mineral vapors before those vapors become visible, and they recognize the long descending shudder that means a support has failed somewhere below. Their world is not made of objects and distances, but of gradients: warmer, denser, thinner, newly exposed, about to break.

At the center, the gathered bodies wait around the raised black knot.

They have been waiting through many cycles. Their shells overlap in pale and dark layers, legs tucked beneath legs, mouthparts folded against the heat. The outermost animals are motionless. Beneath them, others press inward. Beneath those, others are pressed into the floor itself. The entire mass has become a single many-jointed ring.

They are not worshiping the stone. They are not afraid of it in any human way.

They are waiting for release.

When the pulse comes, every body receives it through the floor. The nearest creatures stiffen first. Then the sensation travels outward, and the chamber turns inward around it. The new seam brightens. Plates lift. Gas pushes through.

The animals open their mouths.

What emerges is not a cry but a collective adjustment: claws finding purchase, shells locking together, bodies making room for the first break in the stone. The mountain begins to manufacture its next surface, and the inhabitants gather to become its first occupants.
