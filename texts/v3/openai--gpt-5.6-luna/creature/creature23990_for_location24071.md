What lives on the plateau has learned not to waste movement.

The small herd animals spend most of their lives standing still. They gather in the green hollows where rushes grow tall enough to hide their legs, lowering their narrow faces to the ground and feeding among the moss. Their bodies are long and woolly, built for warmth rather than speed. Each foot divides into several soft, gripping points, and they can climb the slanted faces of the escarpments as easily as other animals cross a field.

They are not tame, though they seem unafraid of being watched. A herd may stand beneath the stars for hours, all of them facing outward toward the cloud sea. When the deep note begins beneath the plateau, they turn together. They do not flee. They listen.

The young are born in the sheltered clefts, where the crooked trees keep a little warmth beneath their black branches. For several weeks they have no voices. They follow their mothers by scent, feeling their way through fog and frost, and learn early that the bare stone is as important as the living ground. The most dangerous places are not always where predators wait. A thin crust over a pool, a bright seam in the rock, a patch of moss stirred by buried machinery—these can kill more surely than teeth.

The predators are pale and nearly without scent.

One kind resembles a cat only because it has four feet and a long body. Its joints are too many, its limbs bending with a flexibility that lets it pass through the silver shrubs without shaking them. It hunts by waiting. A creature may graze beside it for several minutes before recognizing the shape among the leaves. Then the pale animal moves once, quickly and completely, and is gone with its prey into the cleft shadows.

The scavengers are less graceful. They gather beside condensation basins in small groups, long-backed and heavy-headed, their skin the color of old snow. They remain motionless until something comes to drink. In fog they are difficult to distinguish from stones. Their patience is so complete that moss sometimes begins to grow across their backs.

Something larger lives beneath the green.

It moves through the moss as a whale might move beneath water, displacing the surface in a slow, widening wake. The plants rise behind it and settle again. Sometimes a line of root and soil travels uphill against the wind. Sometimes a whole basin trembles, though nothing emerges. The animals give this hidden thing a wide passage. Even the predators avoid its paths.

No one knows whether it is one creature or many connected bodies. It may be an animal with an immense buried shell. It may be a colony whose members have forgotten where one ends and another begins. The roots of the plateau trees descend toward it. When the amber light travels through the ground, the thing beneath the moss follows.

The birds belong more to the air than to the plateau.

They have wide, dark wings and almost no need to flap. They rise from the cliffs and are carried for hours along invisible currents. Their undersides are translucent, veined with fine bones and blood vessels. Pools below them catch their shadows as patterns of moving light.

They nest on the highest shelves, where the wind is strongest and no mammal can reach them. Their nests are made from shed mineral crust, grass, and the transparent skins of insects. The birds leave no feathers behind. When they molt, each feather dissolves into a colorless powder that settles over the rock and feeds the moss.

The smallest creatures gather around the machines.

They are blind, soft-bodied animals with silver whiskers and many delicate feet. Warmth draws them from the cracks, but they feed on the metallic salts that sweat from the old housings. They cluster around open valves in dense, pulsing knots. Their bodies brighten and dim with the rhythm of the buried engines.

They do not seem to be harmed by the machines. The reverse may be true. Where the creatures gather, the mechanisms run more smoothly. A blocked pipe clears. A seized wheel shifts by a fraction. The animals crawl into narrow vents and emerge hours later coated in blue dust.

Moths live in the insulation of the old engines. Their larvae eat almost nothing except the stale, fibrous material packed around the conduits. They remain dormant for years, perhaps for generations, until one of the machines begins to wake.

Then they hatch together.

They pour from the housings in a pale cloud, so numerous that the stars disappear behind their wings. They have no visible markings, no mouths made for feeding, and no interest in the plants. They rise around the turning wheels and settle upon the warm stone. By morning they are gone. The plateau retains only a faint powder in the cracks and the memory of having been briefly obscured.

There are beings that do not fit comfortably among animals.

At night, when the amber current enters the old channels, figures sometimes appear along its path. They are low to the ground and jointed like the machinery, but covered in moss and hair. They move in groups without making a sound. One will pause beside a pool while the others continue, and the pool will tremble as if recognizing it.

They may be the descendants of creatures that once served the machines. They may be machines that have learned to walk. Their limbs are too thin for their bodies, and their faces are hidden beneath plates of black mineral. No eyes can be seen, but they turn toward every living thing that watches them.

They never approach the animals directly.

Instead they pass among the green places, touching stones, inspecting roots, and placing their hands against the walls of buried engines. Sometimes they remove a piece of dead machinery and carry it away. Sometimes they gather around a failing tree. By dawn, the tree has put out new leaves, or has been quietly uprooted and left beside the ravine as if it had never belonged there.

The plateau’s oldest inhabitants are the machines themselves.

They have no single body. Their organs are scattered through the mountain: wheels behind cliffs, pistons beneath pools, chimneys opening from bare shelves, warm conduits descending into the roots of trees. Their movements are separated by miles and years, yet they behave as parts of one slow metabolism.

They drink heat from below. They draw moisture from clouds. They exhale through vents and chimneys. They shed mineral dust, which becomes soil. Their pulses wake insects, move water, and turn the leaves of plants toward buried warmth.

Whether they are alive depends on how narrowly life is defined.

They do not breed, but they replace their parts. They do not speak, but they answer changes in pressure, temperature, and light. They do not remember in any human manner, yet the same channels have opened and closed for thousands of years. Each machine seems to know the others through the stone. A wheel turns in one valley, and a valve opens beyond the ridge. A spring dries, and somewhere below, a piston begins its long ascent.

The animals know this rhythm.

They shelter near it in winter. They avoid it when the ground grows too warm. They follow the lines of light beneath the moss, not because they understand where those lines lead, but because generations have taught them that life gathers there. Roots find the conduits. Insects find the roots. Herds find the insects. Predators find the herds.

Everything feeds on the hidden motion.

Even the trees seem to listen. Their ancient trunks lean toward the buried engines, their waxy leaves trembling when the deep note passes through the ground. They are not parasites of the machines, nor are the machines servants of the trees. Together they form a relationship too old for either to remember beginning: heat becoming water, water becoming moss, moss sheltering animals, animals carrying spores and salts back into the cracks.

And beneath all of it, something keeps watch.

When the line of lights crosses the plateau after midnight, every creature becomes still. Birds fold their wings. Herd animals lift their heads. Predators pause among the shrubs. The blind silver creatures press themselves against the warm pipes. Even the unseen body beneath the moss stops moving.

The lights travel through the empty spaces as though following a path that exists below the visible world. They pass the trees and the pools, the wheels and the chimney, the old channels where rivers once ran. Wherever they go, the plateau briefly remembers itself as a single organism.

Then the lights sink away.

The animals resume their lives. The moss closes over the disturbed stone. Water begins to gather again beneath the ledges. Far below, another machine answers with one slow turn.

Nothing living there is in a hurry.

The plateau belongs to creatures that can wait through frost, darkness, drought, and silence. Some have fur, some have leaves, some have wings, and some are made of iron buried in rock. They are not separate inhabitants so much as different expressions of the same patience: life spread thinly across immense distances, gathering wherever the mountain breathes.
