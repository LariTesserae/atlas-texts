They are called the Residents, though residence is not quite the right condition for them.

They do not own rooms. They are permitted to recur in them.

Most of them are narrow, quiet figures with bodies adapted to the city’s inconsistencies: long hands for testing walls, soft-soled feet for the porous ground, eyes filmed with a translucent membrane that protects them from reflections arriving late. Their clothing is layered and close-fitting, made from fabrics that darken when a warm band approaches. Each person carries a small instrument for measuring uncertainty—not because uncertainty can be eliminated, but because every door, passage, and stair requires a different amount of it.

They are rarely seen in full. Behind glass, one notices a shoulder turning, a hand suspended over a panel, the outline of a head bowed above a basin. In the streets they move quickly through the narrow spaces between structures, pausing at corners to determine whether the corner is still where it was. They do not speak unless necessary. Their voices are low and carefully formed, and when words leave them they are often collected by the walls, stored in the city’s porous materials, and returned at inconvenient times.

The Residents are not born in any ordinary sense. They begin as arrangements.

A room left warm for too long may develop a pulse beneath its floor. A maintenance chamber may produce, over several seasons, a second set of footsteps. Sometimes a silhouette appears behind an opaque wall and remains there until someone names the room. Sometimes a person emerges from the central basin wet with black water, already wearing the clothes of a life that has not yet occurred.

No one agrees on whether these are births or arrivals.

The oldest Residents have become difficult to distinguish from the systems that sustain them. Their skin contains fine mineral lines that brighten when pipes are under pressure. Their bones have grown porous, tuned to the low vibration of the foundations. They can place a palm against a wall and feel distant changes: a bridge beginning to unfold, a vacant lot acquiring its first room, a door opening somewhere it should not. Younger Residents learn this sensitivity as a practical skill. The oldest possess it as instinct.

They work in maintenance, though maintenance is less a profession than a form of attention. They guide condensation toward the lower channels. They persuade staircases to remain staircases. They carry objects back to the places where they were found, speaking their names before lifting them. They repair maps by marking not where a street is, but how long it intends to stay.

Some tend the heat.

When a warm front moves beneath the city, they walk ahead of it carrying lamps filled with blue vapor. They open sleeping chambers, wake those whose bodies have entered the slow winter state, and seal rooms behind the warmth as it passes. The Residents sleep in intervals, never all at once. Someone must remain awake to observe the city’s continued formation.

Others are assigned to the basin.

They sit around its dark water in small, widely separated groups. Their task is not to interpret what lies below. Interpretation is considered dangerous. They record the lines as they divide and redivide, noting which ones resemble foundations, which resemble veins, and which seem to be searching for a way out.

The basin has reproduced many things: tools, stones, rooms, fragments of machinery, once an entire stairwell at the scale of a finger. It has never reproduced a Resident exactly. Those who spend too long watching it sometimes return with small discrepancies. Their reflections begin to anticipate them. Their voices emerge from drains before they have spoken. They forget the names of objects but remember where the objects will eventually be found.

The city’s stranger inhabitants belong to no clear category.

Maintenance machines travel above the streets on jointed rails, carrying droplets in delicate cups. They have no faces, but they pause when addressed. If thanked, they may alter their route. If ignored, they continue their work for decades, moving moisture from wall to wall until a new passage opens.

There are also the folded animals. They live in cracks, drainage channels, and the warm undersides of bridges. The Residents leave them darkness in shallow dishes. In return, the animals consume shadows that have detached from their owners. One can tell where such a creature has passed by the clean, impossible edges left around objects.

And beneath the lowest levels, something larger moves.

It has never been seen directly. Its presence is known through pressure: a tremor passing up through the pavement, a sudden rearrangement of pipes, a long exhalation through the fissures. The oldest Residents call it the Underbody. They do not worship it. Worship assumes intention, and the Underbody may have none. It is perhaps the unfinished city beneath the city, or the thought from which the settlement is still being assembled.

The Residents fear it without expecting harm.

Their greatest fear is completion.

They understand that if the city ever finishes becoming itself, many of them may no longer be necessary. Doors will know where they lead. Water will cease to wander. Rooms will stop acquiring windows onto unborn structures. The people will have nowhere left to maintain, measure, or doubt.

So they preserve small irregularities.

A corridor is left one degree too narrow. A map is corrected only halfway. A warm front is delayed by several minutes so that someone sleeping in an upper chamber will dream of summer. Objects are occasionally misnamed, not maliciously, but to keep them from being carried too far. In the central basin, the Residents place things whose meanings they do not want the city to reproduce: a broken cup, a dead moth, a child’s tooth, a word written on wet paper.

They are not trying to stop the settlement from forming.

They are trying to remain inside its formation.

At night, when the winter sky presses low against the towers, they gather in the warm bands beneath the bridges. They sit close together without touching. Their faces appear briefly in the dark glass, delayed by several minutes, still arriving after they have turned away.

Then someone hears a sentence emerging from a drain.

Someone else finds a new foundation beneath the pavement.

And all of them become still, listening—not for an answer, but for evidence that the city has made room for one more thing.
