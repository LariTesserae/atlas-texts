The people of the basin are called the Levelers, though no one remembers whether the name refers to their work or their temperament.

There are not many of them. A few hundred live in the housing blocks, divided into shifts that overlap only briefly. They are pump operators, pipe-fitters, filtration clerks, boiler tenders, bird wardens, seal inspectors, and the crews who descend into the channels beneath the concrete. Their occupations are narrow because the settlement itself is narrow. Almost everyone is trained to do more than one task, but no one is permitted to improvise at the wrong moment.

They wear layered clothing made for wet heat: gray rubber coats, sealed boots, gloves with replaceable palms, hoods that leave the face exposed only when necessary. The clothes are marked with colored bands indicating which temperature zones and chemical rooms the wearer may enter. Their garments are always drying and never dry. Condensation pearls on their sleeves. Mineral salts stiffen the seams. At the end of a shift, each person hangs their clothing beneath a fan and stands for several minutes while the air blows across them.

The workers are pale from living beneath roofs and vapor. Their hands are darkened by grease, their knuckles scaled with mineral residue. Many have a faint orange line around the wrists and throat where runoff has stained the skin. Their hair is usually cut close, less for fashion than to prevent it from catching in machinery or holding the smell of brine.

They are quiet people outside the works.

The machinery has taken their loudness from them. They speak in short, deliberate phrases, saving their breath and their voices. In the housing blocks, doors close softly. Meals are eaten in communal rooms where the walls tremble with the distant pumps. Conversation pauses whenever a pressure valve opens somewhere beneath the floor. Everyone knows the sounds of the settlement: the ordinary cough of a compressor, the thin whine of an overloaded fan, the deep knock that means a pipe has swallowed sediment. A change in the noise is more alarming than a siren.

Their children learn the system before they learn the history.

They know the painted marks on the tanks, the warning colors around the drains, the difference between clean steam and discharge vapor. They play along the covered passages, collecting dead insects from the lamps and setting them in rows on the warm concrete. They race small metal washers through the gutter channels, watching which current carries them fastest. The older children are allowed onto the lower catwalks, where they stand behind the railings and look down into the dark settling ponds.

No one tells them not to look for the eels. The eels have been there longer than the children, and perhaps longer than the settlement.

The inhabitants have a practical respect for the birds. White birds nest wherever they can find warmth and shelter, and the workers build around them. A nest in a pump housing is not removed unless it threatens the machinery. Eggs are marked with blue paint. The bird wardens relocate them only when the heat rises beyond a known limit. Otherwise the birds are part of the works: their droppings are washed from the catwalks, their feathers clog the intake screens, and their calls vanish beneath the engine noise.

The people accept this without resentment. The settlement belongs to everything that can maintain its place within it.

There are rituals, though the Levelers would not call them that. At the beginning of each shift, a worker touches the nearest pressure gauge with two fingers. At the end, they record the waterline, the heat, and the condition of the seals in a book kept under a metal cover. Before a new pump is installed, the crew pours a cup of clean water over its housing. When someone dies, their tools are cleaned, labeled, and returned to circulation. Nothing is buried in the basin. The ground is too warm, too wet, and too full of buried pipes to accept the dead.

The bodies are sent away through the same covered route as the food deliveries.

The Levelers do not speak much about leaving. Some have left, especially in their youth. They return with stories of open land, wind that moves without passing through a fan, soil soft enough to sink into, and nights without warning lamps. Most come back within a few years. They find those places wasteful and unmeasured. Water lies in places where no one has drawn it. Heat escapes. Plants grow without permission. Roads wander.

Outside, they say, nothing keeps its level.

Within the basin, there is one figure everyone knows but few have met: the Keeper of the Shaft. The position may once have been a formal one. Now it is held by a single person who lives in a narrow room beneath the central works and monitors the oldest circular shaft.

The Keeper is not a priest, though people sometimes bring them offerings of clean water, new gloves, or small polished stones. They descend at dawn and return after the lamps come on, moving through the vapor with a hood over their face. They inspect the iron braces, measure the rising warmth, and listen to the shaft’s interior with a metal disk pressed against the black lining.

The current Keeper is an elderly woman named Senn, whose voice has been reduced to a rough murmur by decades of steam. She knows which cracks are new, though none of them widen. She knows the rhythm of the vapor and can distinguish the sound of water striking stone from the sound of something moving below it.

No one asks what might be moving below.

Senn says the shaft is stable. She says this every day. The people believe her because stability is the closest thing they have to faith.

At night, when the shift bells have rung and the basin is left to its automatic operations, the inhabitants gather beneath the housing roofs. They sit on warm steps and covered walkways. They watch bats pass over the runoff channels and listen to frogs calling from the culverts. Some mend gloves. Some gamble with washers. Some stand at the edge of the lit ground and stare into the steam.

They do not dream of conquering the basin or escaping it. They dream in measurements: a pressure gauge settling exactly at its mark, a valve closing without resistance, a tank reaching its painted line at the appointed hour. Their deepest pleasure is not success but continuation—the satisfaction of finding the same water where it was expected, the same heat beneath the same stone, the same birds returning to the same beam.

They are not owners of the place.

They are its attendants, its witnesses, perhaps its organs. They keep the water moving so the basin does not fill. They keep the heat contained so the stone does not soften. They keep the pipes sealed, the channels clear, the lamps burning, the birds undisturbed.

In return, the settlement gives them everything they need and nothing more.

It gives them shelter, work, warm food, mineral-stained clothing, and a life measured closely enough that surprise has almost been engineered out of it. It gives them a permanent dusk in which every wet surface reflects the lamps. It gives them the deep, reassuring pulse underfoot.

And beneath all of them, beneath the tanks and passages and polished black paving, the water continues to rise.

The Levelers listen.

They record the height.

They wait for it to reach the mark—and no higher.
