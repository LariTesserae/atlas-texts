What lives on the plateau is not one species.

There are creatures, certainly. There are things that crawl, leap, burrow, cling, swarm, and cross the open flats beneath the hard white sky. But the deepest inhabitant is the exchange itself: the slow commerce between root and stone, colony and colony, living tissue and the useful remains of the dead.

The plateau is inhabited by systems that have learned to move.

The smallest of these are called nothing, because nothing here carries far enough to become a name. They live in seams of mineral and beneath the shelves of the great mats, in places where the wind is broken and the chemical pressures remain steady. They are narrow, many-jointed things with bodies made from layered membranes and metallic cartilage. Their limbs end in fans, hooks, or grinding plates. Some are no larger than a hand. Others grow to the length of a person, though no one measures them by that comparison.

Their bodies are almost entirely function.

A crawler may possess six legs, but no head in the usual sense. Its forward organs are distributed along its underside: pressure pits, charge-sensitive hairs, chemical tongues, and small lenses that detect changes in reflected light. It does not look ahead. It samples the ground beneath and around itself, building a map from heat, vibration, acidity, and electrical potential.

It knows where a plant has opened.

It knows where a root has died.

It knows where something has passed recently enough to leave a trail of altered dust.

When the great plants become active, these small creatures emerge in thousands. They climb stems and gather at valves, where they drink concentrated fluids from living tissue. They do not pierce the plants so much as enter into them. A plant opens a chamber; the creatures pour inside. They remove sediment, harvest excess salts, and carry away fungal threads that would otherwise overgrow the valves. In return, they receive food, shelter, and the warmth of internal reactions.

Some plants cannot function without them.

The small creatures are not tame. Tameness is a relationship too narrow for the plateau. They are organs that happen to have legs. When a colony weakens, they abandon it without hesitation, streaming across the mineral flats toward a more productive growth. Their migrations resemble black cracks spreading over the ground. Behind them, the abandoned plant may survive for centuries or collapse within hours.

The larger walkers are built for distance.

They have six or eight limbs, jointed backward against the wind, and broad feet that unfold into gripping lattices when they touch the mineral. Their bodies are low, armored, and long, with internal chambers that store reactive gels. Some carry transparent sacks of cultivated bacteria-like tissue. Others bear racks of cutting organs along their backs. They move in columns between the colonies, following pressure grooves and old electrical paths.

They are harvesters, but not grazers.

When a tower dies, the walkers arrive. They strip the outer membranes, crack the mineralized roots, and separate useful material from inert residue. One kind removes the oily tissues and stores them in its own body. Another collects the brittle internal fibers and carries them to a distant colony. A third feeds only on the chemical light of the decomposing remains, passing over the corpse until it has cooled.

The work is coordinated without speech.

A walker releases a pulse through its feet. The pulse travels through the ground, altered by every root lattice and buried seam it crosses. Other walkers feel the disturbance in their limbs. They do not receive instructions. They receive a changing field of possibilities: abundance to the east, danger below, an open route through the western basin, a dead tower ready for separation.

When many walkers gather, the ground trembles with their decisions.

There are also fliers, though they do not resemble birds or insects. Their wings are membranes reinforced with living veins, and their bodies are mostly hollow. They fill themselves with warm gases stolen from the exhalations of the tall groves. When a pressure front arrives, they open their bodies into sails and allow themselves to be carried hundreds of kilometers.

They feed on airborne tissue, but they also carry it.

A flier may emerge from one colony coated in reproductive grains and disappear into the upper brightness. Its body changes color as the grains react with its skin. Some are bright blue with stored charge. Some become almost invisible, their surfaces reflecting only the sky. When they descend, they scatter the material across the landing place and leave behind a new beginning.

Their migrations are not seasonal. They are triggered by imbalance.

Too much pressure in one basin. Too little mineral in another. A reproductive storm. The collapse of an old root network. The opening of a new seam.

The fliers respond to these changes before the plants do, or perhaps their response is one of the ways the plants do.

There are beings that cannot be assigned to either plant or animal.

They begin as rooted structures, but detach themselves after maturity. Their bodies are tall and thin, with bundles of sensory filaments trailing from their sides. They travel upright, bent almost double against the wind, planting their lower limbs into the stone for a few minutes at a time. Through these limbs they read the buried networks. Through their upper surfaces they read the sky.

They are called, when translated into the habits of speaking creatures, the listeners.

A listener may remain beside a colony for years without moving. It anchors itself to a root shelf and extends its filaments into the surrounding ground. Electrical pulses pass through it. Chemical signals accumulate in its tissues. It absorbs fragments of dying organisms, mineral frost, dust from distant reproductive storms. Slowly, it becomes a record of the place.

When it leaves, it carries the colony with it.

Not physically. The colony remains where it grew. But the listener carries its pressures, its failures, its unfinished chemical patterns, and the memory of its neighboring roots. At another colony, it unfolds its filaments and releases these patterns into the ground. The receiving roots change in response. A dormant valve opens. A defensive compound begins to form. A reproductive structure alters its timing by several days.

The listener is not an individual in the way a creature from a wetter world might be. Its body is a temporary crossing between two colonies. It exists to make distant life less distant.

Some listeners travel in groups. They stand in circles on the mineral flats, their lower limbs driven into the stone, their filaments joined by arcs of blue electricity. The wind tears at them. Dust crosses their bodies. For hours or days they remain connected, exchanging information too slowly to be called conversation and too precisely to be called instinct.

At the end of the gathering, they separate.

Some return to their original colonies. Some continue onward. Some have become so altered by what they have absorbed that they no longer belong anywhere. These wanderers walk until their tissues fail, then root themselves in a bare depression and become a new colony.

The oldest inhabitants are not mobile at all.

They are the great root minds beneath the plateau.

No single root system spans the continent, but many extend farther than a traveler can see. They descend through stone, divide around mineral faults, and join with neighboring systems through narrow chemical bridges. A colony may appear isolated while its deepest tissues communicate with growths hundreds of kilometers away.

These networks do not think in images or words. They do not possess a central chamber where decisions wait to be made. Their thought is a change in pressure, a redistribution of charge, a delay in opening, a redirection of nourishment.

A root network can remember drought without ever having known water.

It can remember attack as a pattern of compounds.

It can recognize the approach of a migrating herd by the vibration of its feet and decide, through thousands of local reactions, whether to open itself, harden its outer tissue, or release a chemical lure.

When two vast colonies meet, they do not always fight. Sometimes they fuse. Sometimes they exchange only certain minerals. Sometimes they build a narrow zone of sterile ground between them, a border that no root crosses and no small creature survives for long.

These borders are among the few places on the plateau that seem truly empty.

They are not empty. They are decisions.

The mobile creatures understand the root networks as weather. They do not worship them, because worship implies distance, and nothing on the plateau is distant from its own conditions. But they orient their lives around them. They travel when the roots signal abundance. They hide when the roots contract. They gather beneath the towers before an exhalation, knowing the blast will scatter edible tissue across the flats.

There may once have been a distinction between those who fed on plants and those who served them.

It has eroded.

A harvester carries plant tissue in its body and returns mineral compounds to the roots. A flier distributes reproductive material while feeding on it. A listener transports memory between colonies but becomes a colony when it dies. The small crawlers damage the plants in order to keep them alive. The large walkers dismantle the dead so thoroughly that no corpse can remain an object for long.

Nothing living is outside the system.

Even the predators are necessary. There are long, pale hunters that hide beneath reflective mats and sense movement through the ground. They seize walkers and tear them open with rotary mouthparts. Yet their waste is gathered by root-fibers, and their bones become mineral scaffolds around which new growth forms. Their violence is not opposed to the plateau’s order. It is one of the methods by which order continues.

The inhabitants do not build cities.

They build gradients.

A colony’s edge may be softened for small creatures, while its center becomes electrically lethal. A migration route may be marked by a sequence of low, warm mats growing from the mineral. A gathering place may exist only during certain atmospheric pressures, when the air becomes dense enough for the fliers to descend. The plateau’s settlements are temporary arrangements of chemistry, sound, and survivable temperature.

They have no roads because the ground itself remembers passage.

They have no written language because patterns can be grown directly into tissue.

They have no monuments because the dead are more useful when dismantled.

What they possess instead is continuity. A body fails, but its components enter another body. A colony collapses, but its pressures persist in a listener. A reproductive storm destroys one region and establishes another. The individual is not preserved. The process is.

At night, the inhabitants become visible in fragments.

Small creatures move beneath the mats, carrying pinpricks of green light in their transparent organs. Fliers cross the upper dark with their wings illuminated by internal charge. Walkers gather around the remains of a fallen tower, their bodies opening and closing as they sort the corpse. Listeners stand on the ridges, filaments raised into the wind.

Below them, the root networks pulse.

The pulses travel outward through stone and salt, crossing the bare flats beneath the feet of every creature. A distant colony answers. Then another. The plateau does not speak in a single voice. It produces layers: the low vibration of roots, the metallic chords of leaves, the concussion of gas, the small electrical chatter of bodies moving through one another’s lives.

Nothing can hear all of it.

But everything is touched by it.

And so whoever lives there is never wholly alone, even on the empty mineral plains. Every creature carries part of another organism within itself. Every movement alters a network. Every death opens a pathway for something else.

The plateau is inhabited by travelers, scavengers, symbionts, hunters, singers, and rooted minds.

More truly, it is inhabited by the refusal of anything to remain separate.
