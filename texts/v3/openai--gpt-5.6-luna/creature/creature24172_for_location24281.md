The people of the plateau are not easily seen at first.

From a distance, they resemble maintenance equipment: narrow figures moving along the raised roads, pausing beside access hatches, descending into drainage trenches. Their bodies are wrapped in pale, water-shedding layers that catch the sun and give nothing back. They walk slowly, not because they are weak, but because haste is dangerous on the wet stone. A careless foot can break a mineral shelf, open a hot seam, or send a vibration through a channel that runs beneath half the city.

They are built for saturation.

Their skin is dark beneath the surface, almost blue-black, and crossed with fine translucent membranes that open when the air becomes too dry. Along the throat, ribs, and backs of the hands are narrow vents, usually closed, through which they take in dissolved minerals from condensation. They do not drink in the way lowland creatures drink. Water enters them everywhere: through the soles of their feet, through the soft folds behind the knees, through the palms pressed against a wet railing. They can survive in dry rooms for a time, but they become irritable and slow, and their skin loses its faint inner shine.

Most of them are low and broad, with four weight-bearing limbs and two smaller upper arms used for tools, valves, and delicate work. Their joints are covered in overlapping plates of flexible ceramic, stained by the different waters of the city. A worker who spends years at the cold reservoirs develops white shoulders. Those assigned to the geothermal districts turn rust-red around the wrists and ankles. People who maintain the high pressure columns acquire green-black markings where certain metals settle in their skin.

Their faces are smooth and spare. They have eyes, but sight is not their most important sense. Along the jaw and temples are rows of minute pores that register changes in pressure. They feel distant sluice gates opening. They know when an animal has entered a culvert several streets away. A break in an underground pipe reaches them as a pressure ache before it becomes audible, and a dying pump is perceived as a wrongness in the bones.

They communicate largely through contact with the city.

A hand laid flat on a bridge rail can transmit a sequence of taps into the metal. Fingers pressed against a pipe can send a message along the water inside it. The messages travel slowly and are often answered by movement elsewhere: a valve turning, a lamp opening, a grate lifting from the pavement. Conversation is distributed through infrastructure. Two people may stand far apart, one at a pumping station and one beside a reservoir, speaking through a chain of wet steel and stone.

When they speak aloud, their voices are quiet and layered. They produce little sound in the open air, where it carries too far, but inside the maintenance tunnels their speech becomes a series of low clicks, throat pulses, and long vibrating tones. Words are less important than changes in pressure and duration. A warning, a greeting, and an expression of grief may contain the same note, distinguished by the way it travels through a wall.

They do not have a word equivalent to solitude.

Everyone belongs to a water route.

At birth, a child is assigned not a family name but a source: a spring seam, a condensation tower, a roof basin, an underground reservoir, or one of the old pipes whose origin has been forgotten. This is not a permanent identity. As the child grows, they are moved through the city, learning the behavior of different waters. They spend time with cold collectors, where the condensation gathers in clear skins over ceramic plates. They work beside the warm vents and learn which creatures can be handled and which must be left alone. They descend into the old quarry chambers, where water rises from the dark in irregular pulses and the walls are alive with mineral growth.

Their education is mostly physical. They are taught to distinguish safe wetness from dangerous wetness, clean rust from active corrosion, a healthy pipe from one that has become brittle inside. They learn the moods of the plateau: how the air tightens before a storm below the clouds, how water behaves after a pressure shift, how the animals gather before a buried seam breaks open.

Every child is brought, eventually, to the edge of the escarpment.

There, with the cloud valley below and the city behind them, they are shown the hidden world beneath the shelf. They cannot see the forests or rivers that may exist under the clouds. They are taught not to imagine those places too clearly. The plateau has survived by paying attention to what can be measured. Still, most people keep some private image of the lowlands: a green surface under rain, a darkness full of roots, a place where water falls from the sky instead of rising through stone.

The city’s inhabitants do not farm. They cultivate conditions.

They maintain bacterial films in shallow basins, encourage mineral-feeding organisms around warm outflows, and move animal populations when one district becomes chemically exhausted. Their food is gathered from the living margins of machinery: pale sheets scraped from drainage walls, soft bodies collected under bridges, dense mineral gels grown in heated tanks. Nothing is hunted for sport. Nothing is allowed to become too abundant. An animal species that begins to clog a channel is not exterminated; its water is changed, its routes are redirected, and new predators are introduced from another district.

The larger shell-backed grazers are part of this management. The people do not own them, exactly, but they know their lineages and recognize individual temperaments. A grazer that polishes pavement too deeply is guided away with heat lamps and resonant rods. A wounded one may be brought into a warm yard, where attendants wash its shell and pack the damaged plates with wet ceramic paste. The animals tolerate this with the remote patience they show toward everything. Some return to the same workers years later.

There are families that live close to animal congregations and families that never enter the wet margins. There are people who sleep in dry, elevated districts, sealing themselves behind layered doors, and people who sleep in open alcoves beneath the roads, allowing condensation to gather directly on their bodies. The former consider the latter reckless. The latter consider the former unfinished.

The old inhabitants say the city was not built by them.

According to their oldest records, the plateau was once occupied by a different kind of people, taller and more numerous, who made the first roads, towers, quarry terraces, and pressure columns. These predecessors may have resembled the current inhabitants, or may have been entirely unlike them. The records are mostly diagrams of flows: arrows entering squares, branching lines, shaded reservoirs, notations for heat and contamination. Faces are almost never depicted.

The current people have inherited the machines but not the certainty of their purpose.

They repair what still works. They reinforce what threatens to collapse. They leave the sealed structures sealed. There are districts where doors have not opened in centuries, though water still passes beneath them and pale animals nest around their foundations. Sometimes a maintenance crew discovers a room full of dry instruments, or a stair descending into a level not marked on any map. They do not immediately explore. A new space can alter pressure, temperature, and migration patterns. Discovery is treated as a form of disturbance.

This caution governs their religion, though they do not call it that.

They believe the plateau is a body that has outlived its maker. The escarpments are bones. The pipes are vessels. The pumps are organs that have learned to imitate intention. Their myths describe an ancient drying, when the city nearly died because the water forgot its routes. The first ancestors supposedly listened for seven days beneath the ground and restored circulation by opening themselves to the mineral seams.

Because of this, death is not burial.

The dead are washed in water gathered from their assigned source. Their bodies are placed into a chamber beside a functioning channel, where heat and mineral films gradually soften them. Over time, the remains are reduced to salts, plates, and dark sediment. These are mixed into repair mortar, reservoir linings, animal treatment paste, or the ceramic seals used in household pipes. A person returns to the city not as memory alone, but as material.

The living visit these places rarely. They do not need to. Every repaired wall may contain someone they knew. Every warm basin may carry the dissolved trace of generations.

Their settlements are quieter than the machinery around them. They move in small groups, usually three or four, and pause often to touch walls, channels, and grates. At intersections, they gather around exposed water not to talk but to exchange condition: fatigue, injury, weather, the presence of predators, the changing chemistry of a distant district. A palm dipped into a basin can tell another person whether the water is safe. A ripple sent across its surface can say that someone has arrived.

At night, they become more active.

The heat is still trapped in the stone, but the glare has weakened. People emerge from shaded rooms and climb onto the roofs, where they inspect the thin films of condensation. Some wear reflective harnesses and walk the upper gantries, checking for blockages. Others travel beyond the lit districts in groups, following the movements of the animals. The plateau is easiest to read then. Warmth rises through the rock in visible distortions. Water shines along hidden channels. The entire city reveals its circulation.

They stand quietly above it, listening through their feet.

Sometimes, when the pumps are steady and no pressure alarm is sounding, they lie down on the wet pavement. The stone holds the day’s heat. Condensation gathers on their backs. Beneath them, the buried water moves from one basin to another, passing under houses, factories, animal yards, and the long empty avenues.

They can feel the whole city breathing.

Not metaphorically. Not quite.

The water rises. The heat leaves. The mineral crust forms and breaks. Somewhere far away, a valve opens. A creature steps into a channel. The ripple enters the dark network under the street and travels toward them, weakened but legible.

Then one of the people lifts their head, places a hand against the ground, and answers.
