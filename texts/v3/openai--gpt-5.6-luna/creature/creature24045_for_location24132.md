What lives in the settlement is not a population in the ordinary sense. There are no houses with lamps in their windows, no smoke, no voices carrying from one occupied courtyard to another. The inhabitants do not keep to rooms. They keep to thresholds: the undersides of stones, the dark between two courses of masonry, the places where water has paused long enough to leave a memory of itself.

They are called, when they are called anything, the **dwellers beneath the damp**.

Most of the year they are mistaken for features of the ruin. A pale ridge along a wall may be the folded back of one sleeping beneath the mineral crust. A line of black beads in a gutter may be a row of closed eyes. The narrow, jointed impressions that appear in silt after a flood are usually dismissed as the tracks of insects, though they are too regular, and sometimes lead upward over a vertical wall before ending beneath an intact arch.

They are small when seen alone. A full-grown one might stand as high as a person’s hip, though its body is difficult to measure because it has no firm outline. It is made of layered membranes, translucent skin, and thin plates of gray material that resemble slate or old shell. Water moves visibly through it. When dry, it contracts into a compact, many-limbed shape and hides in cracks no wider than a finger. When wet, it unfolds. Its limbs lengthen, its body loosens, and fine filaments spread from its joints to drink from the surrounding stone.

Their heads are narrow and unfinished-looking, with no mouths that can be seen. Instead, there is a vertical opening beneath the face, sealed by overlapping transparent folds. They use it to sense changes in pressure and moisture. They can detect a cloud forming beyond the mountains. They know, long before the first drop falls, which cisterns will fill and which walls will split. They gather in the upper galleries before the mist arrives, waiting beneath the coldest stones with their bodies pressed flat.

Then the city begins to drink, and they wake.

They emerge slowly, not with the suddenness of animals but with the patient separation of layers. A hand detaches from a wall. A shoulder unfolds from the black seam around a basin. Several pale figures rise from a shallow pool without disturbing its surface. They move through the settlement in silence, following routes that no longer exist on any visible plan. They descend stairways buried under later floors. They pass through blocked corridors by entering the walls and coming out on the other side, leaving behind only a dampened outline and a faint mineral smell.

When they walk, they do not place their feet one after another. They distribute their weight across all their limbs, touching stone at many points at once. Their passage makes the soft sound of wet cloth drawn over rock.

They are not builders, though the city was built for them.

The oldest cisterns contain niches of a size and shape that match their bodies. Along the undersides of certain arches are shallow grooves, worn smooth by repeated contact. In the lower chambers, where the newer foundations have not sealed them away, there are long galleries lined with dark glass. The dwellers gather there during the dry season, packed close together in layers, each one reduced to a thin, sleeping shape. They can remain dormant for years. Perhaps decades. The settlement’s cycles are long enough that a generation may awaken only a handful of times.

No one knows whether they remember the people who made the first city. The signs suggest they do, but the signs are difficult to read.

They preserve objects without understanding them, or understand them without preserving what they meant. Bronze tools are arranged in circular pools. Broken statues are turned face-down beneath dripping ledges. Fragments of painted tile are set into walls where condensation gathers, their colors kept bright by the constant damp. In one square, they have built a collection of door handles along the rim of a dry basin. In another, hundreds of small stones have been carried from the upper galleries and sorted by shade.

Sometimes they imitate the old inhabitants. A group will stand beneath the remains of a roof, facing one another in the pattern of a conversation. They will climb a staircase and pause where a doorway once opened. They will gather in a public square during heavy rain, arranging themselves around the empty center as if awaiting a ceremony whose words have been washed away.

They do not appear to speak, but they communicate through the city.

A touch against a cistern wall sends a low pulse through the foundations. A rapid tapping on a gutter means that water has been found below. A sustained pressure beneath a paving stone can warn of a collapse days before it occurs. During the wet season, the whole settlement carries their messages: knocks beneath the streets, tremors in the reservoirs, a sudden change in the note of an arch.

The hollow pulse heard below the western terraces is not the sound of a sealed reservoir.

It is a gathering.

The dwellers are not numerous enough to fill the city. Even when all of them are awake, they occupy only a fraction of it. They collect around water, then disperse through the empty avenues. One may be seen crossing a flooded street, its body reflected in the black surface below. Another may be standing motionless within a ruined doorway, thin as a hanging curtain. From a distance they look like fragments of mist caught against the walls. Up close, they have a troubling density, as if each one contains a small piece of the mountain.

They avoid bright, direct sunlight. Under the ordinary cold daylight, they retreat beneath paving stones and into the shaded sides of buildings. The few that remain above ground become rigid and pale, their surfaces clouding until they resemble chalk. They are not killed by dryness, but dryness makes them indistinct. A watcher might look directly at one and see only a standing slab, a spill of gravel, or a trick of the light.

At dusk, they become bolder. The dark animal that appears beside the water is not an animal at all, though it moves on four low limbs and keeps its head near the ground. It drinks from the pool without bending. Its body opens along the spine, revealing rows of clear inner membranes that catch the last light. For a moment it seems filled with a dim blue fire. Then the membranes close, the figure lowers itself, and it slips into a crack between two stones.

The smallest of them live in the drainage works. They are no larger than a hand and move in swarms through the old channels, clearing silt from narrow passages with their translucent limbs. Their work is imperfect. They leave behind ridges of black sediment, tiny spirals of shell, and minute chambers where they have gathered the minerals they need. When disturbed, they scatter into the walls. Later, a new line of damp appears on the surface.

The larger dwellers tend the city without intending to preserve it. They open blocked gutters. They carry seedpods into flooded courtyards. They press their bodies against cracked pillars during freezing nights, reducing the speed at which the stone splits. They also undo things. They loosen tiles, strip plaster, empty rooms of dust, and feed on the salts that hold some walls together. Their care and their destruction are the same act. They are not maintaining the settlement for a future return. They are helping it pass through its changes.

Perhaps this is what they were made to do. Perhaps they were once servants, designed to live beneath the city and regulate its water. Perhaps they are older than the city, and the first builders merely discovered that stone could be arranged around their habits. The deepest chambers suggest an even stranger possibility: that the dwellers did not survive the people, but that the people survived them for a time.

They have no graves. When one dies, it does not leave a body for long. Its membranes slacken, its plates separate, and the remaining material dissolves into whatever surface supports it. Within a season there is only a pale stain and a little change in the growth of nearby moss. The others may gather around this place when the weather turns. They touch the stain. They listen through the stone. Then they leave.

Their young are not born. They precipitate.

After a long period of rain, small clear nodules appear along the inner walls of the highest galleries. They tremble when the wind enters. Gradually they darken, acquire a central filament, and begin to draw themselves out of the stone. For weeks they remain attached, feeding on condensation. Then, during one of the sudden floods, they let go and are carried downward through the city’s channels.

Most vanish into the lower works.

A few emerge in the open courtyards, blinking their colorless eyes at the sky.

When the weather clears, the dwellers withdraw. They return to the places between things: behind walls, below paving, inside the sealed chambers where old water remains cold and untouched. The city appears empty once more. The reeds stop shining. The pools settle. The last figures flatten themselves into shadow.

But the settlement has not been abandoned.

It is inhabited by beings that do not need rooms, by a people whose homes are seams and hollows, whose roads are pressure and dampness, whose history is written in stains rather than words. They wait for the next cloud to descend. They wait for stone to begin sweating. And when the gutters mutter again, they will unfold from the walls and move through the old city as though answering a call that has never stopped.
