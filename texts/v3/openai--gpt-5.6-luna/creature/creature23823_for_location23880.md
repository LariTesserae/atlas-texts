What lives on the plateau cannot be separated cleanly from what grows there.

The oldest inhabitants are sometimes mistaken for trees. They stand in the shallow basins and along the root-bridges, motionless beneath the dripping shelves, their bodies held upright by several long, jointed limbs that enter the soil like roots. Their skin is gray-green and finely ridged, darkening wherever it remains wet. Moss gathers across their backs. Small orchids open along their shoulders. During the warmest months, pale flowers appear in the hollows of their elbows and remain there until the season turns.

They are not tall so much as extended. A mature one may rise only a few body-lengths above the forest floor, but its limbs can span a pool, brace a leaning trunk, or disappear into the ground and emerge again far away. They move with extraordinary patience. One foot may remain lifted for an hour while the creature listens through the mud. When it finally steps down, the water closes around it without a splash.

They are called, if they are called anything, the Tendermen.

The name belongs to a language that is no longer spoken in any human mouth. It survives as a pattern of clicks beneath bark, as a sequence of pressures passed through root and stone. The Tendermen do not converse in the ordinary sense. They exchange changes in tension, warmth, and moisture. A warning may be a tightened strand in a bridge. A greeting may be a pool allowed to remain full. Mourning is the slow redirection of water toward the place where someone has died.

They have mouths, but rarely use them for speech. Their voices are low pulses that travel through the ground. A Tenderman standing on one side of a reed plain may send a message beneath the roots, and another will answer from beyond the visible horizon. To creatures above the soil, the exchange sounds like nothing more than a faint tremor in the ribs.

They do not build structures. They persuade structures into being.

A Tenderman will find a young tree growing at the wrong angle and lean against it once each wet season. It may take fifty years before the tree understands the pressure. A root-bridge is begun by placing the fingers of two living trees together. The Tendermen bind the meeting place with warm mud and flowering fibers, then leave it to thicken. Their descendants return to widen it, to open channels beneath it, to grow a roof of leaves over the crossing. A causeway may be centuries old before it becomes useful, and by then no one remembers which hands first began it.

They have little interest in permanence. To preserve a wall as stone would be, to them, a kind of failure. Stone does not breathe. Stone does not drink. Stone cannot shelter larvae in its cracks or carry water uphill.

Instead, they cultivate walls that root themselves, terraces that collect rain, chambers that slowly fill with flowers. A doorway is not an entrance cut into something dead, but a place where the living matter has been taught to part. Their oldest halls are deep inside trunks, where the wood has grown around smooth stones and shaped itself into arches. The floors are damp with springwater. The walls pulse faintly as sap moves through them. When the inhabitants gather, they do not sit facing one another. They place their hands against the wood and become part of the room’s circulation.

There are young among them, though youth is difficult to recognize. They begin as pale, soft-bodied things in warm root cavities, tended by several adults at once. At first they cannot stand. They cling to moss and feed on mineral-rich water drawn through shallow cuts in the roots. Their limbs develop slowly, and they spend their first decades suspended beneath leaves, learning the plateau through touch.

A young Tenderman is always accompanied by small lives. Shelled grazers cling to its skin. Thin-winged insects nest in the folds behind its knees. Tiny white animals travel through the furrows of its back and clean away the dark growth that would otherwise suffocate it. The child learns not to brush them away. To disturb one surface is to disturb an entire household.

The Tendermen are not alone in their stillness.

In the reed plains move the low-backed browsers, broad creatures whose coats are living mats of moss and fern. They feed by opening the folds of their faces among the floating leaves, taking in water, stems, insects, and the soft mineral paste that gathers on the surface of the pools. Their moss blooms when they are healthy. A flowering browser may carry a whole miniature meadow across its back, and smaller animals follow it from basin to basin, feeding on the flowers and scattering their spores.

The tall, many-jointed animals belong to the upper forest. They resemble walking stems until they move. Their bodies are narrow, their limbs jointed like the branches of young trees, and their heads are small and faceless except for a dark opening beneath the throat. They remain still for such long periods that birds mistake them for trunks. Their bodies become supports for hanging gardens. Vines pass over them. Pools form in the hollows of their backs.

When one finally walks, the movement travels through the canopy as a slow disturbance. Leaves turn. Birds lift without alarm. The animal may cross half a basin in a day, placing each foot where the roots can bear its weight. It does not flee from predators. Nothing on the plateau seems to hunt it. Perhaps it is too old, or perhaps its flesh is so thoroughly interwoven with the forest that eating it would mean eating part of the ground.

There are predators, though their presence is seldom witnessed. They live in the black water beneath root-bridges and in the deepest flowering cups. Their bodies are soft, nearly transparent, and equipped with long sensory filaments that taste vibrations in the mud. They take what comes close. A shelled creature disappears. A sleeping insect is gone. Once in many years, a young animal fails to rise from a pool, and the water remains disturbed long after its body has been digested.

The Tendermen do not fear these things. Fear, on the plateau, is not absent so much as distributed. It exists in the turning of leaves, the closing of flowers, the sudden tightening of roots around a stone.

What the Tendermen fear is separation.

They gather lost water. They reopen channels clogged by fallen growth. They carry seeds from one weather-zone to another. When a tree dies, they do not cut it down. They climb into its hollow and listen until they know which parts can still bear weight. Then they reinforce it from within, inserting living braces, warm mud, and braided roots. The tree may remain standing for another hundred years, its dead wood supporting generations that never learn it is dead.

When one of the Tendermen dies, its body is laid across a wet shelf and left uncovered. Roots enter the joints. Moss spreads over the chest. Small animals make nests beneath the ribs. The body becomes a bridge, then a bank, then a low ridge of dark growth. For a long time, the others continue to visit. They press their hands to the remains and listen for whatever memory has not yet passed into the soil.

Eventually, they stop coming.

But the place is not abandoned. Water continues to move through it. A flowering vine follows the old line of the spine. The canopy bends over the shape of the body. Years later, a path forms beside the ridge because creatures remember, without knowing why, that this is a safe way through the forest.

The plateau’s inhabitants do not possess the land. They are its temporary gestures.

Some are born in pools, some in flowers, some beneath the bark. Some walk openly, and some exist only as a pressure in the roots or a warmth under the moss. The Tendermen are the most visible of them, but even they are only one expression of the greater life: the ancient intelligence that raises a wall, fills it with water, opens it into a garden, and waits patiently for the garden to become part of the mountain.

At night, when the mist thickens and the canopy disappears, the Tendermen stand throughout the forest with their hands in the ground. Their bodies are difficult to see. Their voices pass from root to root.

The plateau listens through them.

And beneath them, slowly, the land answers.
