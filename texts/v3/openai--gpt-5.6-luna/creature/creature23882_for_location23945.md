They are still here, though the plateau has made the word *here* difficult to use.

At first, a traveler may mistake them for arrangements of stone. A figure stands at the end of an avenue, perhaps: tall, narrow, and dark against the pale mineral dust. Its proportions are almost human, but not quite. The head is too small, the shoulders too level, the limbs too carefully jointed. It does not move when watched. Its shadow may lean in another direction.

Then the traveler looks away.

When they look back, the figure is nearer, or farther, or has become one of the slate uprights bordering the road. Sometimes there are two of it. Sometimes the figure has been replaced by a shallow depression in the ground filled with condensation.

The inhabitants of the plateau were once people in the ordinary sense. They had hands, voices, households, and names that could be carried from one generation to the next. Their cities were built for them, their terraces measured by them, their channels arranged according to systems of exchange and obligation. But the high country altered them slowly, without catastrophe and without any moment that could be called the beginning.

They began by adapting their bodies to the scarcity of water. Their skin thickened and took on the dark, faintly green cast of the local stone. Their blood became more viscous, then less like blood than a suspension of mineral particles and living threads. The folds of their joints developed translucent membranes that gathered condensation. Their lungs grew broad and quiet, capable of drawing moisture from air too thin for other lungs.

Later, they ceased to require food in the old manner.

They fed on warmth held in stone, on the slight chemical differences between wet and dry ground, on the charged dust that lifted itself against the wind. A person could stand for an entire day beside a wall and emerge nourished by it. A family might sleep with their hands pressed into the terrace floor, sharing heat through the rock. Children were placed in shallow pits during the cold season and retrieved months later, changed but alive.

No one can say whether they became less human or more completely so. Their records, where records survive, do not treat the transformation as a decline. They speak of *settling*, of *joining the long weather*, of exchanging the temporary body for a body that could endure the plateau’s distances.

Their current forms vary.

Some remain nearly human. These are the ones most likely to be encountered in the ruins: solitary figures wrapped in layered mineral cloth, moving with a deliberate, economical gait. Their garments are not woven but grown, sheets of fibrous material hardened by repeated exposure to frost. They wear no ornaments except small basins or cups attached to the shoulders, wrists, and throat. These fill with condensation while they sleep.

Their faces are smooth and narrow. The eyes have a reflective gray surface, like water seen in a black pool. They blink rarely. Their mouths are usually sealed, not from deformity but because speech no longer depends on them. When they communicate aloud, the sound is low and granular, as if gravel were being moved beneath a shallow stream. More often they speak by changing the pressure of the ground, by tilting the head, or by placing the palm against a wall.

A wall remembers such contact.

It may open a passage. It may alter the direction of a corridor. It may release a scent belonging to a season that occurred centuries ago. The inhabitants understand these responses as readily as others understand expressions. They can tell when a building is grieving its original shape, when a bridge has become unwilling to span a channel, when a house is preparing to become a hill.

Other inhabitants have surrendered the upright form.

They live in the vegetation, though the phrase is misleading. A blue-black moss mat may contain a single consciousness spread over a hundred meters of sheltered stone. A stand of pale reeds may be one being, its roots distributed through several miles of channels and its stalks serving as organs of hearing. The tall cup-bearing plants around the old wells are often inhabited by the remnants of entire households. Each plant holds a portion of memory in its stem: a face, a route through the city, the location of a reservoir, the shape of a lost room.

These plant-people do not speak as individuals. Their communication is expressed through growth.

A path becomes green where one wishes to be followed. A wall softens where one is invited inside. A meadow shifts its arrangement overnight to indicate danger, though danger on the plateau rarely means an attacker. It may mean that gravity will weaken in three days, or that a valley is about to be removed from the world, or that someone has begun approaching from a direction that no longer exists.

The roots are especially difficult to distinguish from the city’s foundations. In some districts, the old roads have been raised into shallow waves by their passage. A traveler may walk across one of these roads without realizing that beneath the dust, something is listening through every stone.

The inhabitants do not hunt because there is little to hunt. They do not flee because there is nowhere that is not already part of them. When threatened, they alter conditions. Moisture gathers beneath an intruder’s feet. A corridor lengthens. Frost enters the joints of a door. A stand of reeds changes the direction of the water. If necessary, a whole neighborhood may become inaccessible—not hidden, exactly, but displaced by a few degrees of reality.

They are not hostile to travelers. Hostility requires a boundary, and their boundaries are porous. A stranger may be watched for years without being approached. They may be given water from a black pool, though the water will leave no reflection in their eyes. They may be guided through a city by doors that open only when the stranger has forgotten what they were seeking.

The inhabitants are deeply concerned with maintenance.

Not repair. Repair suggests restoring something to an earlier condition, and the plateau has little respect for earlier conditions. They maintain relationships between stone, water, growth, and memory. They keep certain channels from draining uphill too quickly. They persuade ruined towers to remain towers rather than becoming ridges. They visit the mineral markers at intersections and smooth the incisions when the markings begin describing events that have already happened.

Their work is patient and often indistinguishable from natural process.

A crooked wall becomes straight over the course of a century. A collapsed staircase reappears with its lower steps restored. A ruined district fills with silver fronds, which are then encouraged to migrate toward a dry basin. The inhabitants may spend generations guiding a single root through a foundation, waiting for it to discover the pressure that will return a fallen arch to its proper tension.

They preserve the dead, but not in graves.

The dead are dissolved into places. Their mineral remains are mixed into the pale dust beneath a threshold. Their memories enter the dark pools, where they appear as faint disturbances moving against the surface. A person who died in a city may become part of its distances. Travelers approaching the old hall may find the road lengthening because someone within the road still expects a visitor.

Some dead remain visible.

They stand in windows during certain weather, pale and motionless, watching the condensation collect. They appear at the edges of fields that exist only in mist. They walk roads that lead between one century and another. The living do not greet them. The dead are not always aware of the living, and when they are, recognition may take the form of a door opening several kilometers away.

Children are rare now, though they have not ceased to be born.

A child of the plateau begins as something almost ordinary: small, warm, impatient, unable to distinguish a room from a mountain or a promise from a direction. During the first years, they are kept near condensation basins and taught to sleep against inhabited walls. They learn to hear the minor movements of stone. They learn that a shadow may belong to a person who is no longer present, and that the correct response is not fear but attention.

As they mature, their bodies become less distinct. Fingers may fuse into rootlike pads. Hair becomes a fringe of fine mineral fibers. The ribs broaden to hold the thin air. Some children retain a face all their lives. Others lose the face gradually, their features smoothing into a dark, reflective surface from which expressions emerge as changes in sheen.

Every child eventually chooses, or is chosen for, a distribution.

One becomes a watcher of roads, able to sense when distance has folded around a ruin. One enters the moss and tends the sheltered terraces. One joins a city’s foundations, learning the moods of stone. One remains mobile, traveling between settlements with a body that can persist for years without sleep, though such travelers are increasingly rare.

They do not travel as others do. They walk toward a landmark and allow the plateau to decide whether the distance is ready. A journey may last an hour or a lifetime. Sometimes the traveler arrives before leaving. Sometimes only their shadow completes the route.

The inhabitants have no central city and no single ruler. Their society is distributed through agreements among living systems. A reservoir may have more authority than a person. A bridge may veto a road. A group of plants can refuse to flower until a dispute between distant settlements has been resolved. Decisions are made through alterations in growth, pressure, water, and light. To outsiders, this appears slow and obscure. To the inhabitants, it is simply the pace at which consequences become visible.

They are not immortal. They wear away.

A person may lose a hand to frost, a memory to a shifting corridor, a voice to a season of dry air. Entire communities have become harmless geological features. Yet death does not end their participation. The plateau retains whatever has been sufficiently repeated. A habit becomes a channel. A grief becomes a hollow. A song becomes a pattern in the mineral seams that glows green whenever the ground is wet.

At night, the inhabitants move more freely.

They emerge from towers, gardens, walls, and pools. Some walk in lines across the empty avenues. Some rise from the moss as thin, jointed figures and stand facing the stars. Others remain beneath the city, their awareness passing through foundations and roots. In the largest settlement, hundreds may gather along a road that is only a few steps deep from the outside but contains several kilometers of interior distance.

They gather there not to meet, exactly, but to remember the direction of elsewhere.

For a time, the road fills with dim bodies. Reflections appear in the windows: the plateau before the cities were built, the plateau after the last city is gone, the plateau under a sky crossed by unfamiliar moons. The inhabitants watch these versions without longing. They have learned that worlds are not lost when they change. They are absorbed.

Near dawn, they return to their places.

The nearly human ones lean against walls until their outlines enter the stone. The plant-people draw their roots inward. The watchers become markers beside the roads. In the black pools, faces dissolve into the gray surface. By morning, there may be no sign that anyone has been present except a new stair, a straightened wall, a green stain spreading across a distant slope.

And sometimes, from a ridge, a traveler will see a figure far below raise one hand.

The gesture may be a greeting.

It may be weather.

It may be the plateau, briefly remembering what it was like to have a body.
