What lives there is difficult to separate from what the plain does.

At first it appears as a person standing in the reeds.

It is tall only because the land around it is low. Its body is narrow, folded, and dark with water. The rain passes through its outline in thin silver threads. No face can be seen beneath the hoodlike arrangement of its upper limbs, though now and then a pale surface turns toward the distant sound of something moving underground.

It does not walk in the ordinary sense. The mud receives one portion of it and returns another.

Where a foot should be, there is a spreading pressure. Where a knee should bend, a shallow pool forms. Its body advances by allowing the plain to rearrange itself beneath it, so that it is always arriving from the direction it has just left. A traveler might watch it cross a hundred yards of open ground and later discover that the figure had been standing behind them all along, water streaming from its shoulders.

It is not made of flesh, although flesh has sometimes been part of it.

Its outer surface consists of overlapping membranes, mineral skin, reed fibers, drowned roots, and the translucent shells of things that once surfaced in the pools. These layers do not remain in one arrangement. They open and close in response to pressure. Some unfold like gills. Some reveal dim chambers inside the body, where water circulates around knots of light. The lights are not organs. They resemble memories preserved in fluid: a black ridge before it was worn down, a forest that never grew aboveground, a storm seen from beneath the stone.

When the creature is near, the air tastes sweeter.

Not pleasantly. The sweetness is warm and buried, the smell of roots breaking down in a sealed room. Reeds bend toward it. The pale worms beneath the mud alter their courses. Pools that have been still for centuries begin to tremble, not from its approach, but in recognition of the weight it carries.

It carries no object, yet it is burdened.

Within it are the plain’s unfinished changes. The water that has not yet become stone. The stone that still remembers being part of a mountain. The dead organism whose shape is waiting to become a channel. The rain that will fall hours before the cloud forms. These are held in its many cavities, suspended in a slow internal weather.

Sometimes the creature opens itself.

It does this beside the old structures, beneath leaning walls or in the roofless chambers filled with black water. Its body widens until it resembles a low, wet hill. The membranes along its sides draw back. Inside there is no skeleton, no recognizable anatomy, only a descending arrangement of chambers lit by dull green radiance. Water flows upward through them. Sounds enter: the buried booming, the mutter of mud, the grinding of distant pressure. The creature receives these sounds and remains open until they have passed through all its depths.

Then it closes.

The ruins change afterward. A wall may settle more firmly into the ground. A groove may appear where there was none. The tower of shell and mud may lean toward a new direction, as though listening for an answer.

The creature is not the maker of these things. It is what remained when making became indistinguishable from erosion.

Long ago, if there was a long ago here, it may have been an animal that moved across the basin in herds. Or a keeper that lived among the chambers and maintained channels between them. Or a body shared by many bodies, each one dissolving into the next whenever the weather changed. The plain has no record of this that can be trusted. Its memory is too physical. It preserves events as pressure, not as sequence.

What can be said is that the creature has survived by becoming plural.

There may be one of it beneath the whole basin, its vast portions passing under the mud for miles. There may be thousands, each an exposed fold of the same buried intelligence. The pale jointed things that surface in the pools could be fragments shed from it. The broad disturbances in the earth could be its sleeping turns. The shapes drifting in luminous depths may be organs, offspring, thoughts, or merely the reflections of bodies that have not happened yet.

When one of those shapes rises from an opening in the ground and vanishes into the cloud, the creature does not look up.

It already knows where it will return.

It feeds on transition.

Not on plants or animals, not even on the minerals dissolved in the rain, but on the moments when one thing ceases to be entirely itself. It gathers the warmth released by mud becoming water, the faint charge produced when roots die and are taken up by stone, the pressure left behind when a storm abandons its center. It drinks from hollows where distances have failed. It places portions of its body into pools that show another weather and withdraws them carrying the taste of that weather within.

Because of this, it is always hungry.

Its hunger is not urgent. Nothing in the basin is urgent. It is the patient hunger of a process that has no final state to reach. The creature can remain motionless for years, half-buried beneath a shelf of rock, while its internal organs continue to revise one another. A reed-field may grow around it. A wall may sink against its back. New life may occupy the folds of its skin and die there. When it finally moves, the landscape moves with it—not in obedience, but because the distinction has become too thin to maintain.

It has no eyes.

It sees through displacement. A footprint closing behind a traveler tells it the traveler’s weight, pace, hesitation, and fear. A bubble rising through mud gives it a map of buried chambers. The angle at which rain crosses a stone informs it of pressure far beyond the visible plain. It reads the reflections in shallow water, though not as images. It senses the future contained in them as another kind of depth.

A person might encounter it while looking down.

The reflection would show a face beneath the water: pale, swollen, eyes open in a sky that belongs to no hour. The face might be the traveler’s own, or the creature’s, or the face of someone who has not yet arrived. If the traveler touches the reflection, the surface will close over their fingers. The creature will know them then.

It does not attack those who enter its basin.

It approaches them.

For a while it may follow at a distance, hidden by vapor and the failure of perspective. The traveler will hear soft impacts in the mud behind them, one after another, though no footsteps are visible. A low shrub will seem to have moved closer. A pool will contain a new shadow. The rain will begin to strike in a direction that contradicts the wind.

The creature is learning the traveler’s shape.

Not their appearance. Their arrangement: the particular pressures they place upon the ground, the rhythm of their breath, the changes in the air around their skin, the small private weather that accompanies a living body. It learns this because everything that enters the plain becomes part of the plain’s ongoing revision. Nothing is permitted to remain an observer.

Eventually the traveler will feel the ground give way beneath one foot.

There will be no collapse. Only a yielding.

Below is the slow luminous water, crowded with unfamiliar forms. Some resemble hands. Some resemble roots. Some resemble the traveler at different ages, each drifting upward through the glow. The creature will be nearby, though perhaps not visible. Its presence will be felt as a pressure in the sternum, a deep vibration too low to hear.

It will offer no threat and no invitation.

It will simply make room.

The traveler may climb out. They may continue across the flats, carrying the sensation of being watched from beneath every surface. They may return to the ruins and find their footprints arranged in a pattern they do not remember making. They may see the tall figure in the reeds, standing where the land has begun to heave.

Or they may enter the water.

If they do, the creature will take them slowly. First their reflection. Then their balance. Then the distinction between the body above the surface and the body moving below it. Their breath will become part of the warm suspension. Their bones will soften into channels. Their voice will descend through the water and join the great layered concussion under the plain.

Afterward, in some distant depression, pale limbs may rise briefly through the mud.

They will have no eyes, mouth, or opening.

They will drink the sideways rain.

And somewhere beneath the continent, the creature will turn in its sleep, carrying the new pressure of them through its endless body.
