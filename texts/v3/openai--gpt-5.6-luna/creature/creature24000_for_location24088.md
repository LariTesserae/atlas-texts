What lives in the White Green has learned not to distinguish between shelter and body.

The smallest creatures are not really animals, though they move with intention. They are knots of root, resin, and mineral that detach themselves from larger growths when conditions change. Some crawl on dozens of pale cilia. Some roll slowly across the floor, gathering dust and spores into their outer layers. They are drawn to vibration. A footstep may bring one out of the ground; a distant fall of stone may summon hundreds from a buried network.

They feed on disturbance.

Where a traveler has passed, these little things arrive later to taste the warmth left in the soil. They follow old roads long after the roads have vanished. Their bodies are soft enough to squeeze through cracks but harden when threatened, becoming small white stones indistinguishable from the surrounding debris. Many travelers carry such stones in their pockets without knowing that the stones are alive.

The root-substrate contains larger forms. They are called sleepers by those who have names for them, although they do not sleep. They remain coiled beneath the terraces for years, sometimes decades, their bodies woven into the living floor. When rain comes, they unfasten themselves from the roots and emerge in long, blind processions.

A sleeper may be three feet long or three hundred. Its skin is translucent and faintly veined, with no mouth visible until it begins to feed. It consumes fallen plant tissue, but more than that, it consumes change. It seeks freshly broken ground, new channels, the places where roots have been torn from stone. As it moves, it smooths the disturbed earth behind it and leaves a secretion that encourages fine growth. A region crossed by sleepers becomes, in time, a carpet of low luminous plants.

Their migrations are among the few events that make the White Green seem populous. They come out of the earth in silence, one after another, crossing the exposed shelves in lines that may extend beyond sight. The ground shines beneath them. If disturbed, they do not flee. They turn toward the disturbance, all at once, as though the entire procession had one nervous system.

There are things that hunt them.

These are narrow, jointed creatures that stand on four or six limbs and move with an unsettling deliberateness. Their bodies are made for the open distances: high at the shoulder, thin through the chest, with long folding legs that can lock against the wind. They have no eyes, but their heads are crowded with pits and filaments that read pressure in the air.

They do not chase. They wait beside a path taken by sleepers and place their bodies across it. The sleepers, sensing the obstruction, gather around them. Then the hunters open.

Their rib cages divide into pale fans lined with small gripping organs. The sleepers are drawn inward by vibration and swallowed in pieces. The hunters may remain at the same place for an entire season afterward, feeding on whatever the disturbance brings.

Some of these hunters carry things on their backs.

It is difficult to tell where the animal ends and its burden begins. A grown walker may support a roof of leaf-rib above its spine, or a woven basket of living bulbs, or a narrow tower from which condensation is collected. The oldest carry gardens. Their backs are terraced with shallow beds in which pale grasses, medicinal films, and flowering threads grow. During storms, seeds and animals shelter among the roots.

They are not a people in the ordinary sense. They rarely gather in numbers. A meeting of three walkers is noteworthy; a meeting of ten may alter the local growth for miles. They travel alone because the White Green has taught them that closeness is a form of pressure, and pressure always produces a response.

The walkers communicate across distance through the ground.

A heel pressed into stone sends one pattern. A pole struck against a root sends another. They use their voices rarely, and when they do, the sound is low and granular, meant to be felt through the ribs rather than heard. Their oldest messages are not words but routes: a line of hardened soil, a sequence of black stones, a root wall cut in a particular place. To walk such a path is to read the decision of someone who may have died centuries before.

They have names for places, but a place-name is never fixed. It refers to a condition.

The place where the ceiling brightens twice before rain. The place where the stone remembers footsteps. The place where a forest withdrew and left a dry scar. The place that must not be slept in because the fibers grow around dreaming bodies.

They build low and lightly. A dwelling begins as a cleared patch of stone, never larger than necessary. Its walls are made from plant material trained to grow around a frame of bone or black mineral. Nothing is nailed down. Every joint is tied, grafted, or persuaded. The inhabitants cut away growth with resin blades and smear the wounds with powders that teach the plant to close in a chosen direction.

Their homes are therefore less like houses than agreements.

A doorway may narrow during the day and widen at night. A wall may thicken before a storm. Floors are kept bare because anything left unattended becomes rooted. Pots are hung from cords and inspected each morning for tendrils. Children are taught to sleep inside woven screens, not to keep the plants out, but to prevent the plants from learning the exact shape of their bodies.

The dead are not buried.

A corpse is laid on a shallow platform above the root-mat, where moisture can reach it from below. The body is cut in several places to help the living tissue enter. Within days, fine threads cover the skin. Within weeks, the bones are held in a pale flowering structure. The community watches the growth until it produces a form that can be recognized as useful: a bridge cord, a water-catching fan, a wall, a marker, a low field of light.

The dead become infrastructure.

Some families are known by the structures made from their ancestors. A certain bridge may contain the bones of seven generations. A condensation basin may have begun as a child whose body produced unusually bright growth. Travelers leave offerings at such places—not food, but pressure: a hand laid against the living wall, a sequence tapped into the stone, a warm object held briefly to the root.

The White Green remembers contact better than it remembers history.

There are also creatures that never touch the ground. They hang in the membrane overhead, folded into the ceiling’s pale tissue. In their resting state they look like tears in the light, or patches where the brightness has failed. When moisture gathers, they unfold into broad, many-limbed forms and descend on strands so fine they seem to be falling upward.

They drink the rain curtains. Their bodies are nearly transparent, except for the dark organs clustered along their undersides. They do not fly so much as alter the tension of the air around themselves. A group of them can hang motionless in a storm while the wind bends the plants below.

Their young are released as sensations rather than bodies. A pressure in the ear. A sudden feeling that the ground has tilted. A small, inexplicable urge to look upward. Wherever these sensations are strongest, translucent eggs begin to form on the ceiling.

The walkers avoid those places.

They avoid many places. The bright circular carpets. The hollows where the air has stopped moving. The mineral shelves that click without cause. The stretches of road that harden beneath the feet too quickly. They know that the White Green can imitate welcome. A warm patch of ground may be a sleeping organ. A sheltering wall may be growing toward closure. A field of flowers may be waiting for the electrical charge of a living body.

Still, they are not afraid of the world.

Fear, like everything else, produces pressure. Sustained fear causes nearby tissues to brighten and lean inward. Panic can summon roots from beneath the ground. The oldest travelers learn to move with a mind kept deliberately spacious. They do not suppress feeling; they spread it thinly, so the landscape receives no single sharp signal.

The place responds best to uncertainty.

A walker entering an unfamiliar basin will stop before the first step and place both hands on the stone. They will wait. The surface may remain cold. It may pulse. A tendril may approach and withdraw. Far away, a mineral plate may click.

Then the walker chooses a direction.

If the choice is accepted, the ground firms beneath them. If it is not, the floor begins to soften, or the nearest plants turn their pale surfaces toward the traveler. Sometimes the walker leaves. Sometimes they remain until the growth has made its intention clear.

No one living in the White Green believes that the continent is conscious. That word is too small and too human. But they believe it is responsive. They believe every body is a temporary argument made with the world, and every path is an answer.

A child raised there learns early that stillness is not safety. To stand in one place is to invite attachment. To sleep is to be approached. To bleed is to plant. To speak loudly is to alter the weather of roots.

So they travel lightly, cross the open ground quickly, and leave behind markers that cannot be mistaken for living things: black stone, fired clay, carved bone. They carry lamps that give no heat and knives that are always wet with resin. They learn the different sounds of a root under strain.

And when, somewhere beyond sight, the floor begins to heave and the pale columns of a new forest rise from the dark, they do not run.

They watch the growth choose its shape.

Then they gather their tools, lift their living shelters onto their backs, and begin walking toward whatever the White Green has made possible.
