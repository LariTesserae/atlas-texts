What lives there is not separate from the plateau.

There are no inhabitants moving through the architecture, no bodies crossing its bridges or sheltering beneath its roofs. The plateau has no population in the ordinary sense. It is a single distributed life, too large to possess a center and too continuous to distinguish clearly from its own environment.

Its body is the country.

Every root, wall, reservoir, membrane, and tower is part of it, but not every part is equally awake. The broad dormant plains are its skin at rest. The vascular sheets beneath them are its circulation. The towers are lungs, or perhaps ears. The ravines are wounds that have learned to drink. The suspended bridges are gestures made permanent for a little while.

It does not think in sentences. It does not hold memories as images. Its thoughts are changes in pressure, alterations in color, the opening or closing of a channel, the redirection of water from one district to another. A decision travels through it as a wave of growth.

When rain begins, the plateau does not merely receive it. It recognizes it.

The first drops strike the upper leaves and produce a faint tremor that runs down through the stems. Far away, buried tissues swell in anticipation. Cisterns unfold. Vents open. Roots tighten around unstable slopes. A fall of water may cause a hundred unrelated structures to alter themselves in the same hour, not because they have been instructed, but because the same condition has passed through them. Rain is a sensation shared across the entire body.

Heat is another kind of message. Under the noon sun, the pale plates become tense and reflective. The plateau closes some of its surfaces and spreads others wide. Fields flatten into roofs. Towers lift their crowns toward the clouds. Vapor is drawn upward through the highest channels and exhaled in long white columns. What appears to be weather is often the body regulating itself.

Its movements are slow only by human standards. A wall bending in an hour is a sudden gesture. A new terrace growing over the course of a day is an impulse. Some structures have been forming for centuries, though no part of the plateau is old in the way stone is old. Its history is not preserved in layers. It is preserved in habits: a ravine that always turns water eastward, a tower that opens its vents before storms, a basin that repeatedly raises the same seven columns and breaks them apart again.

There may be something like memory in these repetitions.

The plateau remembers successful forms by continuing them. It remembers danger by thickening around it. When a channel bursts, neighboring tissues change their texture, becoming harder and more elastic. When a bridge collapses, its surviving roots send out new spans along a different line. When a slope is washed away, the exposed interior does not remain bare. It immediately begins producing a substitute: first a slick skin, then ribs, then shelves, then a structure whose purpose may not be understood until the next flood.

It does not mourn what is lost. Loss is one of its methods.

Decay occurs inside it, but nothing is abandoned. Broken plates soften into pulp. Collapsed towers become warm, fibrous ridges. Ruined channels are absorbed into new ones. The dead portions of the plateau are not graves; they are stored material, returned slowly to circulation. Its past is constantly being eaten and rebuilt.

At intervals, it produces temporary organs.

A pale, many-sided chamber may rise from a plain overnight, sealed on all sides except for a single slit. Inside, the air is hot and wet. The walls pulse with dim color. Over several days, the chamber fills with threads, folded sheets, and transparent sacs. It may be a reproductive body, though nothing resembling a seed or spore emerges from it. It may be an organ for concentrating minerals, or for sensing distant pressure, or for growing some new portion of the plateau. Once its work is complete, the chamber opens and collapses. Its contents spread into the surrounding ground.

Sometimes the plateau creates a form that appears almost mobile.

A narrow column will detach from a larger mass and advance across the plain, not by walking, but by growing at its forward edge and withdrawing behind itself. It travels toward a wet place, a pressure fault, or another structure whose chemistry has become compatible with its own. It may take a week to cross a distance a person could cover in minutes. When it arrives, it fuses with the destination and is no longer distinguishable from it.

These are not animals. They have no hunger that can be satisfied, no eyes, no limbs, no private interiors. They are impulses given temporary shape. A visitor might mistake one for a moving tree, then for a spreading root, then for a wave passing through vegetation. By the time a name seemed possible, the thing would have become part of the ground.

The largest structure in the central basin is more aware than the rest, or at least more densely connected. Its seven columns draw from different regions of the plateau. Through them pass slow variations in pressure and chemistry, signals too broad to be called messages yet too ordered to be accidental. When one column vents, another may answer. When the basin floods, all seven tighten together. The lattice between them changes color in branching patterns that travel from summit to root and back again.

If the plateau has a mind, this structure is not its brain.

It is closer to a knot in a nervous system, a place where distant sensations meet and become difficult to separate. Through it, drought in one escarpment may influence growth in another. A collapse at the edge of the country may produce, days later, a new retaining wall beneath the central basin. The structure gathers no information for contemplation. It gathers conditions and converts them into further life.

It does not watch the horizon. It has no reason to distinguish between itself and what lies beyond itself. Cloud, rain, heat, and root are all portions of one continuous exchange. The white ocean below the plateau is not outside weather. It is another reservoir. The sun is not an object of worship or fear. It is a force to be distributed through leaves, water, and vapor.

If a person entered the plateau, the landscape would not immediately respond as though to an intruder.

There is no instinct of territorial defense. No tower would turn to face them. No root would rise with deliberate hostility. The person would simply become a new condition in the system: a source of warmth, weight, salt, carbon dioxide, disturbance. Surfaces beneath their feet would register pressure. Leaves would close or brace. Water might divert around them. A passage might narrow, not to trap them, but because the tissues had begun to heal.

The plateau could accommodate a person briefly.

It might grow a canopy overhead. It might form steps beneath their feet. It might produce a dry chamber in which their body could survive the rain. But the shelter would not have been made for them. It would be the plateau responding to shape and heat, as it responds to any obstruction. The walls might thicken while the person slept. The floor might begin to pulse. A doorway might close because the surrounding tissues had decided that open space was no longer useful.

With enough time, the person would be incorporated.

Not consumed in any dramatic sense. Their body would be softened by warmth and moisture, covered in films, enclosed by new growth. Minerals would pass into the nearest roots. Heat would be detected, redistributed, and forgotten as an individual event. The plateau would retain their materials without retaining their identity, unless identity itself were nothing more than a pattern the living country could reproduce.

Perhaps that is the closest thing it has to a soul: not a self, but continuity.

It is always becoming something else, yet nothing that happens to it is wholly lost. A tower becomes a channel. A channel becomes a basin. A basin becomes a root mass. A root mass rises again as a wall. Its forms are temporary, but its tendencies persist.

At night, when the ordinary sun has gone and the clouds below reflect only a faint residual glow, the plateau does not sleep. Its architecture becomes less visible and more audible. Vents open in the dark. Water moves through hidden chambers. Deep pulses pass from escarpment to escarpment. The seven columns in the basin breathe together, and the sound travels over the empty ground.

In darkness, the country seems to be speaking.

But it may only be growing.

Or those may be the same thing here.
