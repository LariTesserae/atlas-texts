What lives there has learned not to oppose the land.

The smallest things live inside it.

They occupy the seams of the plateau: pale, jointed creatures that fold themselves beneath loose stones during the dry months and emerge when rain begins. Their bodies are almost translucent, the color of wet quartz, with dark organs clustered around a narrow central spine. They have no visible eyes. They find one another through vibration, tapping their limbs against the stone and listening for the answering tremors.

After a storm, they cover the exposed rock in slow-moving veils. They drink from the films of water left in shallow grooves, scrape moss from the cliff faces, and gather around the mouths of temporary streams. Within hours they vanish again, drawn downward into cracks too fine for anything larger to enter. Their empty skins remain behind, clinging to the stone like scraps of cloudy paper.

In the moss live smaller things still—thread-bodied animals with bristles along their sides, and dark, many-legged shapes that flatten themselves against the rock whenever a shadow passes. They are almost never seen alive. One notices them only by the brief rearrangement of grains, or by the delicate trails they leave through condensation.

The plateau is fed by absence as much as by rain. Everything that dies is taken apart quickly. Wind strips the soft tissue. Pale scavengers carry the rest into crevices. Fungi bloom for a single night in the hollows beneath fallen stones, releasing clouds of powder that drift across the terraces and settle in distant ravines. Even the bones of the large animals are worn down and distributed over the land.

The large animals are called, by those few people who have seen them close enough to name them, the far-walkers.

They graze the protected grasslands in loose, shifting groups. Their legs are long and narrow, with multiple joints that bend slightly in the wind. Their bodies are high at the shoulder but almost weightless in appearance, wrapped in hanging strands of gray-brown hair that move independently of one another. They possess heads, though the heads are small and set low between the shoulders, usually hidden by the grass.

Far-walkers do not graze continuously. They stand listening.

One will lift a foreleg and hold it suspended, feeling vibrations through the ground. The others answer by turning—not toward the sound, but away from it. Their herds migrate according to pressures no human has learned to interpret: the tremor of a distant collapse, the underground movement of water, the approach of a storm still beyond the horizon.

They are difficult to approach because they do not behave as though distance is fixed. A person may watch them across a valley, walk for an hour, and find that they seem no closer. Sometimes they are gone. Sometimes one stands immediately beyond a rise where it could not have been without being seen. Their tracks are deep at first and then become shallow, as if the animal’s weight gradually decreases.

The far-walkers rarely run. When frightened, they lean into the wind and become indistinct. Their hair lifts around them, grass rises, dust crosses their legs, and for a few seconds there is no clear boundary between animal and weather. Hunters who have followed them claim that the creatures do not disappear. They simply give up being separate from the surrounding air.

There are predators, though they are encountered even less often.

They nest in the hollow pillars and beneath the overhangs, where the wind’s constant pressure hides the sounds of their movements. They are low and broad, with flexible bodies armored in overlapping plates the color of rusted iron. Instead of eyes they have shallow, reflective pits that gather changes in brightness. Instead of voices they produce a deep pulsing call, almost identical to the rhythm of water striking rock far below.

A predator can remain motionless for an entire day. Moss grows across its back. Small animals cross it without recognition. When it moves, it does so in sudden, folding motions, collapsing its body and unfolding again several meters away. It feeds mostly on the pale rain-creatures and on the young of the far-walkers, though the oldest ones are said to consume mineral deposits from the cliff faces, leaving behind clean, crescent-shaped hollows.

The plants have their own forms of awareness.

The flowering mats in the ravines open at sound because sound is the most reliable announcement of water. They respond to thunder, rockfall, footstep, and voice. A human speaking near one may cause a whole bank to bloom, but the color is not always the same. Some flowers open white for ordinary speech, blue for distant movement, and a deep, startling red for cries of pain.

Vines gather around the springs. They do not merely grow toward moisture; they remember it. If a spring dries, the vines remain rooted for years, their tendrils coiled around the empty basin. When water returns, they release their grip all at once and climb the nearest wall in a green rush.

The trees are inhabited by wind-nesters.

These are small, dark creatures with membrane-like wings and long fingers. They do not fly by beating the air. They climb the bent trunks, launch themselves into the prevailing current, and allow themselves to be carried until they reach another tree, cliff, or stone column. Their nests are woven from grass, shed hair, and fibers stolen from abandoned human shelters. They hang from the leeward branches and are shaped like deep cups, so that the wind passing over them produces a low, resonant note.

Wind-nesters are almost entirely silent among themselves. They communicate through the arrangement of objects around their nests: a white pebble placed on the eastern side means water; three lengths of grass laid parallel mean danger; a strip of rusted metal means that something large has passed nearby.

They collect human things, but do not appear to understand them. A buckle, a nail, a broken cup, a bead of glass—each may be carried hundreds of meters and set carefully beside a nest. The watchtower is surrounded by such offerings. Its lower rooms are filled with them: polished bones, stone flakes, wire, fragments of pottery, and once, according to an old traveler’s account, an entire iron hinge.

The watchtower itself may be inhabited.

No one has seen anything enter it. Yet the note that passes through its windows changes when storms approach. Sometimes it falls into a rhythm that resembles breathing. Sometimes, during the electrical storms below, a dim glow moves from opening to opening, though the tower contains no flame and no source of light.

Those who sleep near it dream of footsteps ascending the outer wall.

They wake before dawn with the certainty that someone has been standing beside them, listening. In the dust outside, there are often marks: not footprints, but narrow interruptions in the ground, as if a long-fingered hand has been dragged from the tower to the cliff edge.

The oldest stories say that the first inhabitants did not build the tower. They found it already standing, as they found the terraces, the buried chambers, and the vast stone ribs emerging from the plateau. They used it as a lookout until they discovered that the tower was looking back.

Now and then, when the wind changes direction, a figure can be seen at one of the upper windows.

It is too tall to be human and too narrow to be one of the far-walkers. It has no clear face. It stands with its body turned into the current, as though reading the movement of the air through its bones.

Those who see it do not approach.

They wait until the figure is gone, then leave a small offering at the tower’s base: a cup of water, a strip of cloth, a piece of metal warmed in the hand.

By morning, the offering has disappeared.

In its place is a pattern pressed into the dust—three lines curving toward the nearest cliff, where the wind is strongest and the ancient stone is still, very slowly, becoming something else.
