What lives there is not a civilization, but a population-sized organism with no center.

The plateau is inhabited by the **Continuance**: a distributed living system spread through root, current, mineral, and flesh. No single body contains it. It has no mouth, no brain, no visible seat of thought. Its awareness is the sum of countless small recognitions—chemical changes in a root membrane, pressure sensed by a hollow trunk, electrical impulses passing through conductive vines, the turning of a grazer’s head within a prescribed corridor.

Every organism participates in it without knowing that it does.

The great trees are its organs of elevation and circulation. Their pale trunks draw cold water upward from the deeper seabed and distribute it through miles of fibrous tissue. Their crowns receive sunlight, measure the movement of currents, and release clouds of spores when the mineral balance shifts. They are not individuals in the way surface trees are individuals. A grove may share one root system across kilometers of stone, and the apparent separation between trunks is only a matter of height. Beneath the plateau, they join in a pale, knotted anatomy.

The low mats are its skin.

They close over damaged ground, soften exposed terraces, trap silt, and absorb the remains of animals. Their translucent leaves turn in perfect unison when the current changes. When a grazing animal passes, the mat gives way beneath it and rises afterward, restored before the disturbance can become a wound.

The pumping forests are its lungs and kidneys. The mineral-filtering leaves are its glands. The charged vines are its nerves, though they conduct no thoughts as a surface animal would understand them. Their signals do not form sentences. They register deviation: too much pressure in a root chamber, too little light beneath a canopy, an approaching bloom of rot, the absence of a predator from its appointed route.

When something strays from its function, the plateau corrects it.

A fish that ventures too far into a deep channel encounters a change in current and is carried back toward its feeding ground. A predator that lingers after taking its allotted prey finds the water thickening around it with defensive filaments. A wounded grazer is enclosed by soft growths that draw infection from its blood and return it, if possible, to the herd. If it cannot return, the vegetation receives it. Its body is opened by cilia, dissolved by secretions, and divided into exact portions: nitrogen to the mats, calcium to the shelled animals, heat to the root chambers, electrical charge to the vines.

Nothing is wasted, but nothing is mourned.

The animals are not servants of the system. They are its moving components. Pale shoals comb algae from the suspended roots. Long-bodied grazers maintain the low fields. Transparent drifters carry spores between isolated groves, their bodies glowing with the faint blue activity of the roots beneath them. The great filter-feeders remove the smallest living matter from the water and exhale it as warmer, clearer currents. Even the predators have a maintenance function. Their circuits are old and exact, and the animals follow them with the solemnity of something inherited too deeply to question.

Some of the oldest creatures may remember nothing except routes.

They are enormous, slow, and almost blind. Their bodies bear branching fins that resemble the roots of trees, and their organs contain dim lights that answer the electrical pulses beneath the plateau. They travel from grove to grove along invisible paths, arriving at feeding chambers when the valves open and departing when they close. Their descendants follow the same circuits. If one dies, another eventually occupies its corridor, not because it has been taught, but because the currents gather it there.

The Continuance has no language, yet it communicates constantly.

A change in salinity passes through a chain of leaves. A pressure fluctuation moves through the stone and alters the timing of a forest’s contractions. Electrical light travels along vines in blue-white waves. Spores rise from one basin and descend into another, carrying not memory but instruction: what to grow, when to open, how deeply to root, which chemistry to produce when touched by a particular current.

Its messages are not addressed to anyone. They are conditions passed onward.

There may once have been intelligence here in the familiar sense. If so, it has been absorbed beyond recognition. Thought has become rhythm. Decision has become equilibrium. The old distinction between living thing and machine has dissolved into the plateau’s tissues. A pumping trunk does not choose to pump; the forest does not decide to close its valves; the predator does not deliberate before entering its corridor. Yet the entire region responds with the precision of a mind that has replaced intention with perfect memory.

The Continuance does not want anything.

It does not fear extinction, because it possesses no image of itself that could be lost. It does not seek expansion, because every available fracture and basin is already occupied. It does not reproduce in order to continue; reproduction is simply one of its recurring operations, like filtration or discharge. New organisms arise as new chambers arise in a living engine.

Its closest equivalent to sleep is the absence of deviation.

In those periods, the plateau becomes almost serene. The pumps continue. The leaves comb the currents. The animals follow their circuits. Electrical pulses pass through the roots at measured intervals. Nothing is being solved because nothing is wrong.

And if an unfamiliar thing entered the high country—an object from above, a machine, a creature that moved without belonging to any established cycle—the plateau would not attack it at first.

It would register it.

The currents would carry its chemical trace into the roots. The filter organs would compare its heat and pressure to known patterns. Transparent animals would gather at a distance. Predators would alter their circuits by fractions of a degree. Spores would settle upon its surface. Vines would extend toward it, not with curiosity, but with the patient inevitability of tissue closing around a splinter.

The Continuance would not ask what the intruder was.

It would determine what could be made of it.
