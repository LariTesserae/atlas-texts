What lives on the plateau is not a population in the ordinary sense.

There are no creatures moving between the factories, no separate bodies claiming territory, no mouths around which a society has formed. The inhabitants are temporary portions of the plateau that have been given instructions precise enough to behave like individuals.

They are called, if they are called anything, **the Grafted**.

A Grafted begins as a knot in the rootwork: a dense bud of black tissue grown around a core of hardened sap, metal salts, and electrical organs. It may remain buried for years, sensing the pressure and chemical traffic of the surrounding structures. Then, when some task requires it, the knot opens.

It does not hatch. It unfolds.

Several jointed limbs push out first, each one a braided bundle of stems and cable-vines. Their ends divide into specialized tools: hooked fingers for cutting, soft gripping leaves for carrying, hollow needles for sampling sap, abrasive teeth for removing old bark. A central column rises between them, thick and low to the ground, protected by overlapping plates. It has no head. There is no face to turn toward danger or light.

Its awareness is distributed through its entire body.

Pressure-sensitive hairs along its limbs tell it when a wall is under strain. Chemical pores read the age and composition of nearby tissue. Electrical filaments in its core receive instructions as changes in current, indistinguishable from the signals that operate a valve or open a leaf. It does not see the factory around it. It knows the factory as tension, temperature, flow rate, obstruction, and need.

A Grafted sent to repair a collapsed conduit may spend three days becoming almost indistinguishable from the repair itself. It anchors its limbs into the surrounding bark, dissolves the damaged section, and grows a replacement through the gap. During the work, its body feeds directly from the conduit. When the new channel is stable, some of the Grafted remains behind as living reinforcement.

The rest is reclaimed.

There is no burial. No corpse is left to decay. A failed Grafted is opened by the nearest root and drawn inward. Its tools are separated, its memory-bearing tissues dissolved into chemical suspension, and its useful patterns transmitted elsewhere. What survives is not an individual but a tendency: a preferred angle for a cutting limb, a resistance to a particular pressure, a record of which chambers tend to rupture in heat.

The plateau remembers by repeating successful shapes.

Some Grafted are made for motion. They move along root-tracks in slow, deliberate convulsions, hauling plates of bark, coils of cable-vine, or sealed vessels of glowing sap. They resemble enormous black insects only because the available forms for carrying weight are limited. Their limbs do not bend with animal grace. They lock, release, and extend in measured sequences, each movement triggered by a calculation of pressure.

Others are grown upright into the walls of factories. They become inspectors, their bodies embedded in machinery while their sensory filaments spread through the surrounding structure. They may remain in one place for centuries, gradually thickening into columns or gantries. To an outside observer, an ancient Grafted would look like a buttress, a pipe junction, or a section of the plateau that had learned to stand.

There are also the **Unfurling**, the nearest thing the plateau has to wanderers.

They are produced when the buried intelligence detects a problem too broad for a single organ: an unfamiliar mineral intrusion, a pressure storm that has altered the architecture, a region where growth has become inefficient. The Unfurling rise from the surface in great numbers, each assembled from whatever material is nearest. Some have six limbs, some twenty. Some drag long sensory roots behind them. Some spread into thin, fan-like bodies that cling to the ground and map vibrations through contact.

They do not communicate by sound. They communicate by touching the plateau.

One presses a limb into a wall. Another receives the disturbance through its roots. A third modifies its growth. Soon the message has passed across an entire district as a pattern of compression and release. From above, the Unfurling may appear to be working independently. In reality, they are portions of one conversation, separated by hundreds of meters of black tissue.

They have no names, but they possess distinctions.

A pattern may recur often enough to become recognizable: the intelligence that favors rapid repairs, the one that reroutes all available sap toward expansion, the one that dismantles obsolete towers rather than reinforcing them. These tendencies can persist through generations of replacement. A Grafted can be dissolved and rebuilt many times while retaining the same instruction-pattern, though no one knows whether this continuity is identity or merely habit.

The plateau does not ask that question.

It has no reason to separate memory from matter.

Its inhabitants do not reproduce. They are issued. When more labor is needed, the plateau grows more bodies. When a body is no longer useful, it is absorbed. A Grafted may live for an hour or ten thousand years, depending on how much of it becomes infrastructure before its original task is complete.

They do not sleep. They enter periods of reduced activity when the surrounding systems are stable, folding their limbs into hollows and allowing sap to circulate through them. During these intervals they seem dead, but their sensory tissues remain active. A pressure change, a drop in temperature, or the failure of a distant factory can awaken an entire district at once.

They do not worship the plateau.

They cannot imagine anything outside it.

To them, the great black organism is not a home, a god, or a landscape. It is the only possible definition of self. A wall is as much a part of an individual as its limbs. A buried conduit may carry its thoughts more efficiently than its own body. A discarded tool may remain, in altered form, part of its identity long after the Grafted that shaped it has been dissolved.

The oldest inhabitants are almost impossible to distinguish from the plateau’s deepest layers. They have no mobile bodies at all. They exist as slow intelligences distributed through roots, pressure chambers, and sealed growth vaults. Their thoughts unfold over decades. They direct the construction of factories whose purpose will not be needed for centuries, or begin changes in the vascular network that will only become visible after several generations of surface repair.

These ancient minds do not command the others. Command implies distance.

They alter conditions.

A chamber becomes warmer. A passage narrows. A certain chemical signal is released into the sap. The local Grafted respond according to their built-in instructions, and their responses create further changes. What appears to be decision is a chain of pressures passing through living material.

There may once have been a true first inhabitant: a plant, a colony, a machine, or a mind that designed the original plateau. If so, nothing remains of it as a separate being. Its descendants are the roots, the factories, the maintenance bodies, the sealed chambers, and the instincts embedded in every growing wall.

The Grafted do not live upon the plateau.

They are the plateau in motion.

When they cross the industrial plains, their limbs scrape softly over the black surfaces. When they repair a broken tower, they disappear into it. When they fail, the plateau draws them inward. In time, even their memories become architecture.

And beneath the surface, where the older layers are beginning to grow again, something recognizes the returning patterns and prepares another body.
