### The Root-Bearers

What lives most completely in the high country is not an animal, though it walks.

The root-bearers travel in slow, scattered processions across the green islands. From a distance they resemble low hills that have learned to move: dark-backed, broad, and uneven, their upper surfaces crowded with moss, flowering stalks, pale shelves of fungus, and curtains of dangling roots. Some carry whole gardens. On the oldest, trees have taken hold.

Their bodies are six-legged and enormously heavy, but the weight is distributed through wide, flexible feet that spread over the living ground. Each foot is furred with gripping tendrils. These sink into the root-mat as the creature passes, drawing water and tasting the chemistry of the soil. The root-bearer does not simply walk upon the forest. It tests the forest with every step, and wherever it goes, the vegetation answers.

Its back is armored in overlapping plates of dark keratin and stone-like growth. Rain collects in the hollows between them. During the wet season, the pools remain for days, becoming nurseries for insects, small amphibious things, and flowers that open only when the creature’s body warms them. In the dry season, the armor contracts and seals. The mosses turn gray. The hanging gardens fold themselves inward, waiting.

The animal itself may go months without drinking from open water. It draws moisture from its food, from night condensation, from the roots it carries, and from the bodies of the small organisms that live in its hide. Its long mouth is not built for tearing. It is a flexible, many-lipped instrument that can strip a curtain of leaves from a cliff or comb spores from a flowering wall without damaging the deeper growth. When it feeds, the sound is soft at first: a wet rasping, like cloth pulled through fingers. A procession of root-bearers makes that sound large enough to become weather.

They do not travel in herds in the ordinary sense. They travel in agreements.

One may remain in a basin for twenty years, growing heavier with every season. Another may approach from across the stone shelves, drawn by the scent of a particular fungus or the low vibration of water beneath the ground. When they meet, they touch mouths and exchange clouds of spores, bacteria, and chemical messages. The meeting can last an entire day. They stand motionless while the gardens on their backs lean toward one another and mingle.

Then, sometimes, they depart together.

The largest processions move only a few hundred feet in a season. Their passage changes the country. Root curtains drag across the ground and gather loose soil. Seeds fall into the furrows left by their feet. They open sealed channels beneath the vegetation and expose hidden pools. Small animals follow them in such numbers that the earth appears to ripple. Predators follow the small animals. Scavengers follow the predators. For a while, the procession becomes a moving country of its own.

The root-bearers are not blind, though they have no obvious eyes. Their bodies perceive through the gardens they carry. Certain flowers open when they sense heat. Others respond to vibration. Some are sensitive to the chemical traces of blood, fire, or distant rain. A disturbance may pass across the creature’s back as a sudden change of color: white blossoms closing, blue fungal films brightening, thorn-vines lifting their tips.

When danger approaches, the root-bearer does not always flee.

It can raise itself high enough to expose the pale folds beneath its armor, then release a deep, resonant call. The sound is too low to hear at first. It enters the body through the feet and ribs. Smaller animals stop moving. Burrowing creatures press themselves into the soil. The vegetation around the animal stiffens.

If the threat continues, the gardens defend their host.

Vines uncoil. Spores burst from the moss. Certain flowers spit a resin that hardens on contact with air. The armor plates open along their seams and release clouds of stinging insects that have lived there since birth. A root-bearer pursued across open stone can turn its own back into a storm.

Yet people sometimes walk beside them.

The high-country inhabitants know that a root-bearer’s arrival means water, though not necessarily water that can be reached easily. They watch for the dark shapes at dusk and approach from downwind, carrying bowls, hooks, and woven nets. During the wet season they harvest condensation from the leaves growing along the creature’s spine. They collect fallen fruit and the hard nodules that form in its root-webs. They do not kill the animal. A single root-bearer may sustain a camp for generations if treated with patience.

There are old agreements between them.

Human shelters are sometimes built along the routes of the processions, where the ground has been pressed firm and the vegetation grows thick. In return, people clear certain choking vines from the animals’ breathing vents and remove shelled parasites from beneath the armor. The work is dangerous. A root-bearer can crush a person without noticing, and a frightened garden can release enough dust to blind everyone nearby. Those who tend them wear wet masks and paint their arms with the animal’s own scent.

No one claims to own a root-bearer.

The oldest people say that the animals remember every place they have crossed. Not as a map, but as a pattern of thirsts: where the stone holds rain, where the ground opens beneath the roots, where a ravine becomes dangerous after three days of sun. Their movements seem aimless until a drought comes. Then the processions turn, one after another, toward valleys no living person knew still contained water.

Perhaps they follow buried channels. Perhaps the plants guide them. Perhaps they carry, in the dark chambers of their bodies, a memory older than the plateau’s present shape.

At night, a resting root-bearer is difficult to distinguish from its surroundings. Its legs disappear into the vegetation. Its armor cools and darkens. Flowers open across its back, pale as small moons, and the breathing organs of amphibious creatures tremble in the pools between its plates. The animal’s respiration passes through the garden in waves. Moss lifts. Roots tighten. Vines settle.

From the ridge, it looks like another part of the high country: one more mound of living growth, one more ancient formation shaped by heat and drought.

Then the mound takes a step.

The movement travels through every layer of life upon it. Insects rise. Leaves turn. Water shivers in the hollows. A tree-like stalk bends and releases a sheet of silver droplets. Beneath the armor, the great body shifts with patient force, carrying its forest toward some hidden basin where the roots already know to go.
