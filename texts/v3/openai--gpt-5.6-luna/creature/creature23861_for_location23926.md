They are the Keepers of the Level.

No one can say whether they were born in the city or whether the city made them gradually, the way water makes a channel through stone. They are shaped like people, though narrow and compact from generations spent between walls. Their shoulders are rounded, not from age but from habit: every passage has a low ceiling, every bridge a close underside, every dwelling an entrance meant to be entered sideways. Their hands are broad at the fingertips and hard along the palms. The skin there has taken on the fine, pale grain of limestone. Water beads on them and does not seem to dry.

They wear no color that the city can hold. Their clothing is black, gray, brown, and the dull green of tarnished copper, woven from hair, old fiber, and strips of treated hide. The garments are layered to shed water and tied closely to the body so that they do not catch on ladders or drag through channels. Each person carries a small cup, a wedge, a length of cord, and a tool chosen for the day’s work. These objects are not possessions in the ordinary sense. They are continuations of the body, as necessary as fingers.

Their faces are difficult to remember.

The mineral light does not reach them evenly. It catches the cheekbones, the wet curve of a brow, the edge of a mouth, and leaves the rest in shadow. They may have smooth skin or scarred skin, eyes or only dark hollows; a watcher cannot be sure. The Keepers seldom look directly at one another. They recognize people by the sound of their movement, by the pattern of a hand against a wall, by the rhythm with which someone sets down a jar.

They do not speak often. Speech is too easily lost in the city and too easily found again. A sentence whispered into a wall may arrive in a sleeping niche hours later, stripped of its urgency and mistaken for a dream. Instead they communicate by touch and pressure. Three taps on a drain cover mean that the channel is blocked. A palm laid flat against limestone means attention. Two fingers drawn slowly downward indicate water descending where it should not. A hand left on a glowing seam is a greeting, and the impression it leaves behind may remain visible long after the speaker has gone.

They know one another through these deposits.

Some walls bear the accumulated touch of particular hands: a fan of long fingers, a small child’s palm, the repeated outline of a thumb pressed into the same place over decades. The Keepers visit these marks without ceremony. They place their own hands over the old ones and wait. The mineral brightens. Sometimes the old shape responds with a faint afterimage, and sometimes it does not. No one calls this resurrection. It is simply the wall remembering correctly.

They have children, perhaps, but childhood is not a distinct condition among them. A small figure may be seen crouching beside a drain, sorting pale creatures from the silt, or climbing a ladder with a clay bucket strapped to its back. The young are not taught through explanation. They are placed near a task and left there until their hands understand it. A child who puts a cup beneath the wrong drip is not scolded. The mistake is allowed to continue until the cup overflows and the child discovers the city’s preference for itself.

At some point, without announcement, the child begins to notice things: which walls are hollow, which bridges tremble, which channels carry clean water and which carry the city’s waste. They learn the names of tools, though names are less important than their uses. They learn how to hear a damaged wheel tooth beneath the larger roar. They learn that the creatures in the drains must be handled gently, not because the creatures feel pain, but because carelessness disturbs the balance of the channel.

They do not age in a straight line.

A Keeper may look young for a century and then suddenly become bent, or may remain small and quick while the hands grow ancient. The city does not seem to permit ordinary decay. Teeth remain serviceable. Wounds close into pale seams. Hair becomes a fine wet fiber but does not fall out. Those who are nearing the end of their usefulness do not weaken so much as become quieter. They spend more time with their palms against the walls, listening to distant taps and the buried movements of water.

When one of them dies, the city does not stop to mourn. The body is wrapped in absorbent cloth and placed in a narrow compartment above a drainage route. Water passes beneath it for several days. Then the bones are removed, cleaned, and put to work. A rib may become a handle. A finger bone may serve as an awl. A skull may be set beneath a dripping seam to collect water. Nothing is buried because there is nowhere to put the dead, and because the dead are not considered absent while some useful hardness remains in them.

The Keepers do not believe they maintain the city.

They believe the city maintains them.

Their duties are not sacred in the way an outside people might understand sacredness. They do not kneel before the wheel. They do not pray over the shaft. They oil its bearings, replace its cups, tighten the chain, and clear the grit from its paddles. But every task is performed with the gravity of ritual because there is no distinction here between what is practical and what is holy. To keep a channel open is to preserve a promise. To repair a stair is to accept the stair’s continued existence. To return a blind creature to the drains is to prevent the world from becoming slightly less itself.

The central wheel is tended by two people at a time. They sit on opposite sides of the shaft, each watching half the mechanism. They do not converse. One listens for the clunk of the wooden teeth; the other watches the bronze cups rise and empty. If a cup spills incorrectly, both place their hands against the stone housing until they feel the cause. The wheel has its own language of irregularities: a dry click, a wet knock, a pause too brief to be called a pause. The Keepers understand these as a physician understands a pulse.

No one has ever seen the wheel stop.

There are stories that it did, once, before the current arrangement of people and passages. In those stories, the city became unbearable. The roar of water remained, but without the wheel’s measured clunk the sounds lost their places. People could no longer tell near from far. Voices entered walls and did not return. The walls themselves seemed to lean inward. When the wheel began again, the city settled around it, and those who had witnessed the silence forgot what their own names meant.

The Keepers are not afraid of the outside world. Fear requires distance, and the outside is too distant to hold in the mind. They have no word for sky. Their word for soil means “unmade floor.” Their word for sunlight means “a brightness that does not belong anywhere.” The entrance, if it exists, is not a gate to them but a disturbance in the city’s continuity: a place where water becomes black and the sound beneath all other sounds begins to knock.

Sometimes they follow that knocking.

They go one at a time, carrying no tool. The others do not stop them. At the low arch they stand before the black water and listen. What lies beyond may be another chamber, another city, or the same city stretched into a distance that cannot be crossed. A Keeper may step through and vanish. Or may return years later without seeming to have aged, carrying a dry stone from somewhere no dry stone should exist.

The returned do not describe what they found. They place the stone near the central shaft and resume their duties.

The city receives them without surprise.

Perhaps the Keepers are people who have adapted completely to a place that has no use for ordinary life. Perhaps they are the last residue of a population that forgot the world above. Perhaps they are organs of the city given hands and feet so that its channels can remain clear, its wheel can continue turning, and its ancient balance can be enacted rather than merely endured.

In the dimness, they move constantly but never hurriedly. A figure descends a ladder with a jar. Another kneels to scrape mineral from a drain. Two silhouettes cross paths on a bridge and touch wrists as they pass. Somewhere, a child presses a palm to a glowing wall and waits for the older hand beneath it to answer.

They are small, wet, patient beings.

They do not conquer the stone. They do not ask it to change. They live by noticing what has shifted and returning it, as gently as possible, to its appointed place.
