What lives there are the **Keepers of the Circulation**.

They are not quite human anymore, though they retain the human arrangement of limbs, faces, hands, and voices. Generations spent in warm water and heavy pressure have made them narrow and flexible. Their bones are dense. Their fingers are long, jointed, and webbed nearly to the first knuckle. A translucent membrane covers the corners of their eyes, allowing them to see in the flooded districts without lamps. Along the throat and beneath the ribs are soft, frilled structures that open and close when they enter the water.

They can breathe air, but prefer not to. Air is too dry, too light. In the dry chambers they move with a careful discomfort, bracing themselves against pipes and walls. In water they are graceful, though not fast. They drift through the streets with one hand on a guide cable, using the current as other people might use a road. Their clothing is made from layered mesh, oilskin, woven algae fiber, and old industrial fabric. Every garment has hooks. Every object is tethered.

No Keeper lives alone.

The smallest household consists of a body, a sleeping niche, a collection of cultivated organisms, and a section of machinery for which that body is responsible. A person may be known by the pump they tend rather than by a family name: Mara of the East Filter, Oru beneath the Condensers, the Three Who Listen to Engine Seven. Their dwellings are not private rooms so much as pockets in the larger system. Curtains of kelp, chains, and beadlike floats mark the entrances. Behind them are hammocks, water basins, shelves of tools, and shallow ceramic beds where pale plants grow around the sleepers.

They sleep in shifts, rarely for more than a few hours at a time. There is always someone awake to feel the pressure of the walls.

The Keepers do not speak much. Sound behaves badly in the flooded city, and speech is considered wasteful when a touch, a light, or a change in current will do. They communicate through pressure taps against railings, pulses sent through taut cables, and small lamps shielded behind colored glass. A green flicker means that a filter is clear. Two amber pulses mean that a valve is opening. A long blue glow, repeated at intervals, means that someone has died.

Grief is quiet here. The dead are not removed from the city. Their bones are cleaned and placed into the gardens, where roots enter the porous hollows and draw out what can still be used. Their tools are returned to circulation. Their names are etched into metal plates and bolted near the machinery they served. After several years, mineral deposits obscure the letters, but no one polishes them. A name that disappears into the crust is considered to have gone deep enough.

The children are raised communally, though the word *children* has become imprecise. They hatch, or are born, in the warm nursery cisterns beneath the central square. Some have the familiar soft shape of infants. Others begin as translucent knots attached to woven mats, developing fingers and eyes over several months. The Keepers disagree about whether these differences are natural, inherited, or caused by the water. They have stopped trying to decide.

Young ones are taught first to listen.

They learn the ordinary sounds of the city: the healthy pulse of a turbine, the hollow knock of an empty pipe, the tremor of a pressure leak, the faint rasp that means a root has entered a gear housing. They learn to distinguish a fish moving through a conduit from a tool drifting loose. They learn which walls are warm, which doors must never close, which pools contain harmless spores and which contain sleeping things.

At adolescence, each is given a route.

The route may lead through the upper gardens, the generator forest, the black filtration tunnels, or the narrow spaces behind the pressure doors. For one season the child follows an elder through that territory and touches every surface. They learn where the city has weakened and where it has grown strong. They learn what can be repaired with iron, what must be sealed with moss, and what should be left alone because it has become part of the living balance.

No one is permitted to call a place empty.

An empty chamber may contain roots. A dry pipe may contain eggs. A silent engine may be sleeping. The Keepers have found, over the centuries, that neglect begins with false names. Once something is declared dead, unused, or vacant, someone eventually dismantles it. The city has survived by refusing such distinctions.

This is why the machines are treated as inhabitants.

They are fed oil, mineral salts, filtered sediment, and patient attention. Their housings are opened ceremonially. Their interiors are brushed clean by hand. When a turbine begins to fail, the Keepers gather around it and place their palms against the casing. They listen for the remaining rhythm. Sometimes they repair it. Sometimes they build a smaller machine beside it and allow the old one to slow.

The oldest engines are never shut down.

They were built before the current people remember, perhaps by the first settlers, perhaps by another kind of person entirely. Their rooms are warm and difficult to enter. The walls around them have thickened with mineral growth, and their foundations have sunk into the limestone. The Keepers believe these engines do not merely circulate water. They remember the original pressure of the sea.

No one knows what would happen if they stopped.

The plants are tended by a separate order called the Gardeners, though the title is misleading. They do not simply grow food. They negotiate with growth. They teach vines where to spread, cut channels through root mats, graft edible tissue onto old industrial molds, and harvest the clear pods that form in the generator forest. A Gardener can tell, by the color of a leaf and the angle of its fronds, whether a wall is carrying too much weight.

They wear masks made of shell and woven fiber. Their hands are often stained blue-black by spores. Some have allowed algae to colonize their skin deliberately, creating a thin living layer that helps them breathe in polluted water. These older Gardeners move through the plant chambers almost without disturbing them. Foliage closes behind their bodies and opens again after they have passed.

The city does not have priests, exactly.

It has **Listeners**.

They maintain the older rules. They sleep in the chambers nearest the stone and spend long periods with their ears pressed to the walls. They record every knock, groan, and distant vibration. They know which sounds belong to the sea and which come from beneath the limestone.

The Listeners are the only people who still fear the city may be alive in a way the Keepers cannot manage.

They say the settlement is not built inside the hollow but grown through it, like a shell around a soft animal. The pipes are veins. The pumps are hearts. The filtration moss is a skin. The oldest foundations are nerves that reach down into the rock. When the city shudders, they do not call it settling. When a sealed door opens by itself, they do not call it corrosion.

They wait.

Sometimes the stone knocks from the central depths.

When that happens, every Keeper stops what they are doing. The pumps continue their patient revolutions. The plants sway in the current. Fish vanish beneath platforms. In the dry chambers, condensation falls from the ceilings.

No one speaks.

The city listens with them.
