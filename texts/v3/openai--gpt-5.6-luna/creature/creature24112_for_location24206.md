What lives in the high country is not a civilization, though it has the persistence and complexity of one.

It is a world of organisms that have learned to occupy the narrow interval between mineral and weather. Most of them are dark, jointed, armored, or translucent. Color is expensive beneath the hard white sun, and so the living things tend toward black blue, iron red, bone white, and the green of deep water. Their surfaces are rarely simple. Nearly everything carries something else: moss in its seams, fungus beneath its plates, larvae in its fur, mineral salts along its limbs. To live here is to become a landscape for other life.

The smallest inhabitants are the scrapers.

They cling to stone in millions, pale bodies pressed flat against the glare. Some possess glassy mouths capable of shaving mineral dust from a cliff face. Others feed on the black filament mats that spread across damp cracks. They work in bands, leaving behind faintly polished tracks that catch the light. Their waste is rich in salts and metals, and the plants gather it as eagerly as the scrapers gather the stone.

In the spring clefts, whole colonies hang from the vegetation. There are thread-bodied grazers that pass their lives on a single stalk, moving upward as the plant grows and downward as it dies. There are winged pollinators whose wings are almost invisible except where they catch the blue shadow of a leaf. There are soft, many-limbed burrowers that live inside the green cords of water-bearing plants, drinking from the tissues without killing them. Their bodies swell and contract with the pressure of the water. When storms come, they release themselves into the flood and colonize new terraces.

Some insects, if they can be called insects, are less like animals than animated tools. They carry mineral fragments from the mouths of subterranean shafts. They seal cracks with resin. They cultivate warm fungal blooms in the interiors of dead stalks. Their colonies make no nests in the familiar sense. Instead, they alter the rock around them, drilling chambers, lining them with reflective plates, and arranging their young according to temperature and pressure.

The blue-black forests are inhabited from root to crown.

At their bases live blind, blunt animals that consume fallen tissue and exhale heat. Their warmth keeps the roots from freezing during the long clear nights. Higher up, narrow-bodied browsers move along the stems with hooked feet, feeding on translucent leaves. They are cautious but not graceful. The wind can throw them from one stalk to another, and many spend their lives climbing back from the ravines into which they have fallen.

Above them are the frond-eaters: long-necked, many-jointed creatures whose bodies are supported by bands of flexible cartilage rather than rigid bones. They move along the shelves beneath the cliffs, reaching upward into the tallest growth. Their hides are covered in symbiotic gardens. Red lichen grows behind their ears. Small burrowers nest beneath the plates of their shoulders. When they sleep, spores rise from their backs in faint colored clouds.

They do not recognize this as a burden. The life upon them is part of their body, and the body is part of the life around it.

The great herd animals are slower and more ancient than the smaller fauna. They cross the plateau in routes that are not roads but habits of pressure. Each passes repeatedly over the same broad shelves, deepening them by a fraction with every generation. Their bodies are squat and immense, plated in dark mineral armor. The plates are not grown all at once. They accumulate slowly, layer over layer, as secretions from the skin react with dust, water, and the air. Old animals carry geological histories upon their backs: bands of red from ancient iron blooms, green from mineral terraces, white from forgotten storms.

Their feet are broad and soft-edged, designed not to grip the stone but to distribute weight across it. Even so, they leave basins behind. Water gathers in these basins after rain. Moss takes root there. Small animals breed in them. A herd therefore carries water without meaning to, transporting whole communities across the dry flats.

When one of the great animals dies, the plateau changes around it.

The plates are opened by specialized feeders with splitting jaws. Warm organisms enter the body cavity and remain there until the flesh is gone. Roots descend into the softened ground. The skeleton becomes a framework for climbing plants, then a scaffold for nests, then a ridge of mineral and compost. Decades later, the dead animal may be impossible to distinguish from the terrain. A young herd passes beside it without fear.

The predators are fewer, and consequently stranger.

Some resemble long, low cats made of tendon and black reflective skin. Others have too many joints, or no visible eyes, or mouths concealed beneath folds of mineral tissue. They do not pursue the swiftest prey. The plateau is too rich for that kind of waste. A predator finds a colony under stress and waits. It learns the rhythm of the feeding, the timing of the vent, the moment when a herd must cross exposed stone. It may remain motionless through an entire weather cycle.

Certain predators mimic the sounds of the pressure groves. Their calls begin as low mechanical pulses, almost indistinguishable from the breathing of the factories. Smaller animals approach the source, expecting warmth or nutrient vapor, and discover the mouth only when the false engine opens.

Others hunt by condensation. They flatten themselves against a rock and cool their bodies until water gathers around them. In the dry high air, the glistening patch appears to be a spring. Animals come to drink. The predator rises without haste.

There are creatures that cannot be said to belong wholly to the animal kingdom at all. Pale mats beneath the soil rearrange their tunnels in response to distant vibrations. Threadlike colonies pass through the pores of stone, dissolving certain minerals and depositing others. They join root systems to spring chambers and sometimes carry chemical messages across entire valleys. A cliff can be alive in this sense—not moving, not breathing, but responding, exchanging, and remembering.

The industrial organisms are the most conspicuous inhabitants.

They begin as squat colonies around deep mineral seams, no larger than a dwelling. Over centuries they become groves, then complexes, then structures that resemble architecture only because architecture and biology have converged on similar necessities. Their trunks harden into pressure chambers. Their ribs open and close in the wind. Their internal vanes turn with the patience of millstones.

No single part controls them. There is no brain at the center, no monarch, no master chamber. Their decisions are distributed through pressure, temperature, chemistry, and vibration. A drought causes one region to seal while another opens. An excess of heat draws slurry into higher reservoirs. A fall of spores triggers the growth of new membranes. The colony does not think as an animal thinks. It adjusts.

Its products are everywhere.

Warm vapor escapes from its chimneys and nourishes cloud. Salts gather in bright terraces. Nutrient slurry runs through buried channels toward the spring forests. Spores drift for kilometers before settling into new cracks. The machinery feeds on dead animals, mineral dust, root secretions, and the condensed remains of its own exhaust. In this way, it is both factory and stomach, both lung and weather system.

The oldest colonies have become environments in their own right. Their outer walls are forests. Their vents are nesting grounds. Their discarded plates form ridges inhabited by burrowers. Animals migrate toward them during the coldest seasons, and predators gather where the migration narrows. A colony can therefore resemble a mountain from a distance, but inside it are chambers, pressure organs, fungal gardens, nurseries, reservoirs, and slow-moving bodies whose generations have never known open sky.

There are also things that live in the machinery without belonging to it.

Small black creatures enter through maintenance pores—though maintenance is not the right word, because no one maintains the colony. They feed on films of nutrient and nest among the vanes. Their movements alter the pressure slightly. The colony compensates. Over time, the creatures become part of its operation, and their absence would be as disruptive as the loss of a root.

In a few places, the relationship has become more intimate. Animals have learned to sleep beside the warm walls. Plants have grown membranes into the exhaust vents, harvesting vapor before it disperses. Certain predators can identify a failing engine by scent and arrive before the colony collapses, feeding on the organisms it can no longer support.

Nothing here is separate for long.

The creatures of the high country are born into layers of dependence. The scraper eats stone. The scraper’s waste feeds the lichen. The lichen feeds the grazer. The grazer carries spores. The spores enter the industrial colony. The colony raises warm vapor. The vapor becomes cloud. The cloud becomes a spring. The spring feeds the forest. The forest feeds the herd. The herd feeds the soil.

Even death is a transfer of function.

The inhabitants do not seem to possess a concept of ownership. They do not fence territory, preserve resources, or build for an abstract future. Yet they defend their conditions with immense effectiveness. A plant closes its pores during a dry season. A colony redirects its slurry. A herd changes course around a newly fractured shelf. A predator abandons a valley whose condensation has failed. Life moves, withdraws, returns, and reworks the world until the world can support it again.

The highest forms of life may be the slowest.

At intervals far beneath the stone, pressure moves through the porous layers. The subterranean organisms respond by opening and sealing channels. Springs shift by the width of a hand. Entire forests lean toward the new water. The industrial colonies alter their breathing to compensate. Across centuries, the plateau behaves like a single enormous body adjusting to injury, thirst, heat, and abundance.

Whether it is conscious is impossible to say.

Nothing looks out from the black ridges. No voice rises from the factories to name itself. The herds do not gather around a fire or leave messages for their dead. The predators do not worship the machinery. The plants do not know the sky as anything other than a source of light and water.

And yet the whole continent is awake in the sense that a living body is awake: full of gradients, reflexes, hungers, exchanges, and memories stored not in thoughts but in altered paths.

The high country is inhabited by a multitude, but the multitude behaves like one patient creature.

It breathes through forests and pressure groves. It drinks through stone. It grows along every damp crack. It walks in armored herds, hunts in the ravines, and turns its waste into soil. Its oldest organs rise above the weather, exhaling clouds into the hard white sky.

It is not waiting to become a world.

It already is one.
