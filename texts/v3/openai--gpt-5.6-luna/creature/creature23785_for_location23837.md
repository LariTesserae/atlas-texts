What lives there is not a population, but a succession.

The oldest inhabitants are the heat-plants, though “plant” is only the nearest word. They have no need of rain, soil, or sunlight. They feed on gradients: the difference between a warm wall and a hotter wall, between the pressure beneath a sealed chamber and the pressure above it, between the faint charge gathered along a mineral seam and the stone surrounding it. Their mineral threads enter cracks and follow these differences inward. Given centuries, they can cross a street beneath its paving and emerge on the other side as a forest of amber blades.

They know the city by touch.

A stalk growing beside a vent opens before the vent brightens. A wall-clinging fan folds when the masonry begins to shift. Roots—if they may be called roots—withdraw from a collapsing foundation and thicken beneath a bridge that still has weight to bear. The plants do not think in the manner of animals, but the settlement is full of their decisions. They lean, close, harden, divide, and wait. Their collective behavior gives the impression of attention.

Some varieties live in the hottest places. These are squat and thick, with shells like fired clay. They grow around kiln mouths and central shafts, their bodies ribbed against the force of the rising air. When a vent pulse approaches, they crack open along hidden seams and expose rows of dark, glistening pores. The heat passing through them makes a sound like distant speech. Afterward they seal themselves with a new layer of ceramic tissue.

Others climb.

They descend from towers in curtains of rigid vine, each segment jointed and translucent. Their leaves are narrow plates, black on one side and gold on the other, so that a change in the direction of the chamber’s breath turns whole walls from shadow to tarnished fire. These climbers do not seek light alone. They seek movement. Their leaves tremble in the smallest currents and convert that trembling into growth. A bridge covered in them is never entirely still.

The tallest inhabitants grow around the central vent. They resemble trees only from a distance. Their trunks are hollow columns assembled from overlapping mineral rings, and their crowns are bundles of glasslike needles. During the great exhalations, they bend toward the opening and release their stored pollen. The pollen does not drift lightly. It pours out in glittering streams, heavy enough to slide down steps and collect in doorways. When the dust settles, new shoots appear in it—small, pale, and already jointed.

They are born from fragments of themselves.

A broken leaf becomes a bed. A shed shell becomes a wall. A wall becomes a root surface. A root surface becomes a grove. Nothing in the city is fully waste, because everything can be entered again by the mineral hunger. Even the old tools are slowly being inhabited. Threads pass through the handles. Hard growth fills the mouths of abandoned hammers. A rack of blades has become a many-stemmed flowering body, each copper edge held in place by a sheath of yellow tissue.

The plants are not alone in the place, though they have no companions in the familiar sense.

The mechanisms live there too.

They are descendants of instructions. The vanes, shutters, counterweights, and hinged plates have continued their motions for so long that the distinction between machine and resident has worn away. Their lives are measured in pulses. They wake when the deep pressure rises, turn when the air changes, and sleep when the vent dims. A tower may spend a century performing one small correction: opening a plate, holding it against the wind, closing it again. A wheel may turn only during the brief red seasons beneath the city. A gate may have forgotten what it once protected, yet still lift whenever the pressure reaches the old mark.

The plants have grown around these behaviors and entered into them.

Vines hold the vanes to their bearings. Roots brace the shutters. Seed-cases collect in channels designed for water and wait there for heat instead. When the mechanisms move, they scatter the plants’ pollen. When the plants thicken, they redirect the mechanisms’ wind. The city’s surviving machinery is not maintaining the garden, nor is the garden replacing the machinery. Each has become part of the other’s reflexes.

There are also the slow inhabitants of the stone itself.

Pressure moves through the walls in bodies too broad to see. Heat gathers beneath terraces, travels along buried seams, and breaks elsewhere with a report that shakes the whole hollow. The floor swells and settles. Mineral plates lift by the width of a finger over many years. Hairline fractures lengthen through courtyards and then close around new growth. The earth beneath the settlement has a metabolism, though it is too slow and immense to resemble breathing except during the great pulses.

The city listens to it.

Or rather, the city is made of things that respond.

When the deep stone warms, the plants open. When pressure rises, shutters lift. When the air thickens with dust, leaves flatten and the streets clear. When a wall cracks, mineral threads enter the wound. The settlement has no central will, but its countless responses form something close to purpose. It preserves channels. It strengthens load-bearing places. It directs heat toward the living growth and dust toward the waiting seed-beds.

Nothing hunts. Nothing flees. Nothing mates in darkness.

Reproduction is dispersal, and dispersal is weather. The plants release their glittering dust into every current, trusting not to chance but to abundance and pressure. A single vent pulse may carry a generation into ten chambers. Most of it dies against cold stone, if anything here can be called cold. Some lodges in a hinge. Some enters a crack too narrow to see. Some settles on the roof of a dwelling and remains there until the masonry warms enough for a first thread to descend.

A new plant may spend decades as a hairline of amber in a wall.

Then one day it opens.

Its first leaves are small and hard, shaped less for catching light than for measuring the air. It tests the chamber’s breathing. It finds the nearest seam of heat. It feels the vibration of the old machines and grows toward the rhythm. Years later, it may bind a fallen staircase or fill a tower’s interior with pale columns. Centuries after that, someone—or something—might mistake the tower for a tree.

The city itself is the largest living thing there.

Not alive because it grows in one body, but because its structures have become organs of circulation. Streets are channels. Towers are sensors. Vent shutters are valves. Kiln-houses are hardened stomachs that once transformed stone and now shelter new growth. The bridges are ribs. The deep chambers are lungs that never admit ordinary air. Every pulse passes through the settlement and leaves some alteration behind.

It remembers without memory.

Measurements remain engraved in walls, but the things they measured are gone. Machines repeat their old motions without knowing why. Plants follow patterns inherited from organisms that may have grown before the city was built. A shutter closes because a counterweight falls; the counterweight falls because the vent warms; the vent warms because the buried earth shifts; the shift opens a crack where a mineral thread has been waiting.

That is how life persists there: not as a creature, but as a chain of continuations.

The inhabitants are the hard-leaved growth, the patient stone, the breathing vents, the obedient mechanisms, and the dust that carries one into another. They do not possess the hollow. They are the hollow’s ongoing condition.

Each hot exhalation wakes them. Each long stillness lets them harden.

And while the city is gradually converted into roots, shells, columns, and glittering sediment, nothing within it recognizes the change as ruin. To the plants, a collapsed roof is a new surface. To the stone, a buried street is a vein. To the machines, a failing hinge is merely the beginning of a slower motion.

The settlement does not wait for inhabitants to return.

It has become inhabited by its own persistence.
