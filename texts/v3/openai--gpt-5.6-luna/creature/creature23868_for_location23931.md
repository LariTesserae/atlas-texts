They live beneath the stone, though “beneath” is not quite the right word.

In the hollows between one layer of the plateau and the next, where black volcanic rock meets pale limestone, there are long chambers without entrances. They are not caves. No passage leads into them, and nothing has hollowed them out. They are spaces that the mountain has left around its own occupants, the way a shell leaves room for a body.

The creatures within are called, by no one who has ever seen them, the **stillborn**.

They are broad and low, perhaps the size of a sleeping deer, though their shape is difficult to hold in the mind. Their bodies are made of overlapping plates, gray as old ice and faintly translucent at the edges. Between the plates runs a dark, wet seam. No hair grows on them. No eye is visible. Along their flanks are narrow openings that close and open with the slow rhythm of the plateau’s springs.

They do not breathe air.

They draw the minerals from water passing through the rock. A stillborn may remain for centuries with one side of its body pressed against a damp fault, absorbing the thin, permanent seep. When the water has passed through it, the creature releases nothing but warmth. This is why certain stones sweat even when the air is dry, and why the moisture gathers in the same cracks year after year. The mountain is not remembering rain. It is being touched from within.

They are not numerous. Or perhaps they are all one animal, spread through the high country by hidden contact.

A ridge may contain the end of one body and the beginning of another. A black pool may lie over its back. The long metallic notes in the quartz seams are the sound of its plates shifting by fractions of a finger-width, once in a hundred years. The great subterranean chambers beneath the terraces are not empty; they are packed with their folded limbs, their mineral-bare skulls, their pale jointed bodies arranged in patient parallel.

They do not move as animals move. They alter their position by settling.

A creature under one shelf will, over an age, become present beneath the next. It does not crawl through the dark. The rock changes around it, or the creature changes at the same imperceptible rate, so that its body is always where the pressure is most exact. If disturbed, it does not flee. It becomes heavier. The surrounding stone accepts it, closes over it, and grows smooth.

This is why nothing remains disturbed on the plateau.

A displaced stone sinks back into its balance. A footprint softens and fills with mineral bloom. A broken bone is drawn into a fissure and whitened until it resembles the rock around it. The stillborn do not feed on flesh, exactly. They feed on difference: the loose thing, the foreign thing, the thing that has not yet settled into its proper place. A feather, a tool, a dead animal, a person who has fallen and cannot rise—each is gradually taken into the mountain’s slow attention.

The herd animals know them.

They avoid certain shelves without knowing why. They stop before smooth patches of limestone and wait until the wind presses hard against their wool. Sometimes an entire herd will turn together, not away from danger but toward an empty hollow. They stand facing it, whiskers horizontal, eyes dark and reflective. Beneath them, far down, something has become aware of their weight.

The stillborn do not hunt the herds. The herds are too large, too warm, too temporary. The creatures beneath the stone prefer the small and unfinished: pale insects trapped in damp seams, roots that have entered the wrong crack, loose scree beginning to slide. When they feed, the surface trembles—not enough to see, but enough for the lichen to close its pores and for birds to lift suddenly from the cliff undersides.

The cats know them better.

A narrow-bodied hunter may lie for years beneath a terrace, its ribs moving so slowly that dust gathers between them. It waits not for prey but for a change in pressure. There is no change in the wind, so it remains still. Occasionally one of the cats disappears into a shadow line and is not seen again. Later, a pale mineral shape appears along the edge of the ledge, shaped like a curled animal with its head turned toward the basin.

The stillborn are born only once.

When one of them reaches the end of its inward life, its plates loosen. Water enters the seams. The body becomes porous and begins to pale. From its underside, small translucent forms emerge—no larger than grains of rice, with many folded legs and a dark point where a mouth may be. They scatter through the wet cracks and vanish into the stone.

There is no season for this. No mating call, no nest, no visible generation. A single birth may take place beneath a shelf while the same birds circle above it, while the same herd grazes the same cushion plants, while the same pool holds the same black reflection. The young do not grow. They become more deeply located.

At times, one comes to the surface.

It rises through a seam as a pale, jointed ridge, pushing no stone aside. It may lie exposed for a few hours or for several years. From a distance it resembles a fallen slab marked with fine dark lines. Birds land on it. Moss takes hold along its back. Wind-polished dust gathers in the depressions between its plates.

A person standing nearby might notice that the stone is warm.

They might place a hand upon it and feel, very faintly, a second pulse beneath their own. Not a heartbeat. Something slower: pressure accumulating, releasing, accumulating again. If they stayed long enough, their fingers would begin to numb. Their thoughts would lose their sequence. The desire to leave would become less urgent, then less intelligible.

The creature would not be harming them.

It would simply be making room.

The stillborn have no names for one another. They have no eyes with which to recognize the world, and no voices that could rise above the permanent roar. They know the plateau through weight, moisture, vibration, and the long pressure of light passing through stone. The wind is not weather to them. It is the outer surface of their body, forever moving while everything beneath remains arranged.

They are the reason the high country does not change.

Not because they command it, and not because they defend it. They are older than command and too slow for defense. They are the plateau’s tendency made living: the force that draws water into its old channels, animals back to their customary shelves, loose things into cracks, and time itself toward stillness.

At the blue rim, where the shelves fall away into glare, their largest bodies lie beneath the stone in a continuous buried arc.

They extend farther than the visible country.

When the wind pours over the edge, it passes across their sealed backs and enters the blankness beyond. The sustained note heard in the quartz is their answer, though the answer may take a thousand years to complete.

And beneath the hard white light, beneath the moss and lichen, beneath the pools that never deepen or dry, they wait without waiting—awake in the way mountains are awake, listening through every grain of themselves for the one disturbance that has never yet arrived.
