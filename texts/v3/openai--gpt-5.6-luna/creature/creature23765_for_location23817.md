They are called the **Hollow-Born**, though none of them are born in the hollow.

They enter the world in damp chambers near the plant’s base, where their mothers hang in woven root-nests above the black stream. At birth they are wrapped in thin leaf membranes and placed against the stem wall. There they remain until they can feel the plant breathing beneath them. An infant that cannot distinguish the rising pulse of water from the tremor of an approaching animal is considered unfinished. An infant that cries too loudly is moved to a quieter district.

They are small, narrow-bodied people, shaped by ladders and low ceilings. Their shoulders are flexible, their hands broad at the fingertips, their feet long and sensitive. They wear no heavy clothing. Most garments are made from softened inner bark and stitched with tendrils, layered according to the temperature of the wall. In the wet season, they coat themselves in a pale resin that keeps water from entering the nose and ears. Their skin carries the greenish cast of those who live without sunlight, though the youngest have a faint rose color beneath the nails.

They do not speak often.

Sound travels strangely through the hollow. A whisper can cross the stem and become, at the other end, a warning from the dark. A shout can bring down a shelf of insects, or draw the attention of something listening in the walls. So they speak with their hands, with their feet, with the pressure of a palm against a root. A finger drawn twice along a railing means *safe to pass*. Three taps means *water below*. A closed fist held against the throat means *do not make yourself known*.

Their language is partly human and partly botanical. They have words for kinds of darkness: sleeping darkness, flooded darkness, animal darkness, the darkness that comes immediately before a wall opens. They have no simple word for “dead.” A person may be *returned*, *taken into the fibers*, or *still traveling downward*. They do not say that someone has gone away unless they know where the person went.

The oldest among them are called Listeners. They spend their lives pressed against the walls, learning the plant’s internal movements. They can tell where a new root is forming by the pattern of pressure beneath their cheek. They know whether a tightening stem means growth, thirst, or injury. When the plant is ill, the Listeners walk through the city touching door-flaps and whispering into the seams. The plant never answers in words, but it sometimes alters its growth around them.

No one agrees on whether the plant is conscious.

The Children of the Vein say it has always known them. They point to the platforms that thickened beneath crowded districts, to the roots that turned away from sleeping bodies, to the sudden sealing of a wall whenever a predator entered. They believe the stem shelters them deliberately and that their task is to remain gentle enough to be kept.

The Severers believe the plant is only hungry, thirsty, and responsive. They are the ones who study its wounds. They know how quickly a cut closes, how much pressure lies behind the skin, how to coax a tendril around a frame without strangling it. They do not worship the stem. They fear it with precision.

There are also the Unrooted, who believe the city should leave.

They make maps of the outer world from the brief openings in the wall. Their maps are mostly blank, but they fill the blankness with measurements: the duration of gray light, the number of marsh-plants visible beyond a seam, the distance a sound carries across open water. They imagine houses that do not move, roads that do not collapse, a sky with no ceiling. Some have attempted to escape. Most return bleeding, carrying parasites, or speaking of a cold so immense it seemed to enter them through the eyes.

The few who do not return become part of the city’s stories.

The Hollow-Born eat what the plant and its animals provide. Their food is sour, mineral-rich, and often alive. They cultivate mats of moss, raise blind larvae in damp husks, and keep certain beetles in hanging cages until the insects fatten. They make flour from dried fungus and a bitter paste from the inner bark. During lean seasons they trap root-mice, though this is dangerous work, since the mice can vanish into a wall and emerge behind a sleeping family.

Their best hunters are not the strongest but the quietest. A hunter may wait for hours with one hand submerged in the stream, feeling the water for the faint electric tremor of an eel. They carry hooks made from hardened root and wear masks of folded leaf over the mouth. When they return, they mark the threshold of their home with a smear of blood or resin, depending on what they have brought back.

Every household keeps a house-beast.

Some are pale, many-legged things that eat mites from the bedding. Some are small amphibians trained to sit beside a door and croak when the air changes. Some are simply colonies of harmless insects encouraged to live in the walls. A house without a beast is considered vulnerable, and a household that kills its beast without cause is not permitted to trade for three days.

The city’s children are given no toys. They are given routes.

A child’s first journey begins at the family pod and ends at the nearest water shelf. The second reaches a market. The third crosses a bridge that sways beneath the weight of unseen life. They learn which roots may be trusted, which moss conceals holes, and how to freeze when a shadow passes overhead. Games consist of imitating animals, guessing the direction of falling water, or hiding a bright beetle somewhere in the dark and listening for who finds it.

They are never taught to ask whether the hollow is a prison.

That question is too large and too human. The hollow is a body, a weather system, a nest, a road, a mouth. It closes around them and carries them forward. They can resent it, praise it, tend it, fear it, but they cannot stand outside it long enough to judge what it is.

When someone dies, the city gathers quietly.

The body is wrapped in a leaf whose veins have been opened with a bone needle. The dead are carried to a soft place in the wall, usually near a new growth, and pressed into the plant’s warm white fibers. No stone marks the burial. Instead, the family waits for some trace to return: a stair thickened by pale tissue, a root that bends toward their home, a new leaf with an unusual fold.

If the dead appear again in the structure of the city, this is taken as a kindness.

If they do not, it is assumed they have gone farther down.

The Hollow-Born are not a people who hope to conquer their world. Their victories are smaller and stranger. A dry sleeping place. A safe crossing. A wall that closes before the predator reaches it. One more season in the same district before the plant grows through it.

At night, they lie in their seed-pods with the living flaps sealed over them. Their bodies rise and fall with the stem’s breathing. Above, below, and through the walls, the animals move. Water threads its way downward. New roots search blindly for room.

And sometimes, in the middle of the darkness, the whole city feels the plant turn—not violently, not even with intention, but with the slow adjustment of something alive that has noticed its own weight.

Then every inhabitant wakes at once.

For a moment, thousands of hands rest against the walls.

The city listens.

The plant grows.
