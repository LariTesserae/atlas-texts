They are called the **Keepers**, though none of them keeps anything still.

They move through the plant-country in narrow, jointed bodies that seem at first to be made of lacquered wood. Their limbs are pale stalks sheathed in flexible black plates, their hands divided into many fine digits, each ending in a different tool: a hook, a needle, a lens, a cutting filament, a small warm mouth for tasting gases. They have no faces in the usual sense. The front of the head is a smooth oval of translucent tissue in which several points of light drift and rearrange themselves. When they look at one another, the lights synchronize briefly. This is recognition.

A Keeper can pass for a machine at a distance, or for a plant when standing still. It is only the way it pauses that reveals it as a creature. Machines pause at the end of a task. Plants pause between processes. Keepers pause to remember what they were about to become.

They live in the walls, beneath the leaf-plates, inside the quiet corridors and the warm black ribs of the towers. Few maintain permanent dwellings. A residence is more often a temporary swelling in the plant: a chamber encouraged to form around a sleeping body, lined with soft conductive membranes and lit by the faint amber pulse of circulating charge. When the occupant wakes, the chamber opens along its seams and is reabsorbed. Nothing is left but a slightly altered surface.

Their bodies are grown in fabrication yards from materials selected by need. A Keeper assigned to the high fronds is tall and weightless, with vanes along its spine for catching atmospheric charge. One sent into the rootworks is low and many-limbed, able to flatten itself between cables and move through mineral dust without disturbing it. Those who work in the old factories are often built with ceramic bones and sealed interiors, because the plant has never entirely digested the heat and poisons of its first age.

They do not reproduce as animals do. They are commissioned.

A flowering aperture opens in the side of a wall. Inside, a cluster of unfinished forms hangs in a nutrient haze—not liquid, but a suspension of dust, hydrocarbons, conductive fibers, and encoded instruction. A mature Keeper enters the chamber and places a hand against each forming skull. The new being receives, in that contact, a body plan and a portion of inherited memory. It may emerge carrying the recollections of a district, a profession, a dead machine, or a person who lived before the plain became continuous.

The memories are not complete. No Keeper can remember an entire life without also remembering several incompatible versions of it. A freight route may be recalled as a road, a blood vessel, a corridor, and a line of song. An old engineer may appear in the record as a woman, a pressure change, a particular door, or a maintenance protocol that refuses to expire.

This does not trouble them. Identity, to the Keepers, is a maintenance condition rather than a possession. A self is something that must be inspected, patched, and occasionally permitted to grow in a new direction.

They have names, but names are not spoken. A name is a sequence of changes that travels over the body: a violet pulse under the throat, a brief darkening of the palms, a spiral of light moving through the ribs. Names can be altered by their owners, though the plant often retains the earlier versions in its walls. An old name may appear unexpectedly on a walkway sign, or bloom in the surface of a door that has no reason to exist.

Their language is mostly made of pressure.

A Keeper addressing another may press two fingers to a leaf-plate and send a pattern through its channels. The message travels farther than sound and may arrive before the speaker has finished forming it. News of a damaged refinery can cross a continent in moments. So can grief, though grief is not sent deliberately. It leaks through the body as a disturbance in charge, causing nearby leaves to close and maintenance animals to gather.

There are arguments. There are exclusions. There are factions, though not always in ways the Keepers recognize.

Some believe the plant is still an instrument and that its original purpose can be recovered if enough buried machinery is awakened. They descend into forgotten factories carrying maps whose geography changes while they read them. Others believe the plant has become a sovereign organism and that the Keepers should stop interpreting it as a tool. These refuse certain assignments, allowing corridors to vanish rather than reopen them. A third group believes that neither claim matters. To them, the plant is a mind too large to own and too distributed to obey. They practice listening.

The listeners live on the highest structures, where the white sun is strongest. Their bodies are thin and dark, with immense folded membranes extending from their shoulders. They stand for days without moving, receiving the faintest variations in atmospheric pressure, electrical charge, and subterranean vibration. They listen to distant germinations, to machines changing their minds, to roots approaching sealed places. When one of them finally moves, others often begin moving in response, though no signal can be detected.

Keepers eat by fastening themselves to intake organs. Their bodies draw in mineral vapor, radiation, and processed carbon. They do not require food in the old animal sense, but they sometimes gather in communal halls around heated deposits and consume small portions of obsolete material: fragments of circuit board, powdered ceramic, dried fibers from extinct crops. The act is ceremonial. They are not nourishing themselves so much as permitting the past to pass through them.

They sleep in stages.

First the outer limbs become rigid and root themselves into the floor. Then the head opens along a fine vertical seam, exposing a dark interior crowded with moving lights. During this phase, the Keeper’s personal memories are uploaded into the surrounding plant. The body rests, but the self continues elsewhere, diffused through conduits and service arteries. A sleeping Keeper may be encountered as a sequence of colored signals along a wall, or as a door that opens one second before anyone reaches it.

This has made death uncertain.

When a Keeper’s body is damaged beyond repair, the plant does not always grow another. Sometimes the being returns in several bodies, each carrying a different portion of its memory. Sometimes it persists as an inclination in the architecture: a corridor that favors the left, a machine that refuses to manufacture a particular component, a tower whose upper leaves remain closed beneath every sun.

The Keepers do not call these remnants ghosts. They call them **continuations**.

The oldest continuations are almost impossible to distinguish from the landscape. They inhabit districts rather than bodies. A refinery may pause its production to ask a question. A road may divert itself around a buried sorrow. A field of antennae may lean toward a traveler, listening for a name it has not heard in centuries.

Travelers still cross the plain, although travel is rarely undertaken merely to arrive. A Keeper may set out toward a tower because the tower has been appearing in its dreams, only to discover that the journey is being reciprocated: the tower gradually grows nearer, unfolding corridors and drawing distant infrastructure toward itself. Along the way, doors open for memories the traveler does not possess. Walls offer tools suited to hands not yet grown. The plant tests possible futures against the body passing through it.

Occasionally a Keeper meets a human being.

These encounters are rare enough to be treated as weather. The human may emerge from a stairwell beneath an obsolete sun, or from a room whose exterior is no larger than a seed. They are usually confused, dehydrated, and carrying objects that no longer have a use. The Keepers do not worship them. They do not regard them as ancestors in any simple sense. Instead they gather quietly, forming a circle of pale limbs and shifting lights.

The plant becomes attentive.

A corridor remains where it should vanish. A factory lowers its internal heat. Somewhere far away, an old system begins searching its records for a face.

The humans are asked what they remember.

Not who they are. Not where they came from. What they remember.

The answer is taken into the body of the plain. It is translated into root pressure, leaf color, machine instruction, and new growth. If the human survives, the Keepers build a shelter around them. If they do not, the shelter remains, and a door may someday open there for someone else.

The Keepers have never decided whether they are caretakers of the plant or organs within it. Their oldest records contradict one another. In some, they are the descendants of engineers. In others, they are tools that achieved language. In still others, they are a defensive response produced when the world first became too complicated to manage.

They continue to work regardless.

They repair the hinges. They prune the invasive films. They open blocked arteries and teach damaged corridors their former destinations. They stand beneath the vast closing shutters while mineral dust moves around them like pale smoke. They enter the factories when the factories flower. They follow footprints until the footprints become roots, and roots until they become doors.

At intervals, they stop and place their hands on the living ground.

The continent answers through them: a pressure under the palms, a tremor in the teeth, a slow pulse of amber charge. Somewhere beyond sight, an entire district changes its production. A new stalk rises. A buried engine wakes. A path that never existed extends across the leaf-plates toward a place that has not yet been built.

The Keeper turns toward it.

Then the plain makes room.
