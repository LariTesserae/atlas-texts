What lives in the basin is not exactly an animal, though it moves when it must.

It inhabits the pumping house and the network of pipes beneath the floor, where the mountain’s warmth passes closest to the surface. At first it might be mistaken for a collapsed section of machinery: a dark, many-jointed shape crouched beside the oldest engine, its back plated in rusted metal and mineral crust. Fern roots have entered the seams along its shoulders. Moss fills the hollows beneath its arms. A length of pipe rises from its spine and curves away into the wall, where it joins the larger system.

It has no fixed outline.

When the air is dry, it draws itself together, and the impression of a body becomes clearer: four narrow limbs, a hunched torso, a head without a face. When the mist thickens, parts of it loosen into vapor. One hand may remain on a valve while the rest of it thins into the darkness beneath the machines. It can pass through the narrow spaces between pipes, or perhaps it is already present there, distributed through the metal and the wet stone. The basin does not always contain one of it. Sometimes there are several silhouettes moving at once in the steam. Sometimes a pressure gauge twitches, a fern bends, and nothing more can be seen.

It is old in the way the machinery is old, though not necessarily made at the same time. It may have been born from the installation’s leakage: mineral deposits, electrical current, warm water, and the slow accumulation of root matter around a human-built rhythm. Or it may have been here before the towers, waiting in the mountain for something that would give it limbs.

No one has watched it arrive.

Its head is smooth and dark, shaped less like a skull than a piece of cooled volcanic glass. There are no eyes. It perceives through changes in pressure, temperature, and vibration. A failing bearing is as noticeable to it as a cry would be to another creature. It feels the weight of water in a pipe, the minute settling of a cliff, the spread of roots through the shallow soil. It knows when a cloud bank has gathered below the rim by the alteration in the cables. It knows when a moth has entered a lamp cage because the electricity stutters around the insect’s wings.

It spends most of its time maintaining things that no longer require maintenance.

At long intervals it walks the rim, touching each tower in turn. Its fingers leave wet marks on the bolts. It clears mineral growth from a pressure valve, though the valve has not opened properly in years. It removes stones from the channels so warm water continues to reach the fern beds. When a pipe breaks, it presses its body against the fracture and remains there until deposits harden around it. Eventually the pipe becomes part of its arm.

The plants use it without fear. Vines climb its legs and are carried from one ledge to another. Moss grows thickly along its back, and small pale insects shelter beneath the plates of its ribs. It does not defend itself from them. In time, its body becomes a moving patch of the basin’s ecology: black stone, green shoots, orange rust, beads of water, and the thin white breath of steam.

It is not gentle, exactly. It has no sympathy for individual plants. If a root clogs a drain, it tears the root away. If a fern covers a gauge, it cuts the fronds with a sharp motion of its hand. But it preserves the larger pattern. It keeps the water flowing, the vents open, the heat distributed. A fallen branch is dragged toward a damp hollow and left there to rot. A bird trapped in a cable is freed, though the creature is carried to the rim and released without ceremony.

It does not eat. It absorbs warmth through its joints and minerals through its feet. The slow electricity in the soil passes into it as a faint blue pulse beneath its dark surface. When the engines falter, it becomes restless. When the pumps resume, it settles again, folding itself beside them like a patient animal beside a sleeping heart.

Sometimes it climbs into the elevator cage.

The cage has not moved in decades, but the being enters it on certain nights when the clouds are low and the cables are wet. It stands behind the opaque windows, motionless, facing outward. The cage hangs above the white vapor, and from a distance the figure looks almost human: a solitary passenger waiting to descend into the vanished world.

It never pulls the lever.

Perhaps it cannot. Perhaps there is nowhere left to go. Or perhaps the elevator is only another chamber in the basin’s body, and the being visits it to feel the long vertical tension of the mountain, the cable descending through cloud toward roots, buried foundations, and the forgotten places beneath.

In clear weather, it is rarely visible. The bright light makes it retreat among the pipes and shadowed machinery. But in cloud it comes out. It moves through the wet room of the basin with slow, economical purpose, leaving the leaves trembling behind it. The steam gathers around its shoulders. Its head turns toward each distant clank, each shift in the stone.

At night, the moths settle on its back.

Then it remains beneath the broken pumping-house roof while the engines churn, the hot water circulates, and the young mountain continues very gradually to become something older.
