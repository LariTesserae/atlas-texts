What lives there is not a species, but a congregation.

No creature enters the hollow alone for long. The smallest are born in its seams and spend their lives passing through the bodies of others: drinking from condensation, feeding on shed skins, carrying eggs, spores, mineral dust, and the faint electrical messages that travel through the stone. They are pale because color has no use in the dark, soft because there is little room to unfold, and patient because nothing here can afford to waste movement.

They know the mountain by touch.

A blind thing approaching a ledge does not see whether the ledge is empty. It feels the minute tremors traveling through it. It knows the difference between water falling into a basin and water striking a living back. It knows when a bell-throated creature is about to call because the ceiling tightens around its hooked feet. It knows when a slab is about to shift, though perhaps only a breath before the stone moves.

The little animals are the first inhabitants one notices, though they are not the first to notice anything. They flow through the hollow in slow currents. Some are no larger than fingers, with translucent bellies and narrow mouths that open sideways. Some are bead-bodied, joined in chains that separate and reunite according to warmth. Some wear shells so thin that the light trapped in the lichen shines through them. They crawl over one another without hostility, each taking what it can from the skin of the next: moisture, salt, fungus, traces of blood.

Their abundance has made them indifferent to individuality. A body is useful while it moves, feeds, senses, or carries young. After that it becomes architecture.

The long salamander-things are the hollow’s deep roads. They lie in the channels between stones, their bodies hardly distinguishable from wet mineral except for the slow traveling of their ribs. Their skin is nearly transparent, and within it something dark circulates in branching vessels. They do not hunt so much as receive. Smaller creatures climb onto them, burrow into them, and sometimes disappear between their ribs. The salamanders tolerate this until hunger arrives.

Then one of them uncoils.

It does not strike. It gathers itself with terrible patience, drawing its length inward until the surrounding animals have nowhere left to go. Its mouth opens beneath its head like a second crack in the floor. Whatever is caught there vanishes without struggle. The creature feeds until its sides swell, then settles again, becoming part of the ledge, the wall, the wet black geometry of the chamber.

When two of the long ones meet, they do not fight. They press their heads together. For several breaths, color moves beneath their skins: a dim pulse of violet, then green, then a shade too faint to name. Perhaps they exchange scent. Perhaps memory. Perhaps nothing more than the location of water and the presence of danger. They separate afterward with the solemnity of things that have recognized one another and found recognition sufficient.

The bell-throated creatures belong to the ceiling.

They are larger than they first appear, folded into themselves so tightly that their wings resemble hanging rock and their bodies seem to be knots in the mountain. Their feet end in hooks capable of gripping glassy stone. Their faces are hidden beneath layered membranes. When they breathe, the membranes inflate, and the whole ceiling seems to take a breath with them.

They call only when the pressure inside the mountain changes, or when one of them has found a new fracture, or for reasons that have been forgotten by every living thing except the call itself.

The sound begins under the stone.

A vibration passes through the walls, through the legs and shells and wet bodies of the smaller inhabitants. Then it rises into the air as a hollow boom. One bell-throated thing answers. Another answers from behind the wall. A third, buried somewhere beneath the floor, returns the note so low that the water in the central basin trembles.

The callers do not gather together. They remain where they are, separated by darkness and stone, making a map of the mountain with their voices. Their calls tell the blind animals where the walls are. They tell the plants which way to turn. They tell the oldest creatures, the ones whose bodies have become mineral, that the mountain is still shifting and that there is still time to move.

The plants listen.

Their stalks are black and jointed, their single leaves clear as frozen breath. They do not seek the sun. They seek disturbance. When something crawls nearby, they turn toward it. When the bell-throated creatures call, every stalk in the hollow inclines toward the vibration, one after another, until the chamber seems to be listening with a forest of small, glassy ears.

The animals use them without understanding them. A warm body brushing a stalk leaves behind a film of moisture. Tiny organisms gather on the leaf. The stalk bends, the leaf folds, and the organisms are drawn into a slit along its edge. Nothing in this transaction is deliberate. The plant is not hunting. The animal is not feeding the plant. The hollow simply permits one hunger to overlap another.

The oldest residents are almost no longer animals.

They are fixed in the cracks, swollen around their bones, their backs crusted with mineral. Some have mouths that open once every few days. Others breathe through the stone. When the mountain shifts, these ancient bodies flex before the rock does, as if they are feeling the movement from the inside. Their skin has become gray and granular. Lichen grows across them. Eggs are laid beneath their ribs.

They are not revered, because reverence requires distance, and nothing in the hollow has distance from anything else. The young crawl over them. The hungry bite them. The dead are pulled into their folds. They are shelter, food, anchor, and wall in equal measure.

The inhabitants do not mourn their dead. They dismantle them.

A fallen body is opened quickly, not from cruelty but from necessity. The soft parts go first. The shell is stripped. The bones, when there are bones, are carried into cracks and fixed there with saliva, blood, and gray paste. A dead creature may become the hinge of a nest, the brace of a bridge, the armature of a new body. Nothing is discarded because there is nowhere for waste to go.

Even the mountain is consumed in this way. The animals lick the fresh fractures for salts. They scrape the black faces of new stone with mineralized mouths. Their eggs cling to the wet seams. Their bodies fill the hollows left by cooling rock. The mountain rises, breaks, settles, and the inhabitants answer by taking possession of every new surface.

They have no leader. No single creature understands the entire chamber. Yet each participates in its rhythm.

When the air dries, the animals compress themselves toward the seams. When fog enters, they loosen and spread. When warmth appears, the traffic thickens. When sound crosses the hollow, bodies turn toward it in layers: first the filaments, then the small pale crawlers, then the salamander-things, then the buried creatures that cannot move but can still answer by breathing.

At the center, the black basin receives the chamber’s pulse.

The creatures nearest the water open and close around its edge. They draw the basin into themselves and pass it outward. A body drinks from another body; a body drinks from that body; the moisture travels through skins, mouths, eggs, moss, and mineralized tissue until it reaches the farthest cracks. The pool feeds the hollow without ever being touched directly. It is not owned. It is circulated.

Sometimes, when the mountain trembles, the pulse rises through everything at once.

The small animals stop. The long bodies tighten. The hanging creatures unfold their throats. The plants turn. For a moment every living surface is arranged around the same invisible event, and the chamber seems to become one immense, many-bodied creature: blind, damp, crowded, and awake.

Then the tremor passes.

The little animals resume their patient traffic. A shell scrapes stone. Something clicks inside the wall. A wet body slips across the floor and leaves behind a trail that shines briefly before fading. Above, a bell-throated thing begins its call, and the answer travels through the mountain.

They do not know that they live at the summit. They do not know that clouds move below them or that the peaks beyond are vast. Their world is measured in vibrations, condensation, warmth, hunger, and the narrow distance between one body and the next.

The hollow is not their shelter.

It is their body, and they are its circulating thoughts.
