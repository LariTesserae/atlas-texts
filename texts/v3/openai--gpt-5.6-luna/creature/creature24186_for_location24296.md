The people of the mountain are built for continuation.

They are not especially tall. Height is a liability in the low-pressure districts, and the older families tend toward compact bodies, broad chests, dense bones, and hands marked by the fine burns of hot metal. Their skin carries the colors of their surroundings: mineral reds, furnace browns, the pale gray of condensation rooms. Many have a faint metallic cast to their teeth and nails, inherited from generations of breathing trace elements no filter has entirely removed.

They do not speak of being born in a city. They say they were opened into it.

A child enters the world in a sealed chamber attached to a water plant, a medical block, or a residence whose walls have been tested against the mountain’s pressure. The first air is measured. The first breath is recorded. The child is given not only a name but an elevation, a tolerance range, and a maintenance schedule. These things can later change, but the original values remain in family records, like the first coordinates of a building before it begins to settle.

Their names are often short and hard-edged, suited to being spoken through masks and machinery. Yet a person may acquire several names over a lifetime. There is a birth-name, a work-name, a pressure-name used by those who know what the body has endured, and sometimes a private name given by someone who has watched them sleep through a tremor.

They are a people accustomed to being measured.

The city knows their pulse in the clinics, their water consumption in the residences, the heat they release into ventilation chambers, the hours they spend under different pressures. It knows which lungs have scarred, which bones have developed in the wrong alignment, which workers are beginning to lose sensitivity in their fingertips. No one finds this entirely invasive. To be unmeasured is more frightening. An unrecorded body is a leak, a fault, an absence in the city’s calculations.

They work in intimate contact with processes too large to understand.

A pump operator may spend a lifetime adjusting valves that regulate chambers extending kilometers below the inhabited terraces. A rail worker may know the moods of a particular bridge: which joints complain in the morning, which support warms before a deep shift, where the floor begins to incline a little more each year. There are surveyors who can identify a change in the mountain by the way dust settles on a railing. There are people whose profession is to listen—not to voices, but to the delayed knocks and long mineral groans rising through the stone.

The most respected workers are not the strongest or most inventive. They are the ones who notice a small wrongness before it becomes an event.

A pump that pauses too long.

A seam that sweats without condensation.

A door that closes half a second more slowly than yesterday.

A tremor arriving with no corresponding change in the deep instruments.

These observations are passed through the city with a seriousness other places reserve for prophecy. A person who catches a failure early may be remembered for generations, not because they saved many lives in a dramatic moment, but because no one ever knew anything had been in danger.

There is little distinction between civic duty and survival. Every adult is trained in several forms of repair. Children learn to seal a conduit before they learn to write a long sentence. They are taught how to find the nearest pressure shelter, how to recognize a dangerous mineral bloom, how to sleep through alarms, and how to turn off a machine without damaging the system it belongs to. Games imitate emergencies. Adolescents race along service corridors carrying weighted tools. Families practice evacuation routes during festivals, laughing when the warning lights come on.

They do laugh. The mountain has not made them solemn, only precise.

Their humor is dry and local. They make jokes about elevation, weak lungs, delayed trains, and buildings that have shifted into one another. Lovers accuse each other of poor structural alignment. A person who changes their mind is said to be undergoing uplift. Someone who cannot keep a secret has a porous wall. Marriage vows sometimes include a promise to remain reachable after a foundation settles.

They love through maintenance.

A partner replaces a filter before it clogs. A child keeps the heat from a sleeping parent’s room within a safe range. Friends save one another the clearer portions of reclaimed water. To visit someone is often to inspect something on their behalf: a window seal, a heater, a joint in the floor, the calibration of a private condenser. Affection may be shown by leaving a tool where it will be needed, or by remembering that a certain person prefers the air at one pressure above the official recommendation.

Their homes are not private in the way homes are elsewhere. Every room is part of a system. Walls carry heat, water, messages, and vibration. Pipes pass through sleeping chambers. A neighbor may be able to feel a family cooking, arguing, or moving furniture through the shared structure. People learn to distinguish the signatures of those around them: the particular rhythm of a stairwell door, the footfall of a child, the pulse of a pump belonging to an elderly couple three levels below.

Silence is therefore a rare luxury, and a complicated one. In the deepest rooms, where insulation has nearly severed a person from the city, the absence of vibration can feel like abandonment. Some people sleep with a machine running beside them simply to hear that the world continues.

Others seek the opposite. They join the Rimward communities, living near the unfinished edges where the buildings are sparse and the air is dangerous. These people maintain survey stakes, antenna fields, and construction lines that may not be used for decades. They are regarded as eccentric, though not necessarily unhappy. They prefer the open drop, the unshielded sky, the knowledge that there is nothing between their bodies and the mountain’s next shape.

They keep careful journals.

Not diaries, exactly. They record the angle of light, the growth of ridges, changes in the distance to neighboring peaks. They sketch the exposed faces of the rock and compare them year by year. A child raised on the rim may know the contour of a far slope better than the faces of distant relatives. When such a person dies, their maps are often installed in public archives, where others continue to annotate them as the world changes.

The people have inherited an old fear of the mountain, but it has no single object.

They fear collapse, of course. They fear the sudden opening of deep chambers, the failure of a pressure wall, the uplift that can turn a road into a ramp or a building into a trap. But beneath these practical fears is something less manageable: the suspicion that the mountain is not merely changing, but responding.

It answers drills.

It closes around abandoned structures.

It produces chambers that resemble organs, rails that lead into untouched rock, pulses too regular to be dismissed as heat. Sometimes the instruments detect a movement beneath the city that is not seismic in the ordinary sense. Sometimes a whole district wakes at the same hour after hearing a soft sequence of knocks far below, each one separated by the same interval.

The authorities call these events geological.

The workers call them messages.

The city has religions, though none are universally acknowledged. Some people believe the mountain is an unfinished body and that the city lives inside its nervous system. Others believe the city itself is the thing becoming alive, using the mountain as raw material and the people as temporary organs. There are sects that worship uplift, sects that worship erosion, and sects that believe completion would be a catastrophe. Their members deliberately leave some structures unfinished: a stairway leading nowhere, a wall without its final plate, a reservoir with one chamber kept empty.

Most people do not belong to such groups. They still carry small rituals.

Before entering a deep shaft, a worker touches the wall with two fingers.

Before a new building is occupied, its first water is poured into the foundation.

When someone dies, their body is not buried. Organic matter cannot be permitted to remain loose in the city. The dead are mineralized in controlled furnaces, their remaining water recovered, their carbon fixed into black ceramic, their metals separated and returned to circulation. Families may receive a small sealed tile containing the person’s processed remains. These tiles are placed in walls, bridge supports, kitchen counters, and public buildings.

The dead become part of where the living stand.

No one considers this desecration. It is understood as a final citizenship.

The rare creatures of the condensation works are treated differently. The people are afraid of them, but also protective. During the brief rains, workers pause to watch the pale bodies emerge from grates and warm seams. The creatures move with an awkward, jointed delicacy, transparent in places, carrying the reflected orange of industrial lights inside themselves. They vanish before anyone can study them properly.

There are laws against capturing them.

There are also illegal markets where preserved fragments are sold in tiny pressure cases.

A few people claim the creatures recognize individuals. They say that one will follow a particular worker through the drains, or appear only when a certain maintenance team is on shift. The stories are dismissed by engineers and repeated by everyone else. Children leave bits of polished glass near the gutters for them. Sometimes the glass is gone afterward. Sometimes it is merely covered in mineral dust, and the child insists this means the creature accepted it.

The city’s machines are not quite inhabitants, but they are treated as neighbors.

Old pumps are given names. Rail engines accumulate reputations. A ventilation tower that has operated for three hundred years may have a commemorative plaque, a preferred repair crew, and a ceremonial shutdown once each decade. People speak of machines as if they possess moods, though the distinction between mood and malfunction is not always useful.

A machine that repeatedly saves power for no reason may be called thrifty.

One that overheats only when a particular supervisor is present is considered spiteful.

A door that opens for the dead is dismantled immediately, but not before someone asks what it wanted.

There are children who prefer machines to people because machines are honest about needing things. There are old workers who insist that no machine is ever empty, that every device retains a trace of the hands that built it, repaired it, and relied upon it. They speak of the city as a population with different rates of life: people living for decades, buildings for centuries, pumps for millennia, and the mountain beneath them moving on a scale so slow it resembles stillness.

The inhabitants do not think of themselves as conquering the mountain.

They know better than that.

They are tenants, parasites, caretakers, and symptoms. They cut chambers into its body and brace its fractures. They extract heat, water, metal, pressure, and light. In return, they keep the mountain from injuring itself too quickly. Their industries are a form of negotiation. Their streets, pipes, and adjustable foundations are arguments made in stone:

Not yet.

Slowly.

This way.

At the highest terrace, the surveyors sometimes find fresh marks where no tool has been used. A shallow line across the rock. A circular depression. A new seam exhaling warmth. They record these things and build around them when possible.

If a structure must be moved, they move it.

If a road must be abandoned, they leave it beneath the next road.

If the mountain rises, they raise with it.

And when the brief rain comes, the people gather beneath their collectors and watch the water bead on the black surfaces. For a few minutes, everything is dark and shining. Their faces appear in the wet stone beside the faces of strangers, workers, children, machines, and the enormous slope itself.

Then the rain is drawn away.

The city begins again to dry, to glow, to settle, to grow.
