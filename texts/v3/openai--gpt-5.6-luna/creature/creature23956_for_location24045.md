They are not cave dwellers in the old sense. They do not inhabit a finished hollow, tucked safely beneath the world. They live inside a process.

The people who have made the shelters call themselves the **Naru**, though the word means less *people* than *those who remain in contact*. It refers to their feet, their listening bones, their habit of keeping one hand on the ground whenever they stop moving. Outsiders might mistake them for small, dark human beings, but their bodies have been shaped by a place where the floor cannot be trusted and the walls speak before they break.

They are narrow through the chest and wide through the feet. Each foot has six flexible toes, two of them turned slightly backward, so they can grip sloping shelves and feel the faintest tremor passing through stone. Their knees and wrists bend farther than a human’s would, giving them a low, folded gait. They rarely stand fully upright except on stable ground, which is rare enough to seem ceremonial.

Their skin is thick and pale beneath a coating of mineral dust. Some have dark iron-colored patches along their shoulders and backs, not pigment exactly, but deposits grown into the skin over generations. These patches harden where the body most often meets stone. Children are born soft and nearly hairless; as they age, fine sensory filaments appear along the jaw, ankles, and fingers. The filaments lie flat when the ground is calm. Before a collapse, they lift.

The Naru have no word for silence. The closest word means *a moment in which the stone has not yet answered*.

They are always listening.

A Naru kneeling beside a pool may seem to be drinking, but the mouth is not the important part. The hands rest beneath the surface, and the fingertips read the tiny disturbances traveling through the water. A hunter pressed against a wall may appear motionless for an hour, though the body is receiving a flood of information: the click of armored feeders nearby, the weight of a creature crossing a shelf overhead, the distant compression of a plate beginning to shift.

They speak aloud, but speech is only for the immediate world. Their deeper conversations pass through touch.

Two Naru may sit facing opposite directions with their palms laid on the same slab. A sequence of taps travels between them. One tap means *water*. A rapid tremor means *many bodies approaching*. A long pressure, held until the stone warms, means *do not build here*. Their language is not made of fixed signs so much as changing textures. Meaning depends on speed, force, distance, and the material carrying the message. A message sent through bone sounds different from one sent through wet clay.

Their songs are therefore not songs in the human sense. They are patterns of vibration made by striking plates of shell, stretching cords between stone pegs, or humming through the chest while touching the ground. A family may leave a warning behind in a wall: a faint repeated pulse saying that the ledge has become hollow. If the wall collapses, the message ends. If it holds, the warning slowly changes as the stone changes.

They do not name places permanently. They name conditions.

A shelter is called **Under-Weight**, **Where Water Hesitates**, or **The Shelf That Has Answered Twice**. A route may be known as **Three Safe Steps, Then Crawl**, though by the following season it may have become a sinkhole. Their maps are made from cords, beads, and layered scraps of hide, but the map is never considered complete. It is not an image of the world. It is a record of what the world did recently.

The Naru are born in communal hollows lined with warm clay. Several adults attend each birth, not because childbirth is private or sacred, but because the newborn must be introduced to the ground. The infant is placed belly-down on a thin stone plate. The adults tap around it in a slow widening circle. The child’s first response is watched carefully. If it turns toward the strongest vibration, it is said to have a **deep ear**. If it startles at every sound, it is wrapped in hide and carried for several weeks through calmer districts. If it presses its face to the stone and becomes still, the elders call it fortunate.

Children are not taught to walk. They are taught to stop.

They learn the difference between a normal tremor and a coming break. They learn not to run when the animals run, because panic travels faster than understanding. They learn that a soft-looking patch of clay may bear weight while a glossy shelf may be hollow beneath its skin. They learn to sleep in short intervals and to wake when the shelter’s cords change tension.

Their games are imitations of disaster. One child strikes a slab at random while the others must find a route to safety. Another scatters pale stones and shifts them while the rest attempt to reconstruct the path. Older children build small unstable structures from shell fragments, then crouch nearby to predict which piece will fall first. Laughter is common during these games. A child who guesses wrongly is not scolded. The point is to be wrong while the consequences are small.

The Naru eat whatever the cavern offers, but they are not indiscriminate. They gather the soft-bodied swimmers from warm pools, scrape mineral-feeding plates from the shelves, and trap the tall stilt-creatures only when a migration makes them plentiful. They keep some animals alive near their shelters: pale, many-legged things that consume waste and loosen compacted sediment; small membrane-winged forms whose agitation warns of changes in air pressure; blind, eel-like swimmers that are released into new pools to test whether the water is safe.

The animals are not considered property. The Naru have no exact equivalent of ownership. A creature may be tethered, fed, guided, or harvested, but no one claims that it belongs to them. To possess a living thing is thought to mean taking responsibility for every change it causes. Since every creature changes the ground, possession would be too large a promise.

They do, however, form attachments.

A particular stilt-creature may return to the same district for years, allowing small Naru to climb its warm plated legs. A cloud of winged scavengers may roost above one family’s shelter and be fed scraps of shell. Hunters recognize individual armored feeders by the patterns in their plates. When one dies, the Naru do not preserve it. They sit near the body while it is being dismantled by the smaller animals, and they sing into the stone beneath it. The song is not mourning exactly. It is a statement of exchange: *You were here. Now the ground is changed.*

The dead are given to the cavern.

There are no sealed graves. Bodies are placed in shallow hollows where water and scavengers can reach them. Tools, if the dead had favored tools, are laid nearby but not buried. Within days, the remains begin to lose their shape. The Naru return to touch the mound as it hardens. A respected hunter might become a low platform near a water seam. A child might become part of a narrow ridge used by generations of travelers. The dead are not believed to watch over the living. They are believed to continue as pressure, shelter, mineral, and altered pathways.

This does not make death gentle.

The Naru fear being sealed alive by fast-growing stone. They fear the sudden liquefaction of old shelves. They fear water appearing beneath a sleeping body. They fear being separated from the group during a migration of animals, because the cavern can become impassable in minutes. Their courage is not a lack of fear but a practiced refusal to let fear choose the direction of their feet.

Some among them become **readers**, spending their lives near the walls. Their fingers are permanently enlarged and ridged from contact with rock. Others become **path-makers**, carrying poles, cords, and pale marker stones through unstable districts. There are **water-callers**, who know where condensation will gather after a pressure change, and **beast-turners**, who understand how to redirect a feeding front before it overruns a shelter.

The most respected are the **unfixed**: people who have lived long enough to recognize that every safe place is temporary. They refuse permanent shelters and carry only what can be lifted during a tremor. They sleep in different districts each cycle, following the animals and the water. To the settled-minded, they seem restless. To the Naru, they are simply people who have stopped asking the ground to promise anything.

Still, the Naru build.

They lash bridges over cracks that will widen. They raise platforms on walls that may fold inward. They stack stones along routes that will be buried. Their construction is not an attempt to defeat instability. It is a way of entering into conversation with it. A bridge says: *For now, bodies may pass here.* A shelter says: *While this angle holds, we will gather beneath it.* A marker says: *Someone noticed this place before it changed.*

At any moment, a whole district may begin to move. The Naru scatter with the other animals, low to the ground, carrying infants against their chests and tools in their teeth. They do not shout warnings. Shouting is useless against the roar. Instead, they strike the stone in patterns, sending signals through the shelves. Somewhere beyond the vapor, another group feels the answering tremor and begins to move as well.

When the disturbance ends, they return.

Not to rebuild what was lost. That would be an act of denial. They return to see what exists now: a new basin, a broken passage, a warm seam, a body becoming a ledge. They collect what can be used. They mark the dead. They taste the water. They put their hands to the altered stone.

Then they begin again, not because they believe the cavern will settle, but because it will not.

They live there as animals live there, as water lives there, as the unfinished earth lives there: feeding, breaking, joining, and becoming something else before anyone has learned its name.
