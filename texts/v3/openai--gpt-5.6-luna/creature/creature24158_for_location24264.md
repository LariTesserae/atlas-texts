They are people of the warm stone, though they do not call themselves people in the way outsiders would understand it. Their oldest word for themselves means something closer to **those who remain where the mountain breathes**.

They are small, compact-bodied inhabitants, built for climbing and for conserving heat. Their hands are broad and heavily callused, with darkened fingertips from resin, mineral dust, and the constant handling of rough fiber. Their eyes are protected by narrow lids and thin plates of polished shell or mica. In the brightest hours they cover their faces almost completely, leaving only a slit through which to see. In the hollow’s green-brown twilight, they move easily, their pupils widening until their eyes seem black.

Their skin is usually the color of the stone: ash, ocher, old ivory, iron-brown. Dust settles into every crease and is never entirely washed away. They smell of smoke-black roof sealant, bitter plant oil, hot rock, and the animals that live close against their bodies. Children are often indistinguishable from the smaller burrowing creatures until they begin to speak.

They speak in voices suited to the hollow. Ordinary conversation is low and close, made for someone standing within arm’s reach. Across the terraces they use knocks: one strike for attention, two for danger, a rolling sequence to announce the return of a work party. The mountain carries these signals through the walls, and an experienced listener can tell not only where a message began but how urgently it was made. A cracked, hurried knocking means a predator. A slow, resonant pattern may mean that a vent has changed, or that pressure is moving under the floor.

They are rarely alone.

A person working on a terrace is accompanied by the click of grazers beneath the leaves, the wingbeats of things nesting overhead, the soft rasp of insects in the walls. Their houses are not sealed against the living world because sealing is impossible and, in many cases, undesirable. Instead, they build habits of coexistence.

A useful creature is given a passage.

A dangerous creature is given a warning color, a barrier, a name, and sometimes a shrine.

A creature that cannot be tolerated is killed quickly, without ceremony, but its body is not wasted. The shell may become a bowl, the teeth a cutting edge, the oils a medicine, the wings a covering for a lamp. Even the most feared predators are spoken of with a wary respect. They keep the grazing animals from overrunning the terraces. They remove the sick. They remind everyone that the settlement is not the center of the hollow, only one of its more articulate growths.

The people themselves are gatherers, pruners, miners, breeders, and listeners. Their work is never divided cleanly. A gardener must know insects. A potter must know the behavior of the vents. A child assigned to watch a sleeping animal may discover the first signs of a pressure shift. Someone harvesting roots must recognize which black cords belong to food plants and which belong to a vine that burns the skin.

Their most valued skill is not strength or speed but **attention**.

They notice when a cup-flower opens half an hour too early. They hear a change in the rhythm of shells crossing a path. They know that a certain predator’s silence is more dangerous than its cry. They can place a palm against a wall and distinguish the vibration of a burrower from the distant movement of people on another terrace.

Much of their knowledge is held in the body rather than written down. A worker learns the safe angle at which to approach a vent by watching an elder’s shoulders. Recipes are taught by smell. Paths are memorized through the feet: three shallow steps, a smooth bulge of stone, a warm crack, a turn beneath hanging leaves. The settlement has markings, but they are sparse and practical—lines cut into door lintels, colored mineral bands around storage chambers, patterns of raised clay beside stairways. Nothing important is left to a single sign. Signs fade. Plants grow over them. The mountain shifts.

Memory must therefore be repeated.

At dusk, when the heat sinks from the walls and the animals gather around the vents, families sit in close chambers beneath the terraces. Their lamps burn thickly and smell of bitter resin. They eat mineral-fed roots, softened leaves, the flesh of grazers, eggs taken from roof nests, and cakes made from the dense dry felt gathered beneath old plants. Food is chewed slowly. Water is not missed because thirst has never shaped their lives as it shapes the lives of lowland peoples. Their bodies take moisture from what they eat, from the oily plants, from the warm vapor rising through the stone. A drink, when they have one, is medicinal or ceremonial rather than ordinary.

They sleep above the floor.

The platforms are suspended in shallow wall niches or built over the entrances to burrows, where the warmth rises most steadily. Children sleep in clusters. Adults sleep lightly, turning at every unfamiliar sound. There are no private nights in the settlement. A person’s breathing mingles with the hiss of vents, the tapping of insects, the rustle of a neighboring household, and the movements of whatever has entered through the wall.

Privacy exists elsewhere: in the old root chambers, in narrow cuts between terraces, on the upper ledges where only the sure-footed can climb. Courtship often takes place in such places, among the black-leaved plants and mica plates that catch the last reflected light. The young are not formally paired so much as gradually recognized. Someone begins to leave tools beside another’s sleeping platform. Someone else starts pruning a shared corridor. A third person notices and makes room.

Their dead are not buried.

Burial would be difficult, and the mountain does not give back what is placed in it. Instead, bodies are dried in warm chambers above the vents. Useful bones are removed, cleaned, and returned to the family. The rest is wrapped in plant fiber and set into a high crevice, where the dry air and small scavengers reduce it over time. The settlement keeps no cemetery. Its ancestors are distributed through the walls: a rib beneath a bridge, a tooth in a tool handle, ash mixed into the resin used to seal a doorway.

This does not trouble them. To remain after death is not to preserve a body. It is to enter circulation.

Their oldest stories describe a time when the hollow was empty except for plants and the first blind grazers. In some tellings, the ancestors climbed down from the upper cliffs. In others, they grew out of the root mats. In still others, they were animals that learned to make tools after listening to the mountain’s knocks. These stories are not considered contradictory. Origin is not a single event. Everything in the hollow is understood to have passed through several forms.

The mountain is not a god in the simple sense. It does not receive petitions, and no one expects kindness from it. It is older than judgment. It warms, shifts, exhales, and occasionally destroys. To them, this is enough to make it worthy of respect.

They maintain small shrines at the vents, though the shrines resemble workstations more than temples. A stone dish, a strip of red-brown cup-plant, a polished shell, a little dust pressed into a spiral. Offerings are burned or buried into the root mats. Before major changes—opening a new chamber, diverting a plant, killing a resident predator—they place a hand against the wall and listen.

Sometimes the mountain answers with a tremor.

Sometimes with nothing.

The most feared moment in their lives is not the pressure storm itself but the warning that precedes it: the sudden withdrawal of insects, the stiffening of leaves, the silence of the long-backed hunters. When those signs appear, everyone moves. Shutters are closed. Animals are driven into protected chambers. Loose tools and roof fibers are tied down. Children are counted by touch. The oldest residents do not shout; they go from house to house, pressing their foreheads to doorways and checking the stone.

Then the warm mineral wind arrives.

It pours through the hollow with the force of a living breath, flattening plants, filling the air with powder, scattering wings and shells. People crouch in sealed chambers while the mountain groans around them. They do not pray for the storm to pass. They listen to determine whether it is deep, whether it is changing direction, whether a ledge has begun to shift.

Afterward they emerge gray with dust.

They find the broken nests, the overturned jars, the dead insects, the stones that have fallen from the upper shelves. They clear the paths. They reset the mica plates. They repair the bridges and gather whatever the wind has exposed. The children collect the largest shells and call the storm by a new name.

Within a day, the hollow is noisy again.

The people return to their terraces. The grazers resume their streams. The plants unfold their dark leaves. Predators slip back onto the paths as though they had merely stepped aside. Someone lights a lamp. Someone begins knocking a message from the lower ledge to the upper one.

The settlement survives not by conquering its world but by becoming difficult to separate from it. Its inhabitants are another layer of the hollow’s life: warm-bodied, tool-using, story-making creatures threaded among roots, vents, shells, wings, and stone. Their houses are shaped like nests, their roads like burrows, their customs like adaptations grown over generations.

To an outsider, they might appear trapped in a mountain.

To themselves, they are not trapped at all.

They live inside the only creature large enough to contain them, and every day they listen for its next breath.
