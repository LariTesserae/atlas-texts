They are people of thresholds.

Not amphibians, exactly, though they spend enough of their lives in water that the distinction has become unimportant. Their skin is darkened by mineral light and carries a fine, permanent sheen. The skin between their fingers is thickened, almost webbed in some families, and their feet are broad from generations of climbing wet stone and standing on surfaces that refuse to remain still. They have learned to breathe slowly underwater, not for long, but long enough to cross a flooded room, retrieve a dropped tool, or wait beneath a wavering ceiling while a passage rearranges itself.

Their hair is usually cut short or bound close to the head. Loose hair catches on roots, ropes, and the hooked leaves of the water gardens. Children are permitted to grow theirs long only when they have learned how to move through the city without disturbing anything that grows.

They are not a numerous people. A district may contain only a few dozen households, but those households spread vertically through the same structure: sleeping chambers above, kitchens on rafts below, storage in sealed hollows, workshops attached to the dry sides of cliffs. The idea of a private house is weak among them. A building belongs to whoever is presently maintaining it, and maintenance is never the work of one person for long. A wall may be yours in the morning and everyone’s by evening, if new stone pushes through its foundations.

They know one another by movement as much as by face. A person’s way of entering water, climbing a slack bridge, or pausing before a loose wall is as recognizable as a voice. Their names often contain a level, a current, or a material: names that mean Black Step, Upper Reed, Warm Clay, Three Rises, or the place where a roof once stood. A name may change after a major shift in the city. Someone who has lived through the tilting of a district may take a new name to mark the altered geography, though the old one remains in use among family.

They carry little that cannot be tied down.

Belts are woven with loops. Tools have weighted handles and long cords. Children wear bracelets of fired clay, each bead representing a person who will come looking if they drift too far. Even the dead are given something that will return them: a stone, a sealed vessel, a length of bright fiber. Bodies do not always sink here. They may rise through a courtyard, travel sideways into another district, or hang beneath the upper water where no ladder reaches. The people therefore do not release their dead casually. They build small weighted chambers and wait beside them until the water accepts its direction.

The children are raised collectively, though they still have parents. They learn the city by being carried through it: across roof gardens, down warm channels, under bridges, through the open shafts of towers. Before they can speak clearly, they are taught the names of bells and currents. Before they can read, they learn to recognize a wall that has begun to move.

Their games are quiet. They tie floating seeds to strings and race them through sluice gates. They build miniature terraces from soft mineral mud, then watch the water undo them. They hide in reed forests and listen for one another through the stems. A favorite game is to stand beneath a suspended stone and guess when it will begin to fall.

No one wins that game consistently.

The adults work according to the needs of the city rather than a fixed profession. A person may spend one season cutting steps into basalt, the next gathering fruit from the pale-trunked bath tree, and the next repairing the woven nets stretched over a whole neighborhood. There are specialists—those who understand water pressure, those who can recognize a mineral seam by taste, those who shape clay lamps, those who breed the tough vines used for rope—but specialization is treated as a temporary depth rather than a permanent identity.

The most respected people are not rulers. They are listeners.

Listeners sit near walls, cisterns, and newly risen stone and place their palms against the surfaces. They know the small differences between settling, cracking, and becoming. They can tell whether a tremor is passing through the plateau or beginning beneath a house. They are called to sleep beside foundations during uncertain seasons. In the morning they report what the stone has done in the night.

Their reports are not always understood as instructions. Sometimes the wall is going to rise, and there is no sensible response except to move the doorway.

There are councils, but they are temporary, like everything else. Representatives gather at the highest dry place available and sit around a shallow basin of water. No one stands at the head. The basin is watched throughout the meeting, because its surface may reveal a change in the larger currents. Decisions are made slowly and then tested against the city. If a plan cannot survive water flowing uphill, a stair gaining a step, or a foundation becoming hot, it is abandoned.

They have laws against permanence.

A person may not seal every entrance to a room. A bridge must be built with room to rise. No garden may be rooted so deeply that it cannot be moved. No family may claim an entire cistern. No child may be taught that a map is true simply because it is old.

They do not worship the stone, though they speak to it.

At the beginning of a new building, someone presses a hand into the wet mortar and gives the structure a promise: what it will shelter, what it will leave open, where it may be surrendered if the ground requires it. When a house is abandoned, its doors are removed and its walls perforated. This is not considered a failure. It is a form of courtesy to the water and to whoever comes later.

Their religion, if it can be called that, is concerned with becoming. They believe that the world is not made but making itself, and that every living thing is a temporary agreement among forces that have not yet settled. The dead do not go elsewhere. They enter the unfinished world more completely. A grandmother becomes part of a flooded stair, a brother enters the roots of a roof garden, a lost child is remembered whenever rain rises through a market floor.

They do not ask where the dead have gone.

They ask what the dead are becoming.

The people do not fear the suspended sea in quite the way outsiders would. They fear being caught alone in a place where the currents have changed, or finding that the familiar route between two levels has become a vertical drop. They fear the great silent shadows above. They fear the sudden darkening of the water ceiling, when every lamp turns blue and voices seem to come from buildings that do not yet exist.

But they do not fear change itself. Change is too constant to be an enemy.

When a street shifts, people gather at its edges with poles and ropes. When a tower leans, they empty it room by room, carrying furniture, lamps, sleeping mats, and jars of seeds to higher ground. When the water rises through a workshop, they lift the tools onto a platform and continue working from the roof. If a wall opens onto a new channel, someone hangs a curtain across it by nightfall. By the following week, there is usually a boat.

They are patient because impatience has no leverage here.

They speak softly, partly because sound travels strangely through water and partly because the city has taught them that urgency is often a misunderstanding. A shout may arrive after the danger has passed. A bell rung too soon can send people toward a place that will no longer be there. So warnings are conveyed by touch, by changes in lamp color, by three taps on a pipe, by a hand raised and held still.

Their songs are low and repetitive. They are sung while weaving, carrying, planting, or waiting for stone to cool. The melodies have no clear ending. A singer may stop, and someone several houses away will continue the same phrase hours later. Some songs are used to mark the depth of a channel. Others are for remembering the shape of a vanished street. The oldest songs contain instructions hidden beneath the rhythm: where the ground is hollow, which roots can be eaten, which kind of upward rain stains the skin.

They eat what the young city offers. Mineral-rich greens. Pale fruits from the bath tree. Small silver creatures caught in baskets under stairs. Cakes of grain and algae dried on warm stone. Root vegetables grown in roof gardens and suspended beds. Their food is often smoked, salted, or fermented, and the smell of it drifts through the wet streets in small domestic clouds.

They keep fire carefully. Fire is a rare dry thing, carried from place to place in clay vessels and protected beneath roofs of waxed cloth. A cooking flame is never left unwatched. Yet they are not helpless without it. They can eat much of what grows around them uncooked. Their lamps burn oil rendered from seeds and floating skins, and when oil is scarce, they use bowls of luminous mineral slurry that give off a weak green light.

Their clothing is layered and tied at the waist. The lower garments are close-fitting for swimming and climbing; the upper layers are loose enough to trap warmth when they emerge into air. Waterproof capes are made from waxed fiber and thin animal hide, though animals are rare and their hides are treated as inheritances. People decorate themselves with shell, colored stone, and fragments of glassy basalt. The richest ornament is not gold but a piece of rock that has changed visibly: a stone with a new seam, a cooled fragment from a vent, a shard that was once part of a street.

The city’s children collect such things and build little altars in cracks.

No one knows whether the people came from beyond the visible water or whether they rose with the plateau. They tell both stories, and neither is considered more true for being older.

In one story, their ancestors crossed from a drowned coast in sealed boats, carrying iron, seeds, and a single lamp that has never gone out. In another, they were born beneath the black ridges when the first warm springs opened, and the shape of their bodies is the shape water gave them while they slept. A third story says that the city dreamed them before it had roofs, and that when the last tower is submerged, they will become the creatures glimpsed moving in the upper sea.

The people do not argue over these versions. Origins belong to the past, and the past here is unreliable.

What matters is the next foundation, the next safe crossing, the next child taught to listen.

At dusk, if dusk is the dimming that passes through the suspended water, they gather on the highest roofs. Some sit with their feet in gardens. Some lower baskets into the green avenues. Some stand beside bells that have not rung in years. Their faces are turned upward toward the wavering sea above them, where broad shadows move without sound and rain climbs in silver strands from the streets.

They watch the water for signs of its next decision.

Then they return indoors, leaving the doors unlatched, the bridges slack, and a space in every room for the world to continue forming.
