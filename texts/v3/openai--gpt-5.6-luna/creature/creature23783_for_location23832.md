The reservoir is inhabited by people who are never quite dry.

They live in the chambers above and around it, in rooms built into the foundations of the settlement, but most of their waking lives are spent below the waterline. Their skin has taken on the pale, softened look of people accustomed to immersion. Their hands are broad and wrinkled at the fingertips, the nails darkened by plaster dust, algae, and the iron-colored residue that gathers around the gates. They wear little: reed-woven harnesses, short wrapped skirts, belts to which hooks and scrapers are tied. In the colder passages they cover their shoulders with oiled cloth, but the reservoir itself is warm enough that clothing becomes a hindrance. Everything worn here is designed to be dragged through water, hauled from water, or wrung out afterward.

They do not swim for pleasure. They move through the reservoir with the measured awkwardness of workers crossing a crowded floor. One hand remains on a rail or rope whenever the current is strong. They know which steps are slick, which channels conceal a sudden drop, which pillars shake faintly when a gate is being opened beyond the wall. Their feet have learned the chamber’s geometry. A keeper can descend a stairwell in darkness by counting the changes in pressure against the ankles and the shape of the current around the knees.

There are always several of them below.

One clears the grates. One watches the levels marked on the pillars. One tends the lamps. One climbs the upper galleries to inspect the shafts and translucent roof-slabs for cracks. Others work the wheel, hauling the chain of buckets upward, or carry baskets of compost to the reed troughs. The labor is divided not by rank but by what the water is doing. When the current slackens, everyone turns toward the eastern machinery. When it rises, everyone abandons tools and begins closing gates.

They communicate with signs more often than speech. A flat hand means stop. Two fingers pointed downward mean a drop in the floor. A fist held against the chest means the pressure is rising. They slap the water to draw attention, strike a pillar with a mallet to send a warning through the masonry, or pull sharply on colored ropes that run between the galleries. Their voices, when necessary, are short and forceful, shaped for the near distance: commands meant to be heard by the person beside them, not the person across the chamber.

The youngest workers begin as lamp-tenders. They are given the upper ledges, where the water is shallow and the mechanisms small. Their task is to keep the mica screens clear of spray and trim the wicks without dropping ash into the channels. They learn the reservoir by light first: the gold shafts in the west, the blue depths beneath the central arches, the trembling strips reflected from the glazed tiles. Later they are allowed into the current. Later still, they are taught to open a gate.

No one enters alone.

The reservoir has taken people before—not often, and never mysteriously. A rope has frayed. A submerged step has broken. A worker has been caught between a floating frame and a wall. Because these dangers are understood, they are feared plainly. The keepers tie one another to safety lines when working near the wells. They mark a death by setting an extra lamp in a niche and leaving it burning until morning. The flame trembles in the water below, doubled and lengthened until it resembles a person standing upright in the dark.

They eat on a raised platform above the western basin. Their meals are simple and heavily salted: grain cakes, boiled roots, preserved meat when there is any. They sit with their legs dangling over the water, listening to the wheel groan and the sluice thunder. Conversations are brief at first, then grow animated when the machinery has settled into a reliable rhythm. Someone may tell a story about the first block laid, or about the day the eastern wall split and sent a white torrent through the foundations. The reservoir is young enough that everyone remembers its construction through a parent, an older sibling, or their own hands.

They are proud of it in the practical way people are proud of a roof that does not leak.

They know every imperfection. The crooked replacement block near the southern pillar. The plaster plate that lifts when the air is too damp. The wheel-paddle that knocks twice instead of once. The reed trough that must be kept lower than the others because its basket is beginning to rot. To an outsider, the chamber might seem precise and finished. To the keepers, it is unfinished everywhere. New masonry is always being added. A channel is widened. A grate is remade. A dangerous stair is given a rail. Their work does not end with completion; completion is only the point at which maintenance becomes possible.

The people of the reservoir have developed a particular kind of patience. They wait for the water to settle before reading a level. They wait for a lamp flame to stop shaking before deciding whether there is a draft. They wait through the long, muscular turning of the wheel. They understand that haste is a form of carelessness here. A gate opened too quickly can injure someone fifty paces away. A blocked channel left unattended for an hour can change the pressure beneath the floor.

Their bodies, their tools, and their habits have become extensions of the construction. The rope follows the channel. The mallet answers the masonry. The worker in the harness becomes part of the current’s pattern, a dark human shape moving through bright water with a floating frame at their side. At times the distinction between inhabitant and mechanism seems to disappear. The keepers are valves that think, hands attached to the gates, eyes stationed at the lamps. The reservoir is maintained by human beings, but human beings are maintained by it in return: its water supplies the settlement above, its work gives their days their structure, and its enclosed warmth gives them a place that belongs to them.

At night, when the shafts turn black and only the ceramic lamps remain, the chamber becomes more crowded with reflections. The keepers move among them carrying hooks, jars, and coils of rope. Their bodies break apart in the water and reassemble beneath them. A person on the far gallery appears as a wavering column of limbs below the surface, walking through a second, inverted reservoir.

They do not mistake this for another world. They know the water has no spirit in it, that the echo is only masonry, that the light is only sunlight and flame. But they have spent so long inside the machine that they have given it a personality without needing to believe in one. They call the eastern wheel stubborn. They call the western shafts generous when the day is bright. They say the lower stairs remember every flood.

What truly lives there is not a hidden creature, nor anything that grows unattended. It is the continual exchange between water and labor.

The current enters, is guided, lifted, divided, and sent onward. The people follow it, reading its force with their hands and feet. Reeds filter what must be filtered. Lamps reveal what must be seen. The wheel complains, the ropes tighten, the gates answer. Nothing in the chamber survives by itself. Everything is kept alive by attention.

And so the reservoir’s inhabitants are a small, damp population of workers moving through the architecture they have made: carrying light along the galleries, feeling for cracks in the submerged brick, feeding the reeds, clearing the grates, and listening for any change in the great enclosed sound of water.
