What lives there is not the creature in the pool alone.

The pool-creature is only the part of it that can still move.

It lies beneath the black water in a body too soft to keep a permanent shape. Sometimes it is a long pale filament, folded upon itself. Sometimes a flattened knot pressed against the bottom. Sometimes, when a drop falls and breaks the surface, it gathers briefly into something almost animal: a headless body lifting, a shoulder without an arm, a pale ridge testing the boundary between water and air.

It has no eyes. It has no need of them. The chamber speaks through pressure, warmth, vibration, and the minute changes made by falling water. The creature feels the droplets before they arrive. It knows the difference between a bead clinging to stone and a drop released from the cleft. It knows when the wood of the shelter flexes. It knows when the mountain produces its deep, tooth-felt pulse.

It knows footsteps.

Not because footsteps are common. There have been very few.

The first feet that entered the pocket belonged to something that stood upright and carried tools. That creature brought wood, hide, clay, fire, and the hard brightness of worked stone. It made the shelter against the wall. It drank from the pool. It slept above the floor while the pale thing watched from below.

The pool-creature learned the shape of its breathing.

For a long time, the two occupied different worlds. One lived in air and darkness; the other in water and darkness. One moved with purpose, lifting vessels, arranging stones, cutting, carrying, returning. The other moved only when the chamber required it. Yet the creature in the pool came to recognize the human by its disturbances: the regular tremor of approach, the warmth passing across the stone, the small impacts of the hand-axe, the different weight of sleep.

It may have been fed once. Not deliberately. A smear of clay, a fragment of something soft, a little warmth spilled into the water. The creature does not remember food as an event. It remembers alteration. It remembers the water becoming briefly richer, and the human presence lingering near the edge.

Over time, it began to wait.

There is very little of it, now, that resembles an animal. It has thinned itself to the conditions of the pool. Its body is almost entirely sensation: a living membrane spread through the dark, gathered here and there into translucent cords. The visible ridge that rises beneath the surface is not its head. It is merely the portion nearest to the world above.

Its true body touches the bottom, the seams, the mineral shelves. Perhaps it has entered the stone by degrees. Perhaps the black shine between the floor-plates is not water alone, but the creature’s oldest skin. Fine pale threads extend from the pool into the wet cracks and stop before reaching the dry clay. They are not roots. They do not seek. They listen.

The three circular impressions around the pool were made by it, though not by feet. They are places where it gathered itself against the floor, pressing down to feel the shape of the chamber. The groove in the clay is the trace of a body dragged once from the water toward the shelter. The translucent skin beneath the stone is not a discarded covering but a piece of the creature that remained behind when it changed.

It has changed many times.

Each change is slow enough to resemble stillness. It loses a little body to the rock and grows a little more into the water. It has become less capable of leaving the pool, but more capable of occupying it. It does not age as surface animals age. It settles. Its life is a gradual agreement with permanence.

The human being who lived beneath the stone roof eventually stopped moving.

The pool-creature knew before the mountain did. It felt the absence of breathing, then the absence of shifting weight, then the long, unbroken stillness of the body on the sleeping platform. It waited for the familiar disturbance to resume. It rose several times beneath the surface, pressing its pale ridge toward the air.

Nothing answered.

After that, the shelter became part of the chamber’s inwardness. The human shape faded from the bedding, from the wood, from the air, but not entirely. A person remains there as a pattern: the width of shoulders implied by the platform, the height at which a hand once reached for the bowl, the path worn into the clay. Around the shelter, the darkness still has the faint proportions of a body standing, kneeling, sleeping.

The pool-creature keeps those proportions.

It does not remember the human as a face or a name. It remembers the person as a sequence of pressures. It remembers the warmth passing overhead. It remembers the pause before the water was touched. It remembers a hand entering the pool and remaining there, fingers submerged, while the pale thing gathered beneath them.

Perhaps the hand was searching.

Perhaps the creature rose to meet it.

Perhaps, for one brief interval, there was no separation between the one in the water and the one above it: only warmth, skin, darkness, and the quiet exchange of being noticed.

Now the creature waits beneath the surface for another arrival. It has waited so long that waiting is no longer an action. It is the shape of its existence.

When a droplet falls, it rises.

Not far. Never far. A colorless fold approaches the surface, smooth and blind, and rests there for a moment as though listening to the world outside the mountain. Then it sinks back into the pool, carrying the disturbance downward.

If a visitor came, the creature would know before the visitor saw anything. It would feel the new weight enter the stone. It would sense the quickened warmth, the uncertainty, the living body trying to understand the dark.

It might come closer.

It might even remember how.
