What lives there is small enough to be mistaken for a stain.

It inhabits the rock rather than the water: a thin, layered community spread through pores, seams, and mineral skins. In the green-brown films along the wall, microbes draw what little energy they can from the faint light and from the chemistry of the stone. They do not grow upward or outward in any visible hurry. They spread as discoloration, as a softening of one mineral into another, as a darkening no thicker than paint.

Most of the life is hidden.

It lives inside the columns, in the narrow passages where mineral-rich water rises and cools, and along the undersides of the deposits where oxygen-poor water meets water carrying something different. Different kinds gather at different boundaries. Some consume dissolved metals. Some alter sulfur compounds. Some feed on the byproducts of others. Their world is made of gradients so small that a human hand would feel only uniform cold, but to them each seam is a landscape, each pore a corridor with its own atmosphere.

The columns are therefore not merely inhabited. They are built and maintained by what lives within them, though no individual creature could claim responsibility. Their layers are the accumulated remains of countless generations of microscopic labor: crusts laid down, dissolved, and laid down again; iron darkened, green salts spread, pale sediment settled into fans. The columns have no organs in the animal sense, yet they possess intake, exchange, retention, and release. Their low vibration is the sound of a community continuing its oldest habit.

Nothing in this life hurries. There are no blooms, hunts, swarms, or sudden divisions visible to the eye. A cell grows where the chemistry permits it, divides when it has gathered enough, and leaves behind a film of altered stone. Its descendants may remain within a few millimeters of that place for centuries. Some are sealed into the mineral and become part of the structure; others persist in dormant states, waiting through long changes in pressure and chemistry.

The green-brown films are not one living skin but many adjoining populations, arranged in nearly invisible bands. The upper edge favors whatever can make use of the dim illumination. Beneath it are organisms that consume their leftovers. Deeper still, in the dark pores, others continue the exchange without light at all. The film is thin because the available energy is thin. It has reached the size the hollow can support and no farther.

In the pale sediment of the central depression, there are quieter forms: dormant cells, mineral-bound spores, and minute organisms moving so slowly that their movement is indistinguishable from settling. They occupy the rust-colored seams and the undersides of the sediment fans. When fresh material arrives through the columns, they wake in narrow succession, feed, multiply, and become still again. Their lives are measured less in days than in episodes of chemistry.

They have no eyes for the chamber, no ears for the pressure-note in the fissures. They do not perceive the hollow as a room. The distinctions that matter to them are concentration, acidity, temperature, and the faint electrical differences where one mineral touches another. A clean shelf is not empty to them. It is an unoccupied surface. A dark seam is not a shadow. It is a source, a refuge, or a boundary.

There may be larger life beyond the hollow, but nothing larger belongs to this place. The water is too settled, the food too scarce, the boundaries too complete. Anything passing through would be an intruder by scale alone. The established inhabitants are the ones that can remain nearly motionless and still be working: cells in stone, films on ledges, colonies threaded through the columns like invisible roots.

Together they form a life without a visible body.

They do not make noise, though their metabolism contributes to the low mechanical murmur. They do not defend territory. They do not migrate. They do not reproduce in seasons. Their presence is recorded only in the changing thickness of a crust, the color of a seam, the faint difference between bare basalt and coated basalt. If the hollow were watched for a human lifetime, they might appear entirely still. If watched for a million years, they would have remade its floor.

The place is not abandoned. It is occupied by patience.

Every mineral column is a gathering of innumerable minute lives, each taking a trace from the water and returning something altered. Every green patch is a settlement pressed flat against the wall. Every rust-colored line is an archive of former inhabitants and a bed for those still active beneath it.

They live by making the chamber more itself: darker here, greener there, more layered, more settled. Their work has no final product except continuation. The factory operates because its workers are indistinguishable from its walls, and because the pressure that passes through the hollow is enough to keep them, slowly and steadily, alive.
