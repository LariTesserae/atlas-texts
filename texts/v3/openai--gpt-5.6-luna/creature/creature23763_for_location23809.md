What lives there is not one species, but an arrangement.

The largest population is made of small, pale scavengers that occupy the margins of the machinery. They live in cable trenches, beneath access plates, inside the hollow feet of towers, and in the warm darkness behind pump housings. At first glance they resemble eels or elongated fish, but they have grasping forelimbs and broad, jointed mouths suited to stripping insulation, opening filter grilles, and cracking the shells of anything smaller than themselves. Their bodies are nearly colorless except for bands of rust-red along the spine, where symbiotic organisms feed on the minerals in the water. They gather around vibration: a newly started motor, a leaking pressure door, a train passing through its tube. To them, the basin is not a collection of buildings but a map of heat, current, noise, and edible growth.

They are not the builders. They are the first permanent residents.

In the inhabited districts, there are people as well.

They move slowly in the open water, not because they are weak, but because haste is expensive. Their bodies have been altered for pressure and cold. The hands are broad and webbed, with hard pads at the fingertips for gripping wet metal. Their eyes are large, protected by translucent membranes that close whenever silt thickens. Along the throat and ribs are rows of dark filtration folds, artificial organs that allow them to remain submerged for hours without returning to an air-filled chamber. Some still use tanks and masks for difficult work, but most breathe the basin directly. Their voices, when they speak, are low and distorted by the water, more vibration than language.

They wear little clothing in the old sense. Instead, they cover themselves in equipment: ceramic plates over the chest, tool harnesses around the hips, lamps mounted along the shoulders, braided lines clipped to the wrists. Their suits are assembled from whatever remains available—industrial membranes, pressure seals, sections of conveyor belt, the flexible skin of old transport machines. Every individual carries an identifying pattern of lights, because at a distance a person is only a dark shape among pylons. Names are flashed as sequences: three blue pulses, a long white bar, two red sparks.

They live in clusters around functioning systems.

A pump station may contain a dozen families in chambers built into its walls. A rail junction may support hundreds. They sleep in narrow niches lined with warm ceramic, their bodies held in place by straps or gentle fields of current. Infants are kept in floating cradles near the ventilation and filtration systems, where the water is cleanest. They are born with thin gill-folds and oversized hands. For the first few years they cling to nets suspended above the floors, learning not to drift into intakes, not to approach the pressure gates, and not to mistake warning lights for stars.

Their settlements have no streets, but they have routes. A green lamp means that the current is safe to cross. Amber means that something large has recently passed through. White means machinery, and machinery is approached only with a tether. Red is not a prohibition so much as a fact: something is about to open, close, rupture, discharge, or move.

Children learn the basin by sound before they learn it by sight. They can identify a pump beginning to fail from the changes in its vibration. They know which rail lines carry food, which carry replacement parts, and which have not been checked in a generation. They can distinguish the pulse of a hunting animal from the pulse of a loose cable striking a pylon. In the darkness beyond the floodlights, they navigate by the pressure changes against their skin.

The people do not own the machines. They belong to them in a more complicated way.

Every settlement maintains a section of the basin. Some keep the transit tubes clear. Some harvest the filter-feeders that colonize the cooling grilles. Some descend into the lowest chambers to repair pumps that have been running since before their ancestors were born. Others tend the living systems: algae beds, shrimp nurseries, fish corridors, and the sparse hanging gardens that escaped from the old vertical farms. There are divers whose entire work consists of following a warm plume through a dark service channel to find where it has begun to cool. There are pressure-readers who sit motionless with their palms against a wall, feeling for changes in the deep structure.

The most respected are the fault-walkers.

They travel alone or in pairs across the bare distances between stations. Their suits are dark and heavily weighted. They carry long, flexible probes that they drag across the floor, listening to the resonance of the concrete and plating. They inspect seams, marker pylons, unfinished channels, and temporary barriers. Often they are gone for days. When they return, their lamps appear first in the blue, tiny and steady, and the settlements open their gates to them without question.

No one is entirely sure how much of the original human population remains human.

Some still have familiar faces beneath their membranes and equipment. Others have grown dense, blunt bodies adapted to heavy pressure and low light. Some possess external sensory filaments that tremble in the currents. Some have lost the ability to survive above the water. A few are so extensively modified that they are considered living infrastructure: warm-bodied technicians who sleep inside control housings and wake when a system begins to deviate from its pattern.

They do not think of themselves as trapped.

The ocean above is an ancestral place, perhaps, but it is too bright, too unstable, and too open. The basin is safer because it is legible. Every wall has a purpose. Every current can be measured. Every danger announces itself through vibration, pressure, or light. Even the animals are part of the known order. Whales use the transport corridors. Rays rest on the inspection floors. Predators patrol the cooling channels. The people leave certain gates open so that migrating creatures can pass and close others so that the animals do not enter the machinery at the wrong hour.

The largest animals have become inhabitants in their own right.

One whale circles the central works each year, its body scarred by old collisions with submerged towers. The people call it the Bell because its calls activate sympathetic vibrations in the pipe networks. When it arrives, workers stop for several minutes and listen. Fish stream behind it in clouds. Smaller whales follow at a distance, using the basin as a sheltered route between deep-water regions. The Bell knows which chambers are open and which are dangerous. It avoids the turbines, but sometimes passes close enough to a wall that everyone inside feels the pressure of its body moving through the dark.

There are predators that have learned to hunt by the infrastructure’s design. Long, narrow hunters lie beside current outlets and wait for fish to be carried toward them. Broad-headed animals hide beneath trains, seizing whatever gathers in the turbulence behind the carriages. Some have learned that maintenance crews drive prey out of their hiding places. They follow humans at a distance, not with fear, but with patience.

The people tolerate them when they can.

Nothing is wasted. Dead animals become food, fertilizer, or bait. Failed machines become habitat. Cable insulation is stripped and woven into nets. Damaged pressure glass becomes greenhouse roofing. A collapsed warehouse may be left untouched for decades because its flooded interior supports three species of shrimp and a colony of small, luminous fish. The basin’s inhabitants repair what must continue and abandon what can be allowed to change.

In the open chambers, one may see no one for a very long time.

Then a light appears beneath a gantry. Another answers from a distant tower. A figure emerges from the blue, towing a bundle of tools. It pauses beside a dead control panel, opens its chest harness, and connects itself to the exposed wires. For several seconds the person is outlined in a weak electric glow. Somewhere far away, a pump changes rhythm. A pressure door unlocks. Fish scatter from a drainage trench.

The figure remains there until the system is stable.

Around it, the basin continues to live: algae trembling in the warm flow, crabs moving through bolt holes, transparent bodies feeding in the light, distant whales sounding through the metal. The inhabitants are not trying to restore the place to what it was, nor to turn it into a natural sea. They are maintaining an uneasy middle condition—part habitat, part machine, part unfinished country.

They keep the water moving.

They keep the doors balanced.

They keep the lights burning for the generations that will come after them, though no one can say whether those generations will still be recognizable as people, or whether they will be something more perfectly suited to this vast, cold, constructed ocean.
