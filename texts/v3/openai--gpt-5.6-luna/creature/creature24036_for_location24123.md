What lives in the Green Aperture is not one people, nor even a collection of species. It is a truce between hungers.

Nothing there survives by being complete. Every creature is partly borrowed from something else: mineral, plant, gas, echo, corpse. The basin’s life has no clean boundary around it. A root may behave like a tendon. A stone may open like a mouth. An animal may spend its first years as a drifting bladder and its last as a rooted thicket, still responding when touched.

The oldest inhabitants are the plants.

They are called **breath-takers** by the dwellers in the five houses, though this name is misleading. They do not merely take the gases from the vents. They listen for them. Their roots spread through the rock in pale, branching mats, feeling out pressure changes and the faint tremors that precede an opening vent. Before the ground sings, the plants already know. Their leaves turn, their stems tighten, and their flowers begin to prepare themselves behind their sealed plates.

A flowering is less like blooming than an attack.

The plates spring open in sequence, sometimes by the hundreds. The sound travels through the basin as a rapid, dry clattering. Amber petals unfold to expose dark inner surfaces veined with light. The plant’s filaments rise and tremble. For several breaths it becomes almost animal, scenting the gas, drawing it inward, digesting it with a heat that can be felt from a few paces away.

The breath-takers are not rooted in the passive sense. They move slowly, but they move. A colony may migrate along a seam over the course of a season, abandoning cold stone for a more generous vent. Hanging gardens reel themselves outward at night, lowering fresh roots toward the warmth beneath them. When threatened, some plants retract into the rock, leaving behind only hooked fibers and a faint smell of sugar.

Others hunt.

Their flowers close around insects. Their root-filaments knot around the legs of small runners. A dead animal left too long among them disappears from the outside inward, its bones polished clean and incorporated into the colony’s supporting ribs. The inhabitants say that every mature grove contains the memory of what it has eaten. Whether this is true is difficult to determine, because the groves sometimes produce noises resembling speech.

The smaller animals are the **drummers**. Pale, folded things, light enough to be lifted by a passing breath, they gather around vents and beat their abdomens against the stone. They do not appear to feed while they drum. They perform the act with such concentration that their bodies become rigid and translucent. The sound passes into the ground, and dormant seeds answer.

No one knows whether the drummers awaken the plants for their own benefit or whether the plants breed them for this purpose. The distinction has never mattered. Wherever the drummers gather, the basin becomes fertile, and wherever it becomes fertile, the drummers multiply.

The six-legged shears hunt the drummers in deliberate arcs. They are lean, nearly hairless creatures with flexible necks that fold and unfold like measuring rods. Their mouths open sideways, not vertically, and can remove a limb without slowing them. They are clever enough to wait beside a vent until the drumming begins, then strike through the pale swarm.

The burrowers are less elegant. They are broad, low things with moss-grown backs and blunt forelimbs made for breaking the thin crust over the gas chambers. They eat roots, mineral crust, small animals, and sometimes one another. Their moss is alive and communicates by changing color. A healthy burrower carries a green-black mantle; a frightened one turns silver; a dying one becomes covered in yellow points that resemble stars seen through smoke.

The tall grazers are the strangest of the ordinary animals. They feed from the upper leaves, but they have almost no weight. Their inner sacs fill with the basin’s warm gases, and their limbs are long and jointed, with no visible effort behind their movement. They seem to be perpetually falling in a direction the air has chosen.

When alarmed, they rise.

They do not fly. They simply cease to belong entirely to the ground. Their feet leave the stone, their bodies tilt, and they drift sideways until something receives them. A plant may catch one. A cliff may catch one. Another grazer may catch one, and both may hang suspended for several moments, kicking at nothing.

The creatures are not frightened by this. Their fear is quieter and more practical. They become still, allowing the basin to carry them wherever it intends.

The five dwellings belong to the **listeners**.

There are never many of them. Sometimes there are twelve. Sometimes three. Once, according to the marks cut into the northern columns, there was only one. They are small and narrow-bodied, not because their ancestors were small but because large bodies are inconvenient in a place where every doorway, crevice, and shelter has been shaped for compression. Their shoulders are flexible. Their hands have two opposing thumbs. Their eyes are dark and heavily lidded against the metallic dust.

They wear plates shed by the breath-takers, sewn together with tendon and plant fiber. The plates overlap along the spine and ribs, making their bodies resemble the backs of insects. Their garments are beautiful in the way a knife can be beautiful: hard, functional, and full of edges. Mineral disks are fastened at the wrists and throat. Each disk has a different tone when struck.

The listeners do not name themselves aloud.

Names, in the Green Aperture, are dangerous things. A spoken name may be carried to the columns, returned from an unexpected direction, and heard by something that has learned to recognize it. Instead, the inhabitants identify one another through rhythm: three taps against a wall, a pause, two taps; a scrape of bone across stone; a pattern played on the hanging seed-cases outside a dwelling.

An individual can be summoned without being called.

They sleep in shallow hollows lined with gas-bladder skin. They eat the trapped drummers, the soft organs of shears, the sweet inner tissues of certain flowers, and a paste made by grinding mineral powder with the remains of dead plants. Their water is metabolic and carefully conserved. They do not wash. They scrape themselves clean with hooked slivers and allow the dust to blow away.

They have no fire, but they do have warmth.

At night, a listener may sit beside a sealed vent and place both hands against the stone. The rock will sometimes pulse beneath the palms. The listener remains there until the pulse changes. Then they rise and mark the alteration on a wall, not with symbols but with a sequence of cuts whose spacing records pressure, duration, and direction.

Their knowledge is not written in sentences. It is stored in arrangements.

A row of mineral disks means that a path is safe. A bone turned point-down means that a plant has become predatory. A seed-case split along its length warns of spores. The dwellings themselves are instruments, built to amplify the basin’s changes. When the ground shifts, the ribs creak. When the gas rises, the walls hum. When something approaches through the dark, the ornaments outside begin to strike one another.

The listeners are not masters of the basin. They are its interpreters, and sometimes its prey.

They know which groves can be cut, which burrowers may be followed, and which vents can be approached during the first stage of their opening. They know how to harvest the translucent plates without causing the roots to pursue the blade. They know how to lie beneath a slab while airborne creatures pass overhead.

But they do not know everything.

There are nights when a listener wakes to find pale filaments resting across the entrance of the dwelling. There are mornings when an unoccupied house has acquired a new ornament, a black disk warm to the touch. Sometimes one of the five structures is empty, though the tools remain neatly arranged and the occupant’s footprints lead only as far as the center of the floor.

The others do not search for the missing person.

They remove the door covering. They hang a new line of bone and seed-cases outside. Then they wait to see whether the basin returns a sign.

The columns are not considered alive, but the listeners treat them with the caution reserved for living elders. The small white ear-things in their grooves repeat voices, though never perfectly. They alter pitch, timing, and emphasis. A statement made near the columns may come back as a question. A warning may return as laughter. A dead person’s voice may emerge from the lowest groove years after it was spoken.

The listeners do not answer.

This rule is older than the houses. It is older, perhaps, than their arrival. Some say the columns were grown by the first inhabitants as a way to communicate with the plateau. Others say the columns are the exposed organs of something buried beneath the basin. One tradition claims that the white animals are not repeating voices at all, but practicing them, and that one day they will speak without needing anyone nearby.

The basin itself may be the largest inhabitant.

It has a rhythm that none of its creatures fully share. It inhales through its vents. It shifts in its sleep. It feeds on bodies, pressure, heat, and sound, and gives back growth. Its plants are extensions of its skin. Its animals are mobile sparks of appetite. The listeners are perhaps no more separate from it than the white things in the columns are separate from the stone.

When the great breathing begins, every life in the Green Aperture becomes briefly obedient.

The drummers rise. The burrowers break the crust. The grazers lose their weight. The plants open in ranks, and the listeners emerge from their houses carrying hooked poles and vessels of bladder-skin. They do not flee. There is nowhere outside the basin that would sustain them for long.

They stand among the flashing stones and watch the invisible wave pass through.

For a moment, the whole place becomes one organism: root, claw, rib, dust, echo, and breath. Then the vents close. The animals scatter. The plants begin their slow rearrangement. The listeners return to their houses and repair what has fallen.

By morning, life has resumed its separate shapes.

But not its former ones.
