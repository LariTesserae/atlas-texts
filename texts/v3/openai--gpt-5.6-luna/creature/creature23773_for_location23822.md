They are called, by the nearest approximation to a name, the **underwalkers**.

No one sees an underwalker whole.

A person might see its head rise from a wall of vegetation: long and pale, with no eyes, no ears, and two branching structures that resemble antlers stripped of bark. Flowers open along the branches as it breathes. They are not decorative. Each flower is a listening organ, a pressure-sensitive cup that receives the movement of water through the roots, the tread of distant feet, the slow settling of stone.

The head may remain above the growth for an hour, or for a season.

The rest of the creature is elsewhere.

An underwalker’s body extends through the plateau in many forms. A cord beneath the turf may be part of its spine. A black root chamber may contain its lungs. The jointed plants at a tarn’s edge may be fingers, flexing and testing the water. In one basin, a traveler might meet the forelimbs of an underwalker without realizing that the animal continues for kilometers beneath the ground. In another, the traveler might hear its call echoing from every direction, though its mouth is far away, buried under a ridge.

They are not large in the usual sense. They are distributed.

Where their bodies pass, the land is subtly altered. Water rises through the mats in regular pulses. Rock becomes softer along certain seams. Dead vegetation is drawn inward and reduced to dark slurry. New stalks appear in lines that do not correspond to slope, wind, or sunlight. The underwalkers do not merely inhabit the plateau. They persuade it to remain inhabitable.

They move slowly, but their slowness is difficult to measure. A single limb may cross a valley in an afternoon. A whole animal may take a century to turn around.

Their visible heads are smooth and pale, covered in a skin like wet stone. The blind face has a broad, flexible mouth, but it does not eat in the manner of grazing animals. When the mouth opens, it gathers mist, spores, loose minerals, and the chemical traces left by other living things. Tiny feeder organisms live in folds beneath the jaw and digest most of what enters. The underwalker receives the remainder as information.

Its antlers are living networks. In spring—if the plateau can be said to have spring—they grow translucent shoots along their length. These flower in response to distant events. A burst of amber blossoms may mean that a basin has drained. Blue flowers indicate a body moving beneath the mats. Black, tightly closed buds appear before a collapse, sometimes hours before the first stone begins to slide.

The underwalkers do not speak, but they communicate constantly.

Their language is made of pressure, vibration, sap chemistry, and changes in the direction of growth. A warning passes through the roots as a sequence of heavy pulses. A summons may begin in one tarn and end in a grove on the far side of the plateau, where every trunk shudders at once. Their calls are the metallic sounds heard behind the wind: impacts, rotations, valves opening, the great hidden machinery of the land.

Some of those sounds are work.

An underwalker may spend decades widening an underground passage, grinding stone to powder and lifting it into the plant layers. It may clear a watercourse, then deliberately block it again when the basin has become too dry. It may enter a collapsing ridge and hold the surrounding roots in tension while smaller animals evacuate. When the danger has passed, it lets itself be buried.

The plateau does not distinguish between death and useful transformation. Neither do the underwalkers.

A dead one becomes a region.

Its flesh hardens into dark shelves. Its blood remains in the roots as a slow-moving mineral fluid. The flowers along its antlers continue to open for several years, recording pressure and weather after the animal no longer responds. Small white creatures colonize its chambers. Grazing herds feed across its back. Eventually the whole body becomes a valley, and another underwalker may grow through it, inheriting pathways it does not remember making.

Yet there is memory here, though not in a single mind.

When underwalkers meet, they do not approach one another. Their root systems touch beneath the ground, sometimes over distances too great for their visible bodies to come close. The meeting may last for days. Water changes direction. The mist thickens. Nearby plants lean toward the contact. At the end, one animal may take on the other’s coloration, or grow a new branch on its antlers, or begin traveling toward a place neither had previously visited.

It is unclear whether they recognize one another as individuals.

They recognize events. A basin that failed three centuries ago. The pressure of an extinct animal crossing a certain ridge. The mineral taste of an old collapse. These things can persist in the tissues of a creature, passed from body into body through root contact and decay. An underwalker born in a chamber beneath a tarn may carry the impressions of mountains that no longer exist.

The smaller animals treat them as weather.

White burrowers travel along their bodies and sleep in the warmth of their inner chambers. The blue-gray grazing herds follow the safest routes, spreading apart when an underwalker stirs below them and closing again when it settles. The thin-winged creatures land on the antlers to drink from the flowers. Even the great unseen animals in the chasms seem to be attached to them in some obscure way, their calls traveling through the same buried channels.

The underwalkers rarely hunt. They do not need to. Their food is the continual breakdown of the plateau: stone, peat, bodies, water, the exhausted remains of earlier growth. They are scavengers, gardeners, excavation engines, and nervous systems for the high country.

Sometimes they do kill.

When something foreign enters the plateau—an animal carrying an unfamiliar chemical scent, a fallen object that does not root, a patch of growth that consumes without returning—the underwalkers gather around it. The vegetation tightens. Water withdraws from the surrounding ground. Pale limbs emerge from the mats and press inward with patient force. Within days, the intruder is covered in moss, opened by roots, and translated into the local economy of matter.

Nothing is allowed to remain separate for long.

Only the travelers are given the illusion of separation. They see plants, animals, stone, machines, pools, and mist as different things because they arrive and depart from the surface at different speeds. The underwalkers experience no such divisions. To them, a plant is a slow limb. A lake is an exposed organ. A machine is a temporary gesture made by metabolism. A mountain is an old body not yet finished being digested.

When the false horizon appears, the underwalkers react before anything else.

Their antlers fold downward. Their bodies tense beneath the ground. Every water-bearing root begins to pulse toward the impossible landscape. For a few minutes, the plateau’s distributed inhabitants become almost one creature. Heads emerge from distant chasms. Groves vibrate. Tarn water turns in broad, synchronized spirals. The thin-winged animals rise from the mist and fly toward the bent horizon, while the herds divide and rejoin in frantic patterns.

Perhaps the underwalkers remember that other country.

Perhaps it is where they came from.

Or perhaps the inverted landscape is not another place at all, but the plateau seeing its own buried body for an instant: the underside of its roots, its old dead layers, its engines and chambers exposed beneath the world.

When the horizon straightens, the underwalkers withdraw. Their antlers close. The water resumes its local motions. The groves return to their separate rhythms.

For a while, the plateau is quiet enough to hear something moving beneath the turf.

It may be one underwalker turning in its sleep.

It may be the whole land, carrying its weight from one ancient side to the other.
