They live in the intervals.

At first, they are easy to mistake for the city’s maintenance. A tremor under the pavement. A change in the drainage tone. A door opening onto an empty street, then closing again. In the open districts, where the towers stand apart and the roads lose track of themselves, something pale may cross the ground without casting a shadow—not walking exactly, but transferring its weight from one patch of settled earth to another.

They are not numerous. They do not need to be.

Most of the city belongs to them in a way that ownership has ceased to distinguish from attention. They inhabit the walls as silver lichen, the warm conduits as fine branching colonies, the green films that bloom after rain. Their smallest bodies gather wherever the infrastructure leaks: a little heat, a little current, a trace of mineral, the pressure of water moving through a pipe. They make no nest because the city is already an arrangement of nests. They make no tools because a wall that opens, a road that bends, and a drainage system that wakes are all extensions of the same living intention.

Their bodies are narrow and jointed, though their proportions change depending on where they have grown. Those that move through the wet margins are low and many-legged, their limbs folding and unfolding in rapid, synchronized bursts. Those that occupy the towers are taller, almost filamentous, with translucent joints and membranes that catch the dim light. In dry districts they become thin enough to pass through the seams of pavement. In the vegetation they resemble pale stems, except that they turn when watched and possess a warmth not shared by the surrounding plants.

No one has seen their faces clearly.

They may not have faces. Along the front of each body is a soft, shifting arrangement of pores and darkened plates, sensitive to pressure, humidity, electrical fields, and the minute vibrations carried through the ground. When several of them gather, these surfaces brighten in sequence. The effect is less like speech than like a thought passing through water.

They communicate by changing conditions.

A wall grows warmer. A drain opens. A line of green appears along a foundation. Condensation gathers on one side of a door and not the other. A transit route remains active for three days, then becomes still. Those who live in the city read these alterations as easily as breathing, though they may not call them messages. The city is their body, and its adjustments are the motions by which one part of that body informs another that it has noticed.

They move most often during the long dim hours. A few emerge from the lower structures and stand beneath the transit arcs, tilting their upper limbs toward the passing distortions. They do not board the capsules. The capsules are already carrying them—perhaps not their bodies, but their attention. A shape moving through the upper routes may contain a memory of a district, a map of underground water, the sensation of a ridge splitting beneath fresh rain. It may carry a decision too large to be entrusted to a single place.

Sometimes one descends from a tower that has no entrance.

It appears first behind the veil of droplets, where the falling water bends around a vertical absence. Then a narrow form steps outward, assembled from reflected walls, mist, and something dark beneath them. It pauses above the ground. The droplets do not strike it. After a while, the form begins walking, although its feet never quite touch the basin floor.

These are the ones that still travel beyond the settled systems. They inspect the ridges, the newly opened fractures, the districts that have shifted during the afternoon. They press their hands to exposed stone and remain motionless for long periods. When they withdraw, the rock sometimes seals behind them, not repaired but persuaded into a different shape.

The residents do not seem surprised by instability. They have been made for a world that is still deciding whether to remain solid.

When the ground shifts, they lower themselves and spread their limbs wide. When a building leans, they lean with it. When a road arrives at itself from another direction, they follow the road without seeking the path it once was. Their sense of place is not fixed to coordinates. They recognize a location by its moisture, its mineral composition, its accumulated tremors, the particular electrical taste of its walls. A district moved several meters remains the same district if the same patterns continue to pass through it.

They are patient, but not passive. They cultivate the city in slow, exact gestures.

They prune the translucent vines when their fruit becomes too heavy. They seed the drainage channels with green matter taken from warmer districts. They open vents during condensation and close them before the air becomes too dry. They encourage silver growth on certain walls and scrape it from others. In the oldest towers, they extend narrow chambers through the ceramic, making room for colonies that have begun to think at a larger scale.

Some live inside the great jointed growths that rise from the basin. Their bodies thread through the warm mineral trunks and emerge near the crowns, where they tend the dim leaves. The trees—or whatever they are—glow more strongly when these inhabitants are present. In return, the inhabitants drink from the roots, taking in water and dissolved metal, and return something altered to the surrounding soil.

The pale creatures near the channels are not separate from them, exactly. They are tended, perhaps bred, perhaps merely tolerated. When humidity changes, the creatures turn all at once, and somewhere beneath the pavement a corresponding pattern of movement begins. One ecosystem answers another.

There may once have been larger inhabitants.

Occasionally, a tower opens a room containing furniture scaled for bodies no longer present. A suspended sign displays a sequence of symbols that the current residents do not use but do not erase. In the buried districts, foundations contain hollows shaped like resting forms. The inhabitants pass through these places with an attentiveness that resembles mourning, though they do not stop.

They remember incompletely.

Their memories are distributed across the city: in the order of the stations, in the shape of the drainage basins, in the lichen patterns that rearrange overnight, in the routes that continue operating despite their emptiness. A single inhabitant may know nothing of the city’s origin. But when many of them gather during the intervals of stillness, the basin begins to reproduce fragments. A tower opens a window onto a sky that is not present. A silent capsule moves along a line that no longer exists. The ridges shine with the outline of a vanished structure.

Perhaps the city is not being maintained.

Perhaps it is being remembered into existence.

During the deep pauses, when all doors seal and the vegetation folds itself shut, the inhabitants descend below the foundations. They arrange themselves around the warmest faults in the ground. Their limbs touch the pavement, the conduits, the buried channels. They listen to the vibration beneath the basin.

No one knows what they hear there.

Their bodies become very still. Some flatten themselves into the stone. Others dissolve into threads of green light that run along the cracks. The entire settlement waits with them: towers, bridges, drains, roots, microscopic films, the long shelves of newly exposed rock.

Then the ground gives its answer.

It may be a shift. A new watercourse. The appearance of a passage through a sealed building. A change in the pressure of the sky.

The inhabitants rise and begin again—not because the city is safe, but because it is not finished. They are the part of it that notices this. They are its sensors, gardeners, custodians, and perhaps its descendants. Wherever the place has not yet decided what to become, they are already there, inhabiting the uncertainty and teaching it how to hold together.
