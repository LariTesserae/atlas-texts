What lives there is not a species, but a succession.

The smallest creatures are called motes only because there is no better word. They are nearly invisible in the open air, thin as filings and quick as a thought abandoned halfway through. They gather in the lee of escarpments, in the narrow warmth of cracks, beneath the armored plates of the grazers. Each is no more than a moving angle: a glint, a pressure, a brief interruption in the dust. Yet they are responsible for much of the plateau’s apparent emptiness. Where they pass, edges lose their certainty. A cliff becomes farther away. A shadow acquires a second owner. A footstep arrives before the foot is placed.

They feed on distinctions.

A mote does not consume rock, or heat, or flesh. It consumes the difference between one thing and another. In a swarm, it can make a ravine resemble a plain, or turn a herd into a pattern on the horizon, or persuade the eye that a fallen stone has always been there. The larger animals depend on them. Without motes, the plateau would become too sharply defined, and nothing could cross it without being fixed in place.

The transparent ravine-creatures are their blooms.

They rise in spirals when the air becomes sufficiently thin, their bodies made of membranes, articulated joints, and pale internal sparks. Seen directly, they are almost absent. Seen in the corner of the eye, they are immense. They climb through one another without contact, passing from altitude to altitude by changing not their position but the permission of the surrounding space. A single animal may appear at the base of a wall, inside its shadow, and high above the escarpment at the same time.

They do not hunt. They count.

Their bodies open and close around the movements of everything below: the scrape of a grazer’s furnace-mouth, the rise of a buried ridge, the tremors sent through the shelves by distant herds. They collect these motions and carry them upward. At a great height, beyond the reach of ordinary sight, the spiral breaks apart. The animals become a fine metallic rain that falls nowhere. The information remains in the air.

The six-legged grazers are more deliberate.

They travel in lines along the escarpments, always with one flank turned toward the drop. Their legs are not symmetrical. The outer three are heavy and blunt, made for bearing weight; the inner three are narrow, jointed, and tipped with instruments that test the stone. Their heads are low, almost buried in their shoulders. At the front of each is a mouth like a furnace door, opening vertically to reveal concentric grinding surfaces bright enough to erase detail.

They strip the rock of its oxides and leave behind long pale trails. This is not nourishment in any simple sense. The minerals pass into their bodies, where they are sorted, melted, and arranged into layers. Their armor grows inward as much as outward. Old sediment becomes new muscle. Fault-lines become nerves. On some of the eldest, entire ravines have formed between the plates of their backs, and colonies live there: salt-legged crawlers, blind burrowers, tiny things with hinged mouths that feed on the heat stored in the animal’s spine.

A grazer may spend a century crossing a single shelf.

It remembers the shelf by pressure. When it returns, long after the surface has altered around it, its feet find the old pattern beneath the new one. Then the animal stops, lowers itself, and listens. If the mineral answer is correct, it resumes its route. If not, it opens its furnace-mouth and calls.

The call travels through the ground.

At first it is only a deep report. Then it becomes a vibration in the teeth. At last the nearby salt forests tremble, and their branches ring in a sequence too slow to be mistaken for music. The answer may come from beyond the horizon, or from beneath the animal itself. Sometimes no other grazer responds. Sometimes the entire shelf kneels.

The burrowers are older than the routes.

Their bodies occupy no single shape for long enough to be named. Beneath the plateau they are miles of appetite, pressure, and retained heat. A raised line moving across the plain may be the back of one, though it may also be its thought, or the shadow of a movement taking place much deeper down. They travel through stone without displacing it. The rock simply becomes, for a time, the kind of rock that has always contained a passage.

When one surfaces, the plateau receives it without alarm.

A ridge swells. Black glass appears in the seams. The ground opens along a perfectly straight line, and something passes through: a circular mouth, a cluster of jointed limbs, a crown of rotating plates, a head with no face except a deep orange aperture. The creature exposes only what is required. It does not emerge to feed. It emerges to alter the pressure.

For a season, the animals in the region change their routes. Salt fans lean toward it. The transparent things gather overhead. Sound bends around the new presence and returns altered. The burrower makes its low industrial breathing, not loud enough to be called a roar, but broad enough to fill every available silence.

When it sinks, the field is left level and black.

Nothing grows there afterward for a long while. Then, slowly, the first motes arrive.

There are predators, though predation is not always an act of killing. Some are long-winged forms that sail through the thin atmosphere with their bones set at impossible angles. Their wings are not surfaces but decisions: when one turns, the world agrees that it has turned. Their undersides carry false anatomies—eyes, teeth, vents, landscapes—and smaller creatures flee from whichever image is nearest them.

They feed on trajectories.

A flying animal, a thrown stone, a falling body, a migrating swarm: each leaves a path through the air, and the predators seize the path before they seize the thing. Their prey may continue moving, but without the line that joined one moment to the next it becomes stranded in place, preserved as an event. For years afterward, a stone may hang beside a cliff, still falling but never arriving. A winged creature may be found crossing the same patch of sky every noon, its body intact and its future consumed.

Other predators have no visible bodies. They are recognized by the pressure they place on dust, or by the mechanical breathing that seems to come from several directions at once. Their presence makes nearby creatures simplify. A many-jointed animal will stand with fewer joints. A flock will become one bird. A complicated thought will lose its middle.

The unseen predators do not always eat what they catch. Sometimes they follow it for centuries, waiting for the prey to become sufficiently itself.

The salt forests belong to no animal, though animals visit them.

They grow where pressure has been held too long in one arrangement. Their trunks are translucent and angular, branching into fans, combs, ladders, and delicate towers. The largest are taller than buildings that have never existed there. They have no roots. Their lower branches disappear into the stone and return somewhere else, perhaps kilometers away, perhaps in another version of the plateau.

When the wind moves through them, they ring.

The sound is not random. Each forest has a vocabulary of tones, and over long periods the vocabularies merge. Creatures come to listen. Grazers stand among the white branches with their heads lowered. The ravine-things descend. Even the burrowers pause belowground.

The salt does not speak of events. It speaks of inclinations.

A forest may announce that a cliff will soon be elsewhere. It may tell the animals that a shadow has become hungry. It may repeat a sound made by something that has not yet arrived. Those who listen too long begin to lean toward the branches themselves. Their bodies grow narrow. Their feet become brittle. If they do not leave, the salt takes their outline and adds it to the forest.

There are many such forms among the branches: the shapes of extinct grazers, unfinished wings, a hand with too many fingers, the suggestion of a door.

No one living on the plateau treats these as memorials.

The animals do not distinguish between the dead and the durable. A carcass cools into an outcrop, and the outcrop becomes a shelter, a signal, a feeding ground, or the foundation of another body. The mineral grazers tunnel through their ancestors. Motes nest in their joints. Salt grows across their mouths and preserves the last angle of their opening.

In time, the dead become part of the land’s machinery.

That is what all the inhabitants are moving toward, though not toward an end. Their bodies are gradually converted into functions. A tendon becomes a belt. A shell becomes a resonating chamber. A lung becomes a pressure well. A nervous system spreads into the rock and continues to register footsteps long after the animal itself has vanished.

The oldest creatures are no longer recognizable as creatures.

They are the glowing openings in the cliffs. They are the horizontal chambers that exhale at regular intervals. They are the warm seams that produce a current when crossed. They are the buried engines whose cycles cause distant herds to kneel. They are not asleep. They are maintaining something.

No inhabitant can say what.

Perhaps the plateau is being kept at the correct distance from another world. Perhaps the hum is the sound of a vast body repairing itself, one shelf at a time. Perhaps all the animals are organs of a single life whose skin is the black stone and whose thoughts travel as tremors through the mineral shelves.

At certain hours, the possibility becomes visible.

A flock crosses the sky in a formation too exact to be a flock. A buried ridge pauses beneath the plain. The salt forests ring once, together. Across many kilometers, grazers lower their furnace-mouths to the ground.

Then the plateau inhales.

The breath rises through ravines and fissures. Dust lifts without wind. The black seams brighten. Far cliffs show their inner arrangements: chambers, loops, rotating masses, enormous folded structures extending beyond sight. For one moment every animal is connected by heat and pressure. The flying things, the motes, the grazers, the unseen predators, the burrowers beneath the stone—all become parts of one immense and patient mechanism.

Then it exhales.

The false horizons settle. The salt forests stop ringing. The animals separate again and resume their separate movements, as if nothing has happened.

But beneath the feet, the hum continues.

And if a person remains long enough—long enough for the shadows to point in several directions, long enough for a distant ridge to become familiar, long enough for the land to begin recognizing their weight—they may notice that the hum has acquired a rhythm.

Not a message.

A place in the rhythm.
