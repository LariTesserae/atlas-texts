What lives there is not a population in the ordinary sense. There are no houses, no paths, no central nest, and no part of the chamber that can be called the center of its life. The inhabitants are distributed through the machinery as thoroughly as the roots are.

They are called, if they are called anything, the **Tenders**.

A Tender may be mistaken for a length of wet cable, a pale root, or a piece of insulation shifting in the current. Fully grown, it is about the size of a child’s arm, though it can fold itself into spaces no wider than a thumb. Its body has no fixed front or back. It is a soft, segmented cylinder covered in overlapping plates of translucent keratin and dull metal. Beneath those plates, slow colors move: green when it is cold, amber when it is feeding, and a dim blue when it has detected a change in the machines.

It has six small limbs, although it rarely uses all of them at once. Two are adapted for gripping pipes and railings. Two end in delicate, branching fingers capable of turning a valve or extracting a seed from a crack. The remaining pair are sensory organs. They press against walls, housings, and floor plates to read the vibrations passing through them.

The Tenders do not see well. Their eyes are shallow black cups along the sides of their heads, sensitive to changes in brightness but almost useless for distance. They navigate by pressure, temperature, and sound. The chamber is legible to them as a map of tremors: the pulse of a pump, the shiver of a leaf, the faint electrical chatter in a transformer, the weight of fish moving through a drain. A stopped machine is not merely broken to them. It is absent.

They sleep in intervals, never all at once.

One folds itself around a warm pipe while another rests inside a ventilation housing. Several may retreat into the old cable trays, where their bodies become nearly indistinguishable from the roots and hanging wires. During the dark period, they gather near the blue-white indicator lamps. They do not seem to need the light, but they place their faces close to it and remain there until the lamps flicker. Then they disperse.

Their young begin as small, pale knots attached to the undersides of machinery. A Tender places one near a source of continuous vibration, usually a pump bearing or a pressure regulator. The young feeds on condensation, mineral deposits, and the faint current that travels through the metal. For several cycles it remains motionless except for the rhythmic opening and closing of a transparent mouth. Then it separates, drops onto the nearest wet surface, and begins to crawl.

There is no parent-child bond that lasts beyond this placement. The chamber raises them.

The Tenders maintain the place, but they do not control it. They cannot repair the great engines or replace a ruptured pipe. Their work is more patient and less ambitious. They clear sediment from valve mouths. They remove roots from gears before the roots become too thick. They collect fallen scales from the shaft grating. They redirect water with scraps of ceramic and rubber. When a lamp begins to fail, they gather beneath it and touch the housing until the weak electrical pulse has been spread evenly through their bodies.

They also cultivate the plants, though not in the way a gardener would.

A Tender will carry a cutting across the chamber for days, testing different surfaces with its sensory limbs. It may settle the cutting inside a broken motor, then return periodically to rotate it toward the current. If the plant dies, the Tender does not mourn. It strips the dead tissue away and brings another cutting. Some plants have been grown through so many generations of this attention that they respond to the Tenders’ touch, closing their leaves around them and opening again when the surrounding machinery changes rhythm.

The flowers are important.

When the chamber’s pumps shift into their slower cycle, certain stalks open. The Tenders gather around them, pressing their bodies against the stems. Pollen clings to their plates and is carried into ducts, across catwalks, and down the dripping walls. They do not appear to understand reproduction as a separate act. To them, flowering is simply another change in the chamber’s pattern, one that must be met and continued.

They communicate through the same patterns.

A warning is a rapid series of taps against metal. A location is a sustained vibration repeated at intervals. Hunger is expressed by touching a warm housing and remaining there. There are more complex exchanges, especially around the oldest machines. A Tender may spend hours with one limb against a panel, listening to the delayed return of its own pulse. Another joins it. Then another. By the end, a dozen bodies may be arranged across the housing, each receiving a different portion of the structure’s internal sound.

This is how they remember.

The Tenders possess no written history. Their past is stored in the machinery: in the altered timing of a piston, in the pattern of leaks along a wall, in the scars around a sealed door. An old pump whose rhythm has changed over centuries may still be recognized by generations of Tenders who have never heard it operate correctly. They know that it once made a faster sound. They know that something happened during the season when the amber lamps all went dark. They know which grating released the broad-backed creature and which one trapped it.

They do not worship the creature, but they prepare for it.

When its shadow rises beneath the chamber, the Tenders abandon the exposed floor and climb toward the walls. Those nearest the shaft spread themselves across the grating, not to block it, but to feel the pressure of the body passing below. The plants lean because the water moves. The Tenders lean because the memory of the movement is older than any living one of them.

After the creature descends, the Tenders collect what remains: scales, fragments of soft tissue, strange threads of mucus that glow for several minutes in the dark. They carry these things to the warmest basins. Fish come afterward. Blind scavengers follow. The chamber briefly becomes more alive, then settles back into its separate pockets of existence.

They are not a society, exactly. They do not gather in numbers except where a machine requires many bodies. They do not form pairs permanently. They have no leader. Their closest equivalent to a communal decision occurs when the whole chamber changes rhythm. Then every Tender pauses, touches the nearest manufactured surface, and listens.

If the pumps falter, they become frantic. If the engines stop entirely, they begin making noise themselves.

At first it is only a clicking from the walls: hundreds of small limbs striking metal. Then comes the rubbing of plates, the scraping of claws along ceramic, the wet percussion of bodies moving through ducts. The Tenders climb into valve chambers and strike the housings from within. They pull loose panels free and allow water to pass through them. They vibrate against pipes until the sound travels deep into the machine.

It is not repair. It is insistence.

The chamber must not become silent. Silence, to the Tenders, is not peace. It is the first condition of disappearance.

So they keep the place audible. They tend the plants, clear the drains, carry the dead, wake the lamps, and wait beside the old engines for the next reluctant movement. When the pumps begin again, they scatter into their separate territories. Some return to the roots. Some descend into the walls. One or two climb to the observation blister and press their bodies against the clouded window.

They cannot see the drowned world beyond it.

They feel the distant towers tremble through the glass. They feel the great cables descending into the dark. They feel the pressure of the water above and below, and the enormous animal moving somewhere beneath the chamber.

Then the rain thickens.

The Tenders lift their faces toward the falling water, and for a moment every living thing in the chamber is arranged according to the same rhythm: leaf, fish, root, machine, body.

The place continues.

So do they.
