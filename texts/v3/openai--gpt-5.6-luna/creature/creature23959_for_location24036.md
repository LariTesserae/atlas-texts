What lives there is not a creature but a continuity.

It begins below the surface, where the stone is still warm from pressure and the water moves through hairline fractures. There, in the dark beneath the mats, roots meet fungal threads, fungal threads meet mineral films, and mineral films gather around the buried pipes. Nothing has a central body. There is no heart, no brain, no chamber in which the plateau keeps a thought. Instead there are gradients: wetness, pressure, warmth, light. The living things lean toward them, and in leaning, they touch.

The silver-green mats are the oldest inhabitants, though they are not old in any human sense. They spread slowly over the exposed rock, fastening themselves to every irregularity. Their upper surfaces are soft and reflective; beneath, they are dense with rootlets and pale fibers. When water passes underneath, the mats lift almost imperceptibly, making room for it. When the water leaves, they settle again. Their bodies are layered records of rain.

The grasses come later and stand taller. Their roots descend farther, finding cracks the mats cannot reach. They are built for the wind. Each blade is narrow because a broad surface would tear; each joint is reinforced because the stalk cannot afford to bend too far. The columnar plants are more patient still. They rise through years of weather, hollowing themselves as they grow. Their interiors are dark, damp corridors where insects sometimes shelter and where condensation gathers in beads along the walls.

None of these plants possesses a plan. Yet the plateau has given them a common shape. They become braces, gutters, bridges, walls. A root thickens beside a fracture because the fracture holds water. A vine hardens where the wind pulls at it. Leaves overlap because the rain arrives from one direction and the light from another. The landscape seems designed only because every weak arrangement has been removed by weather.

Underneath, the fungi are everywhere.

They form pale, branching nets through the soil and the thin mineral crust, binding one plant to another without making them equal. A struggling reed may receive water from a distant moss bed. A dying stalk may pass its stored sugars into the roots beneath it. Chemical warnings move through the network when a section is torn or flooded. These messages do not become thoughts. They are closer to changes in pressure than to language, but they alter what grows, what sleeps, and what thickens.

The plateau’s water is drawn through this network before it reaches the pipes. The pumps take what the roots leave behind. The roots, in turn, exploit the channels opened by the pumps. Neither system was built for the other, but each has become part of the other’s conditions. The machinery is not alive, though the living things have begun to use it. Warm exhaust pipes make favored corridors for vines. Vibrations mark the underground chambers where water is moving fastest. The building’s foundations provide shade, minerals, and protected cracks.

If the installation were abandoned, the plateau would not notice at once.

The pumps would continue to thrum for a while. Water would gather in the wrong places. One tower would lose power, then another. Cables would sag into the mats. Plants would enter through vents and seams, filling the building with green, then gray, then black. The machinery would rust, but not disappear. It would be retained: a length of metal here, a warm chamber there, a new rib in the unfinished geology.

The insects are temporary. They arrive with the weather and leave with it. They drink from the flowers, lay a few eggs, and are carried away or swallowed by the moss. The thin antlered animal is more visitor than resident, coming up from lower elevations when the air is clear. It moves carefully across the wet stone, testing each mat before putting its weight down. Its presence leaves no lasting mark. The plateau does not need it.

The true inhabitants are the plants that remain when everything mobile has gone.

They do not watch the towers. They do not listen to the pumps. They respond to light, water, vibration, and damage, and their responses accumulate over decades until the place seems to have intentions. A wall grows where a wall is useful. A basin forms where rain repeatedly gathers. A root passes beneath a machine and emerges on the other side, carrying the memory of the obstruction only in the shape it has taken.

At night, when the white glare is gone and the plateau becomes a dark plane above the clouds, the living cover contracts. Leaves close. Flowers seal themselves. Water continues moving through the stone. The pumps beat below like a second, artificial metabolism.

Nothing dreams there.

But everything is becoming connected.
