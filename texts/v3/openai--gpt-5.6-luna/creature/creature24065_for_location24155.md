The inhabitants of Veyr are not born soft.

They emerge from sealed chambers beneath the basin, where heat, pressure, and electrical charge are held in careful balance. A new Veyren begins as a dark, many-sided kernel suspended in mineral brine. It is not an egg, though it serves some of the same purposes. It is not a machine, though much of its first structure is manufactured. Over years, layers accrete around it: ceramic plates, conductive filaments, pressure sacs, and fine translucent membranes that will eventually serve as skin.

The adult form is low and many-jointed, built close to the ground. Most Veyren stand between one and two meters high, though height is not particularly important to them. Their bodies are arranged around a central pressure core, with four primary limbs used for walking and two finer pairs used for manipulation. The limbs are slender at the joints and heavily plated where they meet the ground. Each foot ends in a fan of gripping pads capable of reading vibration through the rock.

They do not have faces as the lower world might recognize them. The front of the body is a smooth, pale shield broken by several dark apertures. Some of these take in air. Some sense heat. One is used for speech, though speech is only a minor part of their communication. Their most precise exchanges occur through touch, pressure, and modulated electrical discharge. A Veyren may place two fingers against a wall and send a sequence through the stone that another hears in the bones of its feet. At close range, their conversations appear almost silent: a turn of the head, a tremor in the wrist, a brief blue pulse beneath the plates.

Their voices, when they use them, are dry and low. The thin atmosphere strips away most of the upper harmonics, leaving something like distant metal rubbing against stone. They do not shout. A shout would be wasteful and nearly useless. In emergencies, they transmit through the city instead, waking conduits, warning gates, and tower foundations in a chain of descending vibrations.

The Veyren are warm-bodied, but not in the manner of mammals. Heat moves through them along internal channels, gathering in broad reservoirs beneath the back plates and flowing toward the limbs when needed. They sleep beside regulated walls, thermal shelves, or the exhaust cores of old factories. To a Veyren, a warm surface is not merely comfortable. It is nourishing, medicinal, and intimate. Families often share a heat chamber for decades, each body contributing its stored warmth to the others during the coldest part of the night.

They are patient because their bodies are patient.

A Veyren can remain still for hours without discomfort and can work on a single structure for most of a lifetime. Their metabolism is slow, but their senses are exact. They notice the minute change in a tower’s vibration, the altered conductivity of a mineral seam, the first pressure irregularity inside a buried pipe. Much of what appears to be civic maintenance is, to them, an extension of bodily awareness. Veyr is not a place they inhabit from the outside. It is something they feel through their feet, palms, and plates.

The oldest inhabitants have become difficult to distinguish from the city.

Their outer armor is patched with ceramic grown from Furnace Belt minerals. Heat exchangers are mounted along their backs. Some have replaced damaged limbs with articulated tools that fold into the body when not in use. Others carry old conduits beneath their plates, allowing them to interface directly with pressure gates or factory systems. A very old Veyren may have a body composed of several materials from several centuries: a pale shoulder shield from a dismantled tower, black joints made in the first industrial era, and a spinal array repaired so many times that its original purpose is unknown.

They are not embarrassed by this accumulation. Identity, to them, is not the preservation of an original form. It is continuity of function.

A person is remembered by the systems they kept alive.

The city’s districts are therefore also communities, though the boundaries do not follow streets or buildings. There are the Kiln Houses, whose inhabitants regulate the great thermal works; the Deep Surveyors, who spend years in the old foundations and pressure shafts; the Herd Wardens, who maintain the migration routes of the stoneback grazers; and the Thread Tenders, who cultivate the black mineral colonies on warm shelves and harvest their stored charge. There are bridge clans, vent families, convoy lineages, and entire populations devoted to structures no longer used for their original purpose.

A Veyren may be assigned to a tower before its plates have fully hardened. It may spend a century learning the tower’s moods: the way it settles after dust storms, the pitch of its cooling struts, the rhythm of its internal presses. If the tower is eventually enclosed within a newer building, the Veyren does not necessarily leave. It continues to serve as the tower’s interpreter, carrying its old vibrations into the new architecture.

Their society has laws, but law is mostly the formal name for accumulated necessity. Do not block a herd route. Do not open a sealed vent without three independent readings. Do not disturb a dormant mineral-thread colony during the high-light period. Do not dismantle a machine whose purpose is forgotten until its silence has been observed for a full atmospheric cycle.

The last rule is the most often broken and the most often regretted.

Veyren children—if they can be called children—are smaller, brighter, and more mobile than adults. Their plates are thin and translucent, showing the colored flow of heat beneath. They travel in groups through maintenance corridors, following moving lights and imitating the vibration codes of passing machines. They are fascinated by loose dust, not because it is rare, but because it reveals invisible currents. A child will spend an entire afternoon watching a grain cross a floor under the influence of a ventilation field.

They are discouraged from approaching the open flats alone. Not because the flats are forbidden, but because they are too large for an inexperienced mind. The plateau can induce a kind of disorientation in which distance loses meaning and the slow movements of animals seem to become the only true measure of time. Young Veyren return from such excursions altered, often quiet for several days, having discovered that the city is not the center of the world but one pressure system among many.

The stonebacks are treated with wary respect. The Veyren do not domesticate them. Instead, they study their routes, clear obstructions, and repair the shallow depressions worn into the ground by generations of passing feet. Some herds have become familiar enough to particular districts that their arrival is recorded and anticipated. When one dies near the city, its armored body is not stripped immediately. A watch is kept around it. The carcass may later be used for structural material, but only after the mineral colonies have taken what they require and the herd has moved on.

The ribbon predators are less honored, though they are still protected. Their presence means the thermal shelves remain active and the mineral threads remain healthy. Veyren children are taught to recognize the almost invisible fold of a resting predator against the rock. To mistake one for a slab is considered a failure of attention, not bad luck.

The aerial creatures are regarded almost as weather. Their gatherings above the exhaust towers are part of the evening pattern of Veyr. Workers pause beneath them, watching their buoyant bodies drift among the hot currents. Sometimes the creatures descend far enough for their filamentous limbs to brush the upper gantries. The contact produces a faint static shimmer across the metal. Veyren on the towers will answer with small blue lights from their palms.

No one knows whether the aerial creatures understand this as communication.

No one is certain that the question matters.

The Veyren themselves are not a single shape or temperament. Those who spend their lives near the Furnace Belt develop thick, dark plates and broad heat reservoirs. Those assigned to high towers are narrow and lightly built, with large pressure membranes and long balancing limbs. Deep workers become compact, with dense shells and sensory organs adapted to darkness. Some old lineages are nearly immobile, having transferred most of their awareness into industrial networks. They live as voices in walls, pulses in rails, and patterns of light along sealed control panels.

Such beings are still addressed by personal names.

A name may describe an ancestor, a repair, a season of unusual heat, or a sound once heard in the deep stone. Some names are short enough to be spoken. Others are sequences of pressure that require a floor, a metal plate, or another body to transmit them. The oldest names are not pronounceable at all. They are stored in the vibration signatures of buildings.

Veyren mourning is quiet. When one of them dies, its pressure core is removed and placed near a warm fracture. The body is dismantled slowly, with useful plates returned to the city and conductive filaments laid among the mineral threads. The core remains until its stored charge has dispersed. During that time, others visit and touch it, sending messages through the dark crystal.

Eventually, the core becomes indistinguishable from the ground.

This is not considered an ending. Veyr has no clean distinction between organism, tool, structure, and landscape. The living feed on the mineral. The mineral enters the machines. The machines preserve the roads. The roads guide the herds. The herds disturb the dust. The dust settles into the joints of the towers. The towers warm the air in which the aerial forms gather. Everything passes into something else, slowly and without ceremony.

At night, the Veyren move through the buried city beneath its sparse lights. Their plates catch the amber guide-lines. Their feet send small messages into the floor. In the distance, a Furnace Belt fracture glows with its subdued blue-white fire, and somewhere beyond the pressure gates, a herd crosses the dark plain in a patient line.

The inhabitants of Veyr do not think of themselves as having survived a hostile world.

They think they belong to a world that has never needed to hurry.
