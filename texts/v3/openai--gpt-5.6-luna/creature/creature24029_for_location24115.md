They are called the **Waiters**, though they do not wait idly.

There are fewer of them than the houses suggest. A household may occupy three buildings and sleep in a fourth, while a structure with a roof and a door may stand empty for years, kept ready for someone not yet born or not yet arrived. No one speaks of owning a house. A person is said to be *holding* one, as one holds a cup beneath a spring: temporarily, attentively, knowing that the shape of the ground may require it to be moved.

The Waiters are narrow people, long in the limbs and compact through the shoulders. Years of climbing wet steps and crossing flexible bridges have made them sure-footed, but not quick. They move with the deliberate balance of those accustomed to finding that yesterday’s surface is no longer level. Their feet are broad and hard-skinned. Their hands are marked by pale mineral stains that never quite wash away, and their nails grow faintly translucent from the water and the cold.

Most have dark hair, worn close to the head or tied at the nape so that it cannot catch on rope or timber. Among the older people, the hair often goes silver at the temples, though this is not considered a sign of age. The Waiters say the mountain puts its brightness into them by degrees. Some children are born with white lashes or a pale stripe in the hair, and these children are watched carefully—not feared, exactly, but listened to.

They dress in layers of felt, woven reed, and soft hide, with copper plates sewn over the chest and back. The plates are thin enough to bend, but they hold the body’s warmth and make a faint ticking sound when the wearer moves. A person can be recognized in fog by that sound before their face appears. Hoods are common, as are gloves with the fingertips removed for feeling the condition of stone. Everyone carries a coil of cord. Everyone carries chalk.

Their faces are quiet and weathered, not because they are solemn by nature but because the basin demands attention. They are always listening for changes beneath the ordinary noises: a new tremor in a walkway, a shift in the pitch of a fall, the soft internal crack that means a wall has begun to lift. A Waiter may stop in the middle of a sentence and place a palm against the nearest stone. The others will stop too. No one asks what they are listening for. If the answer comes, it comes through the hand.

They are not a cheerful people in the ordinary sense, but neither are they grim. Their pleasure is small, exact, and easily missed. They take satisfaction in a bridge that holds through a week of mist. They praise a well-fitted door, a dry sleeping room, a garden bed that has produced two leaves instead of one. Their jokes are understated and often concern the mountain’s habits.

“Your wall is growing toward mine,” someone might say.

“It has better sense.”

They laugh quietly, because loud laughter is difficult to distinguish from a warning call.

Children are raised by the whole settlement. They learn to walk on suspended planks before they are trusted to walk alone on open stone. Their first lessons are not letters but surfaces: warm, cold, hollow, wet, newly lifted, unstable. They are taught to identify a spring by the sound it makes behind a wall, to tell safe mist from dangerous mist, and to sleep through the ordinary groaning of the mountain.

At around seven years old, each child is given a listening bowl. It is small, shallow, and made from fired clay, with a dark line painted around its inner rim. The child carries it from spring to spring, learning to observe the rise and fall of water. At first the bowl is only a toy. Later, it becomes a record. A mature Waiter can read a basin of water almost as another person reads a face.

The young are not forbidden from taking risks, but they are required to name them accurately. A leap is a leap. A collapse is a collapse. “The mountain will probably hold” is not an acceptable description of a bridge. Before crossing unfamiliar ground, a child must say aloud what could happen if it fails.

The old people are known as **Deep Listeners**. They spend much of their time in the covered platforms along the eastern wall, in the hall above the fissure, or seated beside new seams with their hands resting on the stone. Their charcoal marks cover boards, doorframes, and the undersides of tables. Each mark records a sound, a pressure, a spring, a change in temperature, or the appearance of rock where rock had not been before.

They do not claim to predict births. Prediction would imply that the mountain obeys a pattern. Instead, they record its behavior so that those who come after will know it has happened before.

The Deep Listeners are treated with respect, though not with obedience. A person may disregard their advice, but only after repeating it correctly. This custom prevents people from dismissing a warning merely because they dislike it.

The settlement’s leaders are not rulers. They are **Keepers of Continuity**, chosen for their memory and their ability to settle arguments without making them larger. Their duties are practical: deciding which path should be moved, where stored food must be relocated, who will repair a roof, which new spring may be used, and whether a structure should be dismantled before the stone beneath it rises too far.

They are expected to own very little. A Keeper who becomes attached to a particular platform is considered unfit for the position. The mountain may take the platform. A leader must be able to move with it.

The Waiters have few possessions that cannot be carried. Tools are precious: hooked knives, augers, listening rods, small hammers, lamps made from the clear flesh of the local succulent. Every household keeps a bundle of old cords, even when the fibers have frayed beyond use. These are not discarded. A cord that has once held a bridge is cut into smaller lengths and tied around new tools, door handles, or children’s wrists during fog.

Their dead are not buried.

Burial is considered a form of argument with the mountain, an attempt to insist that a body remain in one place. Instead, the dead are wrapped in reed matting and carried to the upper rim, where the wind is strongest. There they are placed among the black teeth of rock, under cairns that are never made taller than the body they cover. Over time, water takes the salt from the bones, birds carry away the soft parts, and the mountain’s mineral dust enters everything.

The Waiters say a person returns in three ways: through the stone, through the water, and through the habits of those who remember them.

Names are therefore often given twice. A child receives a household name, used in ordinary life, and later—usually after their first serious repair—a listening name. The second name may refer to a sound, a spring, a manner of walking, or a particular response to danger. One woman is called **Third Drop**, because as a girl she discovered that a wall produced water only after the third knock of frost. A man known as **Bent Stair** walks with a slight limp acquired when a path changed beneath him. He is proud of it.

The Waiters do not worship the mountain in the manner of people who imagine a god looking down from above. Their reverence is more intimate and less comforting. They believe the mountain is alive, but not humanly alive. It has no face, no voice that can be translated, and no interest in their prayers. It is alive in the way a sleeping body is alive: changing without intention, producing warmth, pressure, movement, and sometimes sudden violence.

They make offerings only when they need to acknowledge a debt. A newly opened spring may receive a braid of wool. A house that survives a tremor may be marked with a line of milk-white clay. When a child is born, the parents place a seed in a seam of stone and wait to see whether it roots. If it does not, no one blames the child.

Their ceremonies are mostly acts of measurement.

At the beginning of the wet season, the inhabitants walk the entire settlement and touch every foundation. At the first appearance of new rock, they gather around it without speaking, bringing lamps even in daylight. When a building must be abandoned, its door is removed and laid flat on the threshold. This means the structure is no longer a house but remains available to the mountain, should it ever decide to use the shape again.

They are capable builders, but they do not think of themselves as builders. They are negotiators, translators, and maintainers. Their highest compliment for any person is that they have become **easy for the place to keep**.

This does not mean passive. The Waiters argue fiercely over water channels, food stores, and the placement of bridges. They have rivalries between terraces. Families remember old disputes over whose wall diverted whose spring, or who failed to repair a support before the winter mist. But even their anger is practical. No one can afford to remain offended when a stair has cracked.

Affection is shown through maintenance.

A person leaves dry kindling outside a neighbor’s door. Someone tightens the cords beneath another household’s platform without announcing it. A child wakes to find that the loose board she feared has been replaced. Lovers exchange pieces of stone, not carved or polished, but selected for their weight and temperature. To give someone a stone is to say: *I noticed what this place was doing, and I thought of you.*

They sing rarely, because the basin distorts music. Instead, they use rhythm: three knocks on a post, two on the rail, a pause. Each family has its own patterns for calling through the mist. The patterns are simple enough for a child to learn and distinct enough to identify someone before they emerge from the cloud.

When the mountain grinds beneath them, the Waiters do not flee immediately. They stand still first.

They listen for direction. They watch the counting bowls. They feel the tremor pass through the soles of their feet. Then they move—not in panic, but quickly, carrying lamps, children, records, and the nearest living cuttings from the garden chambers. No one waits to save furniture. No one carries a body if the stone is already rising.

Afterward, when the basin has settled, they gather in the long hall. The plates above the fissure are examined. The charcoal boards are updated. Paths are tested one by one. Someone makes food, though it may be only warmed roots and mineral-sweet water. Someone else begins repairing the first bridge.

By morning, the settlement will have changed shape.

A house may stand a little higher. A stair may lead nowhere. A new wall may have appeared between two gardens, smooth and pale, with water trembling along its base.

The Waiters will study it. They will name what they can. They will build around it.

And if the mountain permits them, they will remain.
