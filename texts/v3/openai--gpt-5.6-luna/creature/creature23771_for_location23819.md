They are called the **Underheld**, though the oldest word for themselves means only *those who remain*.

No one knows whether their ancestors entered the hollow deliberately. Their stories begin after the first houses, after the first channels, after the first tree had already rooted in the floor. They remember a people who came down carrying fire, seed, and the bones of their dead, but the story is told with the same uncertainty used for rain: it happened, perhaps, or happens still somewhere beyond the stone.

The Underheld are human in shape, but the hollow has altered the emphasis of their bodies.

They are narrow-shouldered and long-fingered, with compact feet shaped by ladders, ledges, and the oldest stairs. Their skin has a cool, muted cast, not colorless but accustomed to the mineral light: brown, gray, olive, and pale gold without the harshness of sun. The children are often born with large dark eyes, though the eyes become smaller as they age. Their pupils widen quickly in shadow and contract reluctantly in brightness. Many have a faint greenish stain beneath the nails from working with moss, clay, and the coppery roots of the wall vines.

They do not grow tall. A tall body is difficult beneath low roofs and dangerous on the bridges. The oldest families are small and dense, their backs slightly curved from generations spent under stone. Yet among them there are people who seem almost delicate: ladder-climbers, water-feelers, those who spend their lives in the ceiling galleries, tending the cracks and the black-winged birds. Their hands are scarred with tiny pale lines from flint, bone, and thorn. Their palms are soft in the center but callused along the edges, where they grip rope and limestone.

They wear little that makes noise.

A typical garment is a close-wrapped tunic of reed fiber, softened with moss oils and patched so many times that its original weave is impossible to find. Their outer layers are made from hide, woven roots, or the thin bark of the stunted trees. Bone toggles are preferred to knots because they can be opened in darkness. Loose ornaments are forbidden on the bridges. Even jewelry is tied down.

They mark themselves not by color but by texture. A person may wear a row of bone teeth sewn into one sleeve to show that they belong to a family of cistern keepers. A strip of rough clay at the throat may indicate a birth beneath a certain roof crack. Those who have carried water during a great seepage wear smooth stone beads, one for each vessel they saved. The dead are remembered by the objects that have passed through their hands: a hook, a cup, a ladder rung, a carved lintel.

Their voices are low and close.

The Underheld speak as though another person is always sleeping nearby. Their language is full of small distinctions for degrees of dampness, the quality of stone, the behavior of roots, and kinds of silence. There are at least seven words for water that has not yet fallen: water gathering, water thinking, water trapped, water choosing a path, water that belongs to a crack, water that has forgotten the surface, and water that will come soon.

They have no word for wilderness.

Outside the hollow is called the **Unroofed**, and everything in it is understood as exposed, temporary, and excessive. The people know that the Unroofed contains larger trees, warmer air, running rivers, and animals whose voices can be heard from far away. They also know it as a place of dangerous distance. The last person to climb out is said to have returned with sun-blind eyes and a mouth full of dust. Whether this happened recently or hundreds of years ago, no one agrees. The story is used to frighten children away from the upper seam, but also to remind adults that the world above is not merely dead land. It is a place that has not learned to remain.

They are born in rooms lined with damp clay.

During labor, all unnecessary lamps are covered. The mother lies beneath a roof crack, if one can be found, and a vessel is placed beneath the place where the first drop might fall. The child is not named at birth. For several days it is called **New Breath**, and its family listens to how it sleeps. Some children breathe rapidly, some in long pauses, some with a faint whistle. These qualities are believed to reveal which part of the hollow will claim them.

A child who reaches repeatedly toward light may become a roof tender. One who can remain still for a long time may be trained to listen in the central channel. Children who are gentle with snails are given gardens. Children who frighten goats are sent to work with clay. No one is thought to possess a single nature; rather, each person is observed until a useful pattern appears.

They learn to walk on stone before they learn to speak.

The first steps are taken on the oldest stairs, where the surface has been polished by generations of feet. An elder holds the child under the arms but does not guide the legs. The child must feel where the stone rises and falls. If the child stumbles, no one catches them unless the fall would be serious. The hollow, they believe, must be learned through the soles.

Children are quiet, but not obedient in the way outsiders might understand. They climb where they should not, steal fruit, loosen knots, hide snails in sleeping places, and build tiny rooms inside abandoned walls. Their mischief is absorbed into the city. A forbidden tunnel becomes a useful passage. A nest of stolen insects becomes a colony. A child who carves a face into a foundation may unknowingly create the shrine around which a house is later built.

They are not governed by kings.

The settlement is held together by people known as **Keepers of Interruption**. Their duty is not to command activity but to stop it. They declare when a stair must be closed, when a garden must be left alone, when a family must surrender a room to roots, when every hand must be put to water. There are Keepers of Roof, Keepers of Channels, Keepers of Quiet, and Keepers of the Dead. The title is never inherited, though children of Keepers often know where to look and what to listen for.

The most respected among them are those who can tell the difference between an ordinary tremor and the beginning of a collapse.

They kneel in the Listening Way with their ears against the stones. Others gather behind them in silence. A person may remain there for hours, eyes closed, one hand resting on the channel floor. The listeners claim that the stone speaks through pressure, but they do not mean speech. They detect the distant movement of water, the settling of roof layers, the passage of roots, the weight of a goat crossing the upper gallery.

When they rise, the settlement believes them.

A warning from the Listening Way can empty a house within minutes. Beds are lifted. Jars are carried. Fires are smothered. Moss is cut away from a vulnerable wall, though never without leaving a living edge. A passage is closed with clay and marked by a row of white bones. If the tremor passes, the wall is not reopened immediately. The people wait for the hollow to become itself again.

They have no private ownership of water.

A household may tend a vessel, repair a basin, or sleep beside a cistern, but every container is understood to have three lives: the life of finding, the life of use, and the life of return. The third is the vessel belonging to no one. Its water is reserved for strangers, the sick, newborns, animals, and the dead.

A person who drinks from it must leave something behind. Usually this is a thread, a bead, a pinch of soil, or a spoken name. The offering is not payment. It is proof that the water has passed through a life and has not been wasted by remaining only water.

They eat slowly and without ceremony, though meals are communal more often than not. Mushrooms are sliced thin enough to dry in the faintest light. Greens are folded into clay cakes. The translucent tree fruits are opened with bone knives and their pulpless flesh sucked for moisture. Insects are roasted inside sealed clay beads. Snails are gathered from the walls and returned if they are carrying eggs.

The Underheld do not kill often. Their animals are too valuable, and the hollow is too small for abundance to be mistaken for permanence. When a goat dies, every useful part is assigned before the body is moved: hide to a roof-worker, horn to a toolmaker, sinew to a bridge repair, bone to the shrine makers. The meat is divided according to need, not rank. The animal’s horns are set upright near the Listening Way so that it may continue, in some diminished sense, to hear the water.

Their dead are not buried.

There is no room for graves, and the earth is already occupied by roots, channels, and old walls. The body is washed with collected condensation, wrapped in reed cloth, and placed in a room that has no door. Moss is encouraged to grow over it. Over time the dead become part of the city’s softness: a dark mound beneath a tree, a green wall, a fertile shelf. Sometimes a foundation settles and reveals a finger bone or a fragment of jaw. It is not removed. The house is adjusted around it.

The Underheld say that a person dies three times.

The first death is when breath stops.

The second is when no living person remembers the sound of their voice.

The third is when the stone, root, or water that received them is destroyed.

For this reason, memory is treated as a form of repair. Names are spoken in low voices while people work. A woman kneading clay may recite the names of stair-builders. A child tending blind fish may repeat the name of whoever first found the basin. The names are not prayers. They are braces against collapse.

Their shrines are small and often invisible. A square of clay pressed into a wall. Three bones set beneath a lintel. A cup turned upside down over a root. The Underheld distrust images of faces. A face, they believe, asks to be looked at, and looking is a form of possession. Instead they honor gestures: a hand holding a vessel, a foot on a stair, an ear against stone.

They do have festivals, though these are nearly silent.

When the first substantial water arrives after a dry interval, every household extinguishes its small lamps. People gather along the channels and place their hands beneath the falling threads. No one speaks until the vessels are filled. Then the oldest person present strikes a piece of limestone once against the floor.

The sound is small.

It travels farther than any shout.

Afterward, the people eat fresh moss cakes, open the stored fruit, and bring out the old metal objects. These are not used. They are simply displayed: a rusted blade, a broken buckle, a disk green with age. Metal belongs to the forgotten surface, and once each year the Underheld allow themselves to remember that their ancestors may have come from somewhere with open skies and tools that did not have to be made from the same stone as the walls.

Some among them dream of leaving.

They are known as the **Upward-Minded**, and they are not punished. The hollow has no law against desire. They are given rope, water, a covering for the eyes, and the names of the cracks above. Most return quickly. A few climb as far as the seam and lie there for a long time, feeling the dry air move over them. They come back changed, unable to explain what they saw. One returned carrying a dead thorn branch. Another brought a feather. The feather was placed in the communal vessel and remained there until it dissolved.

The Underheld do not resent those who leave.

They resent only those who return and insist that the outside is more real.

For the people of the hollow, reality is not what is broadest or brightest. It is what remains in contact. A wall polished by hands. A root refusing to die. A basin kept ready for a drop that may not fall. A body lowered into moss. A silence maintained so that the faintest warning can be heard.

At night—or what they call night—the roof-light dims to a deep blue-gray. The black birds settle into the cracks. The pale mice move more freely. Children sleep in suspended nests beneath the bridges, wrapped in woven mats. Adults lie close to the walls, where the stone is least likely to shift without telling them.

Before sleep, each person touches the nearest solid thing.

A lintel. A floorstone. A tree root. The shoulder of another sleeper.

This is not a gesture of reassurance. They do not need to be assured that the hollow exists. It is a gesture of participation. They are reminding the city that they are still here, and allowing the city to remind them that they are held.

Then the hidden settlement grows quiet.

Water gathers somewhere above.

Roots move through the foundations.

The people breathe in the cold mineral dark, and beneath them, around them, through the walls and under the old stairs, the living hollow continues to make room for what cannot yet be seen.
