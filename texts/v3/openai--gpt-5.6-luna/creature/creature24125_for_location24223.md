The one who lives there is not easily seen at first.

A dark shape may be crouched beside the pool, and then the cloud closes, or a bird crosses between, or the shape resolves into one of the basin’s stones. From below, if anyone ever looks up far enough, the inhabitant is no more than a narrow interruption in the gray: a person wrapped in weather-stiff hide, standing motionless beside the cairn.

They are small, though not slight. Years of climbing and bracing against the wind have made them compact and hard, with shoulders bowed forward as if they are always walking into a storm. Their hands are broad across the knuckles, the nails split and darkened. The skin of the face is burned brown by sun and scoured pale at the cheeks. Hair, once perhaps black, has become the color of wet lichen. It is cut or torn short so that the wind cannot seize it.

They do not wear much. Layers of hide, grass fiber, and scraps of woven cloth are bound close to the body with cord. The outermost covering is patched so often that it has lost any original color. Feathers are sewn into the seams—not for ornament, but because the inhabitant has learned which feathers resist rain and which ones tremble before a change in weather. A strip of black wing is tied around one wrist. When the wind shifts, it lifts before the person feels the change.

They live in the shelter behind the stone wall, though “live” there means little more than returning to it. The hollow is too exposed for sleep to be deep. The inhabitant rests in short intervals, curled beneath the hide with one hand touching the floor, waking whenever the shelter creaks or the feathers above the pool begin to move. Sometimes they remain awake through the entire night, listening to the mountain give itself away in small sounds: a stone turning in a crack, ice forming in the watercourse, a goat dislodging gravel far below.

Their fire is rarely more than a red hollow of coals. Fuel is gathered from the dwarf shrubs only when necessary, and never from the living branches if the dead ones will serve. The inhabitant burns dried roots, droppings, scraps of old cord, and wood carried up from lower ground in careful bundles. Smoke does not rise cleanly in the basin. It presses low beneath the roof of cloud and crawls along the stone, so the shelter smells of ash, damp hide, and bitter mountain plants.

They eat what the place permits.

Insects are taken from the lee sides of rocks and crushed between stones. The sour blue berries are collected before dawn, though the inhabitant leaves the first fruit of every shrub untouched. The small cliff mammals are not hunted often; they are too useful as signs of approaching weather, and their paths lead to cracks where water may be found. When a bird dies in the basin, the inhabitant strips it carefully, saving the long feathers and boiling the flesh with roots. Nothing edible is allowed to remain exposed. The wind would take it, or the black-winged birds would.

The inhabitant knows the birds individually, or seems to. They answer the birds’ cries with sounds too low to be called speech. The birds scold from the holes above the basin and wheel away. At certain hours, one or two descend to the stone wall. They do not come close, but they wait there until the person has moved away from the pool. Then they drink.

No animal drinks from the pool while the inhabitant is beside it.

The person keeps the water clear. They remove dead insects, loosened grit, and the pale skins shed by the silver larvae. They do not touch the roots beneath the surface. Those are left to grow where they will. In winter, when the pool begins to seal with ice, the inhabitant breaks only a hand-sized opening and covers it with a flat stone at night. The mountain mammals come after dark to drink from that opening. Their breath clouds the air above it.

The shelter and the cairn are the inhabitant’s true work.

Each season, the wall is rebuilt where the wind has loosened it. Stones are fitted into one another without mortar, their broad faces turned against the prevailing gusts. The shelter’s hide is replaced piece by piece. The bones weighing it down are cleaned, retied, and sometimes changed for larger ones. If a storm tears the roof away, the inhabitant may spend the next several days beneath the rock shelf, waiting for the weather to relent enough to make repairs.

The cairn is tended more carefully.

The inhabitant descends when the stones must be replaced, carrying a sack or a sling, returning with one green-veined stone at a time. The ascent takes most of a day. Sometimes more than a day. The stones are brought from a valley no longer visible from the summit, and each is laid into the column only after being washed in the pool.

No one knows whether the inhabitant began the cairn or merely inherited it. No one may have inherited anything. The column leans toward the drop, and the person leans with it when standing beside it, as though listening for a message carried upward from the vanished valley. They never carve names into the stones. They do not pray in any recognizable manner. They place both hands against the cairn and remain there until the wind passes through them.

Loose things are gathered from the basin and carried to the edge.

The inhabitant finds feathers caught in moss, scraps of hide, seeds, ash, fragments of eggshell, the pale bodies of moths. These are not kept. They are taken to the open side and released. Sometimes the person adds something of their own: a length of cord, a broken tool, a lock of hair, a piece of dried meat gone too hard to eat. Everything must continue outward. Only the stones, the rooted plants, and the living body are permitted to resist.

When clouds fill the hollow, the inhabitant moves by touch.

They know the basin’s boundaries through the soles of their feet: the slick mineral band beside the pool, the shallow trough where water runs, the raised tooth of rock near the wall. They can cross it without sight, bent low, one hand trailing along the ground. In such weather they make almost no sound. The birds stop calling. The goats vanish. For a while the basin belongs to the person alone.

But the inhabitant is not the master of the place.

The wind lifts their clothes. Frost takes their water. Hunger thins them. Storms tear away what they have repaired. Their footprints fill almost as soon as they are made. When they die, if they die there, the mountain will begin undoing them immediately. The birds will find the body. The wind will carry the ashes, if there are ashes. The shelter will collapse by degrees. The cairn will remain.

Until then, the inhabitant continues.

At first light they kneel beside the pool. They drink without a cup, touch the water to their eyes, and look toward the open drop. Then they rise and begin gathering what the night has moved.
