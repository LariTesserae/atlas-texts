The people of the lake are difficult to see at first.

They do not move like visitors. A visitor crosses open water in a straight line, kicking or pulling against the current, announcing the shape of the body. The lake-born move through the city as if the city were an extension of themselves. They slip behind a leaf and emerge from beneath a root. They pause at the side of a stalk, one hand resting against it, and wait for the current to carry them the rest of the way. From a distance they may be mistaken for pale fish, drifting scraps of cloth, or shadows cast by the plants.

They are small and broad through the chest, with limbs made powerful by generations of climbing, swimming, and working against water. Their skin is pale in the deeper districts, almost gray beneath the blue-green light, though those who spend more time near the upper roofs develop a muted brown-gold color across the shoulders and face. Fine dark lines mark the wrists, throat, and ribs. These are breathing folds: narrow, sensitive organs that can draw a little oxygen from the water. They cannot remain indefinitely without air, but they are not helpless beneath the surface either. A lake-born person may work for several minutes in a dark channel where an ordinary human would already be drowning.

The children are born with larger eyes than they will need as adults. Their first months are spent in warm shallows, held in woven cradles beneath broad leaves. They learn to float before they learn to stand. Mothers and fathers do not carry them so much as anchor them: a loop around the waist, a hand on the ankle, a cord tied to a living root. When a child first swims beyond the reach of the cradle, the family gathers nearby and makes no sound. The child must find the current, trust it, and return.

Their eyes have a second transparent lid that slides sideways over the surface. In the darkness below the city, this lid protects them from silt and the fine grit raised by the scavengers. Their ears are small and close to the head. They feel much of what others would hear through the bones of the skull and the soles of the feet. A trembling stalk, a knock traveling along a bridge, or the distant change in the falls may tell them that someone is approaching long before a face appears.

They speak with little breath.

Conversation is a mixture of low pulses, clicks made against the teeth, gestures, and touches to the surrounding plants. A word spoken aloud travels poorly through the open water, but a palm pressed against a stem can send a vibration down the length of it. People who live in the same neighborhood sometimes converse through the rootwork without seeing one another. A warning can pass from house to house as three quick tremors. An invitation is slower and more complex, a pattern that resembles the tapping of falling water. Children spend years learning the differences.

Their names are not given once.

A child receives a temporary name at birth, usually a sound associated with the place where it was born: Under-Leaf, Warm-Stone, First-Current. Later, when the child begins to tend a plant, repair a bridge, or keep watch over a spawning hollow, the city gives them another name. This name may change several times. Someone called Reed-Tied in youth may become Root-Keeper, Deep Listener, or the more solemn name given to those who have outlived the structure entrusted to them.

They wear layers of felted plant fiber and the soft inner skins of water-resistant leaves. Their garments are weighted at the hem so the current does not turn them helplessly. Sleeves are close to the body, and belts carry tools in small shell cases: a stone blade, a bone hook, a spool of vine, a pouch of resinous paste. Many wear breathing hoods when working below the cultivated level. These are not sealed masks but flexible cups fitted beneath the broad leaves of certain plants, where pockets of warmed, oxygen-rich air collect. A worker may draw from one through a narrow tube, then leave it behind for the next person.

No one wears bright colors unless there is a reason. Red-brown indicates a birth or death in the family. White means that the wearer is tending an open wound in a plant and should not be disturbed. A strip of yellow fiber tied around the arm warns that the person has been fasting for a ritual or working in deep water and may be light-headed. Most clothing is gray, green, cream, or the dim blue of the lake, and much of it is repaired so often that the original material is impossible to identify.

They are not a numerous people. A household may consist of two or three adults, several children, an elder, and the plants that have grown around them. A house is not considered empty when its residents leave. If the rootwork remains alive, the house still has a claim upon the city. Someone must visit it, clear its channels, feed the soil around its anchors, and retie any stems loosened by the current.

The lake-born do not think of plants as possessions. They speak of them as partners, though not always kindly. A plant may be persuaded, disciplined, grafted, thinned, or abandoned. Its needs can oppose the needs of the people who live within it. The oldest gardeners say that this is not a failure of kinship but proof of it. A living thing that never resists is only a tool.

Their work is repetitive, but it is never exactly the same. They scrape algae from the public roofs. They clear silt from the channels. They guide young roots into cracks in the old stone and tie them there with living vine. They harvest the floating leaves only after new ones have unfolded beneath them. They collect fish eggs from the spawning hollows and return them weeks later, when the young have grown large enough not to be eaten by the smallest hunters.

Some are open-water gatherers, spending long periods beyond the city among the tall, widely spaced plants. Some descend into the drowned foundations with lamps made from pale, glowing organisms sealed in shell cups. Some tend the upper gardens, where the current is stronger and sunlight reaches through the water in trembling shafts. Others remain in the deep neighborhoods, where every task is done by touch and memory.

The old people are recognizable by the cords they wear around their wrists. Each cord is woven from a plant they helped establish or save. One cord may represent a bridge, another a roof, another a garden that failed despite every effort. The cords darken with age and water. When an elder dies, the cords are not buried with the body. They are untied and placed around the central pillar, where they drift among the older tools.

The dead are returned to the lake in stages.

The body is wrapped in a loose mesh and carried beyond the last cultivated structures, to a region where the floor slopes into darkness. There the scavengers come. No one watches closely. The family waits at a distance, holding the cords of the dead and listening to the mountain’s vibration through the stone. When the body is gone, the cords are washed and taken back to the city. The person is then said to have entered the circulation: not transformed into anything mystical, but carried through the ordinary immense processes on which everyone depends.

The lake-born fear little in the way outsiders might expect. Darkness does not frighten them. Large animals do not frighten them unless they behave strangely. Even the deep water, where the city’s noises disappear and the pressure gathers around the bones, is not considered evil. What frightens them is interruption: a blocked channel, a silent root, an entire flowering grove failing to open. They fear a change too sudden for the city to absorb.

Their festivals are therefore quiet.

When the falls are fullest, the people gather beneath the great central leaf and fasten small shells to the surrounding stems. The current sets them trembling. Children release strings of silver-bubbled flowers, and the flowers rise slowly through the blue water, each carrying a tiny reflection of the city. The people sing then, though the song is mostly vibration: a low, layered hum passed through hands, throats, roots, and stone.

Sometimes one of the broad-winged creatures descends into the court and folds itself around the pillar. No one drives it away. Everyone waits until it leaves.

At night, when the upper water has become black and the city’s pale plants close their flowers, the lake-born settle into their hammocks and root-nests. They sleep lightly, listening through the living architecture. A change in current wakes them. So does the knock of a fish against a wall, the shifting of a foundation, or the distant thunder of ice breaking high in the mountain.

They wake without clocks or bells.

The city wakes around them: leaves opening, channels stirring, small bodies flashing between the roots. One by one, the people emerge from their shelters and place their hands against the nearest living stem. Before they begin the day, they feel whether the city is still breathing.
