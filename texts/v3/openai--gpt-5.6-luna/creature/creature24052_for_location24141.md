The inhabitants are not builders in the way a people might be builders elsewhere. They do not construct toward completion. They build because building is the condition by which they remain alive.

They are narrow-bodied and heavily jointed, with the compact strength of creatures accustomed to hauling weight through confined spaces. Their skin is gray or nearly black, patterned with pale seams at the throat, wrists, and eyelids. In the wet, these seams brighten faintly, as if the body has retained traces of the basin’s mineral light. Their hands are broad and thick-fingered. The fingertips have hard, translucent pads that allow them to grip flooded stone, rope, and slick ceramic without gloves.

They see poorly in open darkness but are sensitive to reflected light. A covered coal, a green film beneath a ledge, the glimmer on a pool—these are enough. Their faces are therefore kept shaded, not only against the rain but against excess brightness. Most wear masks of layered clay, lacquered fiber, or thin metal, with narrow horizontal openings for the eyes. The masks are less protective equipment than ordinary clothing. To appear bare-faced in public suggests illness, grief, or deliberate challenge.

They have no word for wilderness.

Their language divides places into those that are holding and those that are failing. A wall can be safe, doubtful, or already lost even while standing. Water can be obedient, delayed, diverted, or hungry. A person may be described by the last structure they saved, the channel they maintain, or the collapse from which they were pulled. Names are temporary things, often abandoned when their owners move to another district. The basin remembers people less by lineage than by repairs.

Children are raised in work crews rather than households. They learn to distinguish a harmless settling noise from the first sound of a wall giving way before they learn to read marks. They are taught to sleep through hammering, to wake when a channel changes pitch, and to place one hand against the floor whenever the deep knocking begins. A child who can identify the direction of an underground disturbance is considered ready for a tool.

There are few old inhabitants. Age does not make them peaceful or retired. It makes them valuable. The oldest know where the buried rooms lie, which terraces were built over hollow ground, and which channels once carried water in a direction no longer visible. Their bodies become bent from decades of crouching beneath beams and carrying loads along sloped ways. They often wear no outer covering except a mantle of stitched plates, each plate marked with a district, a flood, or a dead work crew.

They are called keepers, though they keep nothing permanently.

A keeper may spend fifty years reinforcing a stair and then be ordered to dismantle it because water has begun to rise beneath the third landing. They may remember a bridge that no longer exists, a cistern sealed before their birth, or a whole neighborhood whose materials now form the floor beneath their feet. This does not make them mournful in the usual sense. For these people, destruction is a form of transfer. Nothing is lost if it can be broken down and made useful again.

Their society is organized around thresholds of danger. During ordinary rain, workers continue. During heavy rain, crews divide into watchers, haulers, and sealers. When the water turns metallic, all unassigned inhabitants report to the channels. When the underground knocking begins, tools are laid down and everyone listens.

The sound is not worshipped exactly. It is obeyed.

No one agrees on what lies beneath the basin. Some say the knocking comes from a buried creature that has grown through the foundations. Others believe it is the voice of the first construction, a machine or chamber still trying to finish itself. The oldest records, preserved on fired tablets and scored with repeating lines, suggest that the basin was built to contain something whose boundaries were never found.

The inhabitants do not call the thing below a god. Gods are expected to want something. The thing below has never made its desire clear.

They call it the Deep Witness.

When the resonant note rises from the black opening, the basin’s inhabitants stop what they are doing because the note passes through their bodies. It reaches the fluid in their inner ears, the water in their lungs, the wet seams of their skin. Some kneel. Some press their foreheads against the paving. Others watch the pools for signs: a tremor moving against the wind, a film breaking into rings, blind cistern-creatures turning in perfect unison.

No one speaks during the note. Speech is considered a way of placing oneself above the sound.

Afterward, work resumes with unusual urgency. Blocks are hauled faster. Channels are cleared. New braces are cut and fitted. Those who have dreamed during the note report their dreams to the nearest keeper, who records them whether or not they make sense.

The basin’s workers are not especially reverent toward the smaller lives around them. The pale creatures under bridges are trapped only when they interfere with a repair. The blind things in the cisterns are handled with care because they indicate contamination. The mineral membranes are scraped away from doorways and cultivated in certain sealed chambers where their ticking can reveal pressure shifts in the walls.

Some inhabitants devote themselves to these membranes. They are called listeners. They press their palms against the translucent sheets and interpret the changes in their contraction: a rapid ticking means water approaching, a slow pulse means stress in the masonry, a complete stillness means that something has entered the chamber and is not moving.

The larger creatures are different. They are feared, but not hated. Their passage is treated as another form of weather. When one moves beneath a district, crews mark the disturbance, abandon unstable structures, and wait. If it breaks through, the dead are retrieved only after the ground settles. The new cavity is then measured, braced, and eventually occupied.

There are stories of people who have gone down into such cavities and returned altered. They come back with their masks fused to their faces, or with a new sense of rhythm in their speech. Some refuse to work above ground again. Some begin building downward, opening shafts beneath existing foundations and claiming that the basin is upside down.

These are not exiles. They are given tools.

The inhabitants have a deep distrust of anything finished. A completed wall is a sealed decision, and sealed decisions conceal weakness. Their doors remain adjustable. Their bridges are built with removable sections. Every large chamber has at least one unfinished corner where future water, future people, or future failure may enter.

They do not believe they own the basin. They believe the basin is borrowing their hands.

So they continue: carrying covered flames through the dark, testing the floor with mallets, scraping growth from the seams, draining pools into assigned channels, raising walls against the rain and then taking them apart when the water proves them wrong.

At night—though there is no true night apart from the darkness that always surrounds them—they gather in sheltered rooms around bowls of mineral water. They eat dense cakes of fired grainlike paste, preserved blind-cistern creatures, and pale growths harvested before they harden. They exchange warnings, measurements, and rumors. Affection is shown by repairing someone’s tools without being asked, or by taking their place beneath a leaking roof.

When the deep note sounds, they fall silent.

When it ends, they return to work.

They are the basin’s inhabitants, but also its temporary organs: hands against its walls, ears against its foundations, bodies moving water from one place to another. If the construction ever becomes complete, they will have no obvious purpose. Perhaps that is why they never allow it. Perhaps, beneath every terrace and channel, they understand that the basin is not a city waiting to be finished.

It is a living thing that survives by remaining unfinished—and they are the part of it that knows how to continue.
