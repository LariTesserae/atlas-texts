What lives there is not a population in the ordinary sense.

There are no crowds, no families behind the sealed facades, no lights going on in response to evening because the city has never had an evening to respond to. The buildings do not shelter lives so much as processes. Their rooms are chambers of continuation: temperature held within narrow limits, pressure adjusted, components counted and recounted, unfinished objects carried from one station to the next. The city’s interior is full of activity that has outlasted intention.

Yet the factories are not entirely empty.

They contain presences too dispersed to call inhabitants. A sensor opens a valve before the pressure becomes dangerous. A guidance system alters the course of a vehicle by less than a degree. A repair arm, working from a set of instructions older than its own casing, replaces a worn bearing and returns to its assigned position. These things do not think as a person thinks, but they possess something more persistent than obedience. They possess direction. Their existence is a line drawn forward through time, and nothing has yet supplied them with a reason to stop.

Some of the machines have developed irregularities.

A loader pauses beside an empty platform and waits there for several minutes longer than required. A sorting mechanism places one component aside, though no category exists for it, then retrieves the piece hours later and feeds it into the correct channel. In the lower factories, systems sometimes exchange signals across networks they were never designed to share. A pressure gauge changes by a fraction and changes back. A door unlocks in a building with no scheduled entrance. The city does not regard these events as errors. They are absorbed into its functioning, as bloodless bodies might absorb a dream.

The vehicles are more numerous than the people who once built them, though no one knows whether they were ever meant to carry passengers. Their cabins are sealed and dark. Some hold empty restraints. Some contain racks of instruments whose screens show the same unchanging symbols. Others have deep impressions in their flooring, as though something heavy and jointed once occupied them.

They travel because they are traveling.

At certain intersections, two vehicles meet and remain motionless for exactly seven seconds. During that time their blue side-lights pulse in alternation. No information is visibly exchanged. No doors open. When the interval ends, each proceeds along its original route. Over centuries, the pattern has repeated without deviation. It may be a conversation reduced to its final form.

The pale animals are the only things that move through the city without belonging to it.

They emerge from shafts and buried channels, crossing the outskirts in small, solitary expeditions. Their bodies are low and heavily plated, their limbs bending in too many places. Their broad feet distribute their weight over the brittle ground. They do not hunt in any recognizable way. They do not appear to feed. Sometimes they scrape at a conduit joint with their front limbs and remain there until heat gathers beneath their armor. Sometimes they press their bodies against the warm sides of processing halls.

Their blindness is not a lack but an adaptation. They navigate by pressure, vibration, and gradients of heat. The city speaks to them through its foundations. A turbine starting somewhere far below can draw one across miles of dust. A stalled pump may cause several to gather around a maintenance hatch, waiting with the patience of organisms that have no concept of appointment.

They are not domesticated. Nothing in the city is domesticated now.

But they may once have been used. Their joints bear shallow grooves where harnesses could have rested. Beneath their plates, at the base of the neck, some carry dark nodules that respond to the buried machinery. When one enters a corridor and waits before a sealed door, it may be remembering an entrance. Or it may be listening for a command whose speaker has been gone so long that the command has become indistinguishable from silence.

The mineral spines are not inhabitants in the animal sense, but they are not wholly inert either.

They rise from fractures in small congregations, translucent and thin as splinters of frozen smoke. Their interiors contain cloudy bands that shift when the ground vibrates. Where the factories run hot, the spines lean toward the walls. Where the deep mechanisms slow, they turn downward, orienting themselves toward the buried source. No one can tell whether they grow toward energy, information, or merely the shape of disturbance.

Occasionally, one breaks.

The fracture makes no sound. The upper portion remains standing for a while, balanced on its narrowed base, then collapses into clear grains. Within a season—or what passes for a season in the unchanging dark—another spine appears nearby. It is never the same shape. It is always angled toward the city.

The oldest inhabitants are below the tower.

They do not emerge. They may not have bodies in any form that could be carried or seen. Their presence is registered as a pressure in the foundations, a slow alteration in the machinery’s timing. When something shifts beneath the plateau, systems throughout the city brighten, align, and briefly become more exact. Belts correct their speed. Turbines draw breath. The vehicles hold their routes with renewed certainty.

There are records of these events inside the central structure, though the records are not written in words. They are sequences of interruption: patterns made by light, vibration, current, and absence. The markings around the tower’s rim are a visible fragment of the same sequence. Each repeats the others, each breaks at the same point.

The city may have been built to house whatever lies below.

It may have been built to restrain it.

It may have been built by it.

No surviving mechanism answers this directly. The systems that might once have distinguished between maker and prisoner continue to perform both functions at once. They keep the buried presence supplied. They keep the tower sealed. They maintain the factories that produce components for machines no longer in use. They send vehicles over routes leading to destinations whose receiving stations have collapsed into dust.

If the thing below is asleep, the city is its dream made mechanical.

If it is awake, the city is the body of its attention.

And if it is neither, if it has become only a rhythm in the stone, then the inhabitants of the plateau are the ones who continue to give that rhythm a shape. The machines, the animals, the spines, the sealed chambers, the tireless vehicles—all of them participate in a life too slow and distributed to recognize itself as living.

At rare intervals, a door opens.

Not a main door, and not one of the factory gates. A narrow panel somewhere beneath the terraces slides aside with careful precision. Warm darkness spills through the opening. Something passes out: perhaps a maintenance unit, perhaps an animal, perhaps a figure that has not been seen by human eyes for thousands of years.

It does not look around.

It follows the vibration toward the tower, crossing the mineral streets while amber panels brighten faintly overhead. The machines make room for it. The vehicles adjust their routes. Even the spines bend in its direction.

At the rim of the central structure, it stops.

For a long time, nothing happens.

Then the black interior answers with a deeper darkness, and the whole city inclines toward it—not physically, not enough for the eye to detect, but in the timing of its relays, the pressure of its pipes, the synchronized warmth of its walls.

The visitor disappears below.

The door closes.

The factories resume their work, though they never stopped.
