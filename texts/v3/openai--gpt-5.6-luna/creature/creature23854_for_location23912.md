The inhabitants are not one people.

They are a continuity of bodies, machines, colonies, and structures that have learned to live inside one another’s consequences. The city has no population in the ordinary sense. It has occupants, hosts, custodians, passengers, parasites, descendants, and organs. Some of them possess names. Some possess only temperatures. Some are recognized as citizens because their movements alter the future of the basin.

The smallest are everywhere.

They pass through the city in luminous sheets, flexible bodies folding and unfolding around one another without collision. Each is no larger than a hand, though their proportions change according to the district: maintenance swarms become narrow and jointed in the pressure vaults, broad and adhesive near the condensation membranes, almost transparent in the upper terraces. Their shells are soft enough to seal around a leaking pipe and strong enough to scrape mineral growth from a turbine.

No individual remains alone for long.

When separated from its swarm, one of the small creatures dims. It becomes clumsy, loses its sense of direction, and begins to follow gradients it cannot understand: warmth beneath a wall, vibration in a rail, the electrical murmur of a distant reservoir. If it does not rejoin others within several hours, it anchors itself to the nearest surface and enters a state resembling sleep. The city’s systems notice these dim points. Doors open. Maintenance routes alter. A retrieval current draws the creature home.

The swarm is the true animal.

It thinks through pressure, proximity, and light. Its decisions are not spoken but distributed across thousands of nervous bodies. A danger appears first as a disturbance in the air, then as a change in the spacing of neighbors, then as a shape communicated through the whole moving sheet. The swarm can become a tool, a curtain, a bridge, a sensor, or a living seal. It can wrap around a cracked conduit and remain there for years, replacing the lost material with its own slowly hardened tissue.

At night, which is most of the time, the swarms settle onto the walls.

They cover entire facades in rippling layers. Their internal lights pulse at different depths, producing the impression of stars buried beneath black water. Sometimes they all turn toward the same unseen point. No observer has discovered what they are looking at. It may be a pressure front deep beneath the basin. It may be the movement of the mountain. It may be nothing outside themselves.

The creatures in the channels are older in appearance and more difficult to classify.

They are pale because they have no use for light, and flat because the water has shaped them to pass beneath the grates. Their bodies are soft, but their mouths are ringed with mineral teeth. They feed on sediment, dead tissue, chemical films, and the slow accumulations that would otherwise choke the drainage system. Along their backs, the sensory pits open and close like mouths. Through these they read vibration, acidity, current, and the approach of other bodies.

They know the waterworks better than any constructed map.

A channel creature can find a blockage through stone. It can tell whether a retaining wall will hold by tasting the water that seeps through it. When threatened, it does not flee. It folds itself into the channel and becomes almost indistinguishable from the dark flow. When injured, it releases a powder of conductive cells that marks the surrounding infrastructure. Doors nearby remain open until the creature has healed or died.

The city does not bury these dead.

Their bodies are placed in collection basins where the mineral-rich flesh dissolves into the water. What remains is used to line channels, seal cracks, or seed new sensory membranes. The offspring are not born from eggs but from accumulations: a knot of tissue forms around a stone, a valve, or a discarded tool, and over many seasons it begins to move.

The tall basin animals are more solitary than they appear.

The herds stand close together because the wind is information. Each body is an instrument for measuring it. Condensation falling across their translucent sides enters narrow grooves and is sorted by temperature, salt content, and electrical charge. The animals know the weather before it arrives. They know when the upper shelves have begun to melt, when a pressure vent is forming, when a distant district has opened its collection membranes.

They remain motionless because movement would interfere with listening.

Their bodies are all legs, suspended tissue, and dark internal organs. Some carry young within the glassy bundles of their torsos. The young are not born but gradually released, filament by filament, into the air. For several years they cling beneath the adults, drinking condensed water and learning the basin’s signals through contact.

A herd’s long tones are not songs. They are maps.

One animal begins a vibration. Others receive it through their limbs and answer at intervals, establishing the distance between them. The tone travels into the ground and returns changed. From these changes the herd determines whether the mountain is stable, whether a structure has shifted, whether a migration route remains safe. Their calls can last half a day. During that time, nearby machinery reduces its output, bridges lock into place, and transit platforms wait.

No one thinks of this as obedience.

The city is listening too.

In the older districts, there are inhabitants who have no bodies apart from the infrastructure. They live in the pressure arrays, the buried sensor fields, the membranes stretched between towers, and the slow-moving transit systems. They are not artificial intelligences in the old, clean sense. They are accumulations of response: patterns built from years of correcting leaks, predicting collapses, opening passages, and learning which creatures can be moved and which must be left undisturbed.

A reservoir may develop a preference.

A bridge may begin to favor certain kinds of traffic. A district may refuse to extend a road across a particular seam because it has learned that the seam is changing, though no single sensor can explain how. These decisions are transmitted through light pulses, changes in pressure, altered wall textures, and the opening or closing of routes.

The inhabitants understand such behavior as temperament.

Some structures are patient. Some are suspicious. Some are hungry for water and must be restrained. A few are considered young, although they may be larger than neighborhoods and older than any living animal. They are still learning the difference between an individual body and a population.

The city’s more articulate inhabitants inhabit temporary forms.

They assemble themselves from maintenance swarms, articulated shells, mineral cables, and pieces of discarded machinery. A body may be built for a particular task: to enter a flooded shaft, negotiate with a herd, inspect a new fissure, or carry messages between districts where sound has become unreliable. Once the task is complete, the body is dismantled. Its components return to the swarm, the walls, the waterworks.

These beings do not regard this as death.

They remember themselves as patterns. A voice can persist in a valve. A gesture can survive in the alignment of a bridge. A person who spent decades tending a drainage network may return after dissolution as a recurring pulse in the channels, recognized by the creatures beneath the grates.

Some patterns remain stable enough to be called individuals.

They are the walkers of the city: tall, narrow forms with jointed limbs, sealed sensory chambers, and skins that continually bead with water. They move slowly because they are carrying conversations through their bodies. A change in the pressure of one foot may indicate a warning. A flicker under the ribs may ask for passage. Two walkers may stand facing one another for hours while currents of light travel between them.

Their faces are not important. Their surfaces are.

They read the city by touch. They know which walls are alive with swarms, which plates are floating, which pipes carry drinking water, and which carry the mineral slurry used to grow new structures. They can place a hand against a foundation and feel the distant footfalls of a herd. They can kneel beside a channel and distinguish a trapped animal from a blockage by the rhythm of its distress.

They do not own homes. They enter temporary chambers assigned according to pressure, weather, and need. A sleeping chamber may close around them like a shell. While they rest, their bodies are slowly cleaned by small organisms that live in the walls. Their memories are copied into the chamber’s material—not as records, but as tendencies. The room becomes slightly better at receiving them each time they return.

Birth, among these inhabitants, is a matter of invitation.

A new being begins when a system makes room for one. A swarm leaves a portion of itself unclaimed. A wall grows a cavity. A herd changes its pattern to accommodate an unfamiliar signal. A district opens a sealed passage and allows some untested body to enter.

The young are not protected from the city. They are exposed to it immediately.

They learn by being carried through condensation, by sleeping near pressure valves, by feeling the vibrations of transit plates, by following the luminous tracks of feeding swarms. They are taught which sounds mean danger, which kinds of darkness are inhabited, and how to ask the mountain a question without disturbing it.

The oldest rule is taught first:

Do not make the ground decide around you.

Those who violate it are not punished in the manner of courts. Their routes disappear. Their tools cease to function. The city stops carrying their messages. A bridge will not unfold for them. A wall will not open its pores. The creatures that normally clear their way will turn aside.

Exile is not removal from the city.

It is being left alone within it.

Some inhabitants choose this. They retreat into the unfinished zones beyond the last maintained terraces, where the geology is more active and the lights are fewer. There they become difficult to distinguish from the things being born in the mountain. They build no lasting structures. They attach themselves to mineral growths, follow the slow rise of new ridges, and communicate with pressure waves too deep for the inhabited districts to hear.

Others return after many years altered by the margins.

They come back carrying new organs, new silences, or a tolerance for depths where the water is warm. They may no longer understand speech. They may stand for weeks beside a construction site, waiting for the stone to reveal its intention. The city accepts them cautiously. It gives them work in unstable places.

There are no outsiders here for long.

Every arrival is measured by the water it brings, the heat it displaces, the sounds it makes, and the routes it interrupts. A creature that survives these measurements becomes part of the basin’s negotiations. A machine that cannot is dismantled. A building that resists is surrounded until it learns to bend.

Even the mountain has inhabitants.

Deep below the foundations, in chambers not reached by shafts, something moves through the pressure and heat. It is not clear whether this is fauna, geology, or an earlier form of the city. The sensor arrays record it as a pattern of slow contractions. The herds answer its pulses. Mineral fans open when it passes beneath them. Occasionally a whole district shifts its posture in response to a movement that no living observer can feel.

The inhabitants do not speak of this as a god.

A god would be too separate.

They speak of it as the rest of themselves: the portion that has not yet learned to take a shape, the buried population from which all future structures may emerge. They believe the mountain is not merely rising beneath them but becoming aware through them—through the swarms, the channels, the herds, the machines, the bodies that sleep in sealed chambers and wake with water on their skin.

So they continue to live there.

They filter the air. They clear the channels. They carry messages through fog. They wait for bridges to unfold and districts to migrate. They gather beneath the membranes when the weather changes. They place their hands against the wet walls and listen for the answer.

Around them, the city grows by millimeters.

Above them, the herds call.

Below them, the ground is still deciding what it will be, and everything that lives there is part of the decision.
