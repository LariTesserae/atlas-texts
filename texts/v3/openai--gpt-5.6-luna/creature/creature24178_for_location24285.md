They are not a people of the dark.

Darkness, to them, is only the farthest distance the stone can hold. They live by gradients instead: bright and dim, dry and wet, firm and yielding, near and deep. A person who has spent a lifetime in the city can tell the condition of a district from the way light lies across its paving. They can feel a coming flood in the soles of their feet. They know a wall is sick before its shell-strings begin to quiver.

They call themselves the **Measured**, though the word has no exact equivalent in any surface language. It means something like *those who remain in proportion to what surrounds them*. It is not a claim of wisdom. It is a reminder. The city has destroyed too many people who mistook endurance for control.

The Measured are small and long-limbed, built low to the ground. Their bodies have adapted to a world in which falling water, shifting floors, and sudden darkness are more dangerous than cold. Their fingers are broad at the tips and heavily sensitive, able to read minute changes in stone. Their eyes are pale and reflective, protected by a second translucent lid that closes whenever mineral dust or flood spray rises. Most have little use for distance vision. A wall across a basin may be less informative to them than the pressure of the floor beneath one foot.

They wear layered coverings of woven mineral fiber, treated membrane, and soft hide from the drainage animals. The garments are close-fitting at the wrists and ankles, with raised seams that serve as tactile marks. A person can identify a companion by touch in complete darkness: the pattern at the elbow, the knot behind the neck, the arrangement of shell pieces sewn beneath the collar. Their clothing is quiet by design. Loose fabric is dangerous near channels; hard ornaments may strike stone and announce a person’s position to creatures that hunt by vibration.

They do not speak often, though they are not voiceless.

Their language is made of touch, posture, reflected light, and percussion too faint for an outsider to understand. A fingertip dragged across a wall can mean *wait*. Two taps beneath the wrist can mean *water coming*. A hand held flat against the chest means *I have measured it and it is safe enough*. Small mirrors of polished shell are used in the brighter avenues, flashing signals from doorway to doorway. The signals are not simple messages but carefully layered statements: who is calling, from which district, under what pressure, and whether the matter is urgent.

Their silence is not reverence. It is etiquette.

Noise travels badly in the cavern, but vibration travels everywhere. A carelessly dropped stone may disturb an entire foundation. A child’s shriek can bring hunters from a drainage trench or send a herd into panic. The Measured learn early to make their bodies legible without making them loud. Infants are carried against the chest and soothed by pressure rather than song. Children play games in which they must cross a courtyard while communicating only through changes in posture. The best among them can make a whole group turn, kneel, or scatter with a movement of one shoulder.

They are not pale because they lack blood, but because the mineral light has shaped them over generations. Their skin ranges from gray to translucent brown, with faint branching deposits around the joints and along the spine. These deposits are not wounds. They are symbiotic mineral plates, cultivated from infancy and renewed throughout life. In some districts they are allowed to grow naturally; in others they are filed into lines that identify a person’s work, household, or origin. A flood-worker may bear white ridges across the forearms. A foundation-reader may have dark iron seams beneath the eyes. Those who tend the wall membranes wear no plates at all, keeping their skin soft enough to sense the membranes’ contractions.

The city is divided into districts, but the Measured do not think of districts as territories. They are conditions.

One quarter may be known as the Dry Reach, another as the Turning Water, another as the Warm Deep. A person belongs less to a street than to a pattern of obligations. There are those who maintain channels, those who test the walls, those who harvest membranes, those who clear animal passages, those who descend into newly opened shafts, and those who remain in the archives to compare present disturbances with ancient ones.

The most respected are not rulers. They are **listeners**.

Listeners lie against the floor in long, narrow chambers and read the movement beneath the city. They can distinguish a settling wall from a widening void, the pressure of a distant flood from the migration of a herd. They work in pairs, sometimes for hours, with one ear pressed to stone and one hand submerged in a basin. Their findings are recorded as arrangements of grooves, beads, and mineral stains. No one expects a listener to predict the future. The task is more modest and more difficult: to notice what has already begun.

Authority belongs to whoever can make a danger comprehensible.

When a district shifts, people do not wait for an official command. A message moves through the city in the old way: a tremor answered by another tremor, a flash on a wall, a line of white clay drawn across a threshold. Doors open. Tools are lifted. Water vessels are moved uphill. The city responds as if it has one body, but the body is not obedient. It is practiced.

Their households are fluid. A sleeping warren may hold several generations, unrelated workers, two or three permanent guests, and someone who has returned after decades below the plain. Privacy exists, but it is measured in distance rather than walls. A person may claim a recess, a shelf, a pool, or a length of warm stone for a certain period. These claims are marked with temporary arrangements of salt and shell. To remove another’s mark is a serious insult, though not an unforgivable one. The city has little room for permanent possessions.

What they own, they own because it must be carried.

Even their dead are not kept still.

The Measured do not bury their dead in sealed chambers, for the ground may shift and the water may need to pass. The body is cleaned with chalk water, wrapped in mineral cloth, and carried to one of the old drainage galleries. There it is placed where the seasonal currents can reach it. The flesh returns to the basin. The bones are recovered if the water gives them back, polished, and divided among the dead person’s kin and work-companions. Some become tools. Some are set into walls. A few are kept in small cloth bundles beside sleeping places.

To them, this is not desecration. A person who has lived by the exchanges of the deep should not be denied participation in them after death.

Their oldest shrines are not statues but arrangements of material: a basin beneath a cracked pillar, a ring of black stones around a spring, a wall where the membrane withdraws and returns each season. Worship consists of waiting, measuring, and making room. Those who seek an answer may sit beside trembling water until they understand that the water has not answered them.

The Measured have stories, though their stories rarely contain heroes.

They tell of the First Wall, which was built so perfectly that the earth had to tilt in order to escape it. They tell of a woman who followed a flood into the stone and returned carrying a child with no bones. They tell of the Hollow Beasts that once ate only memories, leaving their victims alive but unable to recognize the city. They tell of the first person to hear the Breathing Floor and mistake it for a god. That person, according to the story, was not punished. They were simply asked to listen again.

Children are warned against certainty more often than against monsters.

The monsters are real, but predictable. The larger drainage animals can be avoided. The pale grazers are harmless unless driven from their routes. The ceiling-things are dangerous only during condensation season, when they fall in great numbers and cover the streets like pieces of living cloth. The truly dangerous things are the new things: organisms carried in warm water, pale mouths opening in collapsed shafts, creatures that do not yet understand the old boundaries.

When such a thing appears, the Measured do not immediately kill it.

First they observe whether it drinks, feeds, breeds, or merely waits. Then they determine what it changes. A creature that consumes the harmful membranes may be protected. One that follows floodwater into sleeping warrens is diverted or destroyed. A thing that seems intelligent is given distance, food, and a route out of the inhabited districts.

This caution is not kindness exactly. It is memory. Every useful animal in the city was once unknown.

Their economy is made from what the deep releases. They trade in salt, clay, medicinal organs, membrane sheets, shell tools, mineral dyes, and water that has passed through particular strata. Some springs are famous for carrying warm iron. Others produce a sediment that seals cracks better than any paste. A basin may be empty for a generation and then become the center of the city overnight.

No one asks who owns a spring.

They ask who can reach it safely, who can redirect it without starving another district, and whether the water is bringing something with it.

There are artists among them, though their art is difficult to recognize. They lay patterns of dust where floods will alter them. They carve channels that produce different pressures as water passes. They cultivate membranes into temporary geometries and watch them withdraw. They make instruments from bone and shell that are not played in the ordinary sense; they are placed against walls so the structure’s own movement passes through them and becomes visible in shifting grains of powder.

The finest works are made to disappear.

A design may last until the next condensation fall. A courtyard may be arranged so that a flood traces an ancient symbol before draining away. A musician may build a chamber in which the pressure of the cavern changes the color of suspended mineral films. The Measured consider permanence an imperfect form of art. Anything that survives unchanged has perhaps not been properly offered to the world.

Yet they are capable of longing for what does not last.

In the vacant quarters, people sometimes enter sealed houses and sit among the old thresholds. They touch worn inscriptions with their fingertips. They collect objects from collapsed rooms: a child’s hook, a broken vessel, a tablet whose marks no one can read. These are not relics in a formal religious sense. They are evidence that absence was once occupied.

The city’s emptiness troubles them more than its dangers.

A flood can be measured. A shifting floor can be marked. But the abandoned districts contain no instruction about whether the people who left were wise, frightened, or simply too tired to continue. Some say the sealed quarters belong to the dead. Others say they belong to those who have not yet returned. No one opens a slab without recording the date, the names of the witnesses, and the direction in which the first dust falls.

Beyond the city, on the Breathing Floor, the Measured become quieter still.

They travel there in groups carrying braces, water, lamps of reflective mineral, and long cords marked with knots. They do not build permanent camps. They sleep on raised platforms and move them whenever the cracks change. They gather deposits, rescue animals from fissures, and study the black ridges for signs of pressure. Sometimes they find the remains of older settlements beneath the plain: roads leading nowhere, chambers filled with smooth stones, enormous bones that have become part of the floor.

Sometimes they find passages going downward.

The people who enter those passages are not called explorers. They are called **borrowers**, because no one believes the deep can be entered except on loan. Before descending, they leave an object at the mouth of the shaft: a tool, a plate, a personal mark, something that proves the passage has taken a person and that the city is waiting to see whether it gives them back.

Some return changed by heat, mineral growth, or silence.

Some return carrying living things.

Some do not return, and after a suitable interval their objects are moved to a shrine.

For all their caution, the Measured are not resigned. They are industrious, inventive, and occasionally reckless. They repair walls because walls are useful. They open old roads because knowledge is a form of shelter. They redirect water not to conquer it, but to make one more season possible. Their city is broad and low because they have learned not to oppose the ceiling, but they continue to build because yielding is not the same as surrender.

At any hour, beneath the white seams of stone, they are moving.

A worker kneels beside a trembling basin and lowers a cord into it. A child presses both hands to a wall and feels a distant collapse arrive as a shiver. Two traders exchange salt for a sealed organ without speaking. A herd passes through a vacant avenue, its many feet whispering against mineral film. Above them, a folded creature releases its grip and drops through the brightness.

Then, somewhere under the floor, the pressure changes.

The listeners turn.

The workers lift their heads.

Across the city, people begin to carry stones—not in panic, not in prayer, but in recognition of the old obligation. The world has shifted. Something has opened. Water is looking for a course.

And the inhabitants, who have never mastered their home, begin again to make themselves ready for it.
