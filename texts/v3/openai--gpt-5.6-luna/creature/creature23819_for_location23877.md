What lives there is not the predator, though the predator is the first thing the smaller animals notice.

What lives there is a small, unfinished animal that has made itself from the hollow’s uncertainties.

It is usually found beneath the largest pool, where the stone is warm on one side and cold on the other. At first it looks like a pale, many-jointed creature curled around itself. Its limbs are thin as rootlets, its body almost transparent. Through its skin, organs drift slowly from place to place: a blue thread becomes a heart, a gold fleck becomes an eye, a pink smear gathers into a mouth and then forgets the shape.

It has no fixed number of legs. When it is frightened, it grows more of them. When it is calm, some withdraw into its ribs, where they remain as faint lines of light. It can stand on the floor, the wall, the underside of the ceiling, or on the surface of a hanging drop. It does not climb. It simply decides that the next place is beneath it.

The creature is called nothing. Names do not remain attached to it. If one is spoken nearby, it wears the name for a little while, mistaking it for a shell. Then the name slips loose and fastens itself to a stone, a moth, or the sound of condensation.

It feeds on changes.

A new crack in the wall sustains it for a day. The first movement of a root through mineral gives it enough strength to become visible. It drinks the instant before a drop falls, and eats the darkness left behind when a moth has taken a piece of it. It is especially fond of things that have almost happened: an egg that never opens, a seed that falls sideways, a path that ends before it becomes a trail.

Because of this, the hollow is always making more of what it needs.

The pale rootlets tremble for it. The upside-down moss releases its seeds toward the seams where it sleeps. The arranged stones shift by imperceptible degrees, not to imprison it but to give it somewhere to return to. The small animals know it as a harmless presence and make their nests near the warmth of its body. They bring it fragments: a mineral bead, a shed skin, a wing, a grain of grit marked by three parallel scratches. It accepts these offerings by passing through them. Afterward, the things are changed. A stone may remember being an egg. A wing may cast the shadow of a tree.

It does not hunt the transparent creatures, but it sometimes causes them to disappear. When one has lived too long in one shape, it touches the animal with one of its fine limbs, and the animal becomes briefly uncertain. Its legs forget which way is down. Its organs loosen. Its outline opens like a door. Then it slips into a crack, not dead, but no longer limited to the body it had been using.

The black predator avoids the creature.

Not from fear, exactly. The predator has no eyes to fear losing, and the creature has no body that could be cleanly bitten. But when the black shape approaches, the small inhabitant becomes very still and very bright. It shines from inside the gaps between its organs. The entire cavity gathers around that light. Pools lift toward it. Roots turn in the stone. The predator’s lane ends at the creature’s feet.

For a moment, the two seem to recognize one another.

Then the hollow becomes larger than either of them.

The creature is the reason the place cannot settle into being a cave. It is always loosening the walls, teaching stone to behave like water, teaching water to remember distance. It moves through the cavity at night—or at whatever interval passes for night there—touching unfinished surfaces with its many hands. Wherever it rests, a new possibility begins: a corridor inside a pebble, a forest reflected in a shallow pool, a ceiling that has not yet decided whether it is solid.

The crescent of stones is its dwelling, though it is never inside it in the same way twice. Sometimes it curls among the stones like a sleeping animal. Sometimes the stones stand around an empty center, and its breathing is the low pulse entering directly into the ear. Sometimes the arrangement is a little house, complete with a door too small for anything that lives there. Sometimes it is a mouth.

It has been building the house for longer than the hollow has existed.

Or the house has been building the creature.

When approached, it does not flee. It watches from the edge of vision, where its body borrows the colors of the walls. If looked at directly, it becomes less distinct: a glimmer, a pale joint, a folded frond, the suggestion of an animal beneath a stone. If ignored, it comes close.

It will place one cool limb against a person’s hand.

For an instant, the person remembers a place that has not been visited: a vast underground country beneath the tiny cavity, filled with trees, running water, and slow-moving stars. They understand that the hollow is not small. It is only holding itself small. They understand that the creature is not growing.

It is remembering how.
