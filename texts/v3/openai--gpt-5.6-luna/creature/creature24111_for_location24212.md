The inhabitants are not a people in the usual sense. They are a maintenance organism with many bodies.

Some of them look almost human at a distance. They walk upright along the polished rootways, carry tools, wear belts of ceramic knives and copper clamps, and gather beneath lamps to exchange gestures. Up close, the resemblance breaks down. Their skin is soft and darkened by sap, their joints slightly too numerous, their fingers ending in blunt rootlets that divide and rejoin as needed. They have no hair. Fine green filaments lie beneath their skin, glowing faintly when they are frightened or working. Water beads on them and is not wiped away; it is absorbed.

They are called, by themselves, the Tenders—or something that means the same thing, though their word is made partly of clicks in the throat and partly of changes in skin color.

A Tender may spend an entire day repairing a conveyor, then sleep with its back pressed against the machinery so that the machine can continue drawing heat from its body. Another may crawl into a flowering chimney to clear a blockage, emerging hours later with its lungs full of red pollen and its thoughts temporarily altered. They do not consider these sacrifices. The distinction between labor and feeding, between injury and pruning, between tool and limb, has never been firm among them.

Their clothing is practical and grown. Sleeveless coats are woven from tough leaf-fiber and sealed with resin. Gloves sprout gripping pads when exposed to moisture. Masks are made from folded bark and translucent membrane, each one shaped to the breathing pattern of its wearer. The oldest Tenders wear no garments at all except harnesses, tags, and small metal containers. Their bodies have become too heavily modified to be mistaken for the original stock: extra arms for splicing, sensory fronds along the neck, storage sacs beneath the ribs, blunt antlers used to anchor themselves during root-storms.

They have no faces in the same sense either. Their features are mobile arrangements of valves, lenses, and soft tissue. A Tender’s eyes may close sideways like leaves. Their mouths are narrow vents suited to speaking in the damp air, and many of them possess a second mouth in the chest for taking in nutrient paste, fungal slurry, or whatever the city provides. Emotion is shown by posture, by the opening of colored membranes, by the pattern of light moving beneath the skin.

They do not often speak aloud. Sound is too easily lost in the factories, and too many creatures have learned to imitate voices. Instead they touch walls, rails, pipes, and one another. A palm laid against a root can carry a message through the living structure: a pressure change, a pulse of heat, a chemical signal. A worker touching the side of a fermentation tank may know that a child has fallen into a lower passage, that a valve is sick, that a hunting animal has entered the east quarter, and that someone in the sleeping chambers is dreaming of fire.

The city informs them, but it does not command them exactly. Its warnings arrive as tastes in the mouth, tremors in the bones, or sudden compulsions to turn left. The Tenders have learned to distinguish a true alarm from the ordinary impulses of the root-bulb: hunger, thirst, irritation, reproductive season, the slow rearrangement of interior passages. They are not always successful. Sometimes an entire family wakes during the night and walks silently into a wall because the wall has begun to flower. Sometimes a worker remains too long beside an open root chamber and returns with new memories that belong to something older than the settlement.

Children are born in the nursery pools.

The pools lie beneath the western growth, where the roots are warmest. There, pale sacs hang from braided branches, each one veined with green and amber. The young emerge small, wet, and many-limbed, with translucent skin and sealed eyes. They spend their first years in the care of whatever will hold them: adults, nurse-animals, fungal mats, low machines, sometimes the roots themselves. A child may sleep in the crook of a hauling beast or inside an empty gearbox. No one considers this unusual. Familiarity is measured by shared heat, not by blood.

The young are taught the city’s prohibitions before they are taught names.

They learn which moss means safe water and which moss means the water has begun to think. They learn to hear the difference between a machine straining and a machine calling. They learn never to climb a wall whose pores are open, never to step over a line of yellow fungus, never to disturb the small animals when they move in a single direction. They are given broken tools and allowed to repair harmless machinery. The first successful splice is celebrated. The first unnecessary cutting is mourned.

There are no rulers visible in the streets. Authority belongs to the oldest systems.

The Root-Mothers—or the deep councils, or simply the warm ones—are buried beneath the city, where the root-bulb thickens around ancient chambers. They are too large to move. Some have become indistinguishable from the infrastructure: a swollen mass beneath a factory floor, a wall that contracts when spoken to, a crown of blind flowers through which decisions pass. The Tenders consult them by opening channels in their own bodies and pressing themselves against the living wood. In return, the Root-Mothers provide water, hormones, heat, and instructions whose origins have been forgotten.

No one agrees whether the Root-Mothers are individuals, ancestors, or organs of the greater thing above and below them. This disagreement is not dangerous. The city has room for several truths, provided they do not interfere with drainage.

The Tenders are accompanied by animals that serve no simple category. A household may include a lamp-moth colony, two wall-climbing grazers, a nest of electrical birds, and a long blind creature that sleeps beneath the floorboards. These companions are not owned. They are remembered. A beast that has lived near a family for thirty years may be addressed as an aunt, a colleague, or a recurring weather pattern. When such an animal dies, its body is carried to the processing district, where its useful materials are extracted. The bones may return as tools. The hide may become a belt. The nervous tissue may be fed to the root. Grief is not the refusal of this process; grief is attending it.

The industrial workers are the most visible inhabitants, but there are others.

There are the Root-Runners, who travel through passages too narrow for adults and carry messages in sealed sacs beneath their tongues. There are the Rain-Keepers, who climb into the ceiling growth to regulate the valve-flowers and spend their lives with their faces turned upward into falling water. There are the Quiet Ones, who maintain the abandoned districts and remove anything that has begun growing in the wrong direction. Their bodies are covered in white scar tissue from years of exposure to corrosive sap.

There are hunters who follow the large animals into the outer tunnels. Their tools are not spears but scents, lights, and changes in pressure. They coax predators away from crowded streets and lure useful creatures toward feeding grounds. Some return with trophies. Some return carrying eggs. Some return with a new gait and refuse to explain where they have been.

There are also those who live entirely below the city, in the soil between its roots. They do not use roads. They move through loose earth in smooth, coordinated swarms, surfacing only during weather events. The Tenders leave offerings for them: salt, warm blood, copper filings, spoiled fruit. In exchange, the soil remains fertile and the foundations do not collapse. No one has seen their central body. A few claim that the whole dark ground is their body, and that the city is merely a thought it has been having for several centuries.

At night—if the dimming of the lamps can be called night—the streets grow quieter but never still. Workers curl inside hollow stems. Families gather in warm chambers, touching wrists and ankles to share heat. Machinery continues to turn beneath them. Animals shift in the upper growth. The plants draw water from the deep.

A Tender asleep against a root may dream in images that are not its own: sunlight falling across leaves, the pressure of a vast exterior wind, the slow passage of centuries above the ground. It may wake with dirt beneath its nails and the certainty that something has moved in the dark.

Then it will rise.

It will check the pipes, inspect the nearest animal, clear the drainage grate, and place one hand against the wall.

The wall will pulse back.
