The people of the basin are difficult to notice at first.

The city announces its animals, its water, its stone. People are quieter things by comparison, moving in covered passages and under low roofs, wrapped in colors that do not contrast with the walls. Their clothing is layered not against cold but against wetness, abrasion, and the persistent grit carried through the air. They wear close-fitting caps over their ears, thick collars around their throats, and overlapping aprons of hide or woven fiber that shed water into the gutters. In the lowest districts, they bind their calves and forearms with strips of treated skin to keep the channel creatures from fastening onto them.

They are a compact, heavy-handed people. Life in the basin favors bodies that can brace themselves against slick stone, carry awkward loads, and remain steady while the ground trembles beneath them. Their feet are broad and callused. Their fingers are scarred, often stained black from mineral work or red from clay. Many have small ridges of hardened skin along the cheekbones and temples, the result of generations spent in a damp atmosphere full of suspended grit. Their eyes are large and dark, accustomed to seeing movement in poor light. Their hair is usually cut short or bound tightly, since loose strands are forever catching in cords, tools, animal teeth, and wet masonry.

They do not speak much, even when they can.

Children learn the city first through their hands. A palm pressed against a wall tells them whether a passage is safe to enter. Two fingers against the wrist mean wait. A thumb drawn across the knuckles means the water is rising. A touch to the back of the neck is a warning to duck beneath a low beam. Public life is conducted in gestures so old that the signs have become more formal than speech. Traders use fans of colored reeds. Porters wear bright strips on their shoulders to indicate what they are carrying and whether it may be approached. At crossings, a person with a red shutter or white flag can halt an entire lane of beasts without making a sound.

Their language survives, but mostly in close rooms, beneath hoods, and face to face. It has a compressed quality, full of short hard syllables and words that distinguish between kinds of water, kinds of stone, kinds of pressure. There are separate terms for a wall that is merely wet and one that is sweating from an internal seep. There are names for currents that look still, for mud that will hold a foot, and for pools that contain something patient beneath the surface.

They do not say that they are deafened by the city. They say they are surrounded.

Every child receives instruction in reading vibration. Infants are placed on stone slabs above the underground channels so that they learn the ordinary pulses of the house. A parent can feel, through the floor, whether a familiar animal is moving below, whether a cistern is filling, whether a bridge has begun to loosen at one of its joints. Sudden silence is feared more than noise. A gutter that stops running, a roof colony that does not stir, a house whose familiar tremor has vanished—these are signs of blockage, collapse, sickness, or something newly arrived.

The people have made a civilization from paying attention.

Their homes are dark, crowded, and intensely ordered. Nothing is left where a falling object or a running animal can take it. Tools hang from cords in exact positions. Food is stored in sealed clay vessels above the level of known floods. Every household keeps a water ledger, a list of animal debts, a record of repairs, and a set of emergency shutters whose meanings are known to neighbors as well as family.

They sleep on raised platforms beneath the roofs or in chambers cut into the higher shelves of stone. The dead are not buried. There is too much water, too much traffic beneath the ground, and too many hungry mouths. Bodies are cleaned, wrapped in mineral cloth, and placed in designated channels beyond the lake, where the basin’s animals remove them. The bones that remain are recovered when the water permits. Some are set into plaster. Some become handles, needles, hooks, or markers. The most important are kept in family walls, where their pale shapes show through the damp surface.

This is not regarded as desecration. Nothing in the basin is wasted, and the dead are not imagined as absent from the city. A grandmother’s finger bone may support the lid of a cooking pot. A child’s tooth may be set into the threshold. A mason may use powdered ancestral bone to strengthen a wall. The dead continue as material, which is one of the few forms of permanence the people trust.

Their families are not small. A household may contain several generations, apprentices, bonded workers, animal keepers, and unrelated sleepers who have paid for a dry corner. Privacy is rare and therefore valuable. It is created through curtains, partitions, changes in light, and rules about who may touch whom. To enter a person’s sleeping space without permission is a serious offense. To touch the back of the neck is intimate. To place one’s forehead against another’s is a promise, a greeting, or a farewell depending on the length of contact.

Children belong partly to their parents and partly to the city. They are claimed by the work they learn: channel-reading, shell cutting, animal handling, clay firing, load hauling, roof repair, water counting. A child who shows an unusual tolerance for the lower channels may be given to the water keepers. One who can distinguish distant vibrations becomes a bridge watcher. One who remembers signal sequences is taught the shutter code and may spend a lifetime coordinating traffic from a raised platform.

The city has no profession more respected than maintenance.

Its highest officials are not kings but position-keepers: people responsible for remembering where a barrier belongs, how high a flood may rise, which bridge stones have shifted, and which animal pens must be emptied before the channels choke. Their authority is practical and severe. They carry tablets of fired clay impressed with levels, routes, ownership marks, and old failures. They are expected to know not only what is currently true but what has remained true for generations.

The people distrust invention that cannot be repaired.

A new tool is accepted only after it has survived immersion, dropping, misuse, and the attention of hungry animals. A clever mechanism that depends on a rare material is considered childish unless its principle can be explained with stone, cord, weight, heat, and water. They admire improvements that make a familiar thing last longer. A hinge that opens silently. A basket that drains more quickly. A blade that can be resharpened without losing its shape. A roof tile that tells, by the way it cracks, where pressure is gathering.

Their greatest works are not monuments but corrections.

They build channels that divide around foundations. They raise streets by the thickness of a hand. They alter the slope of a courtyard so that waste, rain, runoff, and blood all travel toward different grates. They place shallow bowls beneath drips and move them by a finger’s width when the basin’s levels change. A whole neighborhood may be transformed by the careful relocation of a single stone.

They are not afraid of the animals, though they respect what the animals know.

The city’s creatures are named by behavior rather than appearance. A beast may be called the Underdoor, the Silt-Eater, the Roof Cloud, the Patient Mouth, or the One That Waits Beneath Warm Water. Some are property. Some are kin to a household. Some belong to no one and may be killed if they cross a marked line. Others are protected because they perform necessary work, and harming one brings a fine even if it was trespassing.

Children are taught never to assume that an animal is empty-headed. A blind scavenger may know a building’s hidden exits better than its owner. A filter-feeder may predict a blockage days before the water changes color. Roof creatures flee certain minerals and gather near others. The people observe these things with the patience of those who cannot afford to misunderstand their environment.

There are animal marriages, animal funerals, and animal thefts. A hauling beast can be inherited, ransomed, or condemned. A communal tank may have an appointed keeper whose duty is to learn the temper of every filter-feeder inside it. Some animals are marked with clay bands or bone tags. Some are left unmarked because ownership would be an insult, or because no one can keep them from returning to the same roof.

The people eat nearly everything that moves.

They preserve meat in salt clay, smoke it over mineral coals, ferment it in sealed jars, or feed it live into communal pits where it can be collected later. Pale channel animals are split and hung from racks. Winged creatures are trapped in roof nets and stripped of their thin membranes. The largest beasts are not slaughtered casually; they are used until age or injury makes them unfit to haul, then rendered down with ceremony. Their bones become beams, their hides become doors, their teeth become tools, and their fat burns in lamps.

There is little shame in hunger. Waste is shameful. Carelessness is shameful. A person who lets usable shell go into the drain is judged more harshly than one who loses an argument or breaks a tool.

Their religion, if it can be separated from their habits, is directed toward continuance.

They do not worship the sky. The sky gives them almost nothing. They do not worship trees, fields, or distant horizons. Their sacred places are points of persistence: an old spring that has never failed, a wall that has survived three collapses, a channel whose course has remained unchanged since the first houses were raised. Shrines are built around drips, seams, warm stones, animal nesting places, and fractures that have not widened.

A shrine may consist of a shallow niche, a lamp protected by clay, and a row of wet fingerprints renewed each morning.

Their prayers resemble maintenance instructions. Water, remain within your bed. Stone, hold beneath us. Fire, consume only what is given. Animal, know your passage. House, remember your shape.

Some of their oldest stories tell of a time before the basin was occupied, but not of a golden age. They do not imagine a lost wilderness. In their stories, the world emerged already full of water, mineral, and appetite. The first people were not builders who conquered a hostile land; they were things that found shelter inside an unfinished place and learned which parts would hold.

They believe the world is still being made beneath them.

This belief explains their reluctance to build too high or too far outward. A new shelf may rise. A buried channel may open. A ridge may lift another hand’s breadth. The ground is not dead matter but an ongoing arrangement, and any structure that insists too strongly on its own permanence is considered arrogant. Buildings are expected to hold, but not to claim that they will always hold.

The people themselves are equally provisional.

They do not mark age by birthdays. They mark it by changes in responsibility: the first time a child is trusted near a channel, the first load carried alone, the first dead body prepared, the first flood level remembered without help. A person’s name may change after a major repair, a dangerous crossing, a surviving illness, or the birth of a child. Names are often drawn from conditions rather than virtues. Still-Water. Third Door. Clay Under Nail. Narrow Passage. Warm Below. One Who Watches the Eastern Shutters.

There are no portraits. Faces are too transient, and light too poor. People are remembered by working gestures, scars, tools, debts, and the places where they stood.

When strangers arrive from the outer settlements, they are not welcomed with cheers. They are watched. Their animals are counted. Their feet are examined for unfamiliar mud. Their tools are held up to the dim light. The basin’s people do not ask first where the strangers came from. They ask which way the water runs there, what creatures live under their floors, what materials their dead return as, and whether their walls remain standing after a hundred years.

If the answers are acceptable, a space is found for them.

There is always a space, though not necessarily a comfortable one. The city is crowded, but it is built to absorb another body: a sleeping ledge, a platform above a workshop, a corner beside a tank, a place beneath a stair where the vibrations are tolerable. Occupancy is not an accident. It is the city’s moral condition. An empty room is suspicious. An unused channel is dangerous. A roof without a nest may mean poison, absence, or a change no one has yet understood.

So the people keep filling the basin.

They carry clay, meat, shell, lamps, children, wet bundles, sleeping animals, and the remains of the dead. They move along raised stones while water pours around their ankles. They lean close to exchange signs. They stop at shutters and wait for the pattern to finish. They crouch to examine a crack no wider than a hair. They sleep under the continuous trembling of the city and wake when a familiar vibration changes.

At a distance they seem almost indistinguishable from the things they have built. Their garments are dark. Their tools are pale and mineral. Their faces appear briefly in doorways, then vanish behind curtains or sheets of falling water. They bend beneath the same low roofs where animals cling and pipes drip. They leave wet handprints on walls that may preserve them for centuries.

They have not mastered the basin.

They have made themselves one of its enduring arrangements: another layer of occupation between the stone and the water, another kind of creature moving through the channels, maintaining, feeding, signaling, breeding, carrying, and being carried onward.
