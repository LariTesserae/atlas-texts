What lives there is not one species, but an entire patience of lives.

The smallest things are the most numerous. Bacteria and mineral-feeding films cover every wet surface, making thin iridescent skins over stone, pipe, mud, and bone. They gather around warm outlets and the undersides of submerged platforms, where the water carries dissolved metals and food from the machinery above. Their colonies spread in dull reds, yellow-greens, and bruised blacks, though the colors are usually hidden beneath water or a coat of silt. They are the first inhabitants of every new crack and the last to leave anything that has not yet been buried.

The roots of the pale shoots belong to this world as much as the plants themselves. They run through the mud in branching, tangled mats, finding warmth and the faint nutrients carried from drains and animal nests. Their stems are nearly transparent, their leaves thin scales that tremble whenever a pump starts. They do not grow toward the sun. They grow toward whatever leaks.

Small shelled creatures graze on the films. Some resemble snails, though their shells are soft and translucent, and others are little armored disks that cling to the sides of channels. They move so slowly that they seem fixed in place until one is seen in two different positions. Their feeding leaves pale trails through the sediment. When the water rises, they close themselves and wait, each one holding a small, private reserve of air.

The mud is full of worms.

Some are blind and threadlike, living in the warm upper layers where organic waste settles. Others are thick, jointed things with blunt heads and many short legs, able to burrow through clay that would stop a larger animal. They eat roots, insects, dead fish, and one another. At night—though night is only a lowering of the lamps—they emerge in glistening knots around the drainage outlets. The squat mammals come to dig them out.

Those mammals have broad paws and low bodies built for wet ground. Their fur is dark along the back and almost silver underneath, where it is constantly slick with condensation. They live in spaces beneath platforms, inside abandoned sheds, and among the warm pipes of the pumping stations. They have learned to make nests from insulation, cable sheathing, moss, and strips of rotted conveyor belt. Their young are born blind and pink, then become dark-furred within weeks.

They are not domesticated, but they are accustomed to machines.

A pump beginning its cycle sends them under cover. A valve releasing pressure draws them toward the runoff. They know which pipes become hot, which grates shake loose insects, and which human walkways are safe to cross. They rarely climb high, because the upper platforms are cold and exposed. They prefer the basin floor, where every surface offers food and the water hides their tracks.

They live in family groups, though the families are loose and temporary. Several adults may share one nest during a cold spell, then separate when the warmest banks become crowded. They communicate through short whistles, taps of claw against metal, and scent rubbed into the mud. Their warning calls are often drowned by the machinery, but they have adapted to vibration. A tremor through the ground can mean danger more reliably than a sound.

The frogs occupy the flooded hollows. They are pale beneath, dark above, and mottled with the colors of wet gravel. Some have eyes that are little more than cloudy beads. Others have retained large, reflective eyes suited to the lamps. They call from beneath platforms and inside culverts, their voices overlapping into a continuous throaty weather. When insects swarm around a lamp, the frogs climb the concrete walls after them, gripping mineral-crusted surfaces with wide, adhesive toes.

Their eggs gather in ropes beneath the water. The tadpoles feed in clouds, turning shallow pools dark with movement. Many are eaten before they become frogs. Those that survive leave the water only briefly, spending most of their lives in mud, runoff, and condensation.

The fish are smaller than they ought to be. They live in channels too narrow for anything large, feeding on larvae, films, and fragments carried from one chamber to another. Their bodies are pale and nearly transparent, their spines visible when they turn in the water. In the deepest drainage runs, where lamps are absent, some have lost their eyes entirely. They navigate by pressure and taste, sensing the approach of a footfall through the water before the person above knows they are there.

The eels are longer and more secretive. They occupy the cracks beneath the basin, where water moves through stone with no visible surface. They emerge during disturbances: when a trench fills, when a support collapses, when a machine changes its rhythm. Their skin is smooth and colorless, and they can force themselves through openings no wider than a finger. They feed on fish, worms, drowned insects, and anything small enough to swallow whole.

Above them, the insects own the air.

They hatch in the warmest pools and gather around every working lamp. Their wings are soft from the humidity. Some live only for a few hours, long enough to mate and lay eggs in the mineral film along a wall. Others are tougher, beetle-like things that spend months in the insulation of old machinery. They crawl through control panels, die inside light housings, and collect in the corners of windows that can no longer be opened.

The bats feed on these swarms.

Their colonies occupy the unfinished vaults, though the ceiling is too damp for ideal roosting. They hang from exposed seams, cable bundles, and rough shelves of stone, packed together in dense, shifting masses. Their fur carries the smell of minerals and guano. They are roused by changes in sound more than by light. When a conveyor belt starts or a pump shudders, thousands of them release their grip at once and wheel through the vapor.

For a moment, the basin fills with wings.

They do not fly gracefully in the open spaces. The air is too thick, and the echoes are too confused. They move by rapid, precise pulses of sound, mapping railings, pipes, lamps, and one another. Their calls vanish into the machinery, but the machinery does not prevent them from living. It has become part of the landscape they know.

There are also larger things that are seen rarely: pale salamanders in the flooded trenches, long-legged spiders beneath the walkways, birds that have flown in through some forgotten shaft and never learned to leave. A few snakes follow the drainage channels after the frogs. Mold feeds on the dead. Beetles bury what the water leaves behind. Every carcass is claimed almost immediately.

Nothing here lives apart from the industrial remains. The pipes provide heat. The lamps provide insects. The grates collect food. The abandoned sheds provide shelter. The pumps create currents that carry eggs, larvae, spores, and waste into new parts of the basin. Even collapse becomes habitat. A fallen wall makes a dry ledge. A submerged cart becomes a reef. Mineral growth around a bolt forms a pocket where tiny animals lay their eggs.

The creatures do not understand the works, but they have learned its rhythms. They know the length of a cycle, the delay before a valve opens, the tremor that precedes a belt starting. They sleep through some noises and flee from others. Over generations, the basin has ceased to be a place they merely inhabit. Its machinery, mud, water, and stone have become the conditions of their bodies.

They are warm-blooded, cold-blooded, rooted, shelled, winged, microscopic, and blind. They cling, burrow, swim, roost, root, and wait. They are born in runoff and die beneath lamps. Their lives are short beside the columns forming in the dark, but long enough to establish routes, territories, and habits.

And beneath all of them, unseen in the waterlogged ground, the roots and worms and films continue to spread.

The basin is occupied because everything in it has found somewhere to begin.
