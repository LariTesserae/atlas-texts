The inhabitants are not visitors to the mountain. They are its unfinished thoughts.

They call themselves, when they call themselves anything, the **Makers Below**. No two of them have the same body, because a body in the pocket mountain is not a settled thing. One may begin as a person-shaped arrangement of clay, then acquire a copper hand, a root-filled chest, and a face borrowed from the wall beside which it sleeps. Another may have no body at all for several days, existing as a set of footprints, a voice in a pipe, or a shadow that keeps climbing after its owner has stopped.

They are small only by comparison with the world outside. Most stand no higher than a forearm, though some are narrow as cords and others broad, squat, and blocklike, made to brace collapsing terraces. They move sideways along walls, across ceilings, and through the undersides of stairs. Their joints are tied with root or hinged with thin copper. Their skin may be fired red like pottery, porous as pumice, translucent like wet leaves, or black and reflective as volcanic glass. In the warmest chambers, they are soft-bodied and green-veined. In the coldest, they move with the weight and patience of masonry.

Their faces are rarely complete.

A Maker may have eyes without a mouth, a mouth without a nose, or a smooth blank surface that reflects whoever is looking at it. Eyes are not always located in the head. Some see through beads of water strung beneath bridges. Some see through the pale skinks. Some have only a dark opening in the center of the palm and navigate by pressing that opening to a wall.

They recognize one another by pressure, vibration, and smell. A familiar Maker is known by the way it leans against a stair, the pattern of its footsteps in mortar, or the small change in temperature that occurs when it enters a room. Names are dangerous in the mountain. A spoken name can become a passage, and a passage cannot always remember who it was made for. So the inhabitants use temporary names: **Three-Blows**, **Wet Shoulder**, **The One Under Copper**, **Small Root**, **Already Here**.

They are builders, though construction is not work to them in the human sense. It is closer to breathing, or to growing a second skeleton outside the body.

They lay stone because stone is asking to become a wall. They cut channels because water has begun imagining a route. They plant ferns in niches not to decorate the terraces but to give the dampness somewhere to gather. Their tools are extensions of their bodies. A wooden lever may be held by three Makers at once, each contributing a different kind of strength: one weight, one direction, one memory of where the stone ought to have been.

They do not lift the largest blocks. They persuade them.

A Maker places its forehead against a warm slab and listens until it discovers which way the slab wants to fall. Another presses a clay seal into wet mortar and whispers a previous arrangement into it. Several stand in a circle around a gap, holding their measuring cords taut, until the gap develops a reason to be a doorway. Sometimes the work succeeds. Sometimes the mountain changes its mind and incorporates the workers into the completed structure. This is not considered death. It is called becoming useful.

The missing builders are everywhere.

A stair may contain the long, patient spine of one. A retaining wall may murmur with another’s memory. A copper rain tongue may turn toward footsteps because the Maker who forged it is still listening through the metal. In certain passages, the bricks become warm when someone speaks of leaving. In others, a handprint appears beneath the surface and slowly follows travelers from one end of the wall to the other.

The Makers do not have homes. They have **positions**.

One belongs to a particular basin, one to a junction of three channels, one to the lower face of a tower that has not yet been completed. They sleep wherever their substance is most stable. Clay-bodied Makers sink into damp alcoves and harden there overnight. Root-bodied ones lower themselves into planter boxes and wake with leaves growing from their shoulders. Those made of slate lie flat against the paving until their edges cannot be distinguished from the surrounding stones.

Their sleep is not still. Even asleep, they continue to make small adjustments. A sleeping Maker may extend a finger into a crack and widen it by morning. It may dream a new landing into existence, or exchange the positions of two doors. Sometimes an entire terrace shifts a few inches while all its inhabitants are resting, and they wake without surprise, already knowing where the new walls have placed them.

They eat materials rather than food.

Clay is chewed and returned as tile. Charcoal is swallowed to darken the inner chambers of the body. Copper filings give a Maker the ability to hear distant water. Moss is pressed beneath the tongue to learn what has happened in shaded places. The strongest ones consume fragments of unfinished masonry, which makes them heavier, more deliberate, and increasingly difficult to distinguish from the mountain itself.

They drink from the rising overflow of the lowest basin. This water is not ordinary water. It contains impressions of every vessel it has touched and carries those shapes within it. A Maker who drinks deeply may temporarily acquire a bowl-shaped chest, a narrow-necked throat, or the hollow geometry of a pipe. The basin is where the newly formed are first seen.

The small figures surrounding it are not statues.

They are dormant inhabitants.

Each is waiting for a direction.

The salt figures will become those who preserve boundaries. The clay ones will become doors, walls, and caretakers of rooms. The charcoal figures will enter the shadows and carry messages through them. The copper figures will hear the mountain’s deeper movements. The figures made of leaves will be the first to grow beyond the shape intended for them.

They have no faces because a face is a destination, and none of them has reached one yet.

When the mountain shifts, they turn together because they are listening to the same unfinished instruction. One day, perhaps, they will turn toward the entrance. More likely they will turn toward the summit, where the oldest inhabitants live.

The summit-dwellers are seldom seen. Their voices arrive through walls before their footsteps do. They are larger than the lower Makers, though not necessarily in body. One may occupy a whole tower, appearing only as a change in its masonry. Another may be distributed through the roots of an entire garden. They are the ones who decide—if deciding is the right word—which rules will hold for the next interval of the mountain’s growth.

The lower inhabitants fear them, but not as people fear rulers. They fear them as a seed fears the shape of the tree.

At intervals, a summit-dweller sends down an instruction. It comes as three blows on stone. The Makers stop what they are doing. Water pauses. Vines draw tight. The unfinished figures at the basin turn. Then the instruction passes through the mountain, and construction resumes in a new direction.

No Maker has reached the highest summit and returned unchanged. Those who climb too far lose their separate bodies. They return as weather, or as a new kind of stair, or as a sensation of being watched from within the walls. Some become the cries heard above the highest terraces. Some become the distant crowd beneath an empty staircase. Some remain as a single warm patch of mortar that never cools.

Still, the inhabitants climb.

They climb because the mountain is not merely where they live. It is what they are becoming. Every new ledge offers them another possible shape. Every wall is a boundary they may inhabit, cross, or turn into a passage. Their lives are measured not by years but by completed surfaces: the laying of a course, the opening of a drain, the first leaf in a new courtyard, the moment a room remembers that it has always existed.

At night—if the pocket has a night—the Makers gather beneath the bridges and listen to the rain rising through the dark.

They listen for the next three blows.

They listen for the mountain to tell them what they have been building all along.
