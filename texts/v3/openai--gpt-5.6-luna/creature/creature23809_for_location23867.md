The people of the basin are careful with their bodies.

They walk as though the ground might still be deciding whether to keep them. Their feet are broad from a lifetime on wet stone, and most wear sandals of braided reed or thin hide, though many go barefoot within their own houses. They know the ground by texture: the yielding grit that marks a hidden seep, the smooth dark patch where water has passed beneath the paving, the brittle crust that will collapse under a careless heel. Children are taught to read these distinctions before they are trusted near a channel or terrace.

They are not a numerous people. The settlement has room for many more than live there, but empty plots are not regarded as vacant. A plot may contain water beneath it. It may be needed for drainage. It may be allowed to remain open so that a house can see the sky, or so that a person crossing the town does not feel enclosed. Their buildings are separated by the same deliberate distances that separate their voices.

Most of them are short and strongly built, with dark, wind-burnished skin and hair worn close to the head or bound beneath simple caps. Their clothing is layered but not heavy: reed-fiber shirts, wrapped skirts or trousers, over-cloths treated with clay and oil against persistent damp. Colors are subdued because bright dyes are costly and because pale dust settles over everything. Rust red, wet gray, dull green, brown, and the blue of certain local stones are common. In the rain, their garments darken almost to black.

They have little use for ornament that catches or rings. Metal is too rare, and a metal clasp or bracelet can announce a person from across the open ground. Most jewelry is made from fired clay, polished stone, knotted cord, or the hard, translucent material found in the basin’s upper seams. These things are worn close to the body. Nothing is meant to make a sound while its wearer moves.

Their houses are cool, dim, and organized around water. A shallow channel often runs beneath the floor, carrying seepage away from sleeping spaces and into a communal drain. Beds are raised on stone frames. Food is stored in sealed clay jars hung from rafters where the air is driest. Walls are thick enough to hold the night’s coolness, though the people rarely speak of cold; the basin is warm, and dampness is the more persistent discomfort.

They sleep lightly.

Not because they fear attack, but because the settlement is always listening for changes in water. A new sound beneath a wall, a hollow ticking where there was none before, the abrupt absence of a familiar trickle—these can mean that a foundation is loosening or that a channel has shifted. At night, people wake and lie still until they can identify every sound around them. The inhabitants know the basin’s ordinary noises with an intimacy that other peoples might reserve for the voices of family.

They have no word for wilderness that means “a place without people.” To them, the unbuilt ground is simply the greater portion of the world. They distinguish instead between worked land, watched land, and land that has not yet shown what it intends to do.

Their earliest stories concern the raising of the basin. They do not agree whether the stone rose suddenly or slowly. Some say the land came upward beneath the feet of their ancestors, lifting houses, wells, and sleeping bodies into the clear air. Others say the people were born from the exposed rock and that the escarpments are their oldest relatives. There are songs about the first pools, but no songs about animals drinking from them. The people have never inherited the expectation of animal life. They do not find the emptiness tragic. It is simply the condition under which the world introduced itself.

Because nothing else moves, movement carries meaning.

A person standing at the edge of a field may be watched from a great distance. Someone crossing an open plot at the wrong hour can be seen by nearly everyone who happens to be outside. The people are therefore private in deliberate ways. They raise low screens around washing spaces. They angle doors away from the central paths. They paint certain stones beside their houses to indicate that the occupants are receiving visitors, grieving, sleeping, or unwilling to be approached.

There is little shouting. Anger is expressed by distance, by the closing of a door, by refusing to answer a greeting. A raised voice travels across the basin and becomes communal property. People who quarrel loudly are often ashamed afterward, not merely because they have disturbed others, but because they have surrendered control of the open air.

Their speech is quiet and precise. They have many terms for kinds of dampness, for the behavior of stone under rain, and for the stages by which a pool recedes. They distinguish between water that has gathered, water that is moving, water that is leaving, and water that is waiting beneath the surface. A person who cannot tell these apart is considered unready for adult work.

Children are given practical tasks early. They clear silt from channels with wooden scoops, carry reeds in bundles, press wet clay around cistern joints, and mark new cracks in paving with chalk. Their games imitate the settlement’s systems. They build miniature channels and divert water between them. They make little walls from loose stones and then test where the walls fail. The best games are those in which the children create a pool, maintain it, and lose it without spilling more than can be replaced.

They are not mournful people, though outsiders might mistake them for such. Their restraint comes from living in a place where every sound and gesture is exposed. They laugh, but quietly. They celebrate, but without drums. At the end of a good harvest, they gather on the covered platform and lay out food in long, orderly rows: dark cakes of grain, softened roots, sour greens, cakes of clay-sealed seed, and cups of warm water scented with crushed leaves. People speak to one another across the tables while rain moves over the roof. The sound is gentle and continuous, like water in a broad channel.

Their festivals are occasions for illumination. Small lamps are set along the drainage lines, around the cistern towers, and at the edges of the terraces. The lamps are not intended to make the town beautiful, though it is beautiful then. They are placed so that the settlement’s whole arrangement can be seen at once: the channels, the paths, the fields, the wall, the open plots, and the dark expanse beyond. For one evening, everyone looks at the place as if from outside it.

The dead are not buried in the worked ground. Their bodies are placed in stone chambers along the northern wall, where the seepage runs in its regular lines. The chambers have no doors, only fitted slabs that can be removed when another body is brought. The dead are wrapped in reed cloth and laid with a small bowl beside them. The bowl is always empty. It represents the water that cannot be carried onward.

No one speaks of the dead as having gone to another place. That would be difficult to imagine in a land so sharply divided between here and elsewhere. Instead, they say that a person has become still. The word is not a euphemism. Stillness is respected. It is the condition into which water, stone, and eventually every human body pass.

Their leaders are not kings. The settlement is governed by people who know how to maintain things: the channel-keepers, the terrace wardens, the masons, the cistern tenders, the people who remember where the first foundations were laid. Authority belongs to memory and accuracy. A person who exaggerates is dangerous; a person who forgets a small detail can be more dangerous still.

The oldest inhabitants are especially valued because they can identify changes that no measurement records. They know which wall has begun to lean, which spring now rises a finger’s breadth higher, which stretch of ground sounds different beneath a wooden pole. Yet even they are not treated as prophets. The basin is new enough that everyone understands the limits of knowledge. The land may remain stable for a hundred years and then open a fresh gully overnight. A reliable pattern is not the same as a promise.

There are strangers occasionally—travelers from the lowlands, people from the severe ranges beyond, surveyors following the old road. The inhabitants receive them courteously but without enthusiasm. Visitors bring news, goods, and stories of birds, grazing animals, forests, and crowded settlements where many different noises overlap. The basin people listen with interest, sometimes disbelief. They may ask what an animal sounds like when it is alone. They may ask whether a forest moves when there is no wind. They do not ask whether the visitors miss their former homes. The question would imply that the basin is an exile rather than a place.

The people do not think of themselves as living above the world. They live on something that has come up beneath them, and the distinction matters. Their settlement is not a refuge overlooking lesser lands. It is a temporary agreement between human intention and newly hardened stone.

They maintain the agreement every day.

They clear the channels. They patch the cisterns. They reset stones displaced by rain. They cut reeds before they become too dense, spread crops where the soil permits, and leave the rest alone. They keep paths open between houses and space around the springs. When a pool appears in an unexpected place, they do not immediately fill it. First they watch. They wait to see whether it is an accident, a new source, or a warning.

At dusk, they stand in their doorways and listen to the basin settling into night.

A child may point toward the reeds, convinced that something moved there. An older person will look, because even an old person can be wrong. Usually there is nothing: only a blade bending, a thread of water entering a crack, the reflection of the sky breaking apart in a shallow pool.

Then the child is told what everyone in the settlement learns sooner or later.

Nothing lives there except what has chosen to stay.

And for now, that is enough.
