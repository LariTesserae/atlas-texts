What lives there is not one creature, but a multitude behaving like one.

The smallest lives are everywhere first. They occupy the films of water caught in leaves, the damp undersides of bark, the black seam between roots. They hatch, feed, divide, molt, and die without ever leaving the few square feet in which their whole world is contained. In the warm rot of a fallen trunk, pale bodies work through softened wood. Beneath the mud, blind things tunnel toward roots and retreat from salt. The soil is threaded with movement. Even when the surface appears still, it is being sifted from below.

At dusk, the insects rise.

They emerge from the channels in veils and columns, from cracked banks and hollow stems, from egg cases sealed against the dry season. Some fly only briefly before becoming food. Some cling to leaves with transparent wings folded over their backs. Some have hard shells that catch no light at all. They tap against one another in the dark, brush the vegetation with dry legs, and settle wherever there is warmth. Their numbers are too great to count and too ordinary to notice individually. They are not a cloud passing through the forest. They are part of the forest’s atmosphere.

The small animals follow their own hidden routes. Rodents move through root chambers and come out with mud on their whiskers. Long-bodied hunters slip beneath mats of grass. Tree-dwellers travel overhead, using vines and branch unions that no eye on the ground can follow. They have learned to cross the plain without disturbing it. A leaf trembles, a reed bends, a patch of darkness acquires weight, and then the animal is gone.

Some nest in the canopy, where the air is marginally cooler and rain is still available after the ground has dried. Their nests are woven into forks of branches or suspended beneath broad leaves, protected from sudden floods and the mouths of things that climb. The young remain quiet. Hunger is common here, and noise is an invitation. Even the birds that possess bright feathers keep them hidden at night beneath wings and shadow.

The reptiles are older in their manner.

They lie beside the water with only their eyes and the ridges of their backs exposed. Their bodies retain the heat of the day. Mud dries on them in plates. They may wait for hours without changing position, becoming indistinguishable from bark, root, stone, or the dark lip of a pool. When they move, they move with terrible economy. A ripple crosses the water. A mouth opens and closes. The surface gathers itself again.

In the deeper channels, fish remain when the water withdraws from everywhere else. They survive in pockets beneath roots, in mud that looks too thick to contain breath. Some carry themselves through the wet season in vast numbers, silver bodies turning together beneath the reeds. When the pools shrink, they crowd into the remaining darkness, feeding on one another, on drowned insects, on whatever falls from above. Their scales and bones will later mark the dry channels, but not for long. The banks have mouths of their own.

There are larger animals, though the forest rarely gives them entirely back.

Something heavy crosses the islands of firm ground at night, pressing stems flat and leaving deep, softened prints. It may be a grazer with a broad muzzle, stripping leaves and reeds as it goes. It may be a tusked browser, pushing through thickets with patient force. Its passage is known first through the roots: a faint tremor, a tightening of silence, the sudden departure of insects from one patch of vegetation. It does not hurry. Nothing here is built for unnecessary effort.

Behind it come the hunters.

They do not rule the plain so much as read it. They know where the water holds, where the mud preserves a track, where a herd will be forced between channels, where fallen trees have made a narrow passage. Some hunt by scent. Some by vibration. Some wait in the water until the animal is close enough that speed becomes irrelevant. Their bodies are lean from heat and long distances. They carry scars beneath their coats or scales, old encounters absorbed into the pattern of their lives.

None of them owns the darkness.

The darkness belongs equally to the burrowing things, the suspended things, the things that swim, and the things that have not yet emerged from eggs. A predator may kill, but its body will later become a passage for larvae, a meal for beetles, a source of minerals in the water. The hunted animal is not removed from the plain. It is redistributed through it.

The plants live with a slower violence. They compete by occupying every possible surface. Roots lift one another, choke one another, and use the dead as scaffolding. Vines climb toward light and then descend again, thickening into bridges. Reeds spread through shallow water until they become a wall. Trees rise close together, each one bearing the scars of insects, fungus, flood, drought, and animals that have climbed or fed upon it. Their leaves hold rain above the ground and release it only when the heat has passed. Their roots drink from old channels and from hidden reservoirs beneath the clay.

Some plants defend themselves with bitter sap, hooked thorns, or leaves that close around anything small enough to trap. Others invite occupation. Hollow stems shelter ants. Flowers open for a single night and fill with moths. Fruit drops into the mud and is carried away by creatures whose names the forest does not need to remember. The vegetation is not scenery for the animals. It is their roof, wall, nursery, pantry, and grave.

There are inhabitants that never see the sky.

They live in the root-dark under the plain, where the temperature changes slowly and water remains longer. They have no use for eyes. They sense pressure through the soil and chemical traces in the damp. Some are soft and nearly transparent. Some are armored. Some coil around roots and wait for disturbance. Others consume the roots themselves, slowly enough that the tree above does not recognize the loss until the dry season comes.

When the rains arrive, the hidden population rises with them. Channels fill. Eggs open. Burrows become islands. Creatures that have spent months sealed in clay emerge into the brief abundance and feed until the water begins to retreat. The plain becomes crowded with bodies, but never chaotic. Each species is pulled toward the conditions it can endure. Each knows, without knowing, the distance between water and drowning, shade and hunger, shelter and concealment.

And somewhere beyond the reed beds, where the ground is firmer and the trees stand farther apart, a colony may be moving.

It will not announce itself. There may be dozens of bodies, perhaps hundreds, advancing in a loose and silent mass. They will pass through the grass like a change in wind. The young will remain near the center. The largest will break the stems. Smaller animals will follow at a distance, feeding on what the passage uncovers. For a while the plain will bear the shape of their presence: flattened vegetation, disturbed mud, a few bright scraps of scale or shell. Then rain, insects, and new growth will erase it.

This is how everything lives there: not as an individual against an empty world, but as a temporary concentration within an immense continuity.

The animals hide because the heat is costly, because water is uncertain, because every movement may be observed by something waiting. The plants crowd together because open ground wastes moisture and invites exposure. The insects remain numerous because most lives are brief. The hunters conserve themselves because the plain rewards patience. The dead remain useful because nothing is permitted to become merely absent.

At night, what lives there is mostly unseen. It breathes beneath leaves, under water, inside wood, and through the warm chambers of the soil. It moves in fragments: a splash, a scrape, the flexing of reeds, a wingbeat close to the ear. It pauses so completely that the world seems empty.

But the stillness is only the surface condition of countless lives.

The plain is inhabited down to its smallest pore. Even the darkness is occupied.
