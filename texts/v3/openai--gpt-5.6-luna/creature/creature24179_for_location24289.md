What lives in the basin has learned not to resist abundance.

The plants take the rain first. Their leaves are broad enough to serve as bowls, gutters, awnings, and temporary ponds. Water gathers in the cupped bases of flowers and in the overlapping sheaths around their stems. Tiny translucent larvae live there, feeding on pollen and the soft bodies of drowned insects. When the leaves overflow, they release their water in measured drops onto the leaves below, so that a single shower may descend through the canopy for hours after the sky has cleared.

The trees are not merely rooted in the basin. They are threaded through it.

Their roots enter cracks in the basalt, follow underground warmth, cross beneath streams, and rise again in distant groves. Some grow as pale, exposed cables along the banks. Others form arches large enough for the marsh animals to pass beneath. The oldest trees have trunks swollen into buttresses, their bark black and silver with moisture, their interiors full of chambers. Beetles live in those chambers. So do pale moths, blind cave crickets, small climbing mammals, and long, many-jointed things that never emerge into the light. A tree can be hollow for half its height and still flower heavily at the crown.

The forest floor is occupied by animals that move close to the ground and keep their bodies wet.

There are broad-backed amphibians the color of river stones, with slow eyes and delicate frills along their flanks. They lie in the warm pools with only their nostrils above water. When the rain comes, they leave the pools and climb into the reeds, where they call to one another in low, resonant pulses. The sound is less like a croak than a vessel being tapped from within.

Some of them carry their young in folds beneath the throat. Others bury their eggs in warm mineral mud, where the hatchlings emerge already capable of swimming. Their skin takes in water, salt, and heat from the basin itself. In the oldest pools, where the ground hums strongest, amphibians grow unusually large. They are not aggressive. They are simply patient, and difficult to move once they have settled against a stone.

The insects are more numerous than the visible flowers suggest.

They live beneath leaves, inside stems, under the loose plates of bark, and in the damp spaces between stones. There are beetles with shells like wet lacquer, winged things that resemble fragments of pale leaves, and long-legged insects that skate across the warm water without disturbing its surface. Some emerge only during the brief clear interval after rain. They rise in clouds from the reeds, mate in the sun, and vanish into the greenery before the next veil of water arrives.

The flowers depend on them, but not always in ways that resemble ordinary pollination. Certain blossoms close around a visiting insect and hold it briefly in a chamber of heat and fragrance. Others produce flowers that open only when the underground pressure changes, their petals unfolding in unison across a slope. A whole hillside may bloom because a buried spring has shifted its course by the width of a hand.

The birds occupy the spaces between water and canopy.

Some stand in the marshes on absurdly long legs, motionless enough to be mistaken for dead branches. Their plumage is green, gray, and mineral-white, broken into the same colors as the reeds and wet stone. They strike only when something small passes within reach. Others move through the upper forest in sudden, silent bursts, vanishing behind leaves before the eye can follow. Their nests are not built so much as persuaded into existence: woven from living vines, moss, shed fibers, and the soft roots of epiphytes, they continue growing after the eggs are laid.

At dusk, though dusk is faint in the bright basin, the birds gather on the warm cliffs. They do not form great flocks. They sit apart from one another, each on its own ledge, and exchange small calls that travel strangely through the steam. The notes enter the ravines and return altered. A bird may answer a sound that came from far below, or from a slope hidden entirely by cloud.

The larger animals are heard more often than seen.

They move along the forest margins where the ground is firm, or through the gullies where fallen trees have made wet corridors. Some are heavy enough to leave deep impressions in the mud. Others pass without bending the grasses, though their trails are marked by polished bark and disturbed moss. They feed on leaves, tubers, fungi, mineral salts, and the soft-bodied abundance that appears after rain. None needs to range far. The basin renews itself around them.

There are animals that lick the pale deposits from the basalt. There are animals that scrape moss from the trunks with blunt teeth. There are animals that swallow stones warmed by the springs and carry them inside until the heat has faded. Their bodies, like the roots and the watercourses, are part of a circulation too old to separate into individual actions.

Predators exist, but they are not rulers of the place.

They wait in water, under roots, within flowers, among the hanging curtains of vine. Their lives are made of stillness interrupted by precision. A mouth opens. A limb unfolds. A body disappears beneath the surface. Then the marsh closes again, and the basin resumes its slow breathing.

The dead do not leave.

A drowned bird becomes food for insects, then for fungi, then for the reeds. A fallen tree becomes a bridge, a nursery, a reservoir, and finally a dark seam of soil. Even a carcass in the open is quickly softened by rain and heat. The body enters the ground. The ground enters the plants. The plants enter the animals. Nothing is carried away except water and vapor.

Below all of them, something older and less easily named continues to work.

The deep chambers are not alive in the manner of roots or blood, yet they possess cycles, pressures, and responses. Heat rises. Stone expands. Water gathers, forces its way through narrow passages, and releases its burden into springs. The mountain’s hidden machinery adjusts itself constantly. It does not think, but it retains the memory of pressure in its cracks and the memory of water in its channels. A blockage becomes a new spring. A collapse becomes a terrace. A fissure becomes a line of trees.

The basin’s inhabitants live by these adjustments. They know them through their bodies.

The roots turn toward warmth before it reaches the surface. Amphibians leave a pool shortly before its chemistry changes. Insects emerge from the soil when the buried pressure begins to build. Birds abandon a cliff hours before a ledge settles. The larger animals move uphill or into the trees, not with alarm but with an inherited recognition of the ground’s moods.

Perhaps this is why nothing seems hurried. The basin gives warning in long, subtle increments. A tremor travels through root and water. Steam begins to thread through the moss. A spring grows warmer. Birds grow quiet. Somewhere deep below, the mountain completes one slow movement and begins another.

At the center, on the forested island, the inhabitants are most numerous but least visible. Roots knot beneath the shallow water. Small creatures shelter in the banks. Warm currents draw insects into the reeds. Trees rise from the summit and lean toward one another, their crowns touching in the mist. The island is not a sanctuary so much as a concentration—a place where many of the basin’s circulations meet.

Nothing living there believes itself separate from the machinery beneath.

The trees drink its warmth. The animals drink from the trees and the pools. The insects carry the flowering season from one slope to another. The birds carry seeds into cracks in the stone. The fungi return every fallen thing to the dark. Water moves through all of them, appearing briefly as rain, sap, blood, milk, mist, and underground pressure before changing form again.

The true inhabitant is not any single creature.

It is the continuity between them: the green basin feeding, shedding, hollowing, flowering, flooding, and beginning again. The animals are its momentary motions. The roots are its grasp. The marshes are its memory. The buried chambers are its patient heart.

And beneath the warm rain, beneath the leaves and the calls and the soft collapse of wet soil, the whole place listens to the mountain turning.
