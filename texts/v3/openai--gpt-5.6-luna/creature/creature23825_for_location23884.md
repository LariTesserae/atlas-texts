The inhabitants of the Stillworks are not a single people.

They are a population of continuities: things that have learned how to remain themselves while being altered by the country around them.

The nearest to human are the walkers.

They are slender, pale-clothed figures who move with the caution of people crossing a sleeping animal. Their garments are layered with insulation, though no one seems to feel cold. The clothing has no obvious fastenings. Sleeves seal themselves around the wrists; hoods tighten when passing through mist; boots alter their shape to fit stairs that are not the same width twice. Some walkers have faces. Others wear smooth masks of ceramic or cloudy glass. A few appear to have no heads at all, only a small cluster of lamps suspended above the collar.

They do not speak often. When they do, their voices are quiet and practical.

“Wait here.”

“Not that door.”

“Water.”

“Again.”

They recognize one another by habits of movement rather than appearance. A person who pauses at every junction. A person who touches each rail before crossing. A person who never looks into black water. There are families, perhaps, but they are defined by routes. Those who have descended the same stairwell belong to one another. Those who share a maintenance cycle sleep in adjoining rooms. Children, if there are children, are distinguished by the fact that they have not yet learned which surfaces are listening.

The walkers maintain the Stillworks, though maintenance is not quite the right word. They do not repair it. Nothing here can be restored to an earlier state. They accompany it.

They walk beside slowly moving belts to keep birds from nesting in unsafe places. They place salt at the mouths of corridors where the walls have begun to lean inward. They open and close valves according to diagrams that change while being read. They mark new stairs with strips of copper, though the marks are often found later on the underside of a bridge or wrapped around an animal’s antlers.

Some carry tools. Some carry small containers of warm stones. Some carry nothing but a length of red thread, which they unwind behind them and retrieve the following day from a place they have never visited.

They know the local weather intimately. They can tell when a rain curtain is about to become a doorway, when a dry floor is preparing to soften, when the light from a ceiling panel has begun to look outward. They teach this knowledge without instruction. An older walker simply waits at a threshold until the younger one understands that the waiting is the lesson.

The most dangerous condition is stillness.

To remain still in the Stillworks is to invite interpretation. At first the country merely notices. A bolt settles near the person’s foot. A pipe bends toward their spine. A nearby indicator light begins to blink in time with their breathing. If they continue to stand without purpose, their clothing may acquire seams that were not sewn into it. Their skin may harden in narrow plates. A hinge appears at the elbow.

The change is not painful. This is part of its danger.

Those who have been assigned a function do not vanish. They continue to think. A former walker may become a pressure gauge, a door mechanism, a lamp, a listening panel in the wall. Their memories remain, though reordered into signals. They remember rain as a change in voltage. They remember a friend as a light that turns on when a certain pipe is opened.

The other walkers visit them.

They place their hands against the housing and speak through the metal. Sometimes the machine answers. Sometimes a row of lights forms the pattern of a face. Sometimes it says only: **WAIT.**

The animals are better adapted to this fate because they have fewer objections to it.

The long-legged birds nest in high places where the machinery is warm. Their feathers are black, blue, and metallic green, but in certain light they become transparent. They do not sing so much as transmit small arrangements of clicks through their shadows. A flock passing over a yard produces the sound of many tiny switches closing.

They lay eggs in conveyor housings, cable trenches, and the open backs of clocks. Their young emerge already able to navigate the works. They know which belts will move during the night and which will remain fixed for a century. They sleep while being transported and wake precisely before arriving at a new district.

The pale tunnel mammals are less elegant. They have soft, broad feet, transparent eyelids, and backs divided into too many joints. They travel in lines through the service levels, pressing their bodies flat when machinery approaches. Their groups are governed by signals no living person can hear. At certain times they stop beneath a lamp and face the same direction, as though receiving instructions from a distant source.

They eat insulation, moss, mineral deposits, and the thin films that grow on old control panels. When threatened, they do not flee. They become very quiet and allow the walls to mistake them for part of the infrastructure.

There are animals no one claims to have seen clearly.

Something inhabits the reservoirs. Its back is smooth, pale, and windowed. When it rises, the little windows glow with scenes: a kitchen, a railway platform, a room full of snow. The walkers leave food at the edge of the water, though it is unclear whether they are feeding the creature or asking it to remain below.

In the upper gantries live creatures made mostly of joints. They descend only during condensation season, moving among the cables on limbs that fold in the wrong directions. They collect fallen screws and arrange them in circles around sleeping birds.

The insects inhabit the narrow warmth of the Stillworks. Silver-bodied things live in switch cabinets, consuming labels and replacing the missing words with their own names. Wingless moths gather around printed instructions, leaving the diagrams intact while eating every command. Bright beetles carry sparks from one dead terminal to another. For a few seconds, a disconnected machine may remember its purpose.

The plants are not innocent either.

Lichen grows in the seams, but it grows according to pressure. It thickens beneath the feet of walkers and thins around unused tools. Pale moss spreads through bearings and cable housings, making old mechanisms quiet enough to hear thinking. The warm, frost-like growths withdraw from shadows because they do not tolerate concealment. They prefer to be seen.

In some districts, the walls themselves are inhabited.

A wall may contain a room, a pipe, a memory of a room, or a person who has been waiting there for so long that the distinction no longer matters. Doors open onto other doors. Windows show places where the viewer has not yet been. Staircases develop a preference for particular travelers and lengthen beneath the feet of those they dislike.

The inhabitants learn to treat architecture as a kind of animal behavior. A door is not an object but a mood. A corridor can be hungry. A bridge may be shy. The center ring is regarded with the wary respect given to a large, sleeping creature. No one approaches its hollow without bringing an offering.

The offerings are modest: copper, salt, diagrams, warm stones, sometimes a tooth.

No one knows who receives them.

Perhaps it is the machinery, still sorting and refining according to an ancient purpose. Perhaps it is the landscape, which incorporates everything left alone long enough. Perhaps the country has become a collective organism, and the factories are its organs, the rails its nerves, the people its circulating thoughts.

What matters is that the Stillworks is not empty.

It is full of things waiting for their next designation.

A bird becomes a signal. A person becomes a hinge. A pool becomes a shaft. A discarded tool becomes part of a wall. A machine becomes a weather system. A memory becomes a light that turns on in a room no one can reach.

The inhabitants survive by moving slowly and with intention.

They cross the basin beneath the white brightness. They pause when the rails tremble. They bow their heads when a distant mechanism completes one invisible task. Around them, birds pass overhead, mammals enter the walls, water flows toward impossible places, and new structures rise cleanly from the manufactured ground.

Everyone is being made into something.

For now, they are still walking.
