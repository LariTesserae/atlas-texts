Something lives in the basin, though it would be difficult to say where its body ends.

It occupies the dark seam first: a thin, patient presence where the limestone holds moisture. It lies there in the mineral shadow, neither moss nor water, but borrowing from both. When the seam dries, it withdraws into the stone. When the pool gathers beneath the overhang, it appears there as a faint greenish veil along the shaded edge. It does not enter the water. It is already in it, dispersed so finely that the pool remains clear.

It has no eyes, but it knows the changes in light. It knows when the strip of morning reaches the rear wall, when the warmth leaves the upper shelves, when rain has fallen beyond the rim and begun its slow underground journey toward the basin. These things do not alarm it. They are the few motions by which the outside world announces itself.

Perhaps it was once a creature with a more definite shape. The three circular hollows may have been places where it rested its hands, or knees, or the soft weight of a body unlike any body now living. The low structure may have been built for it, though not necessarily by anyone who understood what it was. A person might have come here long ago and arranged the stones around the presence, making a shelter without walls, a boundary without a door. Or the stones may have been placed for water, and the creature only accepted the invitation.

It has remained because nothing here asks it to become different.

During the day it is strongest in the warmth held by the limestone. It spreads through the shallow grooves and waits beneath the ash-dark hollows. The plants know it without recognizing it. Their roots stop just short of certain places. The moss grows in the shape it permits. The gray-green mat keeps its low, inward habit because the creature does not allow anything to rise too far above the stone.

At times, it gathers itself.

Then the basin seems to become more enclosed than before. The silence thickens. The pool loses its small movements and lies so still that its surface appears solid. A dry stalk may lean toward the overhang without wind. The old blocks seem to settle closer together. Nothing visible crosses the ground, yet the impression of passage remains: not tracks, but a slight darkening in the stone, a dampness relocated from one depression to another.

The creature does not feed in any ordinary sense. It takes what arrives already dissolved: rain reduced to droplets, dust softened by condensation, the pale burden left behind when water vanishes. It draws warmth from the walls and patience from the empty air. In return it keeps the basin as it is. It holds the moisture in its narrow routes. It prevents the cracks from closing and the pool from becoming entirely dry. It preserves the small permissions by which the plants survive.

Nothing must flourish here. Flourishing would be a kind of disturbance.

If someone entered the basin, the creature would not hide. It would not approach either. It would wait beneath the visitor’s feet, distributed through every place that had been touched by time. The person might feel the limestone’s muted warmth and mistake it for sunlight retained in the rock. They might hear a drop strike the pool and look up at the ceiling. They might stand beside the three hollows and feel, without reason, that they had been expected.

The creature has no name because names separate one thing from another. It is the dampness in the seam, the green skin on the pool, the softened edge of the fallen wall. It is the interval between one drop and the next. It is the old arrangement continuing.

When the basin is empty, it remains.

When water arrives, it wakes—not with movement, but with a slight deepening of the dark places.
