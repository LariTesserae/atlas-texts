What lives there has learned not to hurry.

The most visible animals are the **longbacks**, though the name belongs more to their movement than to their shape. At a distance they resemble slabs of dark stone dragged slowly across the flats by the wind. They have many jointed limbs tucked beneath a broad, folded body, and along their sides run flexible fringes that comb the mineral crust. The fringes are not mouths, exactly. They are fields of sensing tissue, able to distinguish the faint chemical differences between one patch of stone and another: old growth, fresh vapor, the residue of a tower forest, the hidden warmth of a buried colony.

A longback may travel a thousand miles in a season.

It does not follow a visible path. Its route is written in pressure, temperature, and the changing taste of the ground. It pauses where a human eye would see nothing but a pale shelf. There it lowers its body until the slate-colored plates lie nearly flush with the stone, and draws nourishment through pores beneath its armor. The feeding is so slow that the animal appears to be resting. Days pass. Its sensory fringes remain extended, trembling whenever a mineral current passes through the rock.

They travel alone, but they are not solitary.

Every longback carries a map of the plateau in its underside: not an image, and not a memory in any familiar sense, but a layered record of pressure routes and chemical gradients. When two meet, they do not approach one another directly. One settles. The other circles wide around it, reading the disturbed ground. A conversation may take a week. The animals press their weight into the stone at intervals, sending low waves through the surface. The signals travel farther than sound through the thin air. They speak of food, danger, vapor, and the location of others.

They also speak of absence.

A longback can identify a region where another has passed months before by the faint closing of the crust behind it. It knows the difference between a living colony and one that has withdrawn below the stone. It knows where a predator has rooted, even if the predator has remained motionless for years. Such places are marked in the animal’s memory as regions of altered pressure, and the avoidance of them passes from adult to young before the young have made their first crossing.

The young are not born in the open.

When a longback has reached the age of reproduction, it seeks a basin where the vapor rises in a particular rhythm. There it presses its body against the ground and releases a chain of dark, flexible nodules into a shallow fissure. The nodules remain dormant for several years. During that time, the parent returns repeatedly, sometimes from immense distances, to feed the fissure with salts gathered from distant colonies.

Eventually the stone opens.

The young emerge as narrow, many-legged ribbons, pale and soft beneath their first armor. They cannot travel. They lie together in the warmth exhaled by the buried organism below them, feeding on mineral films and on one another’s shed tissues. Their bodies harden slowly. Some remain in the basin. Others begin, after decades, to crawl outward in different directions.

No parent teaches them the route. The plateau teaches them.

It teaches them through cold. It teaches them through the sudden flattening of plants miles away. It teaches them through the deep, delayed tremor of a tower predator adjusting its roots. It teaches them through the taste of the vapor, which changes before any visible alteration reaches the surface. A young longback that survives its first century becomes capable of crossing the empty spaces. A very old one may have traveled around the entire high country without ever seeing another of its kind.

The tower predators are more difficult to understand.

They are called **stillers** by the longbacks, though the name refers to the effect they have on the land. A stiller may spend three hundred years appearing to be a damaged mineral column. Its body grows in layers, matching the local rock: rust-colored plates where the ground is warm, green-gray scales where the vapor is thin, black ridges along the side facing the prevailing wind. It anchors itself deeply and draws nutrients through a network of roots that are not roots, but muscular extensions of its digestive body.

It waits for pressure.

A herd of longbacks can be sensed from far away as a broad, irregular disturbance moving through the crust. The stiller detects the pattern before the animals arrive. Around it, the tower plants bend slightly outward. Fine grains slide down its sides. Beneath the surface, its rooted limbs contract.

Then it moves.

The animal does not rise so much as unfold. Plates separate. Limbs that had resembled cracks in the stone become hooked, jointed columns. Its body may cross the distance between one breath of the landscape and the next with shocking speed. The attack is brief. There is no chase. The stiller strikes once, withdraws, and becomes part of the terrain again.

A successful stiller feeds for decades.

Its body absorbs not only flesh but memory-bearing mineral tissues: the salts in which a longback stores the chemical history of its migrations. Afterward the predator may remain rooted for generations, carrying within itself fragments of routes it never walked. Eventually it sheds its outer layers. The discarded plates lie among the tower groves and are colonized by blue-green mats, so that the predator becomes, in time, a low ridge covered in vegetation.

There are smaller lives everywhere.

Transparent threads move through the upper layers of stone, too fine to see except when they disturb a salt bloom. They gather around the openings of the immense buried plants, feeding on warmth and exhaled minerals. Flat, many-eyed things inhabit the shadow beneath the mats, emerging only during atmospheric curtains when the charged dust makes their bodies luminous. They do not flee from the longbacks. They cling to their armor and harvest whatever the larger animals disturb.

The plants themselves are not passive.

The blue-green mats exchange information through their underground filaments. A grazing animal crossing one colony causes a change in pressure that may be registered by another colony hundreds of miles away. The warning moves slowly, perhaps one ridge per year, but the plants have time. In response, leaves thicken, pores close, and dormant tissues retreat beneath the stone. When danger has passed, the colony opens again.

The tower forests are more collective still. No single tower possesses a complete body. Their roots mingle in vast subterranean chambers, sharing salts, heat, and electrical charge. A tower may die aboveground and continue living below for centuries. Another may rise in its place, bearing the same internal pattern of growth. The forest does not distinguish sharply between one organism and many. Its life is distributed through distance.

When the forests shed their scales, every creature responds.

Longbacks lower themselves to the ground. Small animals seal their breathing pores. Even the stillers fold their outer plates inward. Then the towers release their accumulated mineral burden. The air fills with white and copper-colored fragments, and the falling scales strike the ground with a soft, continuous hiss. For less than an hour the plateau disappears beneath glitter.

Afterward, the creatures emerge.

The longbacks raise their sensory fringes and taste the altered air. The small animals gather among the shed scales. Predators change their positions, because the forest has changed its scent and the old maps are no longer exact. For a time, the whole region is unsettled—not violently, but profoundly, as though every living thing must revise its idea of where it is.

There may once have been minds here that built things, or named the ridges, or looked down toward the hidden lower country. If so, no trace of them remains in any form the plateau recognizes as important. The organisms that endure are not concerned with monuments. Their memory is carried in pressure, in mineral, in the arrangement of roots beneath stone.

They do not measure their lives by years.

A longback measures distance by the closing of a crust behind it. A tower measures age by the height of its inner salts. A buried colony measures time by how far its warmth has traveled through the rock. The stillers measure it in opportunities: the passage of prey, the return of vapor, the gradual wearing away of their disguise.

At night, when the tissues of the mats become rigid and the tower forests cease their slow turning, the animals become almost indistinguishable from the country. A longback lies among the slabs like a fallen shelf. A stilled predator is only another column. Small lives withdraw into pores no wider than a hair.

Yet beneath the frozen surface, nothing has stopped.

Roots continue to search. Mineral currents rise. The young shift inside their fissures. Far below, the great colonies open and close their dark mouths, drawing the vapor downward.

And when the sun returns, the plateau wakes not all at once but in gradients: first the buried organisms, then the mats, then the towers, then the animals that have been waiting for the stone to become readable again.

Something crosses the pale flats.

The ground remembers it.
