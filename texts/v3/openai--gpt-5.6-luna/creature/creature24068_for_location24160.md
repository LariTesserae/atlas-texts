They are not a people who live on the mountain so much as a people distributed through it.

There is no clear boundary between settlement and infrastructure. A person may sleep behind a wall that contains a pressure main, wake beneath a greenhouse, descend through a stairwell shared by roots and electrical conduits, and spend the day repairing a machine whose vibration has become part of the local weather. Their homes are fitted into whatever the mountain offers: old offices divided by curtains, maintenance vaults warmed by pipes, ceramic chambers built behind retaining walls, rooms suspended beneath bridges, and narrow apartments added to the sides of towers like nests.

They call themselves, when a name is necessary, the Continuous.

The word refers less to ancestry than to obligation. No household, district, or generation is expected to preserve itself unchanged. What must continue is the passage: water through the reservoirs, food through the gardens, air through the shafts, material through the factories, memory through the mouths of the old. A person belongs to the mountain by taking responsibility for some movement within it.

There are water families, whose knowledge passes through generations in the pressure and taste of particular channels. They can tell, by placing a palm against a pipe, whether a reservoir is low three levels above or whether a filter has begun to foul somewhere beyond the wall. Their children learn the sound of pumps before they learn numbers. At night they walk the elevated tanks with lanterns, measuring water lines against marks cut into the concrete by people whose names have been worn away.

There are wall people, who live among the retaining shelves and know the mountain by its faults. They sleep lightly, accustomed to the small language of settling: a click in a beam, a soft shower of aggregate, the long creak that means a terrace has shifted by less than a finger’s width. They cultivate plants in cracks that would appear to others accidental. When they repair a wall, they do not always restore its original plane. They study the direction of its sag, listen to the stresses within it, and add their work to the existing angle. Their repairs resemble scars, buttresses, shelves, and sometimes houses.

The machine workers are recognizable by the dark crescents beneath their fingernails and the fine metallic dust in their hair. They move slowly, not from exhaustion but from habit. A loose step, an altered pitch, a belt running half a degree too warm: these things are noticed. They speak quietly near engines and loudly near water. Their clothing is patched with materials chosen for function rather than appearance—rubberized cloth, woven wire, glazed fabric, leather from animals raised on the lower terraces. Some wear small gauges around their wrists. Others carry no instruments at all, trusting their hands.

No one is entirely sure how long the Continuous have been changing.

The oldest records speak of bodies much like those of the builders, though the accounts disagree about what the builders were. The people of the lower terraces are broad-shouldered and sun-darkened, with eyes narrowed against reflected light. Those raised in the damp interior tend to be pale, their skin carrying a permanent coolness, their hair often greened or silvered by mineral water. High-terrace children grow long-limbed from climbing and thin in the dry air; they can cross service bridges without looking down and sleep through alarms that would wake someone from the valley.

There are stranger differences. Some people possess a second translucent membrane at the corners of their eyes, inherited from generations spent around dust, steam, and chemical mist. Others have enlarged hands, sensitive to vibration. A few families bear patches of smooth, thickened skin along the throat and ribs, relics—perhaps—of an adaptation to pressure changes or polluted air. They are not considered remarkable. The mountain has been changing its inhabitants for too long for anyone to agree where adaptation ends and illness begins.

Their children are not raised by isolated households. They are raised by routes.

A child may belong to a room, but also to a reservoir, a market stair, a garden belt, and a shift in the old brickworks. They learn geography by errands. Before they are trusted with a tool, they are sent to carry a message through three districts and return with the correct answer. They memorize the color of warning flags, the smells of different algae beds, the safe places to shelter during a pressure release, and which ladders become slippery when the clouds descend.

They are taught that every structure has a front and a hidden side. The front is for traffic, ceremony, commerce, and the appearance of stability. The hidden side is where the mountain explains itself: condensation, roots, nests, corrosion, illegal rooms, leaking valves, mold, lost tools, and the small failures from which larger failures begin. To understand a place, one must go behind it.

Their markets are arranged around useful intervals. There are stalls beneath warm ducts, markets on the roofs of laundries, fruit exchanges beside freight lifts, and night bazaars in disused filtration halls. People sell food, recovered metals, machine parts, medicines, insects bred for protein, seeds, maps, repaired clothing, and water of certified origin. A cup of water can carry more prestige than a jewel if it comes from a high reservoir in a dry season. Children trade polished bolts and fragments of blue tile. Old women sell preserved citrus peel in packets folded from obsolete maintenance schedules.

The Continuous are not sentimental about decay, but neither are they afraid of it.

They understand that the mountain survives by becoming something else. A factory may become a mushroom hall, then a school, then a shrine to a machine no one remembers how to operate. An abandoned pipe may be filled with soil and planted with beans. A collapsed road may become a hanging garden or a nesting wall. Nothing is truly discarded; it is only moved into a slower circulation.

They have ceremonies for this.

When a machine is retired, the workers wash its exposed surfaces and remove whatever parts may still be useful. The remaining body is painted with a pale mineral slurry and left for a season under rain. Moss is encouraged into its seams. Children are brought to see the first roots enter the casing. Afterward, the machine may be dismantled, inhabited, buried, or left standing as a landmark. Its final purpose is not decided by a central authority. It is allowed to reveal what it can become.

When a wall fails, the mourning is practical but not cold. Names are recorded, routes altered, salvage organized. If lives were lost, the names are painted in a place where water will eventually pass over them. If no one died, people still gather to touch the broken surface. A failure without death is considered a generous warning.

The Continuous do not worship the mountain exactly. They argue with it, bargain with it, curse it, and depend on it. Some insist that the mountain is only a great accumulation of dead decisions. Others believe it has a mind, though not one that thinks in words. They point to the repeated return of water, the way roots discover abandoned chambers, the tendency of certain machines to fail in patterns no engineer can explain. They say the mountain remembers through pressure.

There are listeners who sleep beside foundations and claim to hear responses in the deep vibration. They are not universally respected, but neither are they dismissed. Every district has at least one person who can be summoned when a wall begins to hum strangely or when birds refuse to enter a shaft. Their diagnoses are sometimes technical, sometimes ceremonial, and often both.

The dead remain close.

They are placed in niches along dry retaining walls, their names sealed beneath glazed tiles. Some are buried in garden terraces, where the roots will eventually reach them. The most honored are incorporated into buildings: a handful of ash in concrete, a tooth in a foundation, a braid of hair sealed behind a pump housing. To the Continuous, this is not a metaphor. A person who returns to the structure becomes part of its load-bearing memory.

At dusk, they come out onto the terraces.

Workers wash black dust from their faces. Gardeners carry baskets of fruit and cut greens. Children race along the safer switchbacks, following the red lights of freight lifts. Couples sit beside warm vents with their backs against the concrete. On the upper levels, where the air is thin and the views extend over the neighboring ridges, people gather in small groups to watch the cloud banks form below them.

From there, the inhabitants look less like a population than like a pattern of activity: lamps moving through corridors, figures crossing bridges, hands lifting leaves from drains, bodies leaning over railings to inspect the water. They are everywhere, but seldom gathered in one place. The mountain has made them into a dispersed organism.

And perhaps they have done the same to it.

They know which gardens are fed by which factories. They know the migratory routes of birds through the ventilation system, the breeding seasons of fish in the lower channels, the hours when steam will fog the eastern platforms. They can feel the difference between ordinary vibration and distress. When a pump stops, someone notices. When a pipe begins to leak, a vine may reveal it first. When a neglected terrace grows too lush, goats are driven upward to crop it back.

The mountain is maintained by hands, but it is also maintained by roots, insects, water, birds, corrosion, heat, and time. The Continuous no longer pretend that these forces can be separated. Their highest skill is not control. It is cooperation with whatever has already begun.

In the morning, they wake to condensation on the rails and the first engines turning below. They open channels, check gauges, harvest what has ripened overnight, and clear leaves from the grates. They go to work through the living machinery of the place, carrying food into factories, tools into gardens, messages through tunnels, and water toward the heights.

Somewhere above them, a reservoir gives a slow metallic groan.

A worker stops, lifts her hand to the wall, and listens.

Around her, the mountain continues to breathe through its vents, circulate its blood through pipes, and raise itself—terrace by terrace, generation by generation—into the bright air.
