What lives in the White Noise Basin has learned not to oppose it.

The pale shard-things are the most visible of its inhabitants, though visibility is not their purpose. They move in the narrow spaces the basin has already provided, folding themselves over one joint at a time. At a distance they appear to be fragments of the plateau shaken loose and taught to travel. Up close, they are more troubling. Their bodies are made of a clear, gel-like substance that does not quite hold a shape; each segment settles into its position only when the creature is at rest. Dark filaments pass through them in branching patterns, tightening and relaxing with no apparent rhythm.

They have no faces. There is nowhere on them where attention gathers.

Light passes through their bodies and comes out altered. A creature crossing a bright mineral crust casts not a shadow but a thin stain of blue-gray, as though the stone remembers being wet beneath it. When it enters fog, it disappears completely, though the channels continue to pulse around its passage. It may emerge miles away, or it may emerge from the same hollow years later. Whether it has traveled is impossible to determine.

They do not eat. They settle against wet seams and draw water through their bodies, separating it from the dissolved minerals, then returning it almost unchanged. They do not reproduce in any recognizable way. Sometimes a shard divides along one of its joints and leaves half of itself behind. The abandoned half remains still for several seasons, then begins folding. Sometimes two meet, touch along their entire lengths, and separate with their dark inner threads rearranged. No young appear. No old ones are found.

They are not numerous, but the basin is full of their routes.

Every route is exact. The creatures travel around certain stones rather than over them, pass beneath particular sheets of hanging water, and pause at the edges of the same pools. Their paths cross without collision. When one reaches a place occupied by another, it waits—not impatiently, not submissively, but because the place is occupied. If the obstruction remains for a hundred years, the waiting continues. Eventually the other moves.

They seem less like animals inhabiting the landscape than small permissions granted by it.

The dark shapes beneath the water are older.

They are broad, low bodies pressed into the stone under the films and pools. Some resemble folded hides. Some resemble mouths sealed with mineral deposits. Some are only interruptions in the geometry of the water, places where the fixed hexagons fail to form. They may remain motionless for generations. The basin covers them patiently: crust grows over their backs, channels divide around them, transparent films gather and retract. Yet the deposit never quite conceals them. Beneath every layer, something dark maintains its shape.

They do not move when watched.

They move when the observer looks away, but this may be an error of attention rather than a property of the creatures. A dark shape glimpsed at the edge of vision will later be found several inches nearer the black center, or turned at a new angle, or absent from its old hollow with no disturbed water around it. Nothing marks the interval. The basin simply has one fewer stillness and one more.

If touched, they are cold but not solid. A hand would sink through their upper surface as through deep water, meeting resistance only farther down. The resistance would not push back. It would hold the hand in place with the quiet strength of a narrow crack. Beneath the surface are fine ridges, layered and parallel, and between them a slow movement like breathing performed by stone.

The dark ones are not hunters. They have no need to pursue. Anything that enters their reach is eventually incorporated into the arrangement around them: a pebble, a shard-creature, a dropped tool, the bones of a traveler. Nothing is consumed. It is simply given a permanent location.

The central water belongs to something else.

No one sees the thing beneath it because the water does not reflect, and because looking into it produces no sense of depth. The eye finds only blackness, uniform and complete. Yet the water is not empty. It has a pressure. It presses upward against the veil of suspended droplets, against the converging plates, against the sound itself.

The thing below may be immense, or it may have no dimensions beyond the depression that contains it. It does not swim. It does not rise. It is present in the manner of a rule.

The basin’s equilibrium radiates from there.

When a loose stone returns to its hollow, it is answering the black water. When a footprint fills and vanishes, it is answering the black water. When a film retracts into its original pattern, when a sound loses its identity but not its force, when a distant escarpment refuses to come closer, the water has not acted exactly. Rather, the world has remembered itself in its vicinity.

The creature beneath the water may be asleep. It may have no distinction between sleeping and waking. It may have been placed there by nothing, assembled from pressure and mineral and the accumulated refusal of change. Its awareness, if awareness is the word, is not directed outward. It does not notice the pale travelers. It does not notice the dark shapes. It does not notice a person standing at the edge of the depression with their mouth open in the unbearable roar.

But it knows alteration.

A new crack in the shelf would cause the black surface to tighten. A foreign object would lose its edges. A living body would begin to feel its joints settling into older positions. The longer one remained near the water, the less convincing one’s own movement would become. The desire to step forward would persist, but the foot would find itself returning to the same wet plate. The hand would rise and lower along its old arc. The heart would continue beating, though every beat would sound like a repetition of one made long ago.

The basin does not kill intruders. Death would be an event, and events are difficult to sustain there.

Instead, it makes them compatible.

A traveler might discover that their footprints no longer fill behind them because they are no longer making footprints. Their boots would press the water aside and the water would close. Their clothes would remain dry beneath the suspended rain. Their breath would condense in front of their face and hang there, a small permanent cloud. Their skin would pale. Their voice would become indistinguishable from the chord.

After enough time, the pale shard-things would begin to use the traveler’s path.

They would fold along the marks of the knees, pass around the places where the elbows had struck the stone, and pause beside the darkened hollows made by the hands. Mineral crust would gather in those impressions. Water would occupy them. The body would remain among the plates, not decaying, not preserved, but becoming one of the basin’s fixed arrangements.

Perhaps the traveler would still be conscious.

Perhaps consciousness here is simply the ability to endure being repeated.

At night—if night exists above the White Noise Basin—the inhabitants do not emerge, because nothing changes enough to call emergence necessary. The shard-things continue along their channels. The dark forms shift when unwitnessed. The thing beneath the central water remains below, holding the plateau in its final position.

And everywhere, through the clear air and the suspended rain, the basin continues to speak in one immense unbroken tone.

Its inhabitants do not answer.

They have already been answered.
