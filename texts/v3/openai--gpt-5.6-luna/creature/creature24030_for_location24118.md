What lives there is a pale salamander, though the word *salamander* suggests a creature more complete than the one that has endured in that chamber.

It is small enough to lie along a human palm, but it has never seen a palm except perhaps in memory inherited through its body. Its skin is the color of wet ash, nearly transparent at the throat and belly, where the slow workings of its organs can sometimes be seen. The eyes are dark pinpricks beneath a film of cloudy tissue. They have not become useless, exactly; they distinguish the entrance from the inner chamber, gray from black, the movement of a hand from the stillness of stone. But sight has ceased to be its chief way of knowing.

It knows pressure.

The waterfall enters it through the floor, through its ribs, through the delicate bones of its skull. It knows when the ridge is full of rain by the changing weight of the concussion. It knows the difference between water falling cleanly into the basin and water carrying grit, leaves, or a loosened fragment of the upper land. It knows when the cold will sharpen along the walls and when the basin’s edges will take on their thin nighttime ice.

Most of all, it knows the vibrations of approach.

The old path of flat stones is not a path to the salamander, though people once made it one. The creature lives in a crack above the basin, behind a shelf of stone where condensation gathers but floodwater cannot reach. From there it feels each footfall altered by the shape of the chamber. A stranger coming in from the entrance produces a broad, uncertain disturbance. A returning person once made a different pattern: three careful weights, a pause, two more. The salamander learned this without understanding that it was learning. Long after the last of those people stopped descending, it continued to wake at the memory of that rhythm.

Sometimes it mistakes falling grit for them.

Then it draws itself backward into the crack and waits.

It does not live by the waterfall. The fall is too violent, and the basin is too poor. Its food arrives in the moss: pale flies blown or washed inward after rain, tiny larvae, blind mites, the soft-bodied things that gather where decay has collected in a damp ledge. The salamander comes out only when the chamber has been dark for a long time, though darkness is not a condition here so much as the permanent substance of the farther room.

It moves with a slow, almost liquid caution. Its feet spread wide on the stone. Its tail follows without sound. Where the water has polished the floor, it can cross a hand’s breadth in several minutes, pausing whenever the roar changes. Its skin must remain wet. If the season is dry, it retreats deeper into the crack, where the stone sweats faintly and the air remains cold enough to preserve it. It can remain there for days, perhaps weeks, living on the small reserve beneath its ribs.

There may once have been several of them. The chamber contains enough old hollows, enough damp seams, enough isolated ledges to suggest a population that never became one. But nothing here gathers. Each creature, if there were others, would have occupied its own pocket of green and black stone, meeting only by accident and withdrawing from the contact. The chamber does not support a family, a swarm, or a society. It supports continuance.

The salamander has grown old in the only way available to it. Its tail is notched. One hind foot is missing a toe. A white scar crosses its flank where a falling chip of stone once struck it. Mineral dust has settled permanently into the folds of its skin. When it rests on the wet shelf, it resembles a root that has chosen to move.

It has never touched the stone bowl, though it passes near it. Water gathers there in a shallow, trembling skin, and sometimes a fly drowns at the edge. The salamander waits until the bowl is still before approaching. Beside it, the dark chert flakes and the broken bone awl mean nothing. The sharpened length of wood has softened into the smell of rot. The creature knows these objects only as obstructions in the older shape of the chamber.

Still, the people have left something behind that it cannot avoid.

Their path has altered the floor. Their handholds have altered the walls. Their old presence remains as a pattern of vibration embedded in the stone, a faint history beneath the waterfall’s continuous assault. The salamander rests against those worked surfaces because they are warmer, or smoother, or simply familiar. It may be the last living thing in the chamber that responds to human craft.

When rain fills the ridge, the waterfall swells. The basin rises blackly. Water strikes water with such force that the whole chamber seems to buckle around it. The salamander withdraws into its crack and flattens itself against the dry rear wall.

There it waits while the ancient stream speaks in its one unchanging voice.

When the sound lessens, it emerges again—not bravely, and not fearfully, but because the stone is wet, the moss is alive, and something small has begun to move among the roots.
