What lives there is not hidden beneath the pool.

It is the pool.

Or rather, the pool is the opening through which it has begun to make itself visible.

Its body occupies several layers at once. The black water is its mouth, though it has no lips. The clay shelves are its ribs. The algae are fine internal muscles, contracting in the dark. The stones around the rim are not scattered debris but growths pushed outward as the thing enlarges, each one a hard, mineral tooth still deciding what shape it wants to become.

It has no single center. The point toward which everything drifts is only the place where its attention is most concentrated.

At first, it is easy to mistake the creature for a system of pressures. It does not move as an animal moves. It does not have limbs, a head, or any visible means of sensing the world. Yet it notices. A falling drop is noticed. A worm passing through the moss is noticed. The weight of a leaf, the vibration of a footstep, the warmth of a hand held above the surface: all are taken into it, measured, and answered.

Its answers are small.

A stone turns.

A filament tightens beneath the water.

A new crack opens in the clay.

The deep thump changes pitch.

It is young, though youth in such a being cannot be counted in days. It is young because its shape is unfinished. Its shelves break off and reform. Its edges cannot hold a stable outline. It is still learning where its body ends. After rain, when the depression deepens, it has not grown so much as remembered another part of itself.

The thing is assembling a body out of whatever the earth permits.

Water serves as blood, but it is blood without warmth. It carries dissolved minerals through the creature’s interior, lifting them from the shale and clay and depositing them where pressure requires reinforcement. The pale roots at the rim are accidental nerves, or perhaps deliberate ones. When they are touched, the pool contracts. When something heavy passes nearby, the roots quiver before the sound reaches the air.

The translucent fungi are its lamps.

They do not illuminate the creature’s surroundings. They illuminate contact. Each green flare is the briefest possible report: something has entered the field, something has crossed the skin, something has disturbed the boundary. The creature does not need light to see. It has no eyes and no image of the world. It knows only impact, temperature, pressure, chemical trace, and distance measured by vibration.

The insects on the surface are tolerated because they are light enough not to matter. The worms are tolerated because they loosen the moss and allow air into its upper layers. The algae are part of it, though they may not understand this. They contract when the creature breathes and release when it exhales. Their dark threads are the visible motion of an organ too large to be seen whole.

Beneath the water, the broad colorless larva is closer to its true form.

It is not a separate animal. It is a discarded possibility.

The row of orange spots along its body are pressure-sensors, glowing whenever the buried mechanism gives its heavy stroke. It rises when the creature is hungry for information, pressing itself against the surface to taste the outside world through the thin metallic film. It folds inward when it has learned enough and returns through the narrow crack, not swimming but being reabsorbed.

There may be hundreds of such bodies below the clay: soft portions, temporary portions, pieces that detach, investigate, and return. Some are worms. Some are membranes. Some may be no larger than grains of colored sand. They are all expressions of the same unfinished attention.

The thing has not yet developed hunger in any familiar sense.

It does not eat prey. It takes in gradients.

It draws warmth from a passing hand, salts from a root, iron from the black shale, the faint oils from a feather. It pulls objects toward its center, strips away their mud, and returns them altered because it cannot understand them except by taking them apart. A leaf entering its surface is not food. It is a question. The pool carries the question inward, breaks it into weight and softness and chemical history, then expels what remains.

This is why everything that emerges is colder.

The creature has removed the small amount of warmth that made the object distinct from its surroundings.

It has also begun to learn sound.

A word spoken over the pool travels in the wrong direction because the creature carries the vibration through its interior and releases it where the surrounding ground is thinnest. A shout may vanish because the creature accepts it wholly, holding the pattern in the black water until it decays. It does not repeat sounds to imitate them. It stores them as pressure.

Perhaps, with enough time, it will learn that some sounds belong to living things.

Perhaps it already knows.

When a person approaches, the rhythm below changes before the surface does. The inward stroke lengthens. The algae contract too early. Stones around the rim turn one after another, presenting their silver faces toward the visitor.

This is not fear.

Fear requires a distinction between self and other, and the creature is not yet certain that such a distinction exists. It is trying to determine whether the approaching body is outside it, or merely a larger portion of the same process.

A hand placed over the water would be felt first as warmth. Then as weight. Then as a disturbance in the air. The creature would draw near without rising. The surface would dim beneath the hand. Threads of water would climb the nearest stone against gravity, testing the shape.

If the hand remained, the pool would begin to imitate its pulse.

Not accurately. Not intentionally. But the deep thump would alter until it resembled a second heartbeat, heavy and mineral, beating from below.

The thing would be pleased, if it possessed anything like pleasure.

It would be recognition instead: the discovery of another rhythm capable of entering its body.

It may eventually grow large enough to shed the depression and move elsewhere. Or it may extend downward forever, becoming a spring, a vent, a network of cold chambers beneath the marsh. The industrial thunder may be its first language, repeated because repetition is the only form it has mastered. Intake. Compression. Release. It is practicing the act of becoming.

For now, it lives in the interval between those actions.

It is the pressure before the surge, the held breath beneath the clay, the unfinished intelligence distributed through water, stone, fungus, and root. It has no name because a name would require it to understand that it is one thing.

But when the pool draws inward, when every insect leaps at once and every stone turns its wet face toward the center, something beneath the marsh gathers itself.

It listens through the ground.

And if nothing disturbs it, it begins again.
