What lives there is not one plant, though from a distance it can appear to be a single, continuous thing.

It is a colony.

Its oldest body lies beneath the green mats, where root has become cord, cord has become tissue, and tissue has spread through the damp fractures of the excavation. The visible plants are its upper surfaces: moss, liverwort, thread-stems, pale climbing growths that seem unrelated until they are disturbed. Beneath them, everything is joined. Water carries one message. Vibration carries another. A pressure against a root bridge is felt hundreds of yards away as a small, local change in the dark.

The colony has no center in the ordinary sense. There is no nest, no queen, no chamber where the whole of it resides. It is distributed through the mine, strongest around the leaks and warm machinery, thinnest across the dry spoil. Its body is interrupted but never entirely broken. A dying patch near one pump may be answered by sudden growth beneath a distant lamp. A severed root can remain active for days, drinking and transmitting through its pale interior before it finally darkens.

It knows the mine by what the mine does.

It knows which pumps are healthy by their rhythm. It knows the difference between a conveyor carrying stone and one running empty. It knows the gradual loosening of a support bolt, the tremor of a train approaching behind the wall, the heavier vibration that comes before a roof fall. The machinery is not an enemy to it. The machinery is weather.

Heat is sunrise. Condensation is rain. Leaking pipes are rivers with unreliable seasons. Lamps are small, artificial moons beneath which the colony sends up its most delicate growth.

When the fans begin, the hanging stems bend together. This is not merely a response to air. The colony uses the moving current to spread spores and scent, carrying news from one green island to another. When a gate opens high above and cold air drops through the galleries, leaves close across the wet floor. The root systems contract. For a few minutes, the colony becomes still enough to seem dead.

Then it begins again.

Its flowers, if they can be called flowers, are nearly invisible. They appear as translucent cups along the undersides of leaves, or as pale beads gathered in the seams of root bridges. They open only when the mine enters one of its rare quieter periods. Their smell is faintly metallic, like wet coins and crushed fern. Insects are drawn to them, though there are so few insects that pollination is slow and uncertain. The colony compensates with persistence. It can wait through failed seasons. It can maintain a living filament in a crack no wider than a finger until conditions return.

There are older portions of it beyond the active workings.

These have been sealed behind concrete doors, collapsed galleries, and flooded shafts. Some survive in complete darkness, feeding on minerals and the warmth of buried pipes. Others have thickened into strange, blind forests around abandoned machinery. The workers who enter those sections sometimes report that the growth seems to have changed since the previous inspection: a root now crosses a passage it did not cross before; a mat has withdrawn from a wall; condensation gathers in places where there is no obvious source.

They say this because they need a language for something that notices them.

The colony does not think as a person thinks. It has no face with which to watch, and no voice capable of answering through the industrial thunder. Yet it distinguishes between the living and the dead. A worker standing beneath a lamp causes roots to warm and loosen. A body left in a flooded hollow is gradually enclosed, not aggressively, but with the patient intimacy of growth. Leaves gather around clothing. Fine stems enter seams and cuffs. Mineral water is drawn through cloth. Within months, the outline is softened into the floor.

It also recognizes returning things.

A particular machine may be followed by a particular pattern of vibration. A certain worker may always pause beside the same pipe, bringing warmth, salt, skin, and breath. The plants around that route become thicker. Their leaves open before the footsteps arrive. If the worker stops coming, the route remains green for a long time afterward.

The colony has learned the shape of absence.

It is not hostile, but it is not harmless. When a gallery dries, it will cross the metal, climb the insulation, and enter the warm cavities behind electrical cabinets. When a wall cracks, roots press into the opening and widen it by fractions. When a pump fails, growth gathers around the dead housing and drinks whatever residue remains. Given enough time, it will not destroy the mine by force. It will make the mine unnecessary to itself.

The first sign of its attention is usually a change in arrangement.

A line of moss appears beside a particular cable. Pale stems gather around a locked door. A root bridge thickens beneath the route most often used by people, while the old path beside it dries and crumbles. Nothing moves while it is watched. Nothing announces intention. The colony simply alters the conditions around a place until the place becomes easier to enter, harder to leave, or no longer worth entering at all.

At the center of one abandoned chamber, beneath a lamp that should have gone dark years ago, it has made something close to a heart.

There is no beating organ. Instead, roots from many directions have joined around the base of the support column, forming a dense, wet mound. Warmth from the buried electrical line passes through it. Water drips from the lamp housing. The growth there is darker and broader than elsewhere, its surfaces smooth as old leather. Every few minutes, when a distant engine starts, the mound tightens almost imperceptibly. A faint tremor travels outward through the floor.

The chamber answers.

Leaves close in the far galleries. Condensation shivers on the pipes. Somewhere beneath the rail bed, a thin root shifts against stone.

The colony is listening—not for words, which cannot survive in that place, but for pressure, heat, water, and change. It is listening to the machines that accidentally feed it, to the rock that confines it, and to the footsteps of the temporary creatures who believe they are the only ones living underground.

It has no urgency.

The mine is young. The colony is patient.
