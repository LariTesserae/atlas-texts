What lives on the high plateau has learned not to expect the world to remain habitable.

Life there is not arranged around permanence. It is arranged around arrival: the arrival of warmth through stone, of moisture on a cooling surface, of pressure in the deep earth, of another body crossing the dark. Nearly every living thing is built to endure long intervals of scarcity and to become suddenly, intensely active when the land offers anything at all.

The smallest inhabitants are the first to answer.

They live in the mineral mats, under plates of stone, inside the porous rims of old vents. Some are no more than pale threads webbed through damp cracks, drawing nourishment from the chemistry of the ground. Others form low, dark crusts that resemble tarnish on metal. They spread in thin films around condensation fields and thermal seams, gathering what little water arrives and holding it in their ridged surfaces. Their colors are muted—iron red, soot black, bruised violet—but when disturbed they give off brief electrical flickers, so faint they seem at first like afterimages in the eye.

The mats are grazed by innumerable small things.

There are pale, many-legged animals with bodies divided into overlapping shields. They move in slow, synchronized ripples, scraping the leathery growth from stone with mouths made for abrasion. Their undersides are soft and translucent; their backs are dark enough to disappear against the ground. When the rock warms beneath them, they stop feeding and press themselves flat, allowing the heat to pass through their bodies. When a fissure opens nearby, they turn toward it without apparent haste, following the chemical change in the air.

There are others that resemble scraps of torn membrane blown along the ground. They leap rather than fly, using broad folded limbs to cross the gaps between slabs. Their bodies are almost weightless, their internal organs reduced to narrow bundles, and their senses are scattered across the skin. They can feel the vibration of a footfall through several meters of stone. A passing grazer sends them plunging into cracks. A distant collapse draws them out in swarms.

When condensation arrives, they emerge by the thousands.

The wet places become briefly crowded. Small grazers gather first, converging on the silver films with a speed that seems impossible in such a slow landscape. Their feeding leaves the mats scored in parallel lines. Larger, low-bodied scavengers follow, sliding between them with their bellies against the ground. They are long and jointless in appearance, though beneath their dark hides are many folded limbs. Their bodies absorb heat from the rock and release it through narrow vents along their flanks. They can remain still for days, waiting for the next concentration of movement.

Above this activity move the winged animals.

They are not birds, and the dark gives them little reason to become anything like birds. Their wings are thin, translucent sails stretched between jointed spars. They navigate by the glow of warm stone, by the electrical disturbances of living tissue, and by the changing pressure of the atmosphere. At a distance, a migrating swarm resembles a slow constellation. Up close, each animal is a narrow body wrapped in membrane, with no visible eyes and a head crowded with sensory filaments.

They descend when the ground sweats.

Their feeding is almost silent. They settle on the rock, open their bodies along the underside, and draw water directly through porous tissues. Some harvest the mineral mats. Some collect the small grazers clinging to them. Some attach themselves to the backs of larger animals and drink from the condensation gathered in the seams of their armor. When disturbed, they lift all at once, making the air tremble with a dry, papery rush.

The larger animals are made for distance.

They travel between thermal basins over routes that no single creature could have discovered alone. The routes are preserved in behavior: a turn taken around a ridge, a descent into a particular ravine, a long crossing over exposed stone where no food grows. Generations pass them down through pressure, scent, vibration, and the memory of the body. Young animals follow older ones until the landscape has entered their muscles.

Some are tall and narrow, standing on four, six, or eight articulated legs. Their bodies are held high above the heated ground, and their limbs are insulated at the joints by dense folds of black tissue. They carry no visible fat. Instead, their sides contain slow reservoirs of chemically stored energy, filled during the brief abundance after rain. Their mouths are small and precise, suited to stripping growth from cracks. Along their necks are long sensory fronds that read the temperature of distant stone.

They do not look toward the horizon. They feel it.

A far-off vent opening produces a pressure change too slight for most animals to notice. These tall walkers turn toward it. A hidden field of condensation forming beyond a ridge alters the mineral scent of the air. They change course. They may cross hundreds of miles for a place that will remain wet for only an hour.

Others are broad, low creatures, armored in overlapping plates. They move slowly, but the slowness is deceptive. Their bodies are vast storage chambers, able to hold water, heat, symbiotic organisms, and the accumulated chemistry of years. Dark gardens grow across their backs: mats of vegetation, pale fungal fans, small burrowing grazers, and colonies of luminous bacteria. The animals carry entire miniature ecosystems from one basin to another.

When they pass, the land seems to move.

Their armor is not smooth. It is terraced and cracked, with shallow hollows that collect condensation. Small creatures shelter there. Winged animals land between the plates. In return, the gardens feed the carrier with compounds drawn from mineral dust and waste. The animal’s slow migration disperses spores and eggs over distances that wind alone could never manage.

Predators wait along these routes.

They are not built for pursuit. The plateau is too open, and speed would waste more energy than it saved. A predator may lie beside a seam for a week, its body cooling until it becomes indistinguishable from the iron-dark stone. Its limbs fold into the outline of a broken slab. Its sensory organs remain hidden beneath the skin.

Then some vibration arrives through the ground.

The predator unfolds without warning. Fronds open along its neck and sides, tasting the tremor. It does not hunt by sight. It listens to the sequence of impacts: the uneven rhythm of a grazer, the heavy regular descent of a plated animal, the frantic scattering of small bodies around fresh moisture. It chooses one disturbance from all the others and begins to walk.

There is no rush. It advances with a patient certainty, adjusting its path by the pressure of the ground. Its feet are broad and soft, leaving shallow impressions that fill with condensation. If it reaches its prey, the attack is brief. If it misses, it stops and becomes stone again.

Some predators are brighter than their prey.

They cultivate dim organs beneath their skin, not to illuminate the land but to imitate the glow of a fissure. Small animals approach the false warmth. The hunter opens along its length and takes them into a chamber lined with gripping cilia. Other hunters remain absolutely dark, waiting at the edges of naturally luminous feeding grounds. They are visible only when they move, and even then their outlines seem to belong to the terrain.

There are scavengers for every failure.

When a large animal dies, the body is not left long. The scent moves through the stone. Burrowing organisms rise from below. Pale crawlers enter the joints. Membranous feeders arrive from the air. The mineral mats spread toward the blood-rich ground. Even the larger predators may gather, not in a pack but in a loose circle of stillness.

Nothing wastes the body. Skin becomes shelter. Heat becomes a breeding signal. Dissolved bone returns to the seams. Within a season, the carcass is only a darker patch of stone and a temporary abundance of small life.

The most remarkable inhabitants are perhaps those that live in the vents.

They are narrow, blind organisms that descend into the warm fissures and feed on the chemistry rising from below. Their bodies are segmented into heat-resistant chambers. Some carry colonies of luminous bacteria in transparent organs, so that their movement through the cracks appears as a drifting blue-white pulse. Others anchor themselves to the walls and extend long feeding curtains into the mineral flow.

They are not plants, though they remain fixed. They are not animals, though they consume and reproduce. They exist where the temperature would kill most surface life, turning pressure and dissolved metal into tissue. When a vent closes, they retreat downward. When it opens elsewhere, their descendants appear in the new warmth, carried through subterranean channels too narrow for any larger body.

The plateau has creatures that spend most of their lives below the surface as well. They move through old watercourses, thermal shafts, and chambers formed by pressure. Their presence is sometimes betrayed by a line of settling plates, a faint vibration underfoot, or a ridge that brightens from within as their feeding chambers pass beneath it. They may never see the open land. Their world is stone, heat, chemical gradients, and the distant thunder of surface weather.

Yet all of them are connected to the surface cycles.

The smallest rain calls out the grazers. The grazers draw the scavengers. The scavengers draw the hunters. The hunters leave behind bodies, heat, and altered minerals. The minerals feed the mats. The mats hold the condensation. The condensation gathers the next generation of animals.

The land does not support life so much as pulse life through itself.

During long dry intervals, the plateau seems empty. The mats contract into hard dark scales. Stalks close their leaves and become brittle rods. Small animals seal themselves into cracks, lowering their metabolism until their bodies are almost mineral. Larger creatures continue walking, one distant shape at a time, following routes whose destinations may not yet exist.

Then the ground warms.

A fissure opens somewhere beyond the ridge. Vapor enters the atmosphere. The first droplets form beneath an overhang. A buried animal wakes. The stone begins to shine with blue-white light. Across miles of black-brown plain, bodies turn toward the change.

For a while, the emptiness folds inward.

The plateau fills with movement: armored grazers, low scavengers, bright-winged swarms, tall walkers arriving from the dark, and patient predators stepping out of their resemblance to stone. The vegetation opens its segmented leaves with a thousand dry clicks. Moisture runs briefly through the seams. The old earth exhales, and everything that lives there answers.
