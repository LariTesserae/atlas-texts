They are not a numerous people.

They are the remainder of a people, or perhaps the beginning of one: a few thousand inhabitants distributed through the terraces and industrial quarters, living so deeply among the old systems that it is difficult to tell where their dwellings end and the city begins. They do not possess the basin so much as maintain an agreement with it. Their roofs collect what water the air will surrender. Their foundations avoid the oldest fractures. Their roads are laid where the mountain has already permitted passage. When a wall begins to lean, they do not always straighten it. Sometimes they build another wall beside it and allow the first to become part of the slope.

They are narrow-shouldered and compact, shaped by cold, altitude, and the practical need to pass through doors designed to keep out weather. Their clothing is layered and close-fitting, made from dull, water-resistant materials that take on the colors of stone, moss, wet concrete, and old metal. Hoods are common. So are gloves with the fingertips reinforced for handling cables, valves, and frozen locks. In the dim streets, a person can stand beside a retaining wall and become nearly indistinguishable from it.

They have learned not to waste motion.

There is little casual wandering. People move with the purposeful economy of those who know that every journey crosses a gradient, a drainage trench, a broken stair, or an exposed place where the wind can take the warmth from the body. They climb slowly and descend carefully. They pause beneath covered walkways while the rain passes. They read the condition of moss on a wall, the trembling of a cable, the angle of vapor leaving a vent. A resident can tell from the sound of a pump whether water is entering the lower channels too quickly. Another can judge the coming weather by the way the reeds click in the ravines.

They do not speak loudly.

Sound carries strangely in the basin. A voice thrown across an open terrace may return from a rock face minutes later, altered by fog and distance. People call to one another with brief phrases, whistles, or flashes from hooded lamps. In enclosed quarters, they speak softly, often pausing to listen between sentences. Their language has accumulated words for conditions that elsewhere might be dismissed as minor: a wall sweating from within, a fog that has risen too early, a machine that turns with a tired rhythm, a patch of grass growing from a crack that should be dry.

Many of them work in maintenance, though maintenance is too small a word for what they do.

They enter the factories beneath the city and inspect the slow turbines. They descend into shafts where water moves through stone channels older than the buildings above them. They clear roots from electrical cabinets, replace corroded bearings, patch ceramic insulators, and scrape moss from sensors. They walk the elevated service lines in bad weather with safety lamps fixed to their chests. They keep records of pressure, condensation, soil movement, wind speed, and the gradual migration of plants into places once thought secure.

Some care for the reservoirs. Some tend the wind machines. Some work in the upper terraces, repairing walls and guiding runoff into holding basins. Others live among the abandoned factories, where their occupation is less visible: a light behind wired glass, a kettle warming beside a vent, a line of drying clothes in a disused control room.

The old industrial systems are not entirely understood by the people who use them. Manuals survive in fragments. Diagrams have been redrawn so many times that the original symbols have become decorative. Some machinery has no known beginning and no clear means of stopping. It continues because it has continued, and because the basin depends on its persistence.

The inhabitants treat these machines with a restrained respect.

They do not worship them exactly, but they leave offerings in the form of oil, clean filters, new wiring, and careful attention. A turbine that has run for generations is spoken of as one speaks of an elder who no longer answers questions but still remembers the shape of the household. Children are taken to hear the lower generators. They place their palms against the stone and feel the vibration rise into their bones. They are taught that a silent machine is not necessarily a dead one, and that anything which has stopped must be approached slowly.

There are children, though fewer than the buildings seem to require.

They grow up in the stairways and covered passages, learning the city as a vertical maze. They know which roofs can bear weight, which drainage channels are safe to cross, and where the warm air escapes from beneath the factory floors. They collect pale insects from the vents and keep them briefly in glass jars before releasing them. They plant seeds in cracks where adults would see only structural damage. During the first frost, they mark the places where water has gathered and freeze into translucent plates.

Their games involve disappearance.

A child hides in the fog between two lamps. Another climbs through a fern-filled stairwell and emerges onto a roof. They leave small signs for one another in moss, condensation, and chalk: a line on a pipe, three stones turned face-down, a leaf placed inside a lock. The games are quiet because the city is quiet, but they are not solemn. Laughter exists here; it is simply swallowed quickly by weather.

The dead are not taken far.

There is little room for cemeteries in the basin, and the ground is too active to be trusted with permanent borders. Some bodies are placed in stone chambers above the highest terraces, where the wind and cold preserve them. Others are returned to constructed wetlands, their remains sealed beneath reed beds and dark water. Names are engraved on retaining walls, not in neat rows but along the existing courses of stone. Over time, moss covers the letters. The living do not clear it away unless the name is needed.

Because the mountain is always altering the city, the dead remain present as part of its maintenance. A wall repaired by someone’s descendants may contain the dust of that person. A reservoir may draw through soil mixed with old ash. Roots pass through former rooms. No one finds this disturbing. The basin has never respected the boundaries between person, building, and ground.

There are also others.

Small animals move through the city’s hidden margins: black-backed things with narrow paws, pale beneath the throat, nesting in insulation and abandoned ducts. They emerge at night to drink from condensation gathered on pipes. Their tracks appear beside reservoirs and vanish beneath grates. No one agrees on what they are called. Some say they are descended from animals brought by the first settlers. Some say they came up from the ravines before the roads existed. Some insist they are not animals at all, but a kind of moving mold with bones.

The inhabitants leave them alone.

Insects gather where the factories breathe warmth. Translucent moths cling to lamp cages. Long-legged forms with membranous wings appear after rain and disappear before the ground dries. They are not numerous enough to become a nuisance, and so they are tolerated, even protected. A vent may be screened to keep out snow while allowing the pale insects through. A room may be abandoned for a season because a colony has settled in its ceiling.

The plants are given more consideration than the animals.

There are people whose work consists entirely of watching growth. They inspect old buildings for roots, not to remove them but to determine whether the structure can be allowed to yield. They transplant moss from collapsing terraces to new drainage beds. They breed the dark shrubs that can survive in polluted channels. They collect the seeds of the pale reeds and scatter them along the upper ravines, where the plants will hold the gravel in place.

These workers speak of vegetation as if it possesses intention.

They say a vine is searching. They say a tree has chosen a foundation. They say moss remembers the shape of a stone. Their statements may be metaphor, or they may reflect a knowledge too intimate to be divided into science and superstition. They have seen roots enter sealed chambers, cross beneath streets, and emerge around the feet of a machine that has not moved in decades. They know that a plant can wait longer than a family, longer than a government, longer than the name of a district.

At night, the inhabitants close their doors, but not completely.

A door left entirely sealed collects too much moisture. Windows are opened a fraction to let the interior breathe into the cold. Pipes tick in the walls. Water passes beneath floors. The people sleep under heavy coverings while the city continues its cycles around them.

Some sleep in former offices. Some in apartments built into the factory walls. Some in rooms reached only by ladders, behind panels marked with faded warnings. Their possessions are few but carefully maintained: lamps, tools, old photographs, seed packets, waterproof books, instruments for measuring things that may no longer be officially measured.

They keep track of absences.

A light that fails to appear in a familiar window. A pump that has missed two cycles. A person who has not crossed a particular bridge for several mornings. The city is too spread out for constant surveillance, but its inhabitants have developed a sense of distributed attention. Everyone knows the ordinary sounds of their district. Any alteration travels through conversation, maintenance reports, and the informal networks of those who work the night shifts.

If someone vanishes into the mist, a search begins without urgency but rarely without conclusion.

They follow tracks through wet silt. They check the old funicular tunnels, the reservoir edges, the open ground above the retaining walls. They inspect machinery spaces and drainage beds. Often the missing person is found asleep in a warm service room, or delayed by a broken cable, or standing above the basin watching the weather. Sometimes they are not found. In that case, their name is added to a wall, and their tools are redistributed.

The inhabitants do not expect permanence from one another.

They know that buildings sink, roads become channels, and whole districts may be abandoned when the systems beneath them change. A home is not a fixed place but a maintained interval: a room kept dry, a stove that still works, a path that remains passable. Belonging means knowing how to repair what will eventually fail.

Some among them believe the city is alive.

Not in the sentimental sense, and not because it has a single mind. They mean that it breathes through its vents, circulates water through its buried channels, sleeps and wakes in cycles of pressure and light. They mean that the roots, machines, fog, stone, and people form a body too large for any one part to understand.

Others reject this entirely. They say the city is only a collection of systems, and that calling it alive makes people careless. A machine is not a lung. A reservoir is not a heart. A root is not a nerve. The basin does not love them, and the mountain does not remember.

Yet even those who deny the city’s life will place a hand on a vibrating wall as they pass.

They will wait when the fog thickens.

They will listen for the pumps.

And when the wind machines turn above the upper streets, their blades moving with slow, uneven patience against the dark shoulder of the mountain, they will look up—not because they expect an answer, but because something is still keeping time.
