They are people of thresholds.

They live in cells cut into the mountain, each dwelling no larger than necessary: a sleeping recess, a work surface, a water wall, a place to hang the dark pressure-clothes. Their homes do not belong entirely to them. The walls monitor their breathing. The floor adjusts to their weight. At night, the ceiling may open a narrow blue aperture so they can see the weatherless brightness beyond the rim, though most residents keep it closed. The mountain is too near there. It seems less like a view than an exposed nerve.

No one is born in the city without being registered by the rock.

The first sound an infant learns is not a voice but the quiet confirmation of a pressure door. The second is water moving behind a wall. Children are raised in clusters, though rarely in the same place for long. They learn the city through supervised crossings: which bridges lean when the mountain shifts, which stairwells will change their inclination, which drains carry clean condensation and which should never be touched. They are taught to read the small signals that precede an alteration—the tremor in a railing, the warmth beneath a floor, the faint color change in a seam.

A child who can feel a new fracture before the instruments detect it is not considered gifted. They are considered necessary.

The residents do not name streets as people elsewhere might. They name them by behavior. The stair that steepens. The bridge that remembers. The passage where the air goes thin. The chamber that closes during rain. Their maps are not pictures of fixed locations but records of agreements: where the rock has permitted passage, where water has chosen to descend, where the city has learned to brace itself.

They walk alone, but not from loneliness. Solitude is the ordinary condition of the place. To encounter another person in transit is intimate enough to require acknowledgment. A glance, two fingers lifted from the wrist, the brief green flash of a suit’s status light. Conversation is usually conducted through the walls and cables, in small transmitted pulses that can be heard only by the intended recipient. Voices are reserved for sealed rooms, for children, for the sick, and for moments when the mountain is changing too quickly for instruments to interpret.

Their bodies are pale from the absence of open sun. Their eyes have adapted to mineral light, and their hands bear tiny scars from contact with raw edges. They are compact, economical people, built by generations of low gravity and careful motion. They do not waste gestures. They do not hurry unless a system has instructed them to. Even fear is expressed quietly: a hand flattened against the wall, a head turned toward a sound below, the immediate clearing of a route.

Every resident has a trade, though no trade is entirely separate from the rest. Some tend the water. Some inspect the pressure fields beneath the bridges. Some enter the unfinished regions where new shelves have emerged and return carrying mineral samples, damaged instruments, or messages from survey machines. There are those who maintain the sleeping chambers and those who maintain the memories stored in them. There are people whose work is to listen: to the long vibrations passing through the mountain, to distinguish settling from splitting, cooling from collapse.

The most respected are not commanders but interpreters.

They stand in observation wells with one hand against the stone and wait. Their instruments translate the mountain into figures, but the figures are never enough. A wall can be sound and still be about to fail. A seam can be harmless and still be opening toward something deep. Interpreters learn the difference by attending to all of it—the water, the temperature, the movement of dust, the rhythm of distant machinery, the particular silence that gathers before the rock makes room for itself.

The city itself lives in them as much as they live in it.

It has no single voice. Its intelligence is distributed through drains, doors, support fields, transit platforms, and the vast responsive membranes behind the walls. It knows the weight of every resident and the customary routes of each body. It knows when someone has paused too long beside a drop, when a worker has failed to return from a shaft, when a child is following a maintenance organism into a forbidden vent.

It does not speak in sentences. It closes a door. Warms a handrail. Delays a lift. Draws a line of light along the safer side of a platform.

Some residents believe the city is only an immense system of precautions. Others think it has become something more patient and more difficult to understand. They leave offerings to it—not food, since the city has no use for food, but repaired instruments, recovered fragments of old construction, small objects placed in sealed niches along the drains. A person who survives a collapse may return and touch the nearest wall in thanks. The wall does not respond, though it may carry the warmth of the hand for several seconds.

The pale maintenance animals are called witnesses by the children.

They are not native to the mountain, if anything here can be called native. They were made long ago to clean chemical residue from machinery, then altered themselves in ways no one fully understands. Their translucent bodies show blue pulses beneath the joints. They nest in heated cavities and emerge during periods of system stress, moving toward the places where something has begun to fail. A resident who sees one on an open stair does not disturb it. They wait, watch where it goes, and report the direction.

When the animals gather, people evacuate.

When the rain arrives, the city’s inhabitants become briefly visible to one another. They stand under projections and maintenance roofs, their faces turned upward as droplets fall through the chamber. The rain is the closest thing they have to a public celebration. No one speaks much. Children sometimes reach out and let the water strike their gloves. Older residents watch the halos around the droplets and remember other storms, other sectors, other generations of the city that have since been sealed behind new rock.

There are songs, but they are low and narrow in range, made to travel through stone without becoming lost. They are sung during funerals and during foundation work. The dead are not placed in the mountain immediately. For a time, their names are carried through the infrastructure, stamped into maintenance records, reflected in the lights of transit spines. Then a chamber is opened—sometimes a natural hollow, sometimes a newly formed shelf—and the body is given back to the rock.

The residents believe this is not an ending.

The mountain is always making room. It raises clean terraces beneath abandoned routes. It encloses old rooms in blue-white planes. It carries warmth downward and water sideways. A person may disappear into a shaft and return changed by distance, by pressure, by whatever machinery waits beyond the mapped corridors. Some return with new mineral growths fused to their equipment. Some return with memories that belong partly to the transit system. Some never return, but the city continues to reserve a route for them, opening a door at intervals, checking the air.

This is how the people understand belonging: not as possession, and not as permanence, but as continued accommodation.

The mountain does not love them. It does not hate them. It does not know the difference between a dwelling and a fracture except by how each responds to pressure. The residents have built their lives inside that indifference, making tenderness out of maintenance. They repair what shifts. They clear what clogs. They wait when the systems instruct them to wait. They place their hands against the walls and feel, beneath the engineered surfaces, the slow and immense movement of the stone.

At night—though the city has no true night—they sleep while the drains carry water beneath them.

Somewhere in the dark above, new rock is lifting.

Somewhere below, an old passage is narrowing.

And throughout the chamber, behind sealed doors and translucent walls, the inhabitants breathe in the thin, cold air of a place that is still becoming.
