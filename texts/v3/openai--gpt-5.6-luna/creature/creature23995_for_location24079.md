They are not a people in the ordinary sense.

They have no houses separate from their bodies, no clothing separate from their skins, no streets that do not also serve as veins. They inhabit the settlement the way blood inhabits a heart: passing through it, pausing in its chambers, remembering its shape.

At first, they might be mistaken for vines.

They are long and low, jointed creatures with pale, rootlike limbs and soft bodies the color of wet wood. Most remain beneath the moss, where they move through the warm mat in slow, coordinated currents. When they rise, they do so by unfolding. A ridge of pavement lifts. A bundle of roots loosens. A narrow figure draws itself out of the green, shedding clinging tendrils and droplets that may not have come from any rain.

They have no faces as such. Their heads are crowns of small leaves, sensory fronds, and dark openings that close against dust. They taste the air. They feel footsteps through the walls. They recognize one another by changes in pressure, by the chemicals released when a stem is bruised, by the pattern of a shadow crossing a courtyard.

They are not numerous. The settlement was built for far more of them than remain.

Those who still live here are scattered through the architecture, often mistaken for part of it. One may lie flat along a roof-rib for a season, its body thickening into the supporting structure. Another may stand motionless beside a pillar, its limbs sunk into the living fibers, listening through the roots. Some have become so thoroughly joined with the city that it is impossible to say where an individual ends. A wall that leans toward a path may be turning to watch. A stairway that grows a new step may be extending a hand.

They do not speak with voices.

Their speech is the constant biological clamor of the hollow: leaf against leaf, pod against pod, root grinding slowly through stone, insects clicking inside the walls. Meaning travels in pressure and rhythm. A tremor beneath the feet can be warning, greeting, grief, or instruction. The great cry that rises from the central plaza is not merely a sound but an act of assembly. Every surviving inhabitant hears it through its body and answers with whatever portion of the city it has become.

During the cry, they remember themselves.

For a few moments, the root-bound wake from their long stillness. Fronds turn. Hidden bodies pull free of the moss. Pale heads emerge from doorways and drainage channels. Shapes move across the roofs, too quick to follow, or too slow, as though each is crossing several distances at once. The entire settlement becomes a single nervous system, and the inhabitants are its scattered thoughts.

They tend one another, though tending is difficult to distinguish from eating.

The young feed on the newest growth of the old. The old open their bodies to shelter the young during dry weather. A dying inhabitant may be absorbed into a wall, its memories carried in the sap for generations. In this way, nobody quite disappears. Their gestures persist as bends in the roots. Their names survive as flowering patterns, recurring sounds, or rooms that always contain one more doorway than they should.

There are larger inhabitants in the upper green.

They resemble long-bodied grazers, though their backs are ridged with leaves and their feet end in bundles of rootlets rather than toes. They move along the walls, trimming excess growth. Their mouths are hidden beneath folding plates of bark. When alarmed, they become almost indistinguishable from the architecture, flattening themselves against a surface and allowing small flowers to open across their backs.

The winged creatures are not servants, exactly, but they belong to the settlement’s household. They live inside the seed-houses and carry pollen, messages, and fragments of memory from chamber to chamber. When they emerge in black clouds, the city seems to exhale. Their wings produce a dry, rattling speech. They gather around anyone who has come from outside, tasting the stranger’s dust and the history of distant weather.

There are predators too. They pass through the high leaves as interruptions: a folding shadow, a sudden silence, a line of branches bending against the wind. The inhabitants do not hate them. Predation is one of the old agreements. What is taken returns through the roots. What survives carries the shape of what it escaped.

The most unsettling residents are those who still remember being separate.

They are found in the broad chambers around the central plaza, where the walls are veined like maps. These beings sit or kneel in the cool dampness, their bodies thin, their skin marked by pale green channels. They may once have been people. They may have planted the first living pillars, shaped the first root-paths, taught the walls to recognize a threshold.

Now they spend most of their time facing inward.

They place their hands against the walls and listen to the settlement’s buried traffic. Sometimes they rise and walk out into the lanes. Sometimes they emerge from a doorway that was not there a moment earlier. They are capable of speech, but their words arrive altered: delayed, repeated from another room, spoken in the voice of rain.

They know that the city is waiting.

Not for ordinary inhabitants. Those have already been replaced, preserved, or distributed through the green. It is waiting for the return of a particular kind of presence: the weight of many bodies in the lanes, the heat of breath beneath the roofs, the old gestures of gathering and trade. Perhaps the vanished people will come back. Perhaps something else will arrive and be accepted as an answer.

Until then, the residents maintain the shape of habitation.

They open the doors at the proper hours. They keep the paths clear. They raise roofs before storms. They fill the empty plaza with their collective cry so that the settlement will not forget it was once a place where someone might stand and be heard.

When rain comes, they climb.

They climb the pillars, the walls, the suspended rivers, following water that runs upward into the leaves. In the violet reflections beneath the roofs, some take on the forms they had long ago: upright, many-limbed, carrying tools that no longer exist. Others become children, or seeds, or immense green animals with human eyes.

Then the rain withdraws.

The inhabitants sink back into the settlement, one by one, until only the movement remains: roots tightening around joints, leaves turning toward an absent sun, hidden bodies passing beneath the pavement.

The city looks empty again.

But it is not empty. It is full of those who have given up the distinction between living in a place and being the place itself.
