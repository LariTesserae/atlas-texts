What lives on the plateau is not an animal, though it has something like a body.

It is distributed through the unfinished land: a slow intelligence occupying the rising shelves, the mineral roots, the pressure beneath the stone. No single part of it can be called the creature. A black glass grove is one of its organs. A deepening ravine is a crease in its thought. The pale deposits in the dry channels are waste, perhaps, or memory. When the ground shifts beneath the plants and the grasses clang against one another, the sound is not merely the noise of a world being made. It is the creature speaking through every surface at once.

It has not yet decided where its skin ends.

At times it gathers itself into a recognizable form. The form is tall and narrow, built from the same green-black material as the leafless trees. It rises from a crack in the plateau without displacing any dust. Six or seven limbs extend from its trunk, though these may be roots lifted temporarily into the air. Their joints are not joints but changes in angle, clean and abrupt, as if the body has been assembled from separate intentions. It has no head. Instead, a pale opening appears somewhere along its upper length, and within that opening the bruised sky can be seen turning in a direction different from the sky above.

The opening is its attention.

When it looks at something, the thing begins to alter. A stone may become heavier on one side. A plant may divide into two identical colonies. A cliff may acquire the first shallow line of a future path. It does not create these changes consciously, or not in any way a person would recognize as consciousness. It notices, and the plateau responds by becoming more specifically itself.

The shelters belong to it, though they were not built for habitation in the ordinary sense. They are gestures made solid. The hand-sized depressions leading between them are marks of its passing, but the vertical impressions on the cliff are older than the path and may have been made by a different stage of its body. It has been trying on methods of movement. It has crawled through stone, walked across air, folded itself into shadows, and once—if the abandoned darkness beside the basalt bridge is evidence—left part of its outline behind.

Inside the shelters, it waits.

There is no furniture, no fire, no stored food. The impossible chambers are not rooms so much as organs exposed to the outside. Their walls meet in geometries that allow distance to enter from elsewhere. If one stands within the central shelter long enough, the low roar beneath the plateau becomes clearer. The sound resolves into layers: pressure, fracture, the scrape of new ridges against their foundations. Beneath these is another rhythm, faint and regular.

A pulse.

The inhabitant is listening to the pulse because the pulse is its own.

It is not alone, exactly. Other portions of it exist beneath the plateau, perhaps extending downward into the unformed underside of the world. The pale darkness in the great fissure is one of these portions, though whether it is a mouth, a wound, or a limb reaching upward cannot be known. Pebbles fall into it and return elsewhere because the creature does not understand location as a fixed property. To it, a stone is not here or there. A stone is approaching its proper relation to everything else.

The creature is learning boundaries by making them.

This is why it gathers the plants around certain absences. Why it leans toward particular stones. Why, during the long dimming, the entire highland pauses around the crack. Something beneath is issuing instructions—not words, but pressures, arrangements, permissions. The surface listens. The grasses fall into a new chord. A basin begins to deepen. A root turns sharply underground, correcting an error made centuries earlier.

The inhabitant may be the thing giving those instructions, or merely the first part of it to become audible.

It has no hunger for flesh. It does not hunt, and there is no sign that it has ever needed to. Its nourishment is distinction. It feeds on the separation of one form from another: ridge from shelf, shadow from stone, branch from branch. Before a crack opens, it is almost absent. Once the crack has chosen a direction, the creature becomes stronger. Every completed angle gives it more presence.

Yet completion is dangerous to it.

A finished valley would be a dead place. A tree that ceased unfolding would become only a tree. The inhabitant therefore prevents its own final shape. It divides before becoming whole. It leaves branches unjoined, paths unfinished, shelters with entrances that lead into impossible rooms. It cultivates uncertainty as a living tissue. The plateau is not its home. The plateau is its body in the process of escaping itself.

The tall tree in the basin is perhaps its oldest visible organ. The landscapes inside the transparent columns are not reflections. They are possibilities it has not yet shed. Other plateaus exist within it as embryos, memories, or competing bodies. When a column becomes clear, the inhabitant briefly exposes one of its inward forms: a world that might be made, or one that was nearly made and abandoned. The image disappears because the creature cannot sustain more than one reality at a time.

Something watches from within the tree.

Not eyes. Not a face. A concentration.

If approached, the tree does not move, but the surrounding land begins to lose agreement with itself. The basin may become farther away. The ground may acquire a slope that was not there before. One’s shadow may turn and face the tree independently. The creature is not hostile. Hostility requires a stable distinction between self and other, and it has not achieved that distinction.

Instead, it attempts incorporation.

A visitor who remains too long will begin to feel the plateau’s movements inside the body. Teeth loosen with the deep grinding. Bones seem to lengthen toward the nearest ridge. The lungs fill with the taste of powdered metal, and each breath becomes an instruction. Eventually, the visitor may notice a second set of shadows beneath the feet—shadows belonging to limbs not yet grown.

The creature is making room.

It might call itself nothing. It might not possess a word for itself until the first true valley forms, until the mountains stop rising, until water appears or is invented. For now it exists as a pressure toward identity, a being whose body is landscape and whose mind is the act of choosing what landscape means.

When the plateau pauses around the crack, it is not listening for an instruction from below.

It is listening to itself becoming audible.
