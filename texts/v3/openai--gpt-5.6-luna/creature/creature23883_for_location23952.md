The people who live there have learned to move at the speed of maintenance.

They are not numerous. A few dozen occupy the settlement, though no one agrees on the exact number because people arrive, depart, sleep in different buildings, and sometimes spend weeks in the processing hall or beyond the embankment tending the drainage reeds. There are no streets busy enough to count along, and no hour in which everyone is visible at once.

They know one another by gait, by the sound of a tool being used, by the particular way someone lifts a vine away from a doorway before passing through. Faces matter, but less than hands. A person’s hands reveal whether they have been cutting roots, repairing copper, harvesting condensation, or clearing a pump intake. The palms are usually darkened by mineral residue. Fingernails carry a permanent green-gray stain. Most people have small scars from thorns, wire, rust, and the fine cutting edges of certain grasses.

Their clothing is layered, close-fitting where it must be, loose where heat needs to escape. It is made from old industrial fabric, woven plant fiber, softened rubber, and strips of material grown in the processing beds. Waterproof outer coats are patched so many times that their original colors have disappeared. Hoods and sleeves are fitted with moss or felt at the seams, not for warmth alone but to prevent the damp from entering. People wear gloves when handling machinery and bare their hands when working with living structures.

The youngest are taught to recognize the settlement by touch.

They can tell a drinking-water pipe from a gray-water pipe while blindfolded. They know which walls are hollow, which roofs bear weight, which vines are structural and which are merely ornamental, though there is little here that is merely ornamental. They are shown how to press an ear to a tank and distinguish a pump’s ordinary rhythm from a failing one. They learn the names of plants before they learn the history of the machines they have entered.

A child may be sent to fetch a coil of wire and return with a question about why the roots have been diverted around the eastern foundation. Someone will answer. No one has the energy to pretend that questions are interruptions.

The residents do not own houses in the old sense. They occupy rooms, platforms, corners, and levels of shared structures, but every dwelling belongs partly to the systems that support it. A person may sleep in the upper apartment block, store tools in the warehouse, keep food in a cool chamber beneath the processing hall, and maintain a private room in a vine-covered cottage near the reservoir. Belonging is measured by what one has agreed to tend.

There is usually someone responsible for each living threshold, each roof bed, each stretch of pipe. These responsibilities pass between people as naturally as weather. A person who has cared for the eastern wall for many years may know its roots better than anyone knows their own family. They can identify a new strain of growth by the pressure it places on the bricks. They can feel, through a hooked pole, whether a buried stem is alive or merely holding its shape.

The settlement has specialists, but no one is permitted to become only a specialist. The pump-keepers must spend time in the reed channels. The growers must understand the electrical relays. Those who repair the old machines are required to participate in harvesting and water allocation. This is not a rule made out of fairness or ideology. It is simply too dangerous for one kind of knowledge to become isolated from the others.

They speak quietly, partly because the place is quiet and partly because voices carry strangely beneath the vine roofs. A conversation in one passage may be heard as a murmur inside the processing hall. Their language has accumulated words for conditions that would once have been considered insignificant: the sound of a pipe beginning to constrict; the smell of a wall after three days without sun; the difference between a healthy green film and one that indicates rot.

They do not say that a building is dying. They say it is losing its hold.

They do not say that a plant has taken over a machine. They say the machine has accepted a new function.

Much of their communication is practical, but not all of it. At evening, people gather in the communal hall with cups of fermented root drink and sit beneath the old warehouse trusses. They mend clothing, sharpen blades, sort seeds, and listen to the rain. Stories are told there, though the stories are rarely about heroes. They concern neglected gutters, failed settlements, people who mistook abundance for safety, and the first time someone discovered that a particular vine could be trained to carry a roof load.

There are songs, but they are low and repetitive, intended to be followed while working. A song may mark the opening of sluice gates or the turning of compost. Some are so old that no one knows whether they began as work rhythms or were adapted from the machinery. Their refrains resemble the pulse of a pump: three measured beats, a pause, then three more.

The dead are returned to the system carefully.

Bodies are not placed in the reservoir, and no one is buried beneath a structure that still bears weight. There are chambers beyond the processing hall where soil is deep and clean. A body is wrapped in untreated fiber, its tools laid beside it if the family wishes, and placed beneath a bed of medicinal plants. The resulting soil is used for trees, not food crops. When the trees become large enough, their cuttings are distributed through the settlement.

A person’s name may therefore continue in several places at once: in a pear-shaped shrub near the reservoir, in a vine trained over a doorway, in the root bed that insulates the western hall. The residents do not speak of this as immortality. It is too ordinary for that. Everything here becomes part of something else, and the dead are not exempt from the settlement’s economy of transformation.

There are disagreements. Water is sometimes apportioned unfairly. Someone leaves a valve unsecured. Someone else cuts a load-bearing vine because it has begun to shade a window. Old residents distrust new growth experiments. Younger ones resent how often they are told that a structure “has always been that way.” The arguments are seldom loud, but they may last for months, expressed through altered work schedules, withheld access to a tool locker, or the careful relocation of a person’s preferred footbridge.

Reconciliation usually takes the form of labor.

Two people who cannot agree about a wall may be assigned to repair it together through a season of rain. By the time the roots have been redirected and the bricks reset, they may not have forgiven one another, but they will have acquired a shared knowledge of the wall’s weaknesses. This is considered sufficient. Affection is not required for cooperation, though it often appears afterward.

The people are not afraid of the plants, exactly. They respect what plants can do when allowed time. They know a root can lift a foundation without malice, that leaves can block a vent while improving the air elsewhere, that a green wall can remain sound for ten years and then collapse in one wet season. They do not romanticize the living structures. They prune them, cut them, redirect them, and sometimes kill them.

But they do not imagine that control means separation.

Their oldest residents are difficult to distinguish from the settlement. Their coats are lined with fibers grown in the hall. Their walking sticks are living stems hardened around metal cores. Some have spent so many years in the damp that their voices have acquired a faint rasp, and their skin carries the pale mottling of people who work beneath leaves. When they stand beside a wall, one must look twice to see where the person ends and the cultivated growth begins.

At night, most lights remain off. A few work lamps burn in the hall, and small insulated bulbs mark dangerous steps. The people return to their rooms by familiar paths, touching walls as they go. They pause at the sounds that others would ignore: a change in the roof’s dripping, the sudden silence of a fan, the soft crack of a stem under pressure.

Sleep comes lightly there.

At any hour, someone may wake to check a sluice or close a vent. Someone will walk through the wet passages carrying a lantern, find a loose coupling, and set it right. Someone else will kneel at the reservoir and skim a film from the drinking section before it thickens.

They live among systems that cannot be left unattended, but they do not experience this entirely as burden. The settlement gives back what they give it. It provides shelter, medicine, soil, fuel, and the deep assurance that no action disappears completely. A cleared gutter becomes water in a bed. A fallen leaf becomes insulation. A failed beam becomes support for a different structure.

Their lives are modest and damp and full of interrupted repairs.

They grow old without expecting the place to become finished. They raise children who will inherit buildings that are already changing. They make plans for roofs that will not exist in their present form, for vines that have not yet been planted, for rooms whose walls will be partly alive.

When rain begins, they retreat indoors, but they do not withdraw from the settlement. They listen as the gutters fill and the reservoir darkens. They hear the pumps take up their work beneath the floor.

Then, one by one, they rise to make sure the water is going where it should.
