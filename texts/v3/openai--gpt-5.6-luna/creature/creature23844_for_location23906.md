What lives in the notch is not a creature in the usual sense. It has no body that can be separated from the walls, no mouth, no eyes, no season in which it is born and another in which it dies. It is a congregation of mineral growths, and the congregation has learned to behave like one thing.

The oldest formations call themselves nothing. They have no need of names. They stand in the recesses where the air holds moisture longest: blue blades under overhangs, white fans along the shaded seams, amber needles gathered around the channels that descend from above. Each is fixed in place, but none is entirely still. They draw water from the air. They thicken by fractions too small for a person to see. They lean toward pressure, toward cold, toward the faintest accumulation of dampness in the stone.

Together, they make the notch’s body.

The formations are not all alike. Some are brittle and quick, appearing during a wet spell and collapsing before the next clear morning. Others have been growing for centuries. Their surfaces are cloudy with trapped dust and sealed bubbles. Within them, faint lines wander upward and sideways, marking the routes taken by ancient moisture. When sunlight passes through these older blades, it does not simply brighten them. It wakes their buried light. A formation in shadow may gleam after the sun has left it, as though remembering warmth.

The congregation has no central mind, but it possesses something like attention. Its growth gathers around water. A bead forming high on the wall is answered below by a whitening of the grooves, a thickening of the rims, a subtle raising of the floor. If a channel closes, moisture seeks another route, and the formations follow. The notch continually rearranges itself around whatever the mountain is willing to give.

When the pressure deep below shifts, the inhabitants hear it before anything else does. A vibration rises through the shelves. The blades tremble in their close-packed ranks. Some release a dry, brittle whisper; others bend without breaking. Then the floor opens along one of its black seams, and a little darkness appears beneath the pale mineral crust. The congregation does not resist. It collapses, settles, and begins again around the new edge.

The inhabitants are fed by weather, though they do not consume it. Cloud, pressure, frost, and sunlight pass through them and leave alterations behind. They keep what can be kept. Water becomes structure. Structure becomes debris. Debris becomes a new surface on which water may gather. Their nourishment is not food but recurrence.

At certain hours, the formations communicate by fracture.

A crack begins somewhere in the wall: a tiny report, hardly louder than a grain of sand striking glass. The sound travels through neighboring blades. One after another, they answer. The response may be immediate, a brittle cascade in the recesses, or delayed by minutes, spreading around the notch in small, isolated breaks. To a human ear it resembles damage. To the inhabitants it may be a message, or merely the removal of an obstruction.

They have no eyes, but they possess a kind of memory in their surfaces. A plate cut from the wall and left among the tools would continue to shine for a while, retaining the pattern of the light that touched it. A channel long sealed might reopen when a familiar pressure returns. The dark basin at the center is especially important. When water covers it and branches into lines, every nearby formation seems to lean—not physically, perhaps, but in the distribution of its growth—toward the basin. The branching pattern is not a reflection. It is an event the notch recognizes.

The human beings who once inhabited the shelter may have understood this. They did not quarry the formations at random. Their trays are scored with repeated measurements. The rods by the ladder may have recorded the height of a blade, the angle of a crack, the number of days between appearances of water. Their bowls were not merely vessels but listening instruments: shallow copper surfaces on which condensation could announce a change in pressure by forming rings, branching stains, or sudden fields of frost.

Perhaps the people believed they were tending the place. Perhaps the place was tending them.

No human remains, but the notch has not forgotten their hands. The ladder pegs are smooth where palms once gripped them. The shelter’s stone plates have been fitted with an almost patient precision. In several places the mineral growth stops at a clean boundary, as though it once made room for a person to pass. Elsewhere it has resumed its advance and sealed the passage completely.

If a keeper still comes, the inhabitants do not greet them. They do not recoil, either. A living person enters as warmth: an interruption in the air, a temporary source of moisture, a soft disturbance in the pressure of the chamber. Frost gathers on their clothing. Their breath clouds the nearest blades. Their footprints become sealed records, then vanish beneath the next layer.

The keeper might scrape a channel clear, turn a reflective bowl toward the sky, or remove a fallen plate from the central basin. These acts would not be maintenance in any ordinary sense. They would be conversation conducted through alteration. The notch would answer by growing elsewhere, opening a new seam, changing the shape of the water.

Most of the time, no one comes.

Then the congregation continues without witness: thickening in the damp, contracting in the dark, breaking under its own weight, and incorporating its dead structures into the floor. It is not alive because it moves, nor because it grows, but because the whole place is capable of receiving a condition and passing it onward.

The frost is the visible part of that life. The deeper inhabitant is the pattern beneath it: the mountain remembering water, and making a body from whatever the sky permits.
