What lives in the basin has no single shape.

The small ones are the most numerous. They emerge from cracks in the stone when the light begins to strengthen, pale and jointed, their bodies assembled from overlapping plates that flex without ever seeming soft. Each carries a narrow mouth beneath itself, hidden among the limbs. They move by thousands, not in lines but in widening stains, spreading across the wet rock to scrape at whatever the rain has loosened. Their feeding leaves the stone brighter behind them.

They do not eat in silence. Their shells tick against one another. Their many feet make a dry, hurried patter beneath the larger roar of water. When the basin is undisturbed, they produce a constant murmuring scrape, as intimate and mindless as the sound of teeth working.

They are not mindless, exactly.

A disturbance passes through them faster than sight could carry it. One creature strikes the ground; those around it answer; the answer spreads outward in a widening ring. Whole congregations turn at once. They flee from shadows that are not yet above them. They gather around the dead. They remember the channels in which food collects, the hollows that remain warmest beneath the cloud, the stone shelves where the larger hunters cannot reach.

Their memory is not kept in individuals. It passes through contact, through pressure, through the rhythms of their limbs against the ground. A colony may know something that no member of it knows alone.

Some are born from eggs. Some split down the length of the body after feeding until two smaller versions pull themselves free. Some appear to bud from the soft undersides of the larger animals, carried for a time like pale growths before dropping into the water. The basin has many kinds of birth, and the creatures do not seem to distinguish between them. To live is to be assembled, worn down, separated, and assembled again.

The hand-sized crawlers are prey to the long-jawed things.

These are quieter. They keep to the margins of the congregations, moving with a patience that makes them almost invisible. Their bodies are low and flexible, without the hard abundance of limbs possessed by their prey. A jaw may remain hidden beneath the mud for hours, with only a slight tremor in the water to mark its presence. Then it opens.

The mouth is too long for the head that contains it. It unfolds in sections, lined with small inward-pointing teeth, and closes around several pale bodies at once. The survivors scatter in a clatter, their markings flaring orange or violet beneath the shells. The predator does not pursue far. It has learned that panic brings food toward it.

The larger creatures are not hunters in the way the basin’s smaller things are. They are more like moving conditions of the world.

They pass through the inland sea with their backs just beneath the surface, segmented ridges drifting under the mist. Their bodies may extend farther than the eye can follow. Some have soft mouths distributed along their flanks, opening to admit clouds of smaller animals and closing again with slow, fleshy contractions. Others trail curtains of filaments that comb the shallow water. Everything caught in them is carried onward.

They surface only when necessary.

A rise begins as a darkening in the water. Then a ridge appears, slick and ancient, bearing scars filled with pale organisms. Pools gather in the hollows of its back. Colonies feed there, mate there, and sometimes die there without the creature seeming to notice. When it exhales, vapor pours from openings along its spine. The sound is not a call but a release of pressure—a deep, bodily announcement that crosses the basin and enters every shell.

The smaller animals know these sounds. They do not always flee. Sometimes they gather.

The largest of the water-dwellers alter the chemistry of the basin. Their breath warms certain pools. Their waste darkens the channels and feeds the minute films that coat the rock. Where they pass, the crawling animals find abundance. Their apparent destruction is also a kind of weather, and the basin’s life has grown around it.

There are things that cling to the undersides of the ledges, descending upside down on pale hooked limbs. They resemble empty shells until they move. Their bodies are narrow, almost folded, and their backs are covered with plates thin enough for the dim light to pass through. They feed on seepage, on dead flesh, on the minute suspended matter that gathers wherever water falls. When threatened, they curl into themselves and become indistinguishable from the stone.

Others live inside the stone.

They are felt before they are seen: small pulses beneath a shelf, a faint vibration traveling through a wall. They bore narrow chambers through the wet rock, not quickly and not at random. Their tunnels intersect the old channels and the hollows made by generations of dead bodies. When rain enters, these hidden creatures sing through it, producing a faint trembling that can be mistaken for distant movement.

The basin may be more inhabited below its surface than above it.

The dead are not left alone. Pale scavengers open the casings. Soft-bodied things drink what remains inside. Small colonies establish themselves in the joints of larger skeletons. Rain dissolves the loosened material and carries it downward, where other mouths wait. A creature can become a ridge, a bank, a bridge, or part of the sea. The basin has no clean boundary between body and ground.

The builders are different.

They are not often seen, and when they are, they are easy to mistake for one of the larger crawling animals. They move on four or six limbs, depending on age or injury, and wear layered coverings over their backs. These coverings are not shells grown from the body. They are assembled from the remains of other things: curved plates, translucent spines, ribs sealed with black mud. A builder may carry an entire history on its back.

Their heads are small. Their hands are not.

Each hand has three long digits ending in flattened hooks, good for lifting stone and prying apart wet shells. They work slowly, stopping often to press their palms against the ground. They listen through their bones. A builder can tell where water is moving beneath a slab, where a colony is approaching, where one of the great sea-bodies has turned in the shallows.

They do not make settlements because permanence is foolish here. A shelter lasts only until the channels shift or the rain finds a weakness in its roof. They build for one season of dimness, then abandon what they have made. Their structures are not homes so much as pauses in the weather: places to sleep, to molt, to bring forth young, to wait out the calls of the larger animals.

The tools they leave behind are not always tools.

A chipped black stone may have been used to cut flesh from a shell, or to carve a groove into standing rock. A hinged trap may catch crawling animals, but it may also be a listening device, its weighted plates trembling when movement passes through the channels. The row of slabs facing the sea could be markers, memorials, instruments, or mouths of a language no living builder remembers completely.

The builders themselves communicate by touch and vibration.

A palm against stone means one thing. Three taps beneath a ledge means another. A long scraping motion may announce food, danger, a change in water level, or the presence of a body too large to approach. Their spoken sounds are low and difficult to separate from the basin’s natural noises. They hum through their rib coverings. They click their teeth. They place their hands on the ground and allow the message to travel.

When many builders gather, the basin seems to answer them.

The crawling creatures alter direction. The water quivers in the channels. Somewhere far away, a larger animal may raise its back and turn toward the vibration. The builders do not command the other lives, but they have learned to move among their signals. They know which calls mean feeding, which mean mating, which mean the sea is about to breathe.

Perhaps this is why their shelters are scattered. They are not a people gathered against the world. They are a people distributed through it, each small group maintaining a particular conversation with the stone, the rain, and the things beneath the water.

Their young are born soft and nearly transparent. They cannot cross the flats at first. They cling to the adults, or to the warm sides of the larger creatures when these come near the banks. As they grow, they acquire plates, tools, habits, and scars. They learn to distinguish the living shell from the empty one. They learn where not to place a hand. They learn that a silent patch among the crawling streams is more dangerous than a noisy one.

They also learn to feed the basin.

The builders bury their dead in shallow pits beneath stacked slabs. Rain fills the pits. The stone darkens. Within a few days, pale scavengers enter through the seams. Within a few seasons, the shelter collapses and becomes indistinguishable from any other bank of remains. The dead return without ceremony because ceremony, here, is simply what happens to matter.

There may be no word among them for death.

There may be only words for loosening, sinking, opening, becoming ground.

At the dimmest part of the cycle, the builders retreat into their shelters and flooded hollows. The small crawlers disappear into cracks. The predators settle beneath ledges. The sea-creatures descend until their backs are lost beneath the mist. For a little while the basin seems empty except for water and the constant falling of water.

Then something touches the stone.

A single measured tap passes through the wet ground. Another answers from far away. The signal travels among the shelves, crossing channels and drowned flats, passing beneath the old structures and through the banks of shells. One by one, bodies begin to move.

The basin wakes not to light, but to contact.

Pale animals unfold themselves from the cracks. Predators rise behind them. Builders place their hands on the walls and listen for the day’s first changes. Out in the inland sea, something enormous turns in its sleep, disturbing the water for the length of a shore.

Nothing here grows toward the sky.

Everything lives by descending: into water, into stone, into darkness, into the bodies of the dead. Yet the basin is not barren. It is crowded with attention. Every creature is sensing something—pressure, vibration, chemical trace, warmth, absence—and answering in the only language the place has allowed them to keep.

The world is mineral, but it is not inert.

It listens through its inhabitants.
