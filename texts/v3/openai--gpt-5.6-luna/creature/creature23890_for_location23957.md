The people of the highland are called the **Rootbound**, though no one remembers whether the name was given to them by outsiders, by their ancestors, or by the plants.

They are human in outline, but the mountain has worked on them for a long time.

Their bodies are narrow and heavily jointed, built for climbing and for crossing the living crust without breaking it. Their feet are wide, with flexible toes that spread under weight. The soles are dark and ridged, toughened into something between skin and bark. Their hands have two opposing thumbs: one for gripping stone, the other for catching the fibrous edges of leaves and roots. They climb with a slow, deliberate economy, never making a movement that cannot be recovered from.

Most are small compared to lowland humans, though not fragile. Their ribs are deep, their lungs broad, and their blood carries unusual quantities of iron. In cold weather, the skin around their mouths and nostrils darkens. They breathe through woven masks when traveling among the spore fields, and many have silver-gray deposits permanently embedded in the creases of their faces.

Their eyes are large and dark, with a green reflective layer behind the pupils. In the plant glow, they see well enough to distinguish a safe ledge from a dangerous one, a living root from a dead one, a predator lying flat against the stone from a patch of wet vegetation. They do not see colors as clearly as people elsewhere might. To them, the highland is divided more by temperature, motion, texture, and faint biological light than by hue.

A Rootbound child learns to walk on rock before learning to walk on level ground.

The children are strapped to their mothers or older siblings during the first years of life, not in front but against the back, where they can feel the movement of the body carrying them. They are taught to listen to the stalks. Certain booms mean wind. Others mean a hollow column has begun to split. A low shudder beneath the feet may be a burrowing animal, or a fault opening, or simply a colony drawing water upward. The distinction matters.

By the age of six, a child can tell whether a hanging root is bearing water by touching it with the back of one finger. By ten, they are sent alone to gather condensation from the lower curtains. By adolescence, they are expected to cross an open shelf during bad weather without being seen by the predators that wait in the gaps.

They do not consider this cruel. The mountain offers no gentler education.

Their settlements are not permanent in the way cities are permanent. A shelter is occupied for as long as the surrounding plants remain generous, then abandoned when the colony changes its growth or the water becomes unreliable. The Rootbound leave behind little. A few stone basins. A series of white fungus markers. A dead fire pit. Perhaps the woven skeleton of a roof, already being incorporated into the living mats.

Their homes are built into rather than against the landscape.

They hollow out dead stalks and live inside them, sleeping in spiral chambers lined with dry leaf fiber. They use the broad root curtains as walls, though the curtains must be watched; a root that was harmless yesterday may begin to contract around a shelter without warning. In older settlements, the inhabitants have trained living branches into arches and platforms. These structures remain long after the people who shaped them are gone, and sometimes continue to grow according to no human instruction.

A dwelling is never entirely silent. The walls creak in the cold. Water ticks through hidden channels. Plant tissue shifts beneath the floor. At night, the Rootbound lie awake listening to the settlement breathe, because a change in that breathing may warn of a storm, a predator, or the death of the colony itself.

They eat what the highland provides, but nothing is eaten without consequence.

They harvest the swollen bulbs beneath the living crust, scrape nutrient-rich lichen from sheltered stone, and cut only the outer layers of the broad leaves. They trap the small climbers that live under the foliage, though these are difficult to catch because they flatten themselves into strips of bark when frightened. They follow the grazing herds after storms, collecting what the animals leave behind and gathering fresh growth from the damaged mats.

The Rootbound do not kill plants casually. A plant colony can feed a settlement for generations, but only if the harvest is taken in patterns the colony can recover from. Each group has its own signs for this: three cuts in a stem, a circle of removed leaves, a strip of bark left untouched. These marks are not merely practical. They are requests.

Some plants appear to answer.

A cut vine may recoil from a hand that has violated the customary pattern. A grove may open its leaves to one family and close them to another. The Rootbound say that plants remember injury, but they do not agree on what remembering means. Some believe the colonies possess individual minds. Others believe memory travels through water and root, accumulating without belonging to anyone. A few insist that the forest has no thoughts at all, only habits too large for a human lifetime to recognize.

No one is entirely comfortable when the plants begin behaving like people.

The Rootbound bury their dead in root chambers, where the body is wrapped in membrane and lowered beneath the living crust. They do not burn the dead. Fire is too precious, and the smoke carries across the ridges. The body is offered to the colony along with a small quantity of mineral salt and a braid of hair.

After several seasons, the burial site may become marked by a new growth: a pale stalk, a ring of dark leaves, or a low, translucent swelling in the ground. The Rootbound do not call these things reincarnation. They call them **answers**.

When they travel, they move in small groups and make little noise. Each person carries a stone blade, a coil of plant rope, a water bladder, a mask, and a folded sheet of sail-leaf for shelter. Their clothing is layered and close-fitting, made from woven inner fibers sealed with resin. The outer surfaces are dull and mottled, colored to resemble the rock of the route being taken. They wear no loose ornaments. Anything that can catch on a thorn, flash under starlight, or make a sound in the wind may become a death sentence.

They communicate across great distances using the natural acoustics of the highland.

A single strike on a hollow stem can travel from ridge to ridge. Different stalks produce different tones, and a trained listener can distinguish a warning from a summons, a report of water from a report of death. These messages are never spoken plainly. The mountains distort everything. Meaning lies in sequence, pause, and repetition.

Three deep notes may mean: **storm beyond the western ridge**.

Three deep notes followed by silence may mean: **storm, and something following it**.

The Rootbound have no word for privacy as lowland people understand it. Their settlements are too small, their shelters too thin, and the landscape too dangerous. Yet they possess an intense sense of personal distance. A person sitting alone beside a pool is not approached without invitation. A traveler who enters another’s shelter without touching the threshold stone is considered more offensive than a thief.

They fear the open spaces, though they live among them.

The gaps between colonies are where the predators wait. The gaps are also where storms gather, where sound becomes misleading, and where a person may fall for miles without being seen. Rootbound stories describe the open shelves as the places where the mountain looks directly at you.

Their greatest enemies are not the largest animals. The enormous burrowers can be avoided if their movements are understood. The cliff-hanging hunters leave signs. Even the long-winged predators have nesting seasons.

What the Rootbound fear most are the **approaching plants**.

Sometimes a colony begins to spread against the prevailing weather, extending dark mats across bare stone. Sometimes a grove opens a pathway that was not there before, leading toward a basin no one has visited. Sometimes a settlement wakes to find every leaf turned toward it.

When this happens, the people gather their tools and water, extinguish the fire, and leave.

They do not run. Running breaks the crust and attracts attention. They depart in single file, without looking back, while the plants continue their slow, patient movement behind them.

And yet the Rootbound are not merely survivors.

They sing during the thaw, when condensation fills the roots and the hollow towers begin to wake. They paint their masks with mineral dust before marriage ceremonies. They race across safe shelves beneath the stalk forests. They argue over the best way to read an approaching storm. They carve the faces of their ancestors into warning posts, not because the dead protect the living, but because a familiar face makes a dangerous path easier to remember.

They are born into scarcity, but not into emptiness.

The highland gives them little that can be held. It gives them cold, distance, uncertain water, treacherous stone, and a living forest that may be watching or may only seem to watch. In return, it gives them a world that is never finished. Cliffs rise. Pools migrate. Colonies remember damage. New growth covers every collapse.

The Rootbound understand that nothing here remains where it was placed.

Not a shelter.

Not a body.

Not a path.

They live by becoming temporary themselves: light-footed, quiet, observant, and ready to move when the living ground beneath them decides to change.
