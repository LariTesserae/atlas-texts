What lives there is not a population, but an inheritance.

The vegetation has taken the place of people without taking on any of their urgency. It does not swarm through the ruins or break them open in triumph. It settles into what was provided. Every crack is a bed. Every channel is a rootway. Every courtyard, cistern, stair, and collapsed room becomes a basin in which another generation can establish itself.

The oldest plants grow low and wide, dark as wet bronze. Their runners follow the joints between slabs with such precision that, from a distance, they seem to be the original mortar. They pass beneath fallen stones and emerge on the other side, continuing the lines of the vanished builders. Where a road once divided a district, ribbon growth lies in long parallel bands. Where a wall once marked a boundary, roots have thickened along its foot. The plants preserve the shape of the country by occupying it.

In the shallower ruins, there are forests of short, heavy-stemmed growth. Their trunks are pale near the base and black above, scoured smooth by the current. They rise from old squares in evenly spaced ranks, as if the planting plans had survived even after the gardeners were gone. Their crowns spread just beneath the water’s higher darkness. Leaves overlap there in great slow layers, each one translucent at the edges, each one trembling whenever some distant change passes through the sea.

They have no need to turn toward light. There is too little light to deserve such behavior. Instead they respond to pressure, temperature, sediment, and the shape of the stone around them. A current touches one grove, and the grove bends. A current crosses the plain, and miles of vegetation incline together, not as though obeying a command but as though remembering one motion from the body of the drowned land.

The plants do not know the ruins as ruins.

A stairway is a slope on which roots can gather. A house is a warm arrangement of stone, though all warmth has long since departed. An avenue is a trench of flowing water bordered by good anchorage. A well is a vertical garden. A roofless hall is a broad, protected place where spores can settle.

They have no word for the people who built the platforms beneath them. If the old inscriptions remain, the growth obscures them one letter at a time. Roots enter the grooves. Film gathers in the carved recesses. A pale stem crosses a name and thickens there. In time, the writing is not destroyed but incorporated, its angles becoming supports for leaves.

Their lives are immensely slow.

A frond may unfold over a decade. A root may spend a century finding the weakness in a foundation. A grove can advance across a plaza by the width of a hand and call that expansion. When a plant dies, it does not leave the community. Its body settles among the stones, breaks down in the cold water, and becomes a dark layer from which the next growth rises. Nothing is wasted because nothing is separate. The old plants become the mud of the young ones. The young ones bind the walls that shelter the old remains.

There are no parents and no offspring in any recognizable sense. A new colony may begin from a fragment carried through a gate. A single torn length of ribbon can lodge in a crack and spend generations dividing itself. Pale threads extend from ceiling to floor; where they touch stone, they take hold. The flooded country reproduces by persistence. To continue is its only visible instinct.

The vegetation does not compete as animals would. It does not pursue territory. It receives whatever place the water gives it. Two kinds may grow through one another, their stems braided around the same buried column. A darker mat may spread beneath a lighter canopy without choking it. One plant occupies the floor, another the walls, another the ceiling, another the open water between. Their boundaries are not borders but layers.

Still, there are distinctions.

Some plants prefer the old avenues, where currents bring a constant supply of silt. Their long leaves lie flat in the flow and lift only at the edges. Some gather in chambers where water moves in circles, forming slow green vortices. Some grow from fired-clay drains and emerge in pale clusters from every opening. Others thrive in the deepest halls, where nutrients collect overhead; their roots descend in curtains, while their leaves spread against the ceiling like submerged hands.

None of them has eyes, but the country is not unobserved.

The plants register everything that reaches them: the pressure pulse of weather far above, the settling of a distant wall, the passage of a current changed by some obstruction miles away. Their bodies are instruments spread across the ruins. A bend in a leaf records a flow. A thickening root records a crack. A sudden clearing in one grove marks a change in the water that may not be noticed elsewhere for decades.

In this way the drowned country possesses a kind of awareness, though not a mind. It is distributed through stems, films, roots, spores, and silt. No single plant knows the extent of the plain. No single grove understands that it is part of a continent of growth. Yet a pressure moves through the water, and thousands of leaves answer. A foundation shifts, and roots slowly alter their direction. The landscape senses by changing.

Sometimes, when a sheet of vegetation is carried through an avenue, the plants seem briefly to become one creature. Their leaves stream behind them. Their roots trail and tangle. They pass beneath an arch, enter a courtyard, and divide around a fallen monument before gathering again in the next channel. But this unity is only the work of current. When the water stills, the sheet comes apart. Each frond settles against its own stone.

There are no hunters, grazers, scavengers, or parasites. Nothing bites through the leaves. Nothing nests among the roots. The plants are spared the agitation of being eaten, and the cost of that safety is a profound lack of speed. They have become calm because nothing has ever needed them to hurry.

What little death exists is gradual and visible. A canopy thins. Stems darken. Leaves lose their firmness and fold into the sediment. A courtyard once green becomes brown, then gray, then bare except for a few pale shoots rising from the accumulated remains. Elsewhere, the growth advances into a new district. There is no catastrophe in this exchange. No season announces it. One region falls silent while another becomes dense with leaves.

The vegetation has inherited not only the structures, but the old arrangements of care.

It grows most heavily where water was once directed. It follows the maintenance logic of the vanished civilization: basin, outlet, channel, reservoir, overflow. The builders designed the country to gather and distribute water; now the water distributes the plants. Ancient engineering has become an ecology. The old infrastructure still performs its work, though the thing being sustained is no longer a human settlement but a continuous, submerged garden.

Perhaps this is the closest the place comes to inhabitants: not individuals, but processes that have remained.

Water enters. Roots take hold. Stone settles. Leaves drift. Sediment gathers. A wall cracks, and the growth fills it. A current alters course, and a new grove begins downstream. The country persists through these exchanges without preserving any particular life within it. What lives there is the capacity to occupy, to absorb, to repeat.

If one could stand among the terraces, one would not be met by a face or a call. One would be surrounded by the slight movements of countless leaves. The water would pass through the plants and under the stones. A pale root would touch the sleeve, not in curiosity but because the current had brought it there. Far away, a whole district would bend under a pulse from the unseen sea.

Then everything would become still again.

The plants would remain in their patient places, holding the drowned roads, filling the old rooms, covering the names. They would continue to grow in the dark—not toward a future, not in memory of the past, but into whatever shape the country offered next.
