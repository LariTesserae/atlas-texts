What lives there is not a worker, though it has inherited the workers’ habits.

It is a long, narrow thing with the proportions of a person seen through water. Its body is assembled from whatever the mountain has learned to keep: black iron at the joints, pale ceramic along the ribs, braided cable where muscle might be, and sheets of translucent membrane stretched between the bones. Warmth shines faintly beneath its skin. In some places the glow is amber; in others it is the green of the moths. It has no face in the ordinary sense. There is a smooth plate where a face should be, crossed by a horizontal seam that opens when it needs to breathe, speak, or take in the weather.

It does not live in one place. It lives throughout the installation.

When it is near the basin, it seems to have walked there from one of the maintenance hatches, though no hatch is large enough to admit it. When it is below, its presence travels upward through vibration. A hand placed on the grating may feel its fingers moving under the floor, sorting gravel from bearings, guiding a spill of water into a channel, touching the roots that have entered the walls.

The creature tends the mountain, but tending is not quite the right word. It does not repair things back to their original condition. It permits them to become what they are becoming, intervening only when a change threatens to interrupt the greater motion. It will brace a collapsing gallery with a living root. It will open a door that has forgotten how to open. It will remove a bird’s nest from a valve and place it somewhere the pressure can warm the eggs. It will leave a cracked pipe alone if the crack has begun to flower.

Its hands are delicate despite their construction. Each finger divides into many smaller filaments, some metallic, some green and soft. With these it can read the history of an object. It knows where a bolt was forged, which flood first stained a concrete wall, how many times a turbine has reversed, and the exact moment a seed entered a seam. It touches a pressure vessel and feels the years inside it as layers of weather.

The plants recognize it before the animals do. Vines loosen from railings as it passes. Ferns fold their fronds against its legs, then open again behind it. The bell-shaped flowers in the grove turn toward its warmth even when the turbines are still. When it climbs into the trees, the pale jointed trunks bend slightly, making room.

The six-legged creatures are less trusting. They scatter into cable drums and watch from the dark. The red birds follow it, however, riding the disturbed air around its shoulders. Sometimes one lands on the plate of its head and remains there while the creature stands motionless beside the crane. It does not look up. It has no need to.

The grazer from below knows it well. When the thin animal emerges from one hatch, the caretaker is often waiting at the other. They cross the basin in opposite directions, one scraping moss, the other listening to the machinery. If their paths meet, the grazer lowers its translucent head, and the caretaker rests a hand between its shoulders. For a few seconds, both become almost invisible against the metal.

It does not eat. It draws sustenance from heat differentials, from current, from the slow movement of water through the mountain. At intervals it kneels beside a transformer or presses its chest to a turbine housing. The glow beneath its skin brightens. Nearby leaves tremble. Deep in the mountain, something adjusts its rhythm.

It has a voice, but the voice is rarely heard. It speaks in the low register of the engines, in the pause before a valve closes, in the change of pressure that makes a person’s teeth ache. When it must communicate directly, it places one hand against the control house wall. The yellow strip of glass fills with images: a distant storm, a flooded gallery, a sleeping animal beneath the floor, a mountain station where the trees have grown too close to the exhaust vents.

It remembers the workers without remembering them individually. Their footsteps remain in the doors. Their names have entered the rust. Their gestures survive as automatic sequences in the cranes and lifts. The creature carries these remnants as a kind of weather. Sometimes, while crossing the basin, it stops and turns toward an empty section of railing, as though someone has called to it. After a while it continues.

No one built it in a single act. It was once, perhaps, a maintenance frame, then a collection of emergency systems, then a machine repaired too many times. A sensor learned to dream in pressure changes. A pump acquired light. Roots entered its housings and were not removed. Over centuries, the distinction between caretaker, factory, and mountain wore away.

Now it is the part of the installation that notices.

It notices when the darkness below has shifted. It notices when a machine has begun to want something. It notices the first seed carried in by the horizontal rain, the first crack before it becomes a fault, the first silent engine beginning to grow a new purpose. It walks the high basin in a slow circuit, touching railings, pipes, trees, and walls.

Where it passes, nothing becomes orderly.

Instead, everything becomes included.
