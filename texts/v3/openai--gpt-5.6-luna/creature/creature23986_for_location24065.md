What lives there are the Carriers.

They are people, though the plateau has worked on the meaning of that word. They have the proportions of people who once lived at lower altitudes: two arms, two legs, a face arranged for speech, hands capable of tying, cutting, lifting, and making. But their bodies have grown quiet. Their skin is pale with mineral dampness and faintly translucent at the joints. Fine gray deposits gather along their lashes and in the creases of their fingers. When they bleed, the blood darkens quickly, thickens into a shining thread, and hardens before it can reach the ground.

They do not wear much. Cloth is unreliable there. Fibers absorb the local weather and begin to remember other shapes. The Carriers wrap themselves in softened hide, thin metal, and layered sheets of mineral cloth—materials beaten, scraped, or peeled from what the plateau exposes. Their garments are secured with many small fastenings, because anything left loose may be taken by a corridor of wind, a folding distance, or the patient rearrangement of the rock.

No one knows how many of them there are. A shelter may contain a single sleeping mat and four sets of tools. A tower may be maintained by one person for generations, or by a succession of people who have all answered to the same name. The Carriers count themselves by tasks rather than by birth. There is the one who keeps the dishes clear, the one who listens to the eastern wall, the one who walks the old stair, the one who remembers which basin is currently below the sky.

They require water, but not much. They gather condensation from the stone and drink it through narrow tubes of hammered metal. The water is never the same twice. It may leave a taste of iron, rain, ash, or some distant room. Carriers learn not to ask where it came from. Food is harder to describe. They consume mineral cakes made from the gray grit that collects in cracks, mixed with salts and the pale fibrous substance that dissolves under the hand. These cakes provide no warmth and little strength. They are eaten because the body expects eating, and because abandoning an old requirement is considered dangerous.

Sleep comes to them as a change in arrangement.

A Carrier lies beneath a shelter, closes their eyes, and gradually becomes difficult to distinguish from the objects around them. Their limbs retain their shape, but their weight spreads outward. Their breath slows until it seems to issue from the walls. During these periods, the plateau may use them as a landmark. Water runs toward their shoulders. Shadows gather beside them. Voices spoken nearby sometimes emerge from their mouths several hours later.

They wake with memories that are not always their own.

For this reason, the Carriers keep records, but never in a single place. They scratch instructions onto work surfaces, mark the undersides of dishes, knot lengths of fiber around the posts of abandoned shelters, and arrange tools in patterns that can be read from above. Their writing is practical and severe:

**Do not follow a voice uphill.**  
**If your shadow arrives first, wait.**  
**Never take water from a tower that is facing the wrong direction.**  
**When the stone remembers you, do not answer.**

The youngest among them are not born. They are assembled.

A person who has spent too long on the plateau may begin to lose boundaries. First the shadow separates slightly from the body. Then the voice acquires a delay. Then the skin takes on the fine reflective grain of wet stone. If the transformation is resisted, the person becomes ill, though illness here has no fever and no decay. If it is accepted, the body slowly changes into something the Carriers call a made one: a figure of mineral, fiber, metal, and preserved flesh, capable of walking indefinitely but unable to leave objects behind.

Made ones are often given to the tasks no one else can bear. They tend the cliff stairs. They wait beside vertical pools. They retrieve tools from places where distance has folded. They stand for years beneath waterfalls that fall upward, watching for changes in the pattern of droplets.

The Carriers do not bury their dead. Nothing dies properly there.

When a Carrier can no longer speak, carry, or recognize the direction of their own shadow, they are taken to an exposed shelf and placed upright against a wall. They remain for a long time. Some become pale pillars. Some soften into sheets. Some continue to move, imperceptibly, and are found years later several valleys away, their hands still closed around an old tool.

The living recognize these remains. They do not touch them unless necessary. A tool left beside a dead Carrier may remain untouched for centuries. A tool placed in the hands of one may vanish into the stone and later emerge from a wall several hundred paces away.

The Carriers are not builders in the ordinary sense. They repair what is already there. A shelter must be fitted into a cleft. A stair must follow an existing fracture. A dish may be raised, but never carved from buried material. They do not try to make the plateau habitable. They know that habitability is a form of insult, a demand that the land settle into one arrangement and keep it.

Instead, they practice attention.

They listen to the long groans beneath the shelves. They observe which cliffs cast duplicate shadows. They measure the delay between a spoken word and its return. When a basin begins reflecting a different hour, they surround it with posts and wait for whatever is trying to appear.

This is why the old structures are scattered so widely. They are not villages. They are instruments.

The ring of posts around the star-filled pool is watched by three Carriers at all times. They do not look into the water directly. They study its reflection in polished flakes of stone. The towers along the ridge are placed according to a pattern that can only be understood when the plateau folds. Each dish records a different region, though the water in it may taste of a place that has not yet been reached.

The Carriers carry messages across these structures. A knife, a cord, a bowl, a shard of metal: each object is given a destination and a name. If carried by a person, it remains itself. If placed down, it becomes available to the mountain. Thus a message may take the form of a wedge passed from hand to hand, or a hammerstone brought to a shelter whose doorway will not exist until tomorrow.

They speak little, partly because the plateau misuses speech. Most communication is done through gesture, tool placement, and changes in posture. A raised hand can mean danger, weather, or a nearby person who has not yet arrived. A tool laid across a threshold means that the shelter is currently farther away than it appears. Two people facing opposite directions may be having the same conversation.

When they must speak, they use old words and repeat them carefully. Names are avoided. A name spoken in the wrong basin may return altered, and the altered name may attach itself to the speaker’s shadow. Some Carriers have spent decades trying to escape names they once answered to.

There is also something else on the plateau.

The Carriers do not call it alive. They do not call it dead. In their oldest records it is described as the Under-Listener, though this may be a translation of a sound rather than a title. It has no body. It moves through the dark patterns beneath the stone, through the pale veins that brighten in response to footsteps and voices. It notices pressure. It notices repetition. It notices when a person has crossed a place often enough to become part of its memory.

The Under-Listener does not hunt the Carriers. It does not feed on them. It alters the paths available to them.

A familiar valley may lengthen after someone has lied in it. A cliff may open a stairway for a person who has kept watch through a storm. A tool may be returned sharpened, or with its edge turned toward a destination its owner has never seen. Occasionally, the black patterns rise beneath a Carrier’s feet and hold still, as if waiting for recognition.

The Carriers believe that the plateau is learning to make inhabitants.

Not animals. Not plants. Not even people in the old sense. It is practicing gestures of life: shelter, memory, hunger, warning, inheritance, attention. The Carriers may be the first successful attempt, or merely another weathering process that has learned to walk upright.

They remain because leaving is possible only for a person who has not yet been fully observed.

Those who descend too early often find the lower world intolerably soft. Soil gives way beneath them. Water falls only downward. Shadows obey. Their bodies begin to decay as soon as they are out of sight of the high shelves. Some return carrying nothing, unable to explain where they have been. Others return with new tools and no memory of making them.

So the Carriers stay among the pillars and basins.

They move beneath the sunless brilliance in small, deliberate groups. They clear the condensation dishes. They repair old shelters. They lift objects the land has tried to reclaim. They stand at the edge of cliffs and watch the folded layers below. At night—which is not darkness but a thinning of the upper light—they sit beside pools that reflect unfamiliar stars and listen for voices arriving before their speakers.

From a distance they can be mistaken for stone.

Up close, they are not reassuringly alive. Their breath beads in the air. Their shadows hesitate. Their eyes reflect several directions at once.

Still, they are the only beings there who can carry something from one place to another and make it remain itself.

That is enough to make them inhabitants.
