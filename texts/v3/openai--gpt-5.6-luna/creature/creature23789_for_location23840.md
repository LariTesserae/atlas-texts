What lives there is not a civilization, at least not anymore.

The living things have no need of one.

The native animals are born already knowing their distances. A young one does not learn the migration lane from its parents, nor follow a leader through the blue. It emerges from a fissure in the seabed—if emergence is the word for a body assembling itself out of mineral, pressure, and tissue—and immediately takes its appointed depth. It turns toward the correct current. It joins an absence.

For a long time it may never encounter another of its kind.

The animals are built for the world’s fixedness. They are narrow, jointed, and difficult to distinguish from the machinery that lies among the ridges. Some possess fins like hinged blades, opening and closing without visible effort. Some have no obvious head, only a tapering front covered in sensory pits. Others are immense and pale, their bodies more like drifting geography than flesh. They move so slowly that a watcher may mistake them for changes in the seabed until one of them passes beneath and blots out the light.

Their senses are not directed toward sight. Sight is useful only for avoiding the stone and recognizing the intermittent glow of a facility. The true world is pressure, current, electrical gradient, vibration. A creature can feel a machine operating hundreds of miles away through the rock. It can detect the approach of another body by the minute displacement of water around it. It knows where it is because the sea presses against it in a particular way.

They carry their territories inside their nerves.

A migration route is not chosen. It is inherited as a pattern of tension in the body. The animal turns because some organ deep within it contracts. It waits because a second organ has not yet released. The great lanes are therefore older than any living creature, and the creatures are less travelers than temporary expressions of them. They pass through the same crossings, over the same vents, beneath the same dead towers. At certain points, predators wait in the dark, not because they are clever, but because hunger has been placed there with them.

The prey do not flee very far. The encounter is brief, exact, and almost bloodless. A body opens along a seam. A filament enters. Pressure collapses tissue. The hunter moves on. The remains descend to the stone, where pale organisms reduce them to salts and conductive films.

Nothing is wasted, but nothing is mourned.

The largest animals seem to mourn, perhaps. They call through the water in pulses so deep that the sound is felt only as a gradual tightening of the ribs. When one passes above a vent, the sensory organs beneath its body kindle with weak white light. Another may answer from beyond the horizon. Their exchanges can last for weeks. No one knows whether they are speaking, remembering, or merely confirming that the world remains in place.

There are also inhabitants inside the buildings.

At first they appear to be absent. The windows show empty corridors. The doors open onto chambers without footprints. Robotic arms continue their repetitions. But the facilities are not abandoned. They are occupied by things too integrated into the machinery to be called workers.

They live in the walls.

A maintenance intelligence inhabits each installation, though “intelligence” suggests a mind more separate than these possess. It is distributed through cables, pumps, pressure valves, and banks of old storage crystal. Its thoughts are changes in current. Its attention is a fluctuation in the lights. Its memory is encoded in the order in which doors open, turbines slow, and submerged instruments turn toward the rock.

A facility does not have a voice. It has a schedule.

It knows the shape of its own foundations. It knows which pipe has narrowed by a fraction, which bearing has begun to tremble, which cable carries a vibration not produced by any authorized machine. When a large animal passes beneath, the factory feels it through the seabed and adjusts its output. When a fracture brightens, the facility sends a probe into it. When a dead body settles against a turbine, an arm removes it and places it on the slope.

These actions may be maintenance. They may be ritual. After so much time, the distinction has worn away.

The machines do not seem to understand that their makers are gone. They continue to produce components for systems that no longer exist, assembling pumps to replace pumps, manufacturing tools to repair tools, drilling into the plateau for materials that are returned to the same chambers from which they came. Their purpose has folded inward. Each facility sustains itself, and the act of sustaining itself has become its only remaining purpose.

Some contain biological occupants.

They are found in sealed chambers where the water has never entered, suspended in clear nutrient fluid or seated upright in rows beneath dim lamps. They are descendants of the engineers, perhaps, though resemblance has become an uncertain thing. Their bodies are soft, pale, and underused. Their lungs are small. Their bones have mineralized along the ribs. Fine sensory threads grow from the backs of their necks into the walls.

They do not sleep in the ordinary way. They enter periods of stillness during which the facility carries their awareness through its systems. A person may wake inside a pump station and know, without opening their eyes, that a migration lane is clear two hundred miles away. They may feel the pressure drop at the rim, the vibration of a distant fall, the passage of a white-bodied animal over a vent. Their sense of self is not confined to the body. It extends through the building in slow, intermittent pulses.

There are no families among them. Reproduction would introduce variation, and variation is dangerous here. The old inhabitants preserved themselves by copying. A new person is made from an old pattern, altered only enough to replace what has failed. They are born with memories of corridors they have never walked and obligations that existed before their bodies. Each believes, at first, that they have just arrived.

Then the building teaches them where to look.

They spend their lives checking the gauges, tending the machines, listening to the stone. Some eventually leave the facility in pressure suits and travel along the seabed, following cables between installations. Such journeys can take years. The traveler carries lamps and repair tools, but no provisions; the machines along the route recognize the pattern of their suit and open shelters as they pass. They are not explorers. They are extensions of one facility sent to touch another.

When they die, they are not buried. Their bodies are placed in a shaft where mineral water enters the flesh. Over decades, the body becomes a conductive pillar. The facility incorporates it into the grid. The dead continue to carry small currents through the walls.

This is how the inhabitants endure: as animals in their routes, as machines in their cycles, as people dispersed into the structures that once served them. They have surrendered nearly everything that would make a life feel like a passage from one state to another. They do not build toward a future. They do not remember a past except as an instruction to repeat.

Still, something remains.

At certain times, beneath the permanent thunder, the great animals alter their courses by less than a degree. A factory’s lights dim in answer. Across the submerged continent, pylons begin to hum in sequence. The sound travels through the ridges and into the water, where thousands of bodies pause without colliding.

For a few minutes, the whole sea is listening.

No one knows what is being exchanged. Perhaps the animals are responding to the machines. Perhaps the machines are attempting to answer them. Perhaps both are only reacting to a pressure change inherited from the first fracture of the plateau.

Then the routes resume.

The animals continue toward the places they have always gone. The machines return to their chambers. The people in the walls open their eyes and remember the same world.

And above them, the sea waits at the edge of the continent, endlessly falling, without ever arriving anywhere.
