What lives there is not an animal, nor a plant, nor anything that could be said to have entered the hollow.

It is the hollow’s patience.

It has no body in the ordinary sense, though the pale slab is the place where it is most nearly gathered. The grooves in the stone are not writing, but channels of attention. Through them, water passes inward and outward over years. When the slab darkens beneath the sun, the thing is awake—not newly awakened, for it has never slept, but made more present by warmth.

Its substance is distributed through the place.

A little of it lies in the mineral film around each still basin. A little inhabits the silver threads beneath the moss. It is present in the amber veins of the waxy leaves, in the roots that grip the banks, in the mist that refuses to cross from one hollow into another. The trees do not contain it, exactly. They lean according to it. The marsh does not belong to it, but remembers its shape.

It is not a spirit of water. Water is too restless a word for what it is.

It is the knowledge of water’s proper destination.

Every droplet that emerges from the rock is known to it. Every seepage has a place in its long reckoning: the basin where the drop will rest, the seam through which it will descend, the patch of moss that will receive its overflow, the dark pocket beneath the marsh where it will remain until some distant weather calls it upward again. The hollow is full of such arrangements, and the thing lives in the arrangements themselves.

It does not think in sentences. It does not remember events. Its memory is pressure, dampness, weight.

A stone that has shifted by the width of a grain is noticed. A root that has found a new crack is noticed. The absence of an insect is not noticed, because absence is not a change there. The rule against beating hearts requires no enforcement. The hollow does not repel animals. It simply has no place in itself where an animal can become part of the pattern. A bird might cross the surrounding ridges, but it would not descend. A fox might scent the marsh, but would turn aside before seeing why. The threshold is not guarded. It is only absolute.

If a living creature did enter, the thing in the hollow would not attack it.

The mist would thicken around its ankles. Water would gather beneath its feet. The stones would become subtly uncertain. Paths would lengthen. The creature would find itself returning to the edge without remembering having turned. Its heartbeat would remain foreign, an abrupt and discordant insistence. Eventually the hollow would make no room for it, and the creature would leave believing that it had changed its mind.

The inhabitant is not cruel. Cruelty requires desire, and desire requires a direction toward the future. This being has almost no future. It extends.

It extends into the next darkening of the slab, the next filling of a mineral cup, the next slow tilt of a tree. It is always becoming more itself by remaining where it is. Perhaps it began when the volcanic stone cooled and trapped a warmth that never quite escaped. Perhaps it gathered later, from centuries of rain finding the same channels. Perhaps it has always been there, as old as the first hollow made by the mountain.

No one has named it, though a name might be heard in the drops if they were allowed to fall long enough.

Tick.

A pause.

Tick.

Another pause, longer than the first.

The entity is counting—not time, exactly, but continuance. It counts the distance between one change and the next. It counts what remains. It counts the water as it leaves the stone, and the stone as it yields to water. It counts the slow green advance of the marsh, the gradual opening of flowers, the patient lifting of roots. It counts without numbers, because nothing in the hollow is ever separate enough to be numbered.

At evening, when the slab turns copper and its grooves darken from top to bottom, the thing comes nearest to expression.

The mist settles into its appointed hollows. The pools become perfectly still. The trees hold their branches motionless. Even the dripping seems to pause, as though the whole place has drawn in a breath it will spend over the night.

Then the slab lightens again.

The water resumes its descent.

And the inhabitant, spread through stone, root, vapor, and the hidden veins beneath the ground, continues its ancient work of keeping the hollow exactly—not unchanged, but itself.
