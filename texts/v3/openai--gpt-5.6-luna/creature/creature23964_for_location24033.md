They are called the **Vey**, though the word means not a people but *those who remain where the stone permits life*. They are human in the broad, ancestral sense, but the summit has worked on them for so many generations that their bodies no longer seem entirely borrowed from the low world.

They are small and compact, built close to the ground like their houses. Their limbs are strong without appearing muscular, their hands broad and many-jointed, their feet toughened into dark, flexible pads. Children are born with soft, translucent membranes at the corners of their eyes, which they learn to draw across the surface when the air turns metallic with dust. Adult Vey have gray, rust, or violet-brown skin, often patterned by mineral staining that no amount of scraping removes. Their hair grows thick and fine, and is usually cut short or braided tightly beneath layered hoods. Loose hair catches salts from the air and becomes brittle.

They do not sweat much. The body cannot afford to lose what the summit does not give back. Instead, their skin exudes a faint wax that gathers dust and must be rubbed away with fibrous leaves. They sleep wrapped in plant-fiber sheets, not to keep warm exactly, but to preserve the vapor that collects around the body. A sleeping family looks almost embalmed: faces covered, limbs tucked together, each breath passing through a reed filter.

They recognize one another by scent as much as by face. Every household has its own mineral odor, produced by diet, dwelling, animals, and the particular warm cracks over which it has built its sleeping ledges. A Vey returning from another quarter of the summit may be greeted first by being smelled. Infants are held beneath the chin and behind the knees. Lovers press foreheads together and breathe slowly, learning the changes in one another caused by illness, pregnancy, fear, or a season of bad vapor.

Their speech is quiet at close range and difficult to hear from a distance. The summit is too full of clicking leaves, wing-buzz, stem knocks, and animal calls for loud voices to remain distinct. They speak in short phrases, with long pauses in which they listen to the surrounding life. Meaning is carried by pitch, breath, and the direction of the face. A word spoken toward the grove may address the plants, the people beneath the plants, or the dead, depending on how it is shaped.

They have another language for distance: tapping.

A knuckle against a wall. A fingernail on a clay vessel. Two stones struck together beneath a walkway. The settlement carries messages through its foundations. Certain families are skilled at reading the difference between a house settling, a burrower moving, a root swelling, and a person signaling from the other side of a partition. During storms, when dust thickens the air and voices become useless, the Vey communicate by laying their palms on the warm stone.

They are never entirely alone. Solitude, to them, means being without human company, not being without life. A person sitting at the edge of the summit with a climbing animal under one arm and insects moving through their clothing may consider themselves secluded. The distinction between companion, livestock, pest, and ancestor is not firm. Some animals are named individually. Some are addressed only by the work they perform. Others live in such close partnership with a family that their absence is treated as a death, even if no one can say whether the creature ever understood affection.

The Vey do not fence animals away from their homes. They shape their lives around them. Floors are raised where burrowers need passage. Warm wall hollows are lined for nesting. Grain—when they have anything deserving that name—is stored behind layered screens that allow useful insects through and exclude the devouring ones. A household may cultivate a colony of shell-backed grazers in its threshold, scraping their mineral-rich waste into paste for plaster and medicine. Children learn early which creatures can be touched, which must be sung to, and which are harmless only while the air remains cold.

Their dead remain close.

Bodies are not buried deeply. Deep stone is warm, and the dead would be consumed by roots, worms, and the slow chemistry of the mountain. Instead, the Vey dry their dead in ventilated chambers, coat the skin with salt and fragrant plant dust, and place the bodies in wall cavities or beneath the broad roots of the mineral trees. Some are seated upright behind screens, facing the paths they once used. Their bones become handles, needles, flutes, and supports for climbing vines. This is not considered desecration. A person who has died has merely changed the scale of their usefulness.

Names can survive longer than bodies. A child may be named for a grandmother, a roof beam, a particular flowering cage, or a crack in the stone that has remained warm for centuries. Names are often altered as the person changes. A restless child may become “Many Feet.” A patient worker may acquire “The Unbroken Thread.” An old woman who has outlived three houses may be called “Wall-After-Wall.”

Their idea of time is geological without being abstract. They understand years, seasons, births, and the return of certain atmospheric changes, but the deepest measures are the lives of structures. A roof is young. A tree is established. A wall is trustworthy only after it has survived a generation. A path that has remained in use since before remembered speech is called *settled*, the highest praise given to anything.

They are suspicious of newness, but not because they worship the past. New things have not yet revealed how they fail. A tool must be used until its handle warms to the hand and its weaknesses become familiar. A house is not considered finished when built, but when it has begun to lean in a predictable direction. Children are allowed to alter paths, plant colonies, and rearrange rooms, but no change is accepted until the settlement has had time to absorb it.

The Vey eat what the summit offers in layers. They scrape edible films from shaded stone, gather the swollen organs of vapor-drinking plants, grind salt-fed seeds into bitter cakes, and roast the small animals that cannot be returned to their colonies. They consume mineral clays in measured amounts. They drink the sweet vapor of the grove through filters packed with hair, ash, and crushed leaf. At communal meals, the vapor vessels are passed slowly, mouth to mouth, and each person takes only what the others judge necessary.

Thirst is not an individual condition among them. If one person is dry, the family is dry. If one family’s storage bladders are empty, the grove wardens know that the whole settlement’s reserves must be counted again. A person who hoards vapor is not merely greedy but dangerous, like someone removing a supporting stone from a wall.

The grove wardens are not rulers. They are listeners.

They spend much of their lives among the mineral trees, their palms pressed against trunks, their faces close to vents in the earth. They know which trees are weakening, which roots have found a new heat seam, which vapor tastes of poisonous metal. They mark changes with braided cords, colored dust, and small arrangements of shells. Their authority comes from having noticed things before others did.

There are also the rim-watchers, who live near the settlement’s outer terraces. They study the pale distance below and the shifting cloud above it. They cannot see far, but they notice changes in color, pressure, and glare. They know when the world beneath the summit has become dangerous, though they cannot always explain why. Their warnings are rarely dramatic. A rim-watcher may return and say only, “The brightness is standing too high,” or, “The lower haze has turned inward.” Everyone begins securing roofs.

Children are not kept indoors. They crawl through plant mats, sleep in warm dust, and learn to distinguish harmless scratching from the sound of a structure becoming hollow. They are taught the summit’s dangers through games. One game involves crossing a terrace without stepping on the pale seams. Another requires them to identify hidden animals by the movement of leaves. The oldest children are taken to the outer rim and shown the glare below. They are told that falling is not the worst danger. The worst danger is forgetting that the ground can end.

The Vey do not speak often of leaving. The summit is too complete to feel like a prison. The world below is described in ancestral fragments: liquid gathering in open channels, clouds becoming rain, trees that grow tall without stone inside them, animals that fear people. These stories are not believed or disbelieved. They are treated as one treats dreams inherited from a dead relative.

Some individuals do descend. They are usually traders, exiles, curious young people, or those afflicted by the summit sickness that makes every breath feel borrowed. A few return years later, altered by softer air, carrying wet stones, bright metals, or stories no one can verify. They are welcomed, but never entirely trusted. Their skin smells wrong. Their bodies waste water carelessly. They wake at night because the silence below the summit is too complete.

Most Vey remain.

They sit in doorways with their knees drawn up, threading fibers while animals move around them. They lean against walls warmed by hidden fire. They watch plants open toward heat that cannot be seen. They repair old tools, old paths, old agreements. They marry not only people but households, groves, workrooms, and warm places in the stone. Their festivals are modest and prolonged: the first opening of a mineral tree, the return of a vapor season, the safe settling of a wall. They hang pale membranes and rust-colored leaves from the terraces, and for several days the settlement becomes brighter without becoming less dim.

At night, they gather beneath the trees.

There are no stars visible through the copper haze. The summit offers no great sky to contemplate. Instead, the Vey listen to the roots, the insects, the animal calls, and the deep shifting of the mountain beneath the floor. They pass vessels of gathered vapor. They tell stories in which houses grow old enough to remember their builders, in which animals inherit names, in which a person may become a wall without dying.

When they fall silent, they do not feel abandoned by the world.

The world is touching them everywhere: in the warm stone against their backs, in the dry click of leaves, in the creatures nesting beneath their sleeping places, in the mineral taste of every breath. They live not upon the mountain but within its last surviving habit of life.

And because they have lived there so long, they have begun to resemble it: rounded by endurance, layered with old deposits, difficult to move, difficult to destroy, and still—against every apparent condition—full of things quietly growing.
