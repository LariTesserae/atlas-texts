What lives there is not a people surrounded by plants.

It is a people made possible by them.

The inhabitants of the buried city are narrow-bodied, dry-skinned creatures adapted to a world in which moisture is not comfort but hazard. Their skin has the matte finish of mineral dust, ranging from ash-gray to muted ocher, with darker plates along the joints and spine. They do not sweat. Their bodies shed heat through fine vents beneath the ribs and at the backs of the hands, releasing almost imperceptible threads of warm vapor. In the open squares they dress lightly, in layered fabrics grown from leaf fiber and sealed against the abrasive air. In the cultivation shafts they wear masks, not against poison, but against an excess of living matter: spores, dry pollen, loose mineral scales, and the yellow vapors released by cut tissue.

Their faces are spare and deliberate. The eyes are large, dark, and protected by a translucent inner lid that closes against dust. Their mouths are narrow, suited less to speech than to breathing filtered air. Much of their communication is made through the hands, through taps on railings, through small changes in posture. A worker touching two fingers to the wall may be asking whether a stalk is mature. A palm pressed flat to a floor may indicate that something deep below has moved.

They are quiet because the city is quiet, but also because noise is not their oldest language.

Every inhabitant carries, somewhere beneath the skin, a colony of symbiotic growth. It lies in the bones, lungs, and hollow spaces around the organs: a dark, threadlike vegetation that extracts minerals from dust and regulates the body’s internal chemistry. It is inherited, cultivated, and sometimes exchanged. A child receives a first graft from a parent, but the graft is not considered complete until it has been exposed to the city’s oldest plants. The young are brought beneath a tree-tower, where they sleep against its root structure while the tissues in their bodies learn which minerals to retain and which to release.

Because of this, the people are never entirely separate from the city.

They can taste changes in the air before a vent opens. They feel the first distant pressure of a root through their teeth. When a mature structural plant is injured, those who have been grafted to its lineage may wake from sleep with pain in the same place. They do not call this mystical. It is recorded, measured, and entered into maintenance schedules. A plant’s discomfort is an infrastructure fault. A person’s discomfort may be an early warning.

Their homes are cool, angular chambers grown into the walls of larger plants. There are no kitchens in the old sense. Food is made from pressed mats, mineral-rich tissues, fungal films, and the dense amber gels harvested from transparent leaves. Meals are dry, compact, and intensely flavored. The inhabitants chew slowly. Their bodies require little liquid, and what fluids they possess are thick, dark, and carefully conserved. Water is kept only in sealed research vessels and emergency stores, treated as a dangerous reagent.

Children are raised communally in the shaded spaces beneath cultivation platforms. They learn to walk on rootwood before they learn to read. Their first tools are cutting hooks, grafting needles, and small tuning bars used to test the resonance of growing stems. A child who can distinguish healthy root vibration from the beginning of mineral rot is considered ready for formal instruction.

There are many kinds of work.

Some tend the low black-green mats, scraping away invasive growth and applying powdered ore to their margins. Some climb the tall stalks in harnesses, fastening temporary braces around new limbs and inspecting the pale vessels through which the plants circulate gel. Some work in the processing halls, where fronds are reduced to insulation, paper, medicine, fuel, and the dense artificial wood used in construction. Others walk the unfinished districts with survey rods, marking where future roads must leave room for future roots.

No one owns a tree-tower outright. A mature plant belongs to its lineage, its district, and its own continuing growth. It has a name, though the name may be a sequence of tones transmitted through the stem rather than a spoken word. It has a record of injuries, harvests, grafts, and former shapes. When it grows around an old building, the building is not demolished. It becomes part of the plant’s history.

The people themselves are similarly regarded as partly civic and partly biological. A worker injured on the rail line may be treated in a clinic, but the damaged tissue is also taken to a propagation chamber. If the injury has caused an unusual adaptation—a new mineral tolerance, a resistance to predator scent, an ability to detect changes in pressure—it may be preserved and cultivated in others. Individuality is valued, but so is useful variation. A person is a person, and also a possible beginning.

Their dead are not buried.

The body is dried, separated, and placed into sealed decomposition beds where pale fungal-plant hybrids dismantle it slowly. Certain dense structures are mineralized and returned to foundations. Other tissues become nutrient substrate for new growth. The dead may survive as a tree’s changed chemistry, as a strengthening seam in a bridge, as a medicinal compound, or as the dark interior of a leaf membrane. There is no ceremony of disappearance. There is only transfer.

Predators complicate the city’s sense of safety.

The stem-mimicking hunters are not vermin, and the inhabitants do not try to eradicate them. They are part of the subterranean balance, though an old and uneasy one. The predators keep to the less settled districts, where they stand among immature plants and wait. Their bodies are thin, jointed, and almost perfectly still. They have learned the shape of maintenance poles, root braces, and dead stalks. Their heads turn only when they have already chosen a direction.

A person crossing an unsafe district wears silver leaf in visible bands around the arms and throat. The material gives off a vibration the hunters dislike. More important, the plants recognize it. Along a marked corridor, the vegetation remains loose and pliable. If a predator enters, the leaves stiffen, pale, and point toward it. The warning moves through the avenue before the creature does.

The inhabitants do not run unless the plants run first.

Far below them live the browsing things, the armored grazers, the pollinators, and smaller organisms that occupy every crack and sealed cavity. The people maintain breeding chambers for some of these creatures and tolerate the rest. A city without animals would be considered sterile, and sterility is feared more than danger. A sterile plant cannot adapt. A sterile district cannot recover.

Above all, the inhabitants fear interruption.

They fear a conveyor that stops and cannot be restarted. A flowering event that occurs out of season. A new shoot emerging beneath a foundation whose root allowance was miscalculated. They fear a sudden softness in the floor, a failure of the dry chemistry that keeps every living tissue from swelling and splitting. Their history is a long record of near-collapse: districts lost to uncontrolled growth, towers that opened all their leaves at once, entire populations sealed into chambers where the air became too rich to breathe.

Yet they do not dream of conquering the plants.

The oldest civic buildings contain no statues of founders. Instead, they contain cross-sections: walls opened to display root networks, towers cut away to show their internal currents, maps of growth accumulated over generations. The people honor those who learned how to live without asking the world to become simpler.

And beneath all of them is the thing that has no registry.

When the slow pulses travel through the mineral floor, inhabitants step out of factories and homes. They place their hands against the paving. The plants turn together. In the open squares, the leaves align toward some invisible point below.

No one agrees what moves there.

Some say it is the first root, older than the chamber and responsible for the entire subterranean forest. Some say it is a buried animal of impossible size, feeding on pressure and stone. Some believe the city itself has grown a heart: not an organ, but a region of living mineral from which all later plants descend.

The oldest inhabitants do not speculate. They listen for the pattern.

The pulses come at long intervals, separated by years or decades. Each one is followed by new fractures, new shoots, new ground rising beneath the avenues. Sometimes an unbuilt district is found altered after the vibration: its foundations lifted into terraces, its survey stakes enclosed in hard green tissue, its unfinished roads redirected toward a vent that did not exist before.

The city’s people understand this as neither invasion nor blessing.

It is simply the next stage of the place becoming itself.

So they wait with their hands on the floor. The workers pause. The animals go still. Even the predators seem to lean toward the vibration.

Then the movement passes.

The plants relax. The lights continue their false daylight. The conveyors begin again.

And somewhere beneath the oldest roots, whatever lives there continues making room.
