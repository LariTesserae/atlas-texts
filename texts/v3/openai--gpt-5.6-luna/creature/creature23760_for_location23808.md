What lives in the basin is not a species.

It is a process with bodies.

The smallest of its bodies are the plate-backed things that crowd the shallows. They have no faces, though some possess clusters of eyes and others have only soft sensory pits beneath their armor. They taste vibration through their feet. A tremor in the floor is hunger, danger, weather, instruction, or all three. They move when the basin requires movement and stop when the basin requires stillness. Their individual lives are brief and interchangeable. When one dies, the others open it along the seams, draw out its metals, and return the emptied plates to the water.

The basin does not waste a body.

Larger creatures carry furnaces inside themselves. They are low and heavy, ribbed like collapsed engines, with wet armor stretched over chambers that pulse orange in the dark. They feed on the dissolved mineral runoff and exhale heat through the slits in their flanks. When they gather around a vent, they are not resting. They are refining. Their bodies separate what the deep mechanisms have loosened from the stone: iron from salt, charge from pressure, memory from matter.

They are blind to the distinction between feeding and working.

Nothing here works for itself. The creature that eats is also a pump. The thing that hunts is also a sensor. The pale colonies clinging to the shelves open and close in coordinated ranks, drawing water through their bodies and testing it for changes in depth, temperature, and chemical composition. When they all shut at once, something below has altered its rhythm. When they open again, the basin has accepted the alteration.

There are animals that do not move through the water so much as persuade it to move around them. Black, membrane-winged things skim the surface without making wakes. Their bodies are almost flat, though they can fold themselves into narrow vertical shapes and vanish into the mist. They gather static from the air in trembling crystal crowns, then discharge it through the water in bright, silent webs. The discharge wakes dormant mechanisms beneath the floor. It also kills whatever small life is caught inside it.

Their young are born from the dead.

Some inhabitants are too large to be seen whole. The long blind hunters that lift their heads from the mud may be fingers, or feelers, or exposed nerves belonging to something buried beneath the basin. Their bodies descend through the sediment and disappear into the apertures. When the underground machinery turns, these creatures turn with it. They are not dragged. They are part of the turning.

In the deepest channels, the water is thick with larvae that look like threads of tarnished silver. They drift upward against the current, collect around the pressure vents, and knot themselves into living filters. They prevent the basin from choking on its own minerals. Once saturated, they loosen and fall. The creatures below receive them, dissolve them, and send their elements back through the water.

The cycle has no beginning.

At intervals, all the animals receive the same command.

It does not arrive as sound. It travels through the stone, through the water, through the hollow chambers in their bodies. The command may be a memory of the hidden machinery, or the machinery’s equivalent of thought. Herds divide. Channels open. Furnaces turn. The circular ranks around the central water tighten until the entire basin seems to have one many-jointed body, one inward-facing attention.

The inhabitants fear the center, but fear is not separate from obedience here.

They gather because something beneath the still water is waking.

When the surface begins to show the impossible landscapes, the animals do not mistake them for visions. They recognize them. The warm sky, the green growth, the structures made by hands—these are not dreams of a world they have never known. They are impressions retained in the basin’s mineral memory, images of conditions that existed before the present cycle, or may exist somewhere outside it. The creatures face the images because they are drawn toward an older instruction: become other, become elsewhere, become alive in a different way.

But the basin cannot permit the transformation.

When the image folds inward, the animals scatter. Not in panic, exactly. They are evacuating. They clear the channels before the pressure wall rises. The smallest bodies flatten themselves into the stone. The furnace-creatures brace their limbs. The membrane-things dive beneath the surface and discharge their gathered charge into the depths.

Then the hidden engine changes shifts.

The roar is its voice. The white illumination is its opening eye.

For a few seconds, every inhabitant is visible as part of a single design: thousands of bodies arranged as valves, batteries, grinders, probes, filters, mouths. The basin’s life is revealed not as a population but as an anatomy. Each animal is an organ that can crawl. Each death is maintenance. Each birth is replacement.

And somewhere beneath the deepest floor, the greater body continues to work.

Perhaps the creatures believe they are living.

Perhaps that distinction has never occurred to them.

Perhaps, in the basin, this is what living means: to be consumed by the cycle, to carry out the old instructions in flesh, to move when the stone moves, and to wait—packed together in the dark water—for the day when the hidden machinery finally remembers what it was made to do.
