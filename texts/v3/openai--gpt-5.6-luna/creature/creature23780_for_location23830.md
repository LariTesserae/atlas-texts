What lives there is difficult to separate from what it lives upon.

In the warm margins of the vents, beneath the translucent fronds and mineral veils, there are bodies that have learned to become almost indistinguishable from the seabed. They are low, broad things, pressed flat by the weight of the water. Their outer surfaces are dark with iron and silt, ridged like shale, and often furred with the same bacterial films that coat the surrounding stone. A mature one may be mistaken for a fallen plate or a shelf of mineral crust until the current changes and its edges draw inward.

They are called nothing by themselves. They possess no sound with which to name one another.

The smallest are scarcely more than pale threads beneath the mud. They enter through the narrowest seams, following gradients of warmth and dissolved metal. Their bodies are flexible but not soft; each is supported by a series of internal rings that contract in sequence, allowing it to move through sediment without displacing much of it. The mud closes behind them. They leave no tunnel, only a temporary difference in pressure that vanishes almost at once.

As they grow, they stop traveling.

A larger one settles near a vent and anchors itself beneath the surface. Its upper side hardens into overlapping plates, each plate thin and matte, colored by whatever minerals the water carries. Its underside remains alive and sensitive, spread through the mud like a root system. From this hidden tissue, hundreds of hair-fine filaments extend outward. They taste the current. They register vibration. They detect the passing of a scavenger, the collapse of a chimney, the distant displacement of a large body through water.

The creature does not see the basin. It receives the basin.

A current arriving from the east is not wind or motion but a change in pressure along one flank. A vent beginning to weaken is felt as a cooling in the buried tissue. Falling silt announces itself first as a gradual dulling of the water, then as thousands of tiny impacts across the armored back. The animal knows the shape of its surroundings through interruption: where the current splits, where heat gathers, where the floor is hollow beneath its weight.

It feeds by filtering.

Along the margins of its body are folds that open and close with the slow regularity of breathing. Water enters carrying bacteria, dissolved organic matter, mineral dust, and the countless invisible remnants of life. The creature removes what can sustain it and returns the rest as a fine paste that settles around its base. Over centuries, this waste forms a low mound. Other organisms colonize the mound. Their growth changes the current. The animal responds by shifting one edge of itself a few inches, then remaining there for generations.

It does not build a home. Its body becomes one.

At certain times, when the vents grow rich and the water trembles with chemical change, the adults release clouds of minute young into the current. The young are almost weightless. They drift sideways for days or weeks, carried across shelves and hollows, through curtains of silt and the blue-white leakage of living films. Most are buried. Some are eaten. A few encounter the right combination of warmth, pressure, and mineral flow. They attach themselves to stone and begin to flatten.

No one watches over them. No one could.

The creatures communicate through the ground. When one detects danger—a sudden collapse, the passing wake of something immense—it contracts its buried tissues. The contraction travels into the sediment as a small, localized tremor. Nearby bodies feel it and answer. The message spreads outward, weakening with distance: not alarm, precisely, but a brief shared withdrawal. Filaments close. Feeding folds seal. Plates draw tight against the mud.

Then the basin resumes its movement, and they open again.

There are older ones buried almost completely beneath the plain. Their living surfaces may be no larger than a hand, while their hidden bodies extend through layers of compressed sediment. They have survived the deaths of vents, the shifting of shelves, and entire changes in the chemistry of the sea. When a collapse covers them, they do not struggle upward. They wait beneath the new weight. Their metabolism slows until it is nearly indistinguishable from dormancy, and their tissues harden around pockets of warmth that may persist for centuries.

Some never wake.

Their bodies become part of the floor: dark plates in the mud, mineralized folds, shallow hollows where the sediment settles differently. Later generations anchor upon them, feeding from the same buried chemistry. In this way, the creatures do not merely inhabit the seabed. They accumulate into it.

The largest among them may be mistaken for landscape even while alive. A long ridge of armored tissue can extend beneath the silt for many body-lengths, its sensory filaments scattered through the plain. It has no central shape, no obvious head, and perhaps no meaningful boundary. A collapse may kill one portion while another continues to feed. A vent may nourish a distant section whose existence is never registered as separate.

Whether such a being is one animal or many is a distinction the darkness has rendered unnecessary.

When the current reverses, its plates flex in unison beneath the sediment. The motion is too subtle to see, but the mud lifts around it. A faint cloud rises. The nearby fronds fold. Farther away, small scavengers pause and press themselves flat.

For a moment, something vast has moved beneath the plain.

Then it becomes still again, and the water moves over it as though over stone.
