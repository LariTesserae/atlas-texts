What lives there is not a colony in any organized sense, though there may be dozens of its kind hidden in the cracks.

Each is small enough to fit in a cupped hand, though few are ever seen whole. Its body is soft, narrow, and flattened, a pale thing almost indistinguishable from the mineral films it feeds upon. Along its back runs a translucent ridge, not quite a shell and not quite skin, through which the chamber’s blue-white radiance passes. Under the light, the animal seems to contain a dim arrangement of cloudy threads: channels for water, perhaps, or nerves spread so widely through its body that no central shape can be identified.

It has no eyes. There would be little use for them here. Instead, its entire surface is sensitive to heat, pressure, and the faint electrical changes that travel through wet stone. It knows where the rock is warming before steam appears. It knows when a fracture is about to open. A droplet falling somewhere overhead reaches it as a tremor through the floor, and the creature turns toward the disturbance—not from curiosity, but because falling water exposes fresh mineral surfaces.

It moves by slow contractions of its underside. There are no visible legs. The body draws itself forward in a series of minute folds, gripping the dampness between grains of powdered basalt. Its passage leaves a clean, pale line behind, as though it has licked the stone. Along this track, the white deposits are thinner and the yellow-gray crystals have been polished down to a dull sheen. It does not eat the rock itself. It grazes the living edge where water, heat, and mineral meet, scraping away the delicate films that form there and absorbing whatever warmth and dissolved matter they contain.

Most of the time it remains pressed into a narrow seam, with only the front of its body extended. It may stay there for hours, perhaps days, while the chamber changes around it. A new shelf forms. A trickle alters course. A section of black crust falls into a basin and sends a brief cloud of steam over the floor. The creature withdraws whenever the pressure becomes wrong, retreating backward into the crack without any sign of haste. It does not flee the way an animal flees danger. It simply ceases to be present on the exposed surface.

Its body is nearly colorless where it is thin, but thicker portions carry a faint gray-blue tint, like ice formed around trapped smoke. At the edges it is delicate and frayed, with short sensory filaments that spread over the stone. These filaments taste the water. If the water is too hot, the creature folds itself inward. If it is too cool, it waits. If the mineral concentration is favorable, it settles and feeds until the surface beneath it becomes smooth.

There is no face to speak of. At its leading end is a narrow opening surrounded by soft ridges, and behind this a pair of darkened organs respond to pressure changes. They may be remnants of eyes, or something older than eyes. When the chamber’s rock gives one of its deep settling ticks, these organs tighten. When condensation falls, they relax.

The creatures do not call to one another. They have no voices and make no attempt to share territory. If two meet along a wet channel, one turns aside, usually before their bodies touch. Their only communication may be chemical: a faint alteration in the mineral film left behind, a trace that warns another of exhausted deposits or unstable stone. Even this is uncertain. Their lives are too slow and their world too small for society to have become necessary.

They reproduce rarely, if reproduction is what occurs. In the sheltered hollows behind the brightest mineral bands, small translucent knots sometimes appear, attached to the rock by threads no thicker than hair. They remain there through several cycles of heating and cooling, slowly gathering material from the wet surface. Eventually one loosens and begins to move. There is no visible parent nearby. The new creature is simply another pale process among the processes of the chamber.

It has no sense of the chamber as a place. It does not know that the rock is young, or that the ceiling may one day collapse, or that smooth deposits might eventually conceal the sharp shelves and raw fractures. Its world consists of gradients: warmer, cooler; wetter, drier; stable, shifting; rich, bare. It inhabits boundaries rather than spaces. The animal is found wherever one condition gives way to another, where water has not yet become vapor and stone has not yet become sediment.

When the chamber seems empty, it is usually because the creatures have withdrawn into the dark seams—not to hide, but to wait out a change in pressure. When one emerges, it does so without announcement. Its pale body slides from beneath a ledge, crosses a bright ribbon of water, and begins to feed at the edge of a growing white shelf. The radiance passes through it. Steam gathers briefly around its back. Then the chamber resumes its quiet construction, and the creature becomes almost indistinguishable from the mineral surface it is slowly wearing away.
