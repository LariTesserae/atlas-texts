The basin is inhabited by things that have learned to live as parts of a system rather than as separate creatures.

The smallest are the wall-grazers. They resemble flattened seeds or tiny helmets, each no larger than a fingernail, with a pale shell ridged like old mortar. They move in slow, deliberate clusters along the wet seams, scraping away the invisible film that gathers there. Their undersides are soft and many-legged. They do not flee from water; they flee from dryness. If a patch of wall begins to lose its dampness, hundreds of them converge upon it, carrying droplets in the grooves of their shells. By morning the stone is wet again, and the creatures have returned to their places.

The long-bodied animals in the channels are more secretive. They are not fish, though they swim, and not eels, though they bend like eels. Their bodies are jointed beneath a smooth silver skin. Each segment can fold independently, allowing them to turn corners without slowing. They feed on the shell-backed grazers, but only after the grazers have died or molted; living colonies are left undisturbed. At certain hours they gather beneath the reflecting pans and arrange themselves in circles, their blunt heads pointing inward. The water then becomes perfectly still. Something below must be listening.

The insects are less obedient.

They hatch from the tower, the wall vents, the undersides of tiles, and sometimes from drops of water that fall too slowly. Their forms change according to where they emerge. Those born in clay have heavy bodies and wooden wings. Those born in water are nearly transparent. Those born in darkness possess the small blue lights in their heads, and drift in columns above the deepest troughs like fragments of a submerged night sky.

They are drawn to attention. A sound repeated too often will gather them. A face reflected in still water will gather them faster. They swarm around anyone who stands motionless for longer than a few breaths, settling on the shoulders, eyelids, and lips. They are not biting insects. They seem to be trying to prevent a person from becoming part of the basin’s silence.

The reed-dwellers are the loudest inhabitants. They have narrow bodies, wet wings, and long, jointed legs that fold against their chests when they perch. Their feathers—or whatever covers them—are gray, black, and faintly translucent. When they take flight, water pours from their bodies in shining threads. They nest in the black reeds, but never build nests. Instead, they knot shed feathers and strips of moss around the stalks, making small hanging cages in which their young cling upside down.

They do not sing. They announce.

One cry warns of a change in pressure. Another means that a creature has crossed a forbidden line. A third is used when the tower begins to breathe. Their calls are metallic and almost articulate, and in the echoing enclosure they often sound like human speech broken into pieces. Travelers have mistaken them for voices behind walls. The birds have learned to imitate names, though they do not understand them. If a person speaks their own name aloud, the reed-dwellers answer from every side.

There are larger animals, too, though they are rarely seen whole.

Something broad and pale moves beneath the floor. Its passage raises damp blisters in the mortar and sends ripples through channels that have no visible connection. It is older than the present arrangement of the basin, perhaps older than the people who built it. The walls were constructed around its movements. The tower may have been placed above one of its breathing chambers.

When it exhales, the masonry sweats.

When it turns, every pan trembles.

It does not feed on the other animals. It feeds on what is left behind: shed shells, dead insects, feathers, flakes of fungus, fingerprints in soft clay. It consumes evidence of change. A creature may die in the basin, but by the next dawn the floor has taken its outline, and the thing beneath has taken the rest.

The fungus is alive in a more patient fashion. Its pale shapes spread across tiles, but they do not grow randomly. A hand-shaped colony will eventually become a map. A branching one will mark a route through the channels. When the fungus reaches the edge of a raised path, it stops. When a wall collapses somewhere beyond the visible enclosure, it blooms all at once in response.

Some of the maps show places that do not exist yet.

The tower has its own inhabitants. Pale roots descend through its hollow cylinders, though there is no soil for them to feed upon. They hang in the openings like wet hair and withdraw whenever approached. Insects emerge from their tangles, along with small blind creatures whose bodies are soft as tongues. These creatures remain near the tower until they have grown enough armor to survive the channels. A few never leave. They coil around the inner walls and make the steady chewing sound heard from above.

The tower may be eating them.

Or they may be eating the tower.

Either way, the tower is not empty. It is inhabited by a colony that has no single shape. At times it is roots, at times insects, at times breath. When the air pressure shifts, the whole colony moves upward through the cylinders, and the tower groans as though trying to remember a voice.

There are also custodians.

They appear only when the basin has been disturbed.

They are small, stooped figures assembled from whatever the place has available: reeds tied with tendon, shell plates mortared over a clay frame, scraps of black fungus stretched like skin. Their hands are many-jointed and exceedingly careful. They do not walk across the raised paths unless carrying something—a dead insect, a fallen feather, a fragment of tile, a drop of water cupped in both palms. They repair the basin with no tools, pressing shells into cracks, smoothing mortar with their fingers, guiding stray animals back across the proper lines.

No two custodians are alike, and none remains visible for long. A figure may dissolve into a heap of wet reeds when watched. A hand may continue working after the body has vanished. Sometimes a line of fresh thumbprints appears along a wall, leading into a sealed seam.

They are not the builders’ descendants. They do not remember the builders. They maintain the rules because the rules are the only form of life they know.

When a traveler enters, the custodians do not attack. They observe.

They watch where the traveler’s feet fall. They watch what is carried. They watch whether the traveler speaks, whether they touch the water, whether they attempt to cross a path empty-handed. If the rules are obeyed, the basin tolerates the intrusion. The insects keep their distance. The long-bodied swimmers retreat into the deeper channels. The reed-dwellers mutter from the walls.

But if the traveler leaves something behind—a coin, a blade, a strip of cloth, a tooth—the basin accepts it immediately. The object becomes wet. Moss finds it. Shell-backed things gather around it. By the next cycle of dim light, it has been incorporated into the architecture.

The custodians are especially attentive to names.

They cannot bear a name spoken twice. The first utterance is carried through the walls. The second is answered from below. After that, the basin begins to treat the named person as a feature rather than a visitor. Their footprints remain wet. Their shadow may pass over the channels when they are standing still. The reed-dwellers begin practicing their voice.

The oldest inhabitants are not animals at all, but habits preserved in matter.

Water remembers its route. Sound remembers where it was made. Clay remembers the pressure of hands. The walls remember every repair and distinguish the old work from the new. In the deepest pans, faint lights gather around these memories, forming brief shapes: a palm, a fish, a child’s heel, the impression of a basket that has been gone for centuries.

At such times, the creatures become quiet.

The insects stop vibrating. The channel animals rise beneath the surface. The birds fold their wings. Even the thing under the floor seems to pause in its turning.

Then the memory passes.

The water resumes its movement. The wall-grazers return to their seams. A custodian emerges from a crack carrying a broken shell, and somewhere in the tower the hidden colony begins to chew again.

Nothing here is truly wild, and nothing is fully governed. The animals follow arrangements older than instinct. The plants grow where construction permits them. The custodians repair what must be repaired and leave the rest to become part of the design. Even the unseen creature beneath the basin obeys some ancient cycle of pressure, hunger, and return.

The basin does not belong to its inhabitants.

Its inhabitants belong to its continuance.

They feed it, inhabit it, clean it, mark it, disturb it, and disappear into it. They are the movement within the geometry, the life that keeps the old work from becoming dead architecture. And when the rain begins from the walls themselves, every creature turns toward the same unseen opening—not in fear, but in recognition.

Something has come home.
