What lives there is difficult to separate from the water.

At first, the animals among the roots seem to be only the broad, mud-colored amphibians already common in the slack channels. They lie motionless for hours, half buried in silt, their backs furred with weed and small clinging fish. Their eyes are bright, but not alert in the way of predators. They watch the current more than they watch the world. A change in pressure, the distant opening of a gate, the arrival of meltwater from some high ravine—these reach them before sound does.

Then one of them moves.

It unfolds from the mud with the slow, deliberate force of something built for water rather than land. Its forelimbs are long and jointed, ending in wide hands with dark membranes between the fingers. The hind legs are shorter, powerful, and webbed almost to the hips. Along its flanks are narrow folds of skin that open when it dives, not quite gills but enough to keep it beneath the surface for long periods. Its head is blunt and almost featureless except for the eyes and a pair of sensitive pits beside the mouth. There is no visible ear. It hears through the bones of its face.

The oldest of them carry things.

A length of copper wire may be braided through the skin of one forearm. A small ceramic insulator may be fixed into the ridge of another’s back. Some wear rings of colored stone, not as ornament but as records: the river that carried them, the season of their birth, the locations of dangerous machinery. They do not collect the remains of the old world indiscriminately. Iron is too heavy, glass too sharp, most plastics brittle and useless. They favor materials that endure underwater: copper, fired clay, certain alloys, the dense black rubber peeled from old cables.

Their bodies are dark at the back and pale underneath, the colors of a submerged rock turning in the current. In bright water they can disappear by becoming part of the landscape. In deep channels they travel in groups, each animal holding the ankle of the one ahead. They do not swim in a line for efficiency. They swim that way because the last animal feels what the first cannot: the eddies forming behind them, the sudden drop in depth, the approach of a large body moving without haste through the kelp.

They have names for one another, though the names are not spoken.

A name is a pattern of taps and low vibrations made against stone, metal, or the bones of the chest. In open water the sound carries poorly, but through the old retaining walls it can travel a long distance. A family may sit beneath a bridge footing and communicate with the settlement on the opposite bank, their messages passing through concrete in a sequence of faint knocks. The meaning is carried partly by rhythm and partly by pressure. A warning is not merely a sound but a disturbance in the surrounding water.

They speak of places as if places were alive.

The pumping station has a temperament. The western turbine is tired and must not be approached during a rising river. The submerged rail tunnel is hungry in winter because stones accumulate at its lower mouth. A certain drowned road is generous, providing mussels in its cracks. The cable crossing between the peaks is called the Singing Span, though it sings only when the wind comes from the north. These are not metaphors. To the inhabitants, a machine that moves according to hidden forces possesses a kind of behavior, and behavior is enough to make something part of the community.

They do not repair everything.

They understand that some failures are useful. A broken floodgate creates a quiet backwater where young fish can grow. A collapsed tunnel roof makes a cavern where the largest animals sleep through the brightest part of the day. The spreading reeds around a concrete platform provide shelter for eggs. If every old machine were restored, the basin would become poorer.

But certain mechanisms are kept working.

The inhabitants clear gravel from intake mouths. They scrape mineral growth from pressure valves. They wedge stones beneath gates that slam too violently. They replace cables with braided roots and hammered strips of copper. They know which pumps can be left alone and which must be coaxed through their cycles by hand. Their maintenance is not an attempt to recover the old purpose of the machinery. They have no interest in pumping the basin dry, reopening the mines, or bringing the drowned roads back into service.

They maintain thresholds.

A gate that opens at the wrong depth can empty a nursery channel. A failed turbine can alter the temperature of an entire valley. A jammed sluice can send a wall of water through a reed settlement and carry away everything that cannot anchor itself. Their work keeps the violence distributed. They do not control the rivers. They make small corrections, the way a creature shifts its weight to remain upright in a current.

Their homes are rarely built from nothing.

They occupy the spaces the basin has already prepared: the upper chambers of maintenance stations, the hollow drums of machines, the arches beneath old bridges, the dry pockets inside mountainside tunnels. A dwelling may consist of little more than a ledge above the water, a woven mat of reed roots, and a narrow channel cut through the wall so that the occupants can leave without exposing themselves to the open current.

The young are born in warm caverns fed by mineral springs. For the first months they cling to the backs of adults, their hands strong enough to remain attached while the parent dives. They are nearly colorless when newly born, with translucent skin and oversized eyes. Older children spend much of their time in the shallows, learning the names of stones and the moods of the water. They are not taught to swim. They are taught to notice when swimming becomes impossible.

At dusk, they gather near the old machines.

The turbine’s slow thump attracts them from the surrounding reeds. They settle in the mud and on the concrete shelves, arranging themselves according to age. The oldest place their palms against the housing and feel the rotation. Children imitate the sound with their throats. No one knows whether this is music, instruction, or ceremony. Perhaps those distinctions were never useful here.

They remember through vibration.

A flood that happened generations ago can be called back by a pattern struck against a rail. The rhythm describes the first river to break through, the direction the debris traveled, the number of people lost in the eastern channels. A death is marked by lowering a stone into deep water and listening until it strikes bottom. The time it takes to hear the impact becomes part of the dead one’s remembrance.

They do not bury their dead. The basin is too fluid for graves, and the deep channels are understood to be older than any family. Bodies are weighted with stone and carried to places where the current divides. There, the dead are released. Bones settle into the submerged forests. Fish clean them. Silt covers them. In time, the names of the dead become associated with particular ridges, caves, or stretches of river.

The largest animals are not their enemies, exactly.

There is one kind that travels beneath the kelp in the broad valleys, a long, pale-backed creature with a body wide as a boat and a mouth made for filtering whatever the rivers bring down. The inhabitants call it the Slow One. They recognize individuals by scars and by the shape of their wakes. When one approaches, all smaller life gives way. The amphibious people climb onto ledges or press themselves into the roots until it has passed.

Sometimes they follow it.

The Slow One’s passage stirs the bottom, exposing buried shells, old tools, and seams of edible growth. Its wake opens temporary roads through the kelp. Young inhabitants ride behind it for the pleasure of being carried, though the adults pretend this is forbidden. If the creature dies, the body becomes an event lasting several seasons. Fish gather. Water changes color. Families relocate toward it. The skeleton may remain visible for decades, a pale framework around which an entire settlement grows.

There are other things in the basin that have no names anyone agrees on.

Something lives in the deepest flooded shafts, where the water is warmer and metallic sediment hangs in permanent clouds. It does not rise, but machinery near the shafts occasionally begins to move without power. A line of lamps may illuminate one by one beneath the surface. A gate may open against pressure. The inhabitants avoid these places, not because they believe in spirits, but because the water there carries patterns they cannot interpret.

They are practical about the unknown. Practicality does not require certainty.

Above water, a few of them travel the terraces in wet groups, using the old roads when the rain is light. They are awkward on land and become desiccated quickly, their skin dulling to gray. They carry shallow clay vessels filled with basin water and pour it over their hands and faces as they go. At the highest ledges they inspect the cables, the warning signs, the exposed parts of the pumping stations. Birds nest around them without fear. Moss grows on their shoulders if they remain still too long.

They look up often.

The high country is not their roof but their source. Every storm, every waterfall, every distant snowmelt eventually becomes part of the water they inhabit. They can tell where the rain has fallen by its temperature and taste. A metallic bitterness means the northern cliffs. Fine grit means the old mining terraces. A sudden sweetness may mean a new landslide, a fresh wound opening in the mountain.

When the great rains come, they do not flee the basin.

They move toward the violence.

Families leave their homes and gather in the channels where the rivers enter. They loosen blocked gates, cut away mats of floating vegetation, and drive fish toward deeper water. They work in darkness and spray, passing tools from hand to hand while the mountains disappear behind sheets of rain. Their signals become nearly inaudible. They communicate by touch: a palm against a shoulder, fingers closing around a wrist, the brief pressure that means stop, descend, wait.

For a few minutes, the whole population may vanish beneath the water.

Then, when the currents have divided and the gates have settled, they emerge among the reeds. Some have been carried far from where they began. Some will not return. The survivors do not call this a disaster, though it is one. They call it a change in the basin, and change is the condition under which everything here remains alive.

From a distance, they are only interruptions in the landscape: a dark shape rising near a buoy, a hand on a rail, a line of heads moving through the flooded grass. Up close, they are scarred, patient, and attentive. Their lives are measured in river levels, turbine cycles, spawning seasons, and the slow relocation of stone.

They do not believe they inhabit the drowned high country.

They believe the basin inhabits them: water in the lungs, silt beneath the nails, old machinery remembered in the bones, and every mountain current passing through the shape of the body before continuing downward.
