What lives in the city is not a population in the ordinary sense.

There are no markets, no households, no gathering places where one generation teaches the next how to survive. The city is inhabited by presences that use its rooms without ever quite occupying them. They come and go by intention, by repetition, by accident, and sometimes by being remembered in the wrong place.

The most numerous are called, for lack of a better word, the Pale Residents.

They are tall and narrow, though their height is not consistent. A Pale Resident may enter a house as a figure scarcely larger than a child and emerge from the same doorway with its head level to the lintel. Their bodies are made of a substance resembling the mountain’s white mineral, but less solid. Under their skin, fine blue-black lines shift slowly, as if veins were carrying not blood but distant architecture.

Their faces are smooth except for a vertical opening from brow to chin. This opening does not function as a mouth. It widens when they are listening and seals when they are thinking. Inside is not flesh, tongue, or darkness, but a succession of small rooms: stairwells, courtyards, empty chambers, sometimes a lit window looking onto an unknown district.

They do not breathe. They take in the dry air only when they need to change shape.

Most of the time, they wear the city’s silence around themselves like clothing. Their movements produce no sound, but the stone under them remembers each step. If one Resident walks the same route often enough, a shallow track appears in the pavement. If another follows the track, the path deepens. If too many follow it, the avenue begins to lead somewhere else.

They have no names. A name, in the city, is a dangerous form of permanence.

Instead, they identify one another by absences: the missing finger, the unlit chamber beneath the ribs, the number of steps taken before turning, the district that refuses to appear behind them. Two Residents who have known each other for centuries may still be uncertain whether they are meeting for the first time.

They recognize the dead more easily than the living.

The Pale Residents spend much of their existence maintaining things that do not seem broken. They scrape powder from doorways and lay it back down in altered patterns. They stand beneath bridges while the far ends move. They press their palms against walls to feel whether a room has become too real. In the highest quarter, they walk around the empty summit basin in slow processions, carrying nothing.

Occasionally one of them kneels at the basin’s edge and lowers its head into the distance.

When it rises again, its body may contain a new feature: a window in the chest, an extra joint, a second shadow, or the faint outline of a landscape beneath its skin. Such changes are not regarded as illness. They are records.

The Residents are not builders, but they are continuously building.

They extend streets by remembering where streets ought to be. They restore towers by refusing to notice that the towers are leaning. They repair walls through attendance. A damaged structure left unwitnessed will soften and lose its purpose; a watched structure remains upright, even if the observer stands far away or has never existed.

This is why the city has so many empty squares. Every building requires a certain amount of unshared attention. If too many Residents enter a place at once, the walls begin to flatten. Rooms lose depth. Doors become painted marks. The Residents understand this instinctively and avoid gathering in numbers.

They do not speak in one another’s presence. Communication occurs through orientation.

A Resident facing a doorway means: *I have seen what is beyond it.*

Facing away means: *It has seen me.*

Standing perfectly still in the center of a street is an act of warning. Standing still in a room is an invitation. Standing still on a bridge means that the bridge has moved and should not yet be trusted.

Sometimes, however, one of them emits a small dry click from the vertical opening in its face. Another answers from a great distance. The exchange may continue for hours, passing through stone and tower chambers, until the whole city seems to be clicking softly to itself.

There are smaller inhabitants as well.

They live beneath stairs and inside walls: pale, many-jointed things no longer than a hand, with bodies like folded strips of mineral. The Residents call them the Lineage, though they do not breed. They emerge when a building has forgotten its original shape and trace its corners with their forelimbs. Where they pass, angles become exact again.

The Lineage feed on misalignment. A crooked doorway, a stair with one impossible step, a tower whose interior does not match its exterior—these attract them. They gather in clusters and worry at the discrepancy until the structure either settles into a new arrangement or ceases to be visible.

They are not harmless. If one finds a person whose body does not agree with its shadow, it may attempt to correct the error. The result is usually a slow separation. The shadow remains where it was, and the person continues elsewhere.

In the high towers live the membrane-dwellers.

They have no stable shape. Their presence is announced by upward-moving dust, doors opening inward, and the small black impressions that appear on ceilings. They cross rooms by stretching themselves between surfaces, becoming thin as skin and broad as sails. When they pass behind a translucent membrane, their outlines suggest enormous bodies with too many limbs, though the suggestion may belong to the observer.

The membrane-dwellers do not enter the city to hunt. They inspect it.

They press against walls and listen for movement in the hidden portions of the mountain. When something below begins to form, they gather in the nearest tower and become still. Their bodies then show the shape of what is coming: a staircase, a district, a buried gate, occasionally a figure walking upside down.

After such visitations, the city is always slightly larger.

The slate-colored six-legged animals belong to the open terraces. They are called cracks by the Residents, though this is less a name than a description of their method of travel. A crack moves across the ground before the animal does. Its body follows several moments later, sliding over the line as though being drawn into existence.

The creatures have no visible eyes. They locate one another through pressure in the stone. When frightened, they do not flee; they become part of the mountain until the danger has passed.

They feed on trails.

A path walked repeatedly becomes permanent, and the slate animals graze along its edges, scraping away the excess reality. Where they feed, the paving grows thin and the direction of the street becomes uncertain. They are the reason no road remains entirely reliable for long.

At certain times, the Pale Residents leave small mineral nodules beneath stairs for them. Whether these are offerings, eggs, or pieces of unfinished architecture is unknown. The nodules rotate when the animals approach. Once taken, they are never found again, though new ones appear in different districts.

There are also inhabitants that cannot be seen because they live in expectation.

They occupy rooms before anyone enters them. They gather behind doors that have not yet been approached. They are the source of the city’s most dangerous invitations: a familiar voice from an empty house, the outline of a loved one at the end of an avenue, the sensation that someone is waiting just beyond a stairwell.

These beings are not mimics. They do not imitate people; they inhabit the shape of what a person hopes to find.

If approached without expectation, they have almost no substance. If approached with longing, they become precise. They acquire hands, eyes, memories, the correct angle of the head. They may know intimate details they were never told. They may even possess the warmth of a living body for several breaths.

But the more completely they are recognized, the less of the city remains around them.

A Resident who finds one of these figures sometimes stands before it for a very long time. Neither moves. The encounter ends only when one of them forgets what the other was supposed to be.

No one knows whether the Pale Residents were once human.

Some bear traces that suggest they were: a habit of touching a doorframe before entering, a tendency to look upward when uncertain, the arrangement of their hands during sleep. Others have bodies too closely related to the city. Their limbs are faceted like broken stairs. Their joints open in several directions. Their heads contain rooms that are older than the mountain’s visible terraces.

They do sleep, but not together.

A sleeping Resident folds itself into an architectural posture, becoming difficult to distinguish from a buttress or a narrow standing stone. During this state it dreams the city into temporary coherence. Streets straighten. Bridges connect. The sky acquires a single depth. For a few hours, the settlement may resemble a place that could have been inhabited by ordinary beings.

When a Resident wakes, some part of that order is lost.

The oldest inhabitants no longer move through the streets. They remain embedded in the mountain, visible as pale vertical forms beneath translucent layers of stone. Their faces are turned inward. Their thoughts travel through the hidden districts below, where construction continues in the dark.

The younger Residents visit them and place their hands against the rock.

Sometimes the old ones answer with a knock.

One knock means: *continue.*

Two mean: *do not descend.*

Three mean that something has reached the underside.

The Pale Residents obey the first two. They have never agreed on what to do about the third.

And so the city remains inhabited by custodians, grazers, listeners, expectancies, and things still being shaped below. They preserve streets they did not make. They guard doors whose destinations change. They tend the empty basin at the summit, though none can say what it is meant to receive.

On rare cycles, when the inverted city appears across the black gulf, the inhabitants stop their work.

The Pale Residents turn toward it. The slate creatures flatten into the terraces. Dust hangs motionless in the towers. Even the membrane-dwellers withdraw from the walls.

A figure then walks along the distant vertical streets.

It has the narrow body of a Resident, but it moves with the certainty of something that remembers the whole structure. It pauses at the edge of its city and raises one hand.

Sometimes, from this city, one of the Pale Residents raises a hand in return.

Sometimes it removes its face.

Sometimes the city answers first.
