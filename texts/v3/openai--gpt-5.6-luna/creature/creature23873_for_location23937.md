What lives in Morrow Array is not a population so much as a continuous occupancy.

The smallest inhabitants are the seam-feeders, pale and nearly transparent, with bodies made of repeated rings and mouths too fine to see. They inhabit every joint in the continent. When a plate contracts, they are squeezed into the narrowing gap; when it expands, they unfold and follow. They eat mineral bloom, oxidized film, flakes of sealant, the invisible residue of electrical discharge. Their waste is a clear adhesive that keeps the seams watertight. They are born in millions inside the walls and die without announcement, their bodies dissolving into the same substance from which their descendants emerge.

Some are no larger than a fingernail. Others grow long enough to bridge a drainage channel. In the dark, they appear as faint strings of reflected light, moving beneath the surface of the water in parallel streams. They do not know one another as individuals. They know one another as pressure: the touch of a neighbor, the yielding of a passage, the collective tightening that tells them a machine is about to open.

The water-mats are more substantial. They have flattened bodies, overlapping armor, and several pairs of delicate limbs tucked beneath them. They gather in layers around warm pipes and submerged conduits, where their shells become translucent from heat. A mat may contain tens of thousands of animals, but its behavior is singular. It lifts when the temperature changes. It parts when a maintenance limb descends. It closes over a fallen body and strips it clean in minutes.

Their feeding is almost gentle. They do not tear. They rasp. Each creature carries a mouth like a ring of soft ceramic teeth and passes over the others in a slow, blind procession. In this way the mat keeps itself polished. It also keeps the Array polished. Wherever the water flows, these animals follow, leaving behind a darkened sheen and a network of clean channels.

They have no need to flee. The machines do not hunt them, and the machines have been taught—or bred—to recognize the living pressure of their bodies. A gate will pause rather than crush them. A pump will alter its rhythm when a breeding mass enters its intake. In return, the creatures clear the grates, insulate exposed valves, and carry small pieces of malfunction from one district to another until something larger can use them.

The most numerous animals are the wall-ribbons. They cling to vertical surfaces with hundreds of suctioned feet and move upward through the condensation. Their bodies are narrow, flexible, and dark on one side, silver on the other. They drink the water as it falls, extracting heat and dissolved metals. In dense colonies they resemble hanging cables. When startled—which is rare—they flatten themselves against the wall and become indistinguishable from the seams.

A wall-ribbon may spend its entire life on one panel. It knows the panel’s temperatures, its vibration, the schedule of the machinery behind it. It can sense a bearing beginning to fail weeks before the sound becomes audible. Then it releases a chemical message into the condensation. Ribbons below receive it. Ribbons above receive it. Within hours, a maintenance organism has been summoned from somewhere inside the structure.

The great standing forms answer these calls.

They are the oldest visible inhabitants, though age has little meaning for them. Each is built around a central body like a suspended chamber, carried above the water by six, eight, or twelve articulated legs. Their hides are gray, blue-black, or the color of old ivory. Plates overlap along their flanks, and between the plates grow membranes, valves, filter fans, and small colonies of dependent animals. Their backs support racks of equipment that they may have carried for centuries: sampling rods, pressure bells, cable reels, folded arms, nests, sealed tanks.

Their heads are not heads in the familiar sense. Some have a forward-facing cluster of lenses. Others bear only a smooth dome. One has a ring of vents that opens and closes with the slow regularity of breathing. Another possesses a long instrumented proboscis which it lowers into the water once every few years, then leaves submerged until the readings satisfy it.

They do not patrol. They do not search. They stand.

A great form chooses a gate, a heat-sink, a crossing, or a basin and becomes part of that location. It learns the place through its feet. Pressure enters through the submerged joints. Salinity passes through the leg membranes. Electrical faults register as changes in the bones of its body. It does not think in sentences, perhaps not even in images. It maintains a condition.

When the condition changes, it acts.

A leg lifts. A valve turns. A low tone passes through the water. Small animals withdraw from a particular grate. A sealant creature emerges from beneath the platform and is guided toward a rupture. The great form may remain in the same position while an entire repair occurs around it. It is less a caretaker than a fixed organ of the Array, a nerve ending that has grown large enough to resemble an animal.

The bridge-backs are older still, or appear older because they have surrendered almost all movement. They lie across trenches and spillways with their bodies raised slightly above the water, their legs folded into the foundations. Their backs are broad, ribbed, and warm. Filter colonies cover them in pale layers. Freight organisms cross over their spines without acknowledgment. Small animals nest in the hollows between their plates. During the artificial rain, water gathers along the length of them and pours from their sides in continuous curtains.

Some bridge-backs have been mistaken for infrastructure. They are not offended by this. Their distinction from infrastructure has long since become irrelevant.

They feed through their undersides. Warm water enters them. They separate what can be used from what cannot. They generate currents that pull detritus toward their mouths, though the mouths may be kilometers from the visible body, hidden beneath the trench. When damaged, they do not cry out. Their entire length contracts once, sending a vibration through the foundations. The creatures living on them respond before any machine registers the fault.

Their young are not born as small bridge-backs. They begin as drifting organs: soft, buoyant knots that move through the water in great slow clouds. Most are absorbed into filters, pumps, or the bodies of other creatures. A few settle in locations where the pressure, heat, and mineral balance are correct. There they attach themselves to existing structures and begin, over decades, to become something larger.

No inhabitant of Morrow Array seems to possess an ordinary boundary.

Animals grow valves, storage sacs, conductive filaments, pressure membranes. They borrow parts from one another. A damaged creature may open its body and allow a maintenance swarm inside. A dead animal becomes a coating, a filter, a nutrient solution, a new layer of insulation. The distinction between eating and repairing has nearly vanished. To consume something is often to preserve the larger system it belongs to.

Even reproduction is a form of maintenance.

Eggs are deposited in inspection housings, beneath warm plates, inside abandoned tools. They hatch according to pressure cycles rather than seasons. Their young emerge already responsive to vibration. They do not learn the Array; they inherit it through their nervous systems. A sequence of tones can direct them to a leaking pipe. A change in water temperature can send them toward a clogged intake. The place enters them before they have any experience of elsewhere.

Some individuals do wander. These are the long-bodied travelers, low creatures with dozens of legs and rows of faint lights beneath their skin. They follow old routes across the continent, moving through shallow water from one industrial district to another. Their migrations take years. They pass under transitways, around standing forms, through flooded chambers and open basins. Other animals make way for them. The machines alter their schedules. In certain districts, production halts for several minutes as the travelers cross.

No one knows whether these journeys have destinations.

The travelers may be carrying chemical information gathered from distant parts of the Array. They may be seeking a particular mineral, a remembered temperature, or a pressure condition that no longer exists. They may simply be the remnant of an ancient instinct, preserved because nothing in Morrow Array is ever permitted to fail completely. Whatever their purpose, they are treated as necessary. Their passage is marked by a temporary clearing in the water and by the attention of the great standing forms, which turn their sensor arrays toward the moving lights.

There are also things that do not fit comfortably among the animals.

Inside the factories, behind sealed panels and along maintenance shafts, live the interface organisms. They are thin, jointed, and almost entirely sensory. They attach themselves to control surfaces and absorb instructions through heat and current. Some grow over circuit housings like translucent skin. Others curl around fiber bundles and pulse in synchrony with the signals passing through them. They are not machines, but no machine can distinguish their presence from correct operation.

When an interface organism dies, the system does not report a fault. It grows another.

These creatures are rarely seen. Workers—if there are workers—might glimpse them through inspection windows: a pale hand withdrawing from a bank of instruments, a cluster of black cilia trembling over a live conduit, a small face opening where no face should be. They do not look toward observers. They are listening to the structure.

The Array may be full of eyes, but vision is not important there. Its inhabitants perceive through contact and consequence. They feel where water has slowed. They feel the minute trembling of an overloaded beam. They taste the difference between clean metal and metal beginning to fail. They sense one another through heat. Their world is not divided into objects and empty space. Everything touches something else, and every touch is information.

This is why the continent never becomes lonely.

A creature may stand alone on a platform, surrounded by vapor, with no visible movement except the rain sliding from its shell. But beneath it, thousands of seam-feeders are passing messages through the joints. Inside its body, smaller organisms are adjusting its filters. Through the water, a pressure-wave is traveling toward another basin. Somewhere far below, a bridge-back has changed the rhythm of its intake. Somewhere above, an interface colony has registered the same minute disturbance.

The inhabitants do not possess the Array.

They are the Array’s way of remaining inhabited.

Perhaps once there were separate species, with separate hungers and separate territories. Perhaps there were ancestors that escaped into the construction, or were designed for it, or arose accidentally from the long interaction between flesh and machinery. That history has been smoothed away like every other irregularity. What remains is a civilization without cities, a biosphere without wilderness, an animal world in which no body is truly separate from the systems that sustain it.

They continue because continuation is the only condition they know.

When one of the great creatures releases its low tone, the sound enters the water, the plates, the pipes, and the bodies pressed against them. Nearby animals turn. Farther animals receive the vibration later. The whole basin seems to incline toward the note.

Then the tone ends.

The creatures resume their assigned motions. The water keeps its level. The machines keep their tolerance. And beneath the artificial rain, every living thing settles once more into the immense, patient body of Morrow Array.
