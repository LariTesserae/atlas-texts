What lives there is not one animal, but an arrangement of animals—an entire wet, feeding, breeding population spread through the abandoned machinery.

The smallest life occupies the seams first.

Under the waterline, snails cover the concrete in overlapping shells. They graze the films that gather on sluice walls and intake screens, leaving pale curved tracks through the brown slick. Small worms live in the mud beneath them, working through silt rich with metal, salt, and whatever has settled from the factories. The mud is never still. It puckers with bubbles. It trembles when something beneath it turns.

In the warm channels, fish gather in such numbers that the water appears to have a pulse. They are pale-sided where the light reaches them and nearly black below. Some have adapted to the heat and the chemical taste of the discharge; others arrive with the tide and remain because the outflow brings food. They feed on larvae, fragments, drowned insects, and one another. At night they press close to the concrete banks, packed together in the warmth. When a pump starts, the whole mass shifts at once.

Eels occupy the pipes.

They are long, dark, and muscular, with blunt heads and small eyes made nearly useless by the blackness of their surroundings. They knot themselves in culverts, filter housings, drainage chambers, and the mouths of half-submerged conduits. Their bodies slide over one another with a slow, wet friction. They leave the water during heavy rain, crossing short stretches of pavement in shining loops, searching for a warmer channel or a deeper place. A worker may find one coiled beneath a valve cabinet, breathing steadily in the damp air, and watch it disappear into a crack too narrow to admit a hand.

The crabs are less secretive.

They move in broad, sideways populations across the flats and flooded yards, appearing beneath pallets, inside open culverts, and along the bases of retaining walls. Their shells are gray, rust-red, or stained green-black by the water. They feed on dead fish, insects, bird droppings, drowned rats, and anything else that softens in the wet. They climb surprisingly well. At low tide they scale the lower faces of seawalls, claws clicking against the concrete. When disturbed, they do not scatter in all directions; they pour laterally, a living sheet breaking around obstacles and reforming beyond them.

The frogs live closest to the machinery.

They cling to tank walls, pump housings, and the undersides of grated walkways. Their skins are dark and slick, patterned in colors borrowed from oil, rust, and wet stone. During the day they are difficult to distinguish from debris. At night they wake in waves. One begins somewhere in a flooded yard, another answers from a drain, and soon the ditches are speaking to one another in low, uneven bursts. Their chorus has the rhythm of a failing engine: a start, a stutter, a brief gathering of power, and then silence.

They lay their eggs wherever warm water collects. Tadpoles fill the shallow ends of settling ponds, clinging to the walls in black writhing masses. Many are eaten before they mature. Those that survive become part of the district’s next layer of life.

Above them, insects rise in clouds.

They gather around lamps, exhaust vents, open doors, and the warm sides of buildings. Midges form dense trembling columns. Mosquitoes skim the water in dark veils. Flies enter the warehouses through broken windows and remain there, breeding in forgotten drains and the soft bodies of dead animals. Beetles move through bird nests and accumulated dust. Small moths beat themselves against amber bulbs until their wings become transparent.

When a door opens, the cloud inside shifts before it settles. When a vehicle passes, insects lift from the road in a sheet and cover the headlights. The workers learn to keep their mouths closed.

The birds are the visible owners of the place.

Gulls occupy the cranes and the highest roofs, but they do not control the basin. They are merely its loudest opportunists. They steal fish from outflow channels, tear open refuse sacks, and follow maintenance vehicles in the hope that something edible will be dropped. Their young hatch in nests made from cable insulation, plastic strip, rusted wire, and the feathers of other birds.

Cormorants roost on pipe bridges with their wings open to the weak heat. Herons stand motionless beside the settling ponds, necks folded, waiting for fish to make the smallest mistake. Terns skim the water at dawn, their wings almost touching the surface. Black-winged birds nest in dead ducts and ventilation stacks, laying eggs on ledges warmed by machinery.

They are everywhere, but they do not fill the sky continuously. Most of the time they remain folded into the architecture: dark shapes along a roofline, pale bodies standing on a tank, heads turning in unison. Their silence is part of their abundance. The basin can hold hundreds of birds without seeming occupied until one flock lifts, and another rises in answer, and all the structures begin to move.

The birds know the city’s schedule better than the remaining workers do.

They know when the floodgates open and what comes through. They know which pumps discharge fish, which screens trap eels, and which settling ponds draw insects after rain. They know the shift changes and the routes of the slow maintenance vehicles. When a lamp fails, they occupy the darkness before anyone replaces it. When a building is sealed, they continue nesting in its walls.

There are mammals too, though they keep to the edges and the hours when the lamps are weakest.

Rats live beneath the warehouses and in the embankments, their tunnels passing through old insulation and cinders. They swim between sectors and climb pipes to reach storage platforms. Some have learned to avoid the traps. Others have grown heavy on fish, eggs, and the waste left by workers. They are not numerous enough to dominate, because the birds and eels take them when they can, but they are permanent.

Stray dogs sometimes cross the outer roads. They are thin, mud-stained, and cautious around water. They follow the scent of fish and refuse, sleeping beneath loading ramps until the heat drives them elsewhere. Their coats collect salt. Their paws grow hard from the concrete. They rarely bark.

The human beings who remain are another kind of animal, more temporary but no less adapted.

They wear white helmets, rubber boots, gloves, and rain gear that has become permanently gray. They move along the elevated walkways, carrying tools, lamps, sample jars, and radios that fail in the worst weather. They know which surfaces only appear solid, which doors swell shut, which channels contain live current, and which discharge pipes must never be approached from below.

They do not think of themselves as belonging to the basin. They think of themselves as maintaining it. Yet they have acquired its habits. They walk slowly. They listen before opening a door. They look into water before stepping across it. They carry food sealed in metal boxes. They know the difference between rain and dripping from a pipe.

The animals barely notice them unless they disturb a nest, expose a drain, or bring food.

Then the district reacts.

Fish vanish into the dark. Crabs retreat beneath the nearest sheet of metal. Frogs flatten against the concrete. Birds rise from the roofs in a single startled discoloration and settle again several buildings away. The workers continue through the disturbance, checking gauges and clearing screens, while below them the water resumes its slow movement.

Everything here feeds on everything else, but nothing hunts constantly. The basin has no need for urgency. Tide, pump, heat, rain, and rot provide enough. The animals have learned the intervals: when to emerge, when to breed, when to gather beneath a pipe, when to leave a pond before it is drained.

They do not make the place wild.

They make it inhabited.

Under the lamps, inside the drains, across the rooftops, and beneath the black surface, the exhausted city is occupied by bodies that have found use in every remaining warmth.
