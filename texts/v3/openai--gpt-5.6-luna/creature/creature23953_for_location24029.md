They are called, if they are called anything at all, the **Stillborn**.

Not because they were born dead. They are very much alive. They breathe, feed, grow, shed skins, mate, sleep, and wake. But none of them has ever known the first terror of entering the world. They do not struggle into life. They simply appear in the hollow already belonging to it, wet and warm among roots, with the night pressed gently against their closed eyes.

The smallest are the easiest to mistake for ordinary creatures. Beetles gather beneath stones. Mice with velvet-black fur sleep in the moss. Pale larvae feed on the soft undersides of leaves. Salamanders lie in the pools with their limbs folded beneath them, their bodies almost indistinguishable from submerged roots. Yet each animal bears some mark of the hollow’s influence. Their eyes are dark and reflective, lacking the bright pinprick of alarm. Their coats and shells hold water without becoming soaked. Their movements are slow, but never weak, as though haste were an unnecessary and faintly embarrassing habit learned elsewhere.

They do not fear one another.

A fox-shaped animal may lower its narrow muzzle beside a sleeping vole and drink from the same basin. A moth may settle on the open eye of a resting deer. The deer does not blink. There is no trust between them, exactly. Trust belongs to creatures that have imagined betrayal. These animals possess something older and quieter: an absolute certainty that the hollow will not permit one thing to become another’s ending.

They eat when food is placed before them. Grass shortens beneath blunt teeth. Fungus disappears into soft mouths. Small things vanish into large things, but without chase, struggle, or pain. The act of feeding seems less like killing than like being taken back. What enters one body has already belonged to the hollow and remains, in some hidden form, within it.

The larger creatures are difficult to see whole. They are low and broad, built for crouching beneath the cliffs. Some resemble deer, though their antlers have been worn smooth and carry moss in their forks. Others have the shoulders of bears, the long backs of otters, or the many-jointed silhouettes of animals that never existed beyond the mountain. They bear no scars. Their teeth are small for their size. Their claws are rounded, polished by years of contact with stone and root.

They sleep in groups.

At times, one of them rises and walks the perimeter of the hollow. It touches its nose to the moss, the pools, the roots, and the sleeping bodies. Wherever it passes, the surrounding creatures become stiller. Not afraid—attentive. When the creature returns to the trees, the hollow resumes its minute shifting.

The birds are perhaps the strangest. They have wings, but no bird in the hollow has ever flown. Their feathers are dense and dark, and water lies upon them in perfect beads. They roost in the branches in such close clusters that they form living knots. Occasionally one opens its wings, not to take flight but to make room for another. None sings. Their throats move sometimes, and sounds gather there, but the notes never emerge. Whatever language they possess is carried through the pressure of bodies against bodies, through the brief touch of beak to feather, through the shared turning of many heads toward the black pool.

Every creature knows the pool.

The smallest turn toward it in sleep. The insects standing on the water’s surface arrange themselves so that their legs point inward. The salamanders face it from beneath the reeds. The birds wake together when something moves below, though they do not call out. Even the moss seems to lean in that direction, its damp threads extending toward the dark water without ever reaching it.

Something lives beneath the surface.

It is not a fish, though it has inhabited the pool longer than fish have inhabited the world. It is not a god, because nothing in the hollow worships it, and worship would imply distance. The creatures do not fear it or petition it. They know it as one knows the weight of one’s own body.

Perhaps it is a root descending through the mountain. Perhaps it is a blind animal larger than the hollow, curled beneath the stone with its heart in the water. Perhaps the black surface is only the visible portion of it: an eye, a mouth, a thought held perfectly still.

When it shifts, the sound rises from below—a soft settling, a muted click deep in the earth. At once, every living thing in the hollow pauses. Leaves stop trembling. Wings close. The animals lift their heads, not startled but listening.

Then the presence recedes.

Water continues to gather. A beetle resumes its slow crossing of a stone. Somewhere beneath the trees, something large exhales.

The dead belong to it as well. When one of the creatures dies, the others do not gather around it. There is no mourning as creatures elsewhere might understand mourning. The body remains where it has fallen, and the hollow gradually accepts it. Moss covers the fur. Roots pass through the ribs. Mushrooms rise from the joints. In time, a new shelter forms around the bones. The living sleep there without hesitation.

The inhabitants do not reproduce in the ordinary sense. There are young, but no parent remembers producing them. A small animal is found one night beneath a fern, already furred, already able to walk. A bird appears among the branches with its eyes open. A salamander is discovered in a pool where there was none before. The adults make room. The young creature is not taught the ways of the hollow; it has never known any other.

None of them wants to leave.

This is not because they are content. Contentment is too active a word. They have no imagined outside against which to measure the place. The hollow is not paradise to them, nor prison, nor refuge. It is the shape of existence itself: water, darkness, body, root, and the patient presence beneath the pool.

If an animal reaches the rim, it stops.

There is no boundary it can see. Only the drop, the fog, and the suggestion of a world where wind moves freely and things are born hungry. The creature may stand there for a long time, its head raised toward the unseen sky. Then it turns back. Not commanded. Not compelled. Simply returned, as a stone returns downhill.

The hollow’s creatures are not waiting for anything.

They are the ones who remain after waiting has ended.
