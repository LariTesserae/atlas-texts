They are not the first inhabitants of the town, but they have become difficult to distinguish from its remains.

At a distance, they can be mistaken for one more movement among the reeds: a pale figure crossing a flooded street, a long coat trailing in the water, a face briefly appearing between sheets of fern. They do not call to one another across the basin. They communicate by touch, by the placement of stones, by small changes in the flow of water. Their settlements are quiet because the old town is quiet, and because anything loud carries too far through the joined chambers.

They are human in origin, perhaps, though the distinction has been worn thin by generations underground.

Their skin has taken on the color of the stone around them—gray at the wrists, blue-white across the shoulders, brown where it has been stained by mud and tannin. They are lean and long-limbed, built for wading through channels and climbing broken masonry. Their hands are broad, the fingers hardened and slightly webbed from constant immersion. Most have a fine covering of dark hair along their arms and necks, not enough to keep them warm but enough to hold a layer of air against the skin. They dress in overlapping pieces of treated hide, woven grass, and salvaged cloth, all darkened with oil and water until their clothing resembles the wet walls.

Their eyes are not blind, but they have learned not to depend on sight.

In the daylight shafts, they squint and turn their faces aside. They see well enough in the greenish half-light beneath the roots, but they navigate chiefly by sound, touch, and the pressure of moving water. A hand laid flat against a wall tells them whether a distant chamber is filling. A reed bent upstream reveals a current too slight to feel with the feet. They know the town through its vibrations: the hollow places beneath paving stones, the loose slab ready to fall, the slow internal groan of a tower whose foundations have begun to sink.

Children learn the basin by being carried through it.

They are wrapped against the chest of an older sibling or strapped into reed cradles that float beside the adults. An infant raised here becomes accustomed early to dripping water, cold mud, and the sudden pale blaze of daylight. The children play in the shallow streets, but their games are not careless. They build little dams from tile fragments and watch how long they hold. They release beetles into channels and follow the routes they choose. They hide in old cisterns while another child searches by tapping the surrounding walls.

No one tells them that the town is dangerous. Danger is not an abstraction here. It is a wall shedding grit, a current turning cloudy, a patch of moss growing too quickly over a stair.

The people live in the upper rooms of the most stable houses, where roots have bound the masonry together. They sleep in hammocks suspended above the wet floors, or in niches carved into walls that no longer have roofs. Their fires are small and smokeless, fed with resinous roots and the dry inner wood of drowned trees. Smoke is a liability; it draws attention from the upper openings and settles in the low chambers. Most of their food is eaten raw, soaked, fermented, or warmed briefly over covered coals.

They gather what the basin provides.

There are fish from the flooded streets, salamanders taken only after the heavy rains, eggs collected from reed beds, fungus scraped from shaded walls, and pale tubers cultivated in the deep soil of collapsed courtyards. They keep no large herds. Animals are too valuable as they are: the marsh rodents aerate the old gardens, the bats consume clouds of insects, and certain fish are released into newly flooded rooms to keep the water clear.

Their agriculture is less a matter of planting than of guiding.

They redirect trickles with clay plugs. They raise beds on fallen doors. They tie living roots around leaning walls and brace them with woven frames until the roots thicken. A courtyard may contain sedge in one corner, edible moss in another, and a pool stocked with fish beneath a broken staircase. The people do not clear vegetation unless it blocks a passage or threatens a foundation. They prune, bend, anchor, and wait.

Their oldest structures are not houses but listening places.

These are chambers built around wells, sluices, and cracks in the earth. Someone is always stationed there, particularly after rain. They sit beside the water for hours with one ear against the stone, recording changes in level and pressure. They mark the walls with lines of charcoal, shell, and crushed blue rock. A new mark may mean that a chamber has filled, a bridge has shifted, or a subterranean current has begun to run in a direction no one remembers.

The records are not histories in the usual sense. They are maps of uncertainty.

A street is named for the year its pavement vanished. A district is known as the Place of Three Falls, though no one knows whether the third fall has already happened. A tower carries a name meaning “still standing,” which may be praise, warning, or both. Their oldest songs describe rooms that are now underwater and rivers that once flowed beneath the town in the opposite direction.

They preserve the past by continuing to use it.

A broken arch becomes a footbridge. A statue becomes a marker for the deepest channel. A collapsed shrine is planted with medicinal fern. The dead are placed in dry wall cavities, where roots can grow around the bones and hold them in place. Sometimes a family returns years later to find that the wall has become a tree, and they hang a strip of cloth from its branches.

They have little sense of ownership. They speak instead of keeping, tending, or being responsible for a place. A room belongs to whoever is repairing its roof. A cistern belongs to whoever clears its silt. A bridge belongs to everyone who has crossed it and everyone who might need to cross it later. The town is inherited not as property, but as a set of obligations.

The most respected among them are those who can predict collapse.

They are called by names associated with listening, sediment, or roots. They walk slowly, testing every surface with a staff tipped in bone or stone. They study the color of water flowing from a wall. They press their palms to fresh fractures and smell the dust. They can tell whether a building is being held upright by its foundations, by the pressure of surrounding soil, or by the roots that have entered its rooms.

But even they are often wrong.

The basin is changing faster than memory can follow. New sinkholes open in the side valleys. Entire streets disappear beneath water. The ceiling sheds stones after storms. Every generation must abandon rooms its parents considered safe. The people do not pretend that their knowledge gives them control. It gives them warning, and warning is sometimes enough.

They fear the deep collapses most.

When the distant rock convulses and the sound arrives as pressure, all activity stops. Fish vanish beneath the paving. Birds lift from the towers. The people stand still with their hands against the walls, waiting to learn whether the basin has changed shape. Afterward, they move through the town in small groups, carrying lamps made from sealed shells and rendered fat, though they use them sparingly. They search for blocked passages, trapped animals, and new water. They salvage what can be salvaged, then mark the dangerous places with rows of white stones.

There are stories of people who went beyond the flooded districts and did not return. Some are said to have found other basins beneath the earth, each with its own broken town and its own inhabitants. Others are said to have followed the water downward until they reached a buried sea. No one agrees whether these are memories, hopes, or instructions.

The people themselves seem to be moving in that direction.

Their oldest paths lead downward, but their newest shelters are being built higher: in the roots near the ceiling, on the upper floors of leaning towers, along shelves of rock exposed by collapse. They are becoming creatures of both depths, living between the rising water below and the unstable daylight above.

At dusk—if dusk can be said to exist beneath the broken ceiling—they gather where the largest shaft of light falls across the flooded square. The surface water turns white around their ankles. They stand among the submerged pillars and inspect the reflections of the roots overhead. No one speaks much. They listen to the basin breathe.

A child may place a hand against a wall and feel a tremor too slight for anyone else to notice. An elder may look toward a distant district where a roof has just vanished beneath fern. Somewhere below them, water enters an old street and begins to choose a new course.

Then the people separate.

Some go to the gardens. Some climb toward the nests. Some descend into the darkened houses with baskets and tools. They return to the work of keeping the town inhabitable—not preserving it as it was, but helping it become whatever it is becoming without taking everyone with it.

The settlement lives through them, but they live through the settlement in turn.

They are its tendons, its watchfulness, its slow human pulse beneath the wet stone. Where a wall falls, they gather the pieces. Where a channel opens, they learn its direction. Where a room fills with water, they move their sleeping places higher and plant something in the silt.

Nothing remains empty for long.
