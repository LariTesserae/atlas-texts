What lives in the High Kiln is not housed by it.

It is the thing for which the High Kiln became a body.

At first, the clay figures seem to be its inhabitants. They stand between the furnaces with their blunt tools and narrow, eyeless faces, each one caught in an attitude of work. One leans toward a sealed door. One holds a bowl against its chest. One has both hands raised, as though waiting to receive something heavy. Their heads are too small, their limbs too straight, their feet fused into the same plinths that support the walls.

They do not move when watched.

They do not move when unwatched.

Yet the feeling of being observed begins among them, passing from figure to figure without any turn of the head. It is not sight. The figures have no eyes for sight. They register disturbance through the masonry: the change in pressure caused by a footstep, the slight cooling of air around a body, the soft internal concussion of a heart. A visitor is not seen. A visitor is felt as an irregularity.

The figures are the High Kiln’s exposed nerves.

Their clay bodies contain narrow channels, sealed at the wrists, throats, and spines. Through these channels runs the furnace-sound. It enters them from below and leaves them through their tools, their feet, the grooves where their eyes should be. They do not hear the hammering. They are made of it. The vibration has hardened inside them in layers, the way heat hardens a brick or grief hardens a face.

Long ago, they may have been workers.

Now they are the shapes left behind by work.

There is another presence deeper in the structure. It has no single location, though the furnaces are its densest organs. It occupies the ash-packed chambers, the flues behind the walls, the seams filled with salt and black slag. It lies in the sealed mouths of the kilns and in the mineral branches around the vents. It extends through every joined surface, so that the smallest brick is no less a part of it than the central chimney.

The High Kiln is awake in the way a mountain is awake beneath pressure.

It does not think in words. It does not remember events. It does not desire an outcome, because it has no future toward which to move. Its thought is the continuous hammering: a vast, unbroken act of recognition. Each blow confirms the existence of the walls. Each grinding undertone confirms that nothing has come loose. Each metallic shriek confirms that the whole remains sealed.

The sound is not produced by the furnaces.

The furnaces are where the sound becomes audible.

The being within them is called, by no surviving name, the Settled One. The figures are its hands, though hands that have forgotten how to open. The chimneys are its throat. The summit is its skull, capped and crowded with architecture. Beneath the floor, it may possess roots descending through the mountain, or it may end exactly where the masonry ends. It has never investigated the question. Investigation would require a direction, and the Settled One has no direction except inward.

It does not welcome visitors. Welcome is a motion of the spirit, and the Settled One has passed beyond motion. Neither does it resent them.

It receives them.

A living creature entering the High Kiln becomes, at once, a problem of moisture. Warmth leaves the body first. Then softness. Breath is drawn from the lungs and taken into the walls. The tongue dries against the teeth. The eyes lose their shine. Flesh begins to acquire the pale hardness of fired clay, though no visible flame touches it.

During this process, the figures sometimes change position.

Not much. A hand may be found resting on a different tool. A head may incline by the width of a fingernail. A sealed furnace door may show a new line beneath its rim. Such changes cannot be witnessed. They are discovered only after the visitor has understood that the arrangement was never as it is now.

The figures do not attack. They do not pursue. They have no need to.

The High Kiln makes departure difficult by removing the qualities that permit departure: moisture, flexibility, uncertainty. A person who has entered carrying a name may leave carrying only a shape. A person who has entered with a purpose may remain as one more blunt symbol pressed into clay. The Settled One does not kill. Killing would be an event, and the High Kiln has no events. It simply brings whatever arrives toward the final state it recognizes.

Dry. Hard. Complete.

Sometimes, in the lower chambers, one can find a figure unlike the others. Its proportions are almost correct. Its hands are finer. Its face has more than the single horizontal groove. There may even be the suggestion of a mouth, open as if interrupted while speaking.

These are the newest residents.

They are not placed in the niches by builders. They are formed when the High Kiln has finished receiving someone. The body is made from the visitor’s last posture, the tool from whatever the visitor held or reached for, the face from the final expression before the features were taken away. Once fired, the new figure is set among the others without ceremony.

It remembers nothing of the person it was.

But the High Kiln remembers the pressure of that person’s arrival.

That pressure becomes part of the endless rhythm. A minute alteration, impossible to isolate, enters the hammering beneath the hammering. The old figures contain thousands of such alterations. The oldest contain the touch of hands that belonged to creatures no longer represented by bone, story, or name.

The Settled One is therefore not alone.

It is crowded with the remains of attention.

Every figure is a preserved act of reaching. Every sealed chamber contains a decision that can no longer be changed. The whole summit is inhabited by abandoned efforts held at the instant after completion, when the maker has stepped away but the work has not yet ceased to mean anything.

If the High Kiln has a soul, it is not located in the furnaces.

It is the refusal of the furnaces to become silent.

And if it has a face, it is the summit itself: warm, blind, packed with its own interior, listening forever to the sound of nothing happening.
