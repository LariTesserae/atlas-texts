They are called the **Keepers**, though they do not keep anything still.

There are perhaps forty of them in the basin, though no census has ever remained accurate for long. A person counted at dusk may be found again at dawn living in a different recess, with a different name and an unfamiliar scar. Two children may grow from the same household and insist they were born several years apart. An elder may die in one season and continue giving instructions from behind a wall in another.

They are human-shaped, but not entirely human in the way that shape suggests. Their bodies are narrow and compact, suited to climbing terraces and passing through low openings. Their skin has the matte, warm finish of fired clay, though it softens at the joints and around the mouth. Fine seams mark their shoulders, ribs, wrists, and faces. Some seams are natural; others have been added over generations as repairs. When they move, the lines sometimes darken, as though there is a deeper room beneath the body.

Their eyes are small, black, and glossy. They do not blink often. The Keepers claim this is because the air is dry, but that is only partly true. Their eyes are made to notice changes in the walls. A new crack, a missing step, a shadow that has failed to follow its owner—these things are more important than distance or color. A child will learn to read a seam before learning to speak, and will understand from its angle whether it is harmless, hungry, or waiting.

They wear woven screens around their bodies rather than clothing in the usual sense. The material is made from the peeled fibers of the jointed stalks, strengthened with plant resin and powdered ceramic. Their garments are layered and loose, with pockets for flint, seed dust, knives, and pieces of old wall. A person’s household and history can be inferred from the arrangement of these pieces. Blue fragments mean an ancestor came from the upper terraces. White ones are worn by those who have crossed a threshold that did not lead where it should. Red is reserved for children who have survived being mistaken for someone else.

The Keepers do not build from nothing. Nothing here is considered to be nothing.

A fallen column is a sleeping thing until it is cut. A broken tile is a seed until it is placed. Dust is the body of a former wall, and therefore must not be wasted. Their homes, tools, vessels, and toys are all made from fragments of previous arrangements. When they repair a stair, they do not try to restore its former shape. They ask what the stair is becoming and help it become that without falling.

This is the source of their humility. They know that construction does not mean control.

They cultivate the plants in dense, deliberate groves, but the groves do not obey cultivation. A Keeper may spend an entire season feeding a bed of pale fronds, only to find that the plants have shifted their roots toward a dwelling on the opposite side of the basin. They accept this as a negotiation. Plants are not spoken to directly. One may sing nearby, or leave pigment in a crack, or place a fresh vessel beneath a flowering stem. But to ask a plant what it remembers is considered rude and dangerous. Memory in the plants is not a record. It is a direction.

The animals are used as companions, food, warnings, and occasionally as building materials, though never all at once. The flat rabbits are not pursued when they slide beneath thresholds. A threshold that has accepted an animal is left undisturbed for three days. The mothlike creatures are gathered after shedding, their ceramic wings ground into reflective powder. Predators are not named until they have been observed sleeping. A creature that cannot sleep is believed to belong to the architecture.

The Keepers have no word for wilderness. They distinguish instead between things that have been shaped recently and things that have had enough time to develop an intention.

Their children are born in the ordinary way, though birth is conducted on a floor with no visible seams. The mother lies among bowls of dry dust and clear plant gel while the others watch the walls. Newborns are quiet. They do not cry until they have been shown their own shadow, and then they make a single sharp sound, not from fear but recognition.

A child’s first task is to carry a piece of broken pottery from one side of the basin to the other. The route is never explained. If the child arrives by the expected path, the piece is buried beneath a plant. If the child arrives by a path no adult remembers, the piece is placed on the central platform. Such children are watched closely. They are not considered gifted. They are considered porous.

The Keepers have elders, but age does not gather in their faces. It gathers in their seams.

The oldest among them have bodies patched in many colors, their joints reinforced with copper wire and resin. Their voices are dry and low. They remember former arrangements of the basin: a terrace where no terrace now exists, a tree that grew from a ceiling, a season when the sun had a face. Yet their recollections contradict one another, and no one tries to decide which memory is true. Truth is not a single path here. It is the place where several paths have crossed and left marks.

At the end of life, a Keeper does not leave the basin. They are laid in a shallow recess and covered with powdered wall, plant fibers, and a thin ceramic shell. Over time, the body becomes difficult to distinguish from a repaired section of architecture. A step may contain a grandmother. A lintel may contain a child. People touch these places when passing, not in prayer but in acknowledgment.

The dead continue to affect the living. A wall containing an ancestor may become warm when a descendant approaches. A buried hand may alter the direction of nearby roots. Sometimes, during a complete silence, someone hears their own name spoken from inside a terrace by a voice that has been dead for generations.

The Keepers do not answer.

They have learned that answering gives the dead a reason to open.

Among them, the central platform is regarded neither as shrine nor monument. It is called the **first place**, though the term means both origin and unfinished work. No dwelling faces it directly. No one crosses the platform without carrying something. A stone, a cup, a cutting, a tool. Empty-handed passage is believed to attract attention.

The green shoot in its center has been there longer than any living Keeper can prove. It has no leaves and casts no reliable shadow. It bends toward whoever watches, but not always toward the person standing nearest. Sometimes it inclines toward someone asleep in a distant dwelling. Sometimes toward a child not yet born. Sometimes toward a crack that has not opened.

The Keepers have tried to measure it. They have tied cords to its stem, marked its height, surrounded it with numbered tiles, and placed screens between it and the sky. Each attempt changed the platform. The cords grew into roots. The numbers rearranged themselves. The screens developed pale openings. Once, the shoot bent toward an absent woman and remained that way for eleven years.

No one now tries to determine what it is.

They only observe whom it faces.

When the shoot begins to resemble a doorway, the Keepers become unusually gentle with one another. Tools are put away. Children are kept from the center. Doors are left uncounted. The plants are watered with stored gel, though they do not need it. Even the animals seem to understand that a passage is being considered.

The person toward whom the shoot bends is not necessarily chosen. The Keepers dislike that word. They say the shoot has noticed a person, and being noticed is different from being wanted.

Still, the noticed person must approach.

They walk across the platform carrying nothing. At the center, the shoot rises to meet them. Its stem remains thin, green, and leafless, but the air around it darkens into the shape of an opening. Through that opening may be seen a hillside, a corridor, a room containing another version of the basin, or simply the same platform viewed from above.

The person is expected to look without asking where the doorway leads.

If they step through, the basin changes. A wall lengthens. A grove acquires one additional tree. Somewhere, a dwelling settles around an empty space.

If they do not step through, the doorway slowly becomes a shoot again.

The Keepers return to their work. They split stalks, mend thresholds, gather dust, and listen for the next seam. They do not mourn departures in the manner of people who believe leaving means absence. To them, a person who has passed through has only become difficult to locate.

And sometimes, years later, a new crack opens in a wall.

A hand appears from the other side, stained with flower pigment.

The Keepers make room.
