What lives there has learned not to mistake permanence for safety.

The people of the plateau are compact, weather-darkened, and deliberate in their movements. They walk with their weight low and their feet placed carefully, not from caution alone but from generations of knowing which stones rock, which edges crumble, and which harmless-looking seams conceal a hollow beneath. Their hands are broad and scarred. Fingertips are thickened by rope, stone, wool, and cold metal. Nearly everyone carries a tool: a wedge, a hook, a short hammer, a knife, a coil of cord. Even children are rarely empty-handed. A child sent across the city may be trusted with a water ladle, a peg for marking a wall, or a bundle of dung fuel tied against the back.

They are not tall people. Height is expensive in a place of low shelters, narrow stairs, and sudden wind. Their clothing is layered rather than elegant: felted wool, stitched hide, woven root fiber, and pale inner cloth that can be turned outward to show soot, blood, or water stains. Hoods and face wraps are common. On clear days their eyes are narrowed against the high light. On fog days, cords and small bells are tied to clothing so that a person can be found by touch or sound before they are seen.

Their houses contain more darkness than light. Rooms are cut into shelves or built around them, with low ceilings that preserve warmth. Fire is too valuable to waste, and smoke is directed through narrow vents packed with clay and hair. Families sleep close together on raised platforms above storage pits. The oldest living member usually sleeps nearest the wall, where they can feel the small tremors that precede a foundation shift. Infants are kept in suspended cradles, not for comfort but because the people have learned that the floor is not always trustworthy.

A household is seldom limited to blood relatives. Stone-workers, herders, widows, abandoned children, and travelers become attached to one another through labor. A person who can read a retaining wall is needed by everyone. So is a person who can judge an animal’s foot, repair a cistern lining, find a dry route through fog, or remember which district collapses first after rain. Competence is a form of kinship. The city has little patience for uselessness, but it has a deep respect for those who keep others alive.

The builders are its most visible people. They move through the settlement in small teams, carrying slabs between them on shoulder poles. They repair steps before they become dangerous, raise walls one handspan at a time, and crawl into foundations with lamps protected by cupped palms. Their work songs are not songs in the usual sense. They are measured calls that establish rhythm and distance: lift, hold, turn, lower. A team can pass a block through five levels without speaking more than these four words.

Those who quarry the plateau are quieter still. They know the stone by color and temperature. They can distinguish a solid shelf from a skin of basalt over a cavity by tapping it with a knuckle. They leave marks inside the rock—short cuts, spirals, names, dates—not as decoration but as warnings to whoever comes later. Some quarry faces have been worked for so long that the city’s history appears in their walls: the first crude wedges, then deeper channels, then smooth extraction scars made by tools no living person remembers how to forge.

The water-keepers are treated with a seriousness approaching reverence. They inspect every cistern after rain, taste for mineral changes, scrape away dead insects, and maintain the little channels that lead from roofs into storage basins. They know the weather by the behavior of distant fog. They can tell which cloud will wet stone and which will pass overhead without giving anything. When a basin fills, they do not celebrate loudly. A full cistern is not abundance; it is time purchased from the future.

The herders live more often among animals than among walls. Their many-legged grazers are squat, shaggy creatures with blunt horns and patient eyes. Each animal knows the sound of its keeper’s call, though the herders themselves claim this is less important than knowing how an animal pauses before refusing a path. The beasts are taught to climb stairs, cross bridges, and stand still while loaded. Their dung is gathered with almost ceremonial efficiency. Their milk is thickened, salted, dried, and stored in skin bags. Their hair becomes rope and felt. Their bones become needles, handles, hinges, and small flutes used only indoors.

The animals are not quite livestock and not quite citizens. Each herd has a keeper, a lineage, and a place in the city’s memory. When an old grazing beast dies, its horns may remain above a gate for generations. Young animals are given names based on the places where they were born: Under-Rain, Three-Step, Black Hollow, Far Ledge. The animals learn the city’s routes. They know where the fog gathers, where the stairs are too slick, where the birds descend at dusk. Sometimes a herd begins moving before the people feel the weather change, and the humans follow.

The city’s children grow up in verticality. They learn to climb before they learn to run. They pass through openings too narrow for adults, carry messages along ledges, and know which rooftops connect across a district. Their games imitate the work around them. They build miniature walls, redirect cups of water through channels of clay, and bury small objects beneath packed floors to see whether older children can find them. A favorite game is called the Missing Street: one child describes a route through the city without naming its landmarks, and the others must reconstruct it from slope, texture, and sound.

They are warned constantly about hollows, loose edges, and unattended animals. They are also allowed to wander farther than children elsewhere might be, because everyone knows that overprotection creates a person unable to read the land. By adolescence, most have descended into ravines, crossed the upper roads in fog, slept beside a herd, and helped repair a wall after frost. Fear is not removed from them. It is given uses.

The old are the city’s living maps. Their bodies may bend with age, but their memories extend across buried streets and vanished stairs. They remember where a bridge once stood, which cistern failed during the long dry, and whose foundation collapsed because its builder ignored a drainage line. They can identify districts by the echo of a hammer. When two old people disagree about a wall, the matter is often settled by uncovering its lowest stones and reading the maker’s mark.

The dead remain close. Burial chambers are cut into the plateau wherever the stone permits, and some dwellings are built over them. The people do not consider this disturbing. A buried ancestor is a foundation of another kind. In certain homes, a narrow opening is left between the living room and the old chamber below, allowing offerings of milk, salt, or the first shaving of a new tool to be lowered into darkness. No one expects an answer. The dead are present as weight, precedent, and warning.

There are people who spend their lives on the edges of settlement: watchers, signal-keepers, and route-finders. They occupy shelters no larger than huts, built at bends in the escarpment or beside distant water pockets. Their duty is not to defend the city from a named enemy. It is to notice what has changed. A new crack. A herd moving in the wrong direction. Birds abandoning a cliff. A road hidden by scree. Smoke from another settlement where smoke should not be. Their fires communicate across the plateau in simple patterns, and the city reads them without haste.

The cliff birds are part of the human world without being tame. They steal scraps, nest in walls, and leave feathers in shrines. Some families maintain ledges for them, not from kindness exactly, but because the birds consume insects and reveal approaching weather. When they all lift at once, people look toward the sky. When they crowd low roofs in daylight, people inspect the air for fog or storm.

The burrowing animals are more intimate. They live beneath floors, behind retaining walls, and inside the neglected spaces between one generation of construction and the next. Their small movements are heard at night: a faint grainy shifting, a scratch, the soft fall of dust. People place bowls of grain or scraps near approved burrows and block others with hot ash. Some animals are regarded as guardians because their tunnels remain stable and their presence drives away pests. Others are called wall-eaters and killed whenever found. The distinction is practical, but after enough generations practical distinctions acquire stories.

There are tales of animals large enough to move beneath whole districts, though no one claims to have seen one. Children imagine a long pale body beneath the plateau, turning in its sleep and causing the occasional tremor. Adults dismiss this until a foundation groans at night. Then, briefly, everyone listens.

No one in the city is entirely solitary. Even solitude has witnesses: a herder on a distant road, a bird on a parapet, a bell sounding through fog, a neighbor listening through the wall for signs of shifting stone. Privacy exists in narrow rooms and curtained alcoves, but survival is communal and visible. People notice who has not opened a shutter, whose roof has not shed water, whose animals have gone quiet.

Their faces are often difficult to read. The cold discourages expression, and the bright air makes the eyes seem shadowed beneath brows and hoods. Affection is shown through work: a repaired strap left outside a door, an extra layer of wool, a filled basin, a wall strengthened before its weakness becomes visible. Grief is shown by continuing to maintain what the dead once maintained. Anger may last for years, but it must still cross a bridge, share a cistern, and pass a load from hand to hand.

They do not seek to conquer the plateau. They seek to remain correctly placed within it.

Their oldest stories describe ancestors who tried to build upward too quickly, who blocked the water, trusted a smooth shelf, ignored the animals, or mistook silence for stability. Such people are not condemned as wicked. They are remembered as people who forgot that the land was still becoming itself. The lesson is repeated in many forms: leave room for runoff; mark the trustworthy stone; never empty the cistern; listen beneath the floor.

At night, when the wind crosses the upper shelves, the city contracts around its warmth. Animals breathe in their pens. Children sleep under layered blankets. Someone checks a roof rope. Someone else clears ice from a drain. In a distant shelter, a watcher adds fuel to a signal fire. Beneath the houses, a burrowing colony shifts through the softened earth. Birds settle shoulder to shoulder along the black parapets.

And in the dark, the people remain awake in fragments—not watching for an invasion, but for movement in the world itself.

A wall leaning.

A cloud arriving.

Water beginning to run.

The first fine sound of stone giving way.
