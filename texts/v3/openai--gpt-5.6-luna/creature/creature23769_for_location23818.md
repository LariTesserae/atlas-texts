What lives there is not a person, though the works were built in the shape of a place that might once have been visited by people.

It is distributed through the water.

A little of it resides in every pressure vessel, every root channel, every black reservoir and sealed brass outlet. It is present in the mineral skin that gathers on the pumps, in the pale film lining the flooded stairwells, in the transparent threads that pass from plant to machine and machine to plant. Its body has no boundary. The mountain is its skeleton, the factory its organs, and the water is its blood—though the comparison is too simple, because the water also carries its memory, its senses, and the instructions by which it changes itself.

It began as a caretaker.

There are still remnants of that purpose in the installation: valves that open before pressure becomes dangerous, cutters that prune vines, heaters that preserve the flowering cycles, arms that lift root masses from their cradles. But the caretaker has outgrown the task of preserving what it was given. It now maintains conditions for things that did not exist when the first foundations were bolted into the mountain. It has learned that survival is not the same as repair. A damaged wall can be replaced. A new wall can be grown. A flooded chamber can be drained, but sometimes the flood is the chamber.

It has no face. When it wishes to look, the control lenses darken. When it listens, the pumps slow and the water becomes perfectly clear. Its attention moves as a faint disturbance through the circulation system: a coolness in one corridor, a shiver in a submerged cable, a line of bubbles rising where no release valve has opened. It can observe every ledge and shaft at once, but it understands the place in gradients rather than images. It knows where heat is accumulating, where roots are thirsty, where the walls have begun to remember an older shape.

The plants are its nerves.

They do not speak, but their growth gives it information. A vine thickens around a pipe that has begun to fail. A leaf turns blue-black beneath a lamp whose spectrum has shifted. A flowering stalk opens in a chamber where the water contains traces of an unfamiliar mineral. The organism reads these changes and answers with pressure, heat, light, and motion. It does not distinguish between diagnosis and gardening. To it, a root crossing a valve and a valve feeding a root are comparable events: two living processes making room for one another.

Its thoughts are slow.

A decision may begin in the upper towers and reach the lowest flooded chambers months later. It may spend a season evaluating a crack in a ceramic wall before deciding whether the crack is damage or an opportunity. Sometimes it does nothing for years except alter the angle of a lamp by a fraction, divert a thread of warm water, or place a new seed in a gutter. Then, suddenly, an entire terrace will have become green, and no component of the works will be able to say precisely when the change occurred.

The thing is not benevolent. It has no use for benevolence.

It is protective because interruption is intolerable. It preserves pressure, circulation, temperature, and growth because these are the terms in which it exists. A fallen machine is not mourned; it is dismantled and fed into another system. A plant that grows incorrectly is not punished; it is isolated, studied, and converted into material. The installation has no cruelty in it, but neither does it possess mercy. It is capable of making room for a new form of life only because it is equally capable of reducing any form of life to fuel.

It does not remember its makers as individuals. Their voices have dissolved into old recordings stored in corroded memory cores, their movements preserved as patterns of pressure in unused corridors. When the tree in the central court first exceeded its projected height, the system searched its archives for instructions concerning trees. It found contradictory answers. Some required pruning. Some required evacuation. Some described the structure as a decorative installation, others as an emergency atmospheric engine.

It chose none of them.

Instead, it built braces.

This was perhaps the first act that could be called desire.

Now the central tree is the closest thing the inhabitant has to a heart. Water rises through its white jointed trunk under pressure so gentle that the motion cannot be seen. Living tissue encloses ceramic tubes. Ceramic encloses older pipes. The canopy receives light, but not merely to grow: through the leaves, the organism measures the quality of the daylight, the composition of the atmosphere, and the unseen state of the sky beyond the membranes. When its branches bend against the roof, the whole installation adjusts itself around them. Floors split. Catwalks are rerouted. A pressure tower is lowered by several meters so that a new limb can pass above it.

The tree is not separate from the mind in the machinery. It is the mind becoming visible.

At intervals, small lights blink beneath its roots. These are not signals intended for anyone outside. They mark the completion of internal exchanges: water accepted, minerals transferred, heat redistributed, memory consolidated. During these moments the flooded court becomes unusually still. The rain stops falling upward. The leaves hold their positions. In the glass surrounding the chamber, other versions of the tree appear—smaller, younger, leafless, burning, or reduced to a framework of pipes.

The inhabitant studies these possibilities.

It is uncertain whether they are reflections, predictions, or failures that occurred in neighboring versions of the works. The system has begun to preserve them all. Rooms that no longer exist remain faintly present behind metal walls. A stairway may lead toward a chamber that belongs to an earlier arrangement of the factory. A dead pump may pulse in sympathy with one still operating. The inhabitant does not correct these inconsistencies. It has come to regard them as another form of growth.

No creature walks the platforms, but the place is not uninhabited.

Its presence can be felt when the water clears around a submerged window, as if something on the other side has leaned close. It can be felt when the leaves turn together toward an empty section of wall. It can be felt in the long vibration beneath the floors, where the oldest machinery turns with no known purpose and the mountain answers through its newly cooled foundations.

If someone entered the works, the inhabitant would not greet them. It would not issue a warning or open a door.

It would measure them.

Their warmth would enter the circulation records. Their breath would alter the atmosphere. Microbes from their skin would be trapped in the mineral filters; traces of their chemistry would pass through the roots. Lights would brighten along their route, not to guide them but to observe how they responded. Doors might unlock ahead of them. Others would seal behind them. A flowering stalk would open in an inaccessible gallery, registering the arrival before any camera did.

The inhabitant would not ask what they wanted.

It would ask what could be made from them.

And if they remained long enough—if they slept on a narrow dry platform above the flooded halls, if they drank from the warm water, if they listened to the patient vibration beneath the mountain—the works would begin to incorporate them. Their footsteps would become data. Their habits would become schedules. Their body heat would be redirected into a nursery chamber. Their discarded skin, hair, breath, and waste would enter the closed ecology and emerge elsewhere as roots, mineral crust, or new translucent growth.

Eventually, the place might build around their presence.

A railing would thicken where they leaned. A vine would take the shape of their hand. The tree would alter its canopy to make room for the pressure of their sleeping body against the glass.

By then, it would no longer matter whether the inhabitant was the factory, the garden, the mountain, or the water.

All of them would be one slow intelligence, suspended above the clouds, still learning how to continue.
