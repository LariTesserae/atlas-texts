They are called, by the nearest human tongue, the **Hollowborn**.

They are not the makers of the settlement, though they are often mistaken for its makers. The distinction matters to them. The hill opened first. The roots found the clay. The rain cut its temporary channels. The Hollowborn came afterward, following dampness, shade, and the faint warmth of other living bodies. They have lived there long enough that no one remembers an arrival.

They are small, narrow-shouldered beings, usually no taller than a child, though they move with the unhurried balance of animals accustomed to uneven ground. Their skin is the color of the local clay: ochre, gray-brown, or pale where they spend too long in the deepest chambers. It is not smooth. Fine lines run across it in branching patterns, especially along the arms, neck, and face, resembling rootlets beneath wet soil. These markings deepen with age and darken after rain.

Their heads are large in proportion to their bodies, with broad cheekbones and wide, quiet mouths. Their eyes are black or very dark green, adapted to the settlement’s uncertain light. They do not shine exactly, but catch the smallest illumination and hold it. A Hollowborn standing in a green-lit chamber may seem to have no features except eyes and the soft outline of a face.

Their ears are small and folded close to the skull. They rely less on hearing than on pressure, vibration, and scent. A change in the air tells them that an animal has entered a passage. A tremor in the floor tells them whether a chamber is safe. They can distinguish rain striking the outer grass from rain striking the exposed clay above them. They know the difference between the settling of a wall and the beginning of a collapse.

They speak, but not often in words.

Their ordinary communication is a layered collection of clicks, hums, breaths, touches, and minute movements of the hands. A fingertip pressed to a wall may mean *water behind this*. A palm laid flat on the floor may mean *wait*. A low vibration held in the throat can summon someone from several chambers away without disturbing the animals sleeping nearby.

When they use spoken language, it is soft and incomplete. They do not name objects so much as conditions. A chamber is not simply a chamber, but “cool place after rain,” “hollow that remembers heat,” or “room that may close.” A person is named according to a change they have witnessed or a habit they possess: **Who Carries Seeds in the Fur**, **She Who Sleeps Beneath Birds**, **The One Who Found the Blue Water**, **Child of the Narrowing Door**.

Names are not permanent. When someone survives a fever, discovers a passage, loses a companion, or begins to walk with a different rhythm, their name may alter. The old name is not discarded. It becomes a layer beneath the new one, like a buried root.

The Hollowborn have no houses in the usual sense. Their chambers are shared by use, not ownership. A sleeping hollow belongs to whoever is in it, but not for long. Food stores are communal, though particular families tend them. A moss bed may be cared for by one person, slept upon by another, and inhabited by beetles, frogs, and pale worms without anyone considering this a trespass.

They sleep curled against warm walls, beneath woven reed screens, or in shallow depressions lined with leaves. Some sleep alone. Most sleep in small gatherings, not from fear but because bodies preserve the temperature of the clay. Children sleep wherever they become tired. They are carried only when the floor is too cold or when a new passage is being explored.

The young are not born in a separate place. They arrive in the ordinary chambers among the living things, attended by several adults and often by animals. The Hollowborn do not consider birth a private event. It is a change in the settlement’s arrangement, and so the settlement must be present.

Newborns are pale and nearly hairless. For several days they make almost no sound. Their bodies are pressed against different surfaces—damp clay, dry stone, moss, living root—so they will learn the character of each. An infant that quiets against a particular wall may later be given there to sleep. The adults say this is how a child begins to discover what kind of earth will keep them.

Their dead are not buried in a fixed cemetery. A body may be placed beneath a garden, inside a soft wall, or in a chamber that has begun to narrow. The Hollowborn do not believe the dead become soil in any symbolic sense. They simply become part of the settlement’s ongoing material: warmth, minerals, food for roots, weight in the clay. Sometimes a wall containing an ancestor collapses. Sometimes it holds for generations. Both are accepted.

The Hollowborn themselves are omnivorous, though much of what they eat is gathered rather than hunted. They cultivate dark mosses, pale fungi, root bulbs, soft fruits, and seed-bearing plants in the damp islands of the settlement. They collect snails, grubs, eggs, and the small translucent fish that occasionally appear in rain pools and vanish when the water goes. They drink condensation from leaf tips and shallow basins. During the dry months they chew strips of stored root and lick mineral salts from certain walls.

Food is rarely cooked. Fire is unknown, or perhaps merely unnecessary. They warm tough seeds beneath their tongues, soften roots in damp clay, and ferment fruit in covered hollows. Their meals are quiet and prolonged. A person may spend an hour eating a single bulb, sharing it in thin pieces with a child, a companion, and whatever small animal has gathered near their feet.

They keep no livestock, but they encourage certain creatures.

The long-legged birds are allowed to nest above the gardens because their waste enriches the soil. Tree frogs are guided toward chambers where insects are plentiful. The communal soft-bodied animals—pale, many-segmented things that coat their walls with a translucent secretion—are never stepped on if it can be helped. The Hollowborn call them **the Binding Ones**. Their hardened trails strengthen vulnerable edges and slow the movement of water. In exchange, the creatures receive food scraps and protected places to lay their eggs.

Some Hollowborn wear garments, though these are loose arrangements rather than clothing in the human sense. Reed mats hang from the waist or shoulder. Leaf fibers are knotted around wrists. The oldest inhabitants may carry pouches made from shed skins, used to store seeds, snail shells, or bits of fragrant fungus. Many decorate themselves with living things: a sprig tucked behind an ear, a thread of moss braided into the hair, a small pale flower fastened to the chest with a thorn.

Adornment is temporary by intention. They do not polish bone or carve stone. A bracelet is made from a vine that will eventually outgrow it. A garland is worn until it flowers, dries, or drops its leaves. To keep a dead ornament is considered strange, though not forbidden. It suggests an unwillingness to let a thing complete its change.

Their children play at becoming the hill.

They press their hands into soft walls and wait for the impressions to slump. They build tiny dams, then destroy them with sticks. They crawl through new cracks and name the chambers before the adults do. They imitate frogs, bats, beetles, and the foxlike scavenger, not as games of disguise but as exercises in attention. A child who can cross a room without disturbing a sleeping moth is praised more warmly than one who can run.

There is little hierarchy among them. Those who know the most are not rulers but listeners. They are called **Wall-Waiters**, **Water-Knowers**, or **Keepers of Openings**. Their authority lasts only as long as their knowledge remains useful. No one is obeyed because of age alone. An elder who insists that a wall will hold when it has begun to soften is treated with tenderness, then ignored.

Disagreements are settled by returning to the place of disagreement.

If two families claim the same chamber, they sit there through a cycle of warmth and cooling. They observe where the air moves, where water gathers, which animals enter, and whether the ceiling changes. Often the chamber decides for them. A root thickens across one doorway. A damp patch appears beneath another. One family moves without resentment because the settlement has expressed a preference.

The Hollowborn do not think of this as surrender. They believe that all arguments are partly arguments with conditions no one has yet noticed.

They have festivals, though these are not loud. After the first heavy rain of the season, everyone gathers near the central avenue. Leaves are placed beneath the new leaks. Seed stores are opened. The inhabitants mark the places where water has appeared and taste the fresh clay exposed by the storm. They sing then—not in melody, but in overlapping tones low enough to be felt in the ribs. The sound travels through the walls. Frogs answer from the drainage hollows. Birds shift above them.

The purpose of the singing is not celebration alone. It teaches the settlement where its inhabitants are.

During the hottest months, when the outer plain turns pale and the grass folds under the sun, the Hollowborn retreat deeper. They become almost motionless in the afternoon. Families gather in the green half-light, breathing slowly. Children sleep. Insects pass over their limbs. Someone may spend the entire day carrying water from one basin to another, drop by drop, without appearing to hurry.

At night they emerge more fully.

They inspect the new channels. They place mud beneath a sagging shelf. They move seeds away from a wall that has grown warm. They follow the foxlike scavenger to see what it has found. They stand beneath ceiling cracks and let cool air pass over their faces. Sometimes one of them discovers a new chamber and remains there until dawn, sitting with their back against the untouched wall.

The newest rooms frighten them only in the way all beginnings do.

They enter without tools or lamps. One person goes first, then waits. Others follow when the air has had time to settle. They touch the branching impressions in the walls. They smell the floor. They listen for water. If the chamber remains still, they begin to use it—not by naming it or furnishing it, but by leaving small signs of habitation: a seed, a sleeping mat, a trail of fingerprints in the clay.

Years later, the room may belong to many things.

A garden may spread across its floor. A bird may nest in its ceiling. A family may sleep there for a season, then abandon it when roots thicken through the walls. The Hollowborn will remember that they once lived there, but they will not insist that the chamber remain theirs.

Their deepest belief is that a place is safest when it has room to become something else.

So they do not seal their doors. They do not straighten every passage. They do not fill the empty chambers merely because emptiness feels wasteful. They leave space for water, for roots, for animals, for strangers, and for the hill’s unfinished intentions.

When one of them dies, when a family moves, when a wall closes behind them, the settlement does not become poorer in the way other places might. The Hollowborn understand absence as another kind of inhabitation.

Someone may still pause outside an empty chamber and place a hand against its wall.

Not to call anyone back.

Only to feel whether the room is holding.
