What lives on the plateau has no single shape.

At first it seems to be the small life: the quick bodies beneath the leaf litter, the grazers spread wide over the sinking grass, the bright-winged swarms lifting whenever the mist shifts. There are thousands of species, perhaps millions, each fitted to one narrow condition of the place. Some drink from leaves. Some drink from stone. Some spend their entire lives in the warm water under the moss, surfacing only to mate or shed their skins. Others are born in the clouds and descend as larvae into the marshes, where they become things with legs, membranes, and patient mouths.

The animals are not greatly afraid of one another.

They are wary, certainly. They pause when a shadow crosses the reeds. They freeze when the cliff predators begin their slow approach. But panic is wasteful in this heat, and the plateau has selected against waste. Most movement is deliberate. Most creatures remain still until stillness becomes impossible.

The grazers travel in loose, elevated herds. Their feet are broad and flexible, divided into gripping lobes that test the ground before accepting its weight. They feed on the black grasses and on the pale growths that gather around the vents. Along their backs, the fur is thick enough to hold condensation; beneath it, narrow channels carry the water toward the mouth. Young animals learn to stand by following the vibration in the ground rather than the adults’ calls. When the hidden mechanism shifts, the herd knows before the springs do.

The cliff-dwellers are more difficult to name. They have too many joints in their limbs and eyes that close from the side, like shutters. They cling beneath shelves of rock, where their bodies resemble torn curtains of moss. At night they extend long, translucent tongues to collect the mineral film left by cooling water. They are slow to reproduce and nearly impossible to surprise. A predator may wait beneath them for an entire day, but the cliff-dweller will already have felt its weight through the stone.

The great amphibious herbivores spend much of their lives submerged. Their stone-colored hides are veined with faint blue light, not bioluminescence exactly, but a response to the underground signals. They lie in the warm basins with only their nostrils above water, while plants root across their backs. Birds build nests there. Small insects clean their eyes. When one rises, an entire floating garden lifts with it.

There are predators, though they do not resemble hunger as it is known elsewhere.

The narrow hunters move through mud without breaking the surface. Their paws are soft and broad, and the skin between their toes contains tiny pressure-sensing organs. They can feel a heartbeat through several layers of peat. They hunt rarely, and when they do, the act is almost ceremonial: a pause, a single extension of the neck, the brief collapse of a shape that had seemed to be part of the landscape.

Some predators feed on animals. Others feed on heat.

They gather around vents and spring mouths, drinking the gradients between warm and cool water. Their bodies are long and hollow, with dark frills that open and close as the subterranean currents change. They are most active when the buried systems wake. Then they travel along the faintly glowing channels, following paths that may once have been engineered and are now understood only by instinct.

The insects—or their equivalent—are everywhere. They swarm above the marshes in layered veils, their wings cutting the humid air into a papery vibration. Some carry pollen. Some carry spores. Some carry living fragments of the plateau itself: a thread of blue-green cushion, a root tip, a capsule of mineral bacteria. They cross the valleys in seasonal migrations, and their routes are so old that the larger animals make room for them.

At dusk, the swarms become instruments.

They settle across the buried arcs and respond to the dim light beneath them. Each body gives off a minute pulse. Together, the pulses form moving patterns: circles widening through the grasses, lines passing from ridge to ridge, brief symbols that no individual insect could perceive. The animals follow these patterns without knowing whether they are reading instructions, weather, danger, or memory.

There may be beings here that remember.

They are not seen often. They inhabit the stations, the sealed shafts, the dark pillars beside the springs. They may have begun as machines, or as caretakers, or as something made to mediate between the two. Their bodies are narrow and pale, assembled from ceramic, root, tendon, and mineral deposit. They emerge only during certain changes in pressure, when a basin must be opened or a conduit cleared.

One can stand motionless beside a waterfall and mistake such a being for a column of wet stone. Moss grows on its shoulders. Small animals sleep in the hollows of its frame. When it moves, it does so with the patience of geology.

These caretakers no longer maintain the plateau in any obvious sense. They do not repair every broken instrument or prevent every species from dying. Their work is broader and slower. They redirect a spring before it becomes a flood. They alter the angle of a buried antenna. They open the old shaft for an animal whose blood chemistry matches a forgotten requirement. Sometimes they touch the ground and listen.

What they listen for is the plateau’s deeper life.

The mechanical vibration does not come from one machine. It passes through everything: rock, root, water, bone. The plants bend toward it. The animals sleep more deeply when it is steady. The buried systems answer one another through it, adjusting pressures and temperatures by increments too small for any single creature to notice.

Yet the whole landscape notices.

A tree sends roots toward a warmer seam. A marsh opens a new channel. A herd abandons a basin weeks before its water turns mineral-heavy. The animals do not obey the vibration. They are part of its expression. Their migrations, burrows, nests, and deaths alter the flow of water as surely as the ancient conduits do.

The plateau is inhabited, then, by a vast agreement between living and constructed things.

Nothing rules it. Nothing stands above it and issues commands. The old intelligence beneath the mountains has become distributed through roots, membranes, springs, migrations, and memory. Its thoughts are too slow to resemble thought. A correction may take a century. A warning may travel through three generations of animals before reaching its destination. A decision may appear as a change in flowering season, a shift in nesting height, the gradual disappearance of a path.

Perhaps the plateau once had inhabitants who called the buried system a machine.

Those inhabitants are gone, or changed beyond recognition. Their bridges remain, but the animals use them as warm basking shelves. Their stations collect mineral skins. Their instruments are pollinated. The infrastructure has outlived its makers by becoming habitat, and the habitat has slowly become its interpreter.

At night, when the clouds fill the valleys and the lights awaken beneath the plants, the living things gather along the old geometric lines.

The insects settle first. The cliff-dwellers emerge from their shelves. The amphibious herbivores leave the water and stand steaming in the grass. Even the predators pause, their bodies turned toward the same buried pulse.

For a little while, the plateau seems to hold still.

Then the signal passes onward. A spring opens somewhere beyond the ridge. A hidden river changes course. The animals begin to move again, each toward its own shelter, feeding ground, nest, or waiting place.

The mountain continues its slow work, and everything living there goes on living inside it.
