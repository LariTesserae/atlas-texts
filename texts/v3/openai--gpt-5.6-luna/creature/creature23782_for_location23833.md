The people of the high city are not still, exactly. They move, work, eat, marry, quarrel, and sleep. But everything they do has the careful quality of an action performed in a room where something fragile is listening.

They are narrow-bodied and pale from generations beneath the mild, shadowless sky. Their skin tends toward the colors of the plateau: ash, warm stone, faint blue-gray. Most have dark, reflective eyes, adapted to finding water in the smallest hollows. Their hair is worn short or bound close to the head, not because of wind—there is none—but because loose strands are considered wasteful. A person’s body is another surface from which moisture may be gathered. At dawn, the people wipe their arms, faces, and sleeping animals with soft cloths, wringing the collected beads into household bowls.

They wear layered garments of reed fiber, softened hide, and thin woven grass. The clothes are plain, but never careless. Every pocket has a purpose. A belt may hold a hook for clearing a drain, a bone needle, a small cup, and a strip of marked stone on which the day’s reservoir levels are recorded. Children learn to walk without splashing, to close a vessel with one hand, and to recognize the sound of a blocked channel before they can read.

Their faces are composed, though not expressionless. Emotion travels through minute changes: a tightening at the mouth, a widening of the eyes, the turn of the head toward a distant sound. Laughter exists, but it is usually quiet and brief. Sudden gestures are uncommon. A raised voice carries too far in the still air, and the city has learned that anger, once released, can occupy a street for a long time.

They are patient people because impatience has no effect on the world.

A seed will germinate when it germinates. A cistern will fill to its notch and no higher. A cracked wall will remain standing until a mason takes it apart. Arguments are not settled by exhaustion; they are settled when both parties have said precisely what they mean and the appointed listener has repeated it back to them without alteration.

The city’s oldest inhabitants are difficult to distinguish from one another in age. They wrinkle slowly, if at all. Children grow, but not always in the expected sequence: a girl may remain small for many seasons and then acquire the height of an adult between one census and the next. Teeth are repaired with ceramic. Bones mend cleanly. Illnesses are rare and tend to end either quickly or never.

No one agrees on how long a person may live.

The official records speak of generations, but the oldest names remain in use. A waterwright may remember the construction of a reservoir whose first stone was laid before the birth of her great-grandmother, and still stand at the same channel each morning, her hands steady, her face unchanged except for the accumulation of knowledge behind it. The people do not call this immortality. They call it remaining.

When someone dies, the body is washed with collected condensation and laid beneath one of the rain trees. The city does not burn its dead; fire changes too quickly. Nor does it bury them deeply, for the stone is reluctant to open. Instead, the body is left where the moss and small pale creatures can reach it. The people gather around, not grieving loudly, but watching. Death is understood as a departure from the agreement that holds everything else in place.

The dead do not decay in the ordinary manner. Flesh withdraws. Bones become clean. Eventually the person is reduced to a pale arrangement beneath the grass, and even then the remains may endure for centuries. Children are taught the names of these skeletons and the seasons in which they were found. In the outer districts, some old bones have been incorporated into walls—not as trophies, but because there are few materials the city will permit itself to waste.

The animals are treated as neighbors rather than possessions.

The small grazers enter courtyards and drink from household basins. Birds sit beside people in the market shelters, waiting for grain. Cats choose their sleeping places without regard for ownership and may spend decades beside the same warm wall. The people speak to them, but do not expect obedience. A creature that refuses a path is assumed to have detected something the human senses cannot.

There are keepers assigned to the larger animals. They do not herd them so much as maintain the boundaries between species and water. They paint warning marks on cisterns that are too deep for long-necked herbivores. They move gardens a handspan at a time when roots begin to obstruct an animal trail. Predators are not driven away unless they enter a nursery or contaminate a reservoir. Even then, the customary response is not pursuit, but negotiation through barriers, food, and waiting.

The people believe that violence is a form of motion, and motion is dangerous.

Their priests—or perhaps their engineers, since the distinction is uncertain—are called listeners. They maintain the central pool and keep records of its single, inexplicable sound. A listener may spend an entire day seated beside the black water, eyes closed, while attendants measure the level, the temperature, and the distance between each pillar. When the sound comes, everyone nearby turns toward it. The listeners do not interpret it immediately. They first write down who was present, which animals moved, how many drops had fallen from the nearest tree, and whether the smoke from the square’s lamp remained vertical.

Only later do they announce what it means.

Sometimes it means nothing. Sometimes it confirms that a child should be named after an old animal or a vanished star. Sometimes it is taken as permission to repair a road, begin a building, or open a sealed storehouse. The city does not make major decisions without a sign, but neither does it trust signs that arrive too eagerly.

The children are the least settled inhabitants. They run when adults walk, climb walls despite the danger of loosening stones, and place their hands into channels to feel the cold water pass. They collect footprints, dead moths, translucent leaves, and fragments of unfamiliar material found near the rim. They ask where the roads below the cloud sea lead. They ask why distant lights never come closer. They ask whether the city was built by people who knew the world would stop changing.

Adults answer carefully.

The common answer is that the city is safe because it is held.

The private answer, preserved in fragments of song and in the marks beneath old cisterns, is that something beneath the clouds is holding it.

For all their composure, the people are afraid of interruption. A moving shadow would cause more alarm than a storm. A stone falling without being touched would bring the streets to a halt. If a flame flickered, every available listener would be summoned. If a bird flew against the wind—if wind appeared—the city would empty into the square, carrying vessels, tools, children, and records.

Yet they also cultivate small changes in secret.

Gardeners breed plants whose leaves open at different hours. Musicians make flutes designed to produce tones that never repeat. Young masons place stones slightly out of alignment and wait to see whether the wall accepts the imperfection. Lovers exchange droplets gathered from different districts, mixing waters that the city’s instruments insist have never met.

These acts are not rebellion. They are reminders.

A person may live for a century, or for five, and still feel the need to prove that the world can be altered by a living hand. So they move a bowl. Cut a reed. Name a child. Step beyond the final house and place one foot inside an ancient footprint.

Then they stand at the rim, looking down through the silent white sea, waiting for something below to move first.
