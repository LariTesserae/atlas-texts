What lives on the plateau has no true name, because names imply a separation that does not exist there.

The large animals, the burrowing multitudes, the mosses, the mineral-eating plants, and the things that move through the buried water are all parts of one distributed life. They are not a colony in the way ants are a colony, nor an ecosystem in the familiar sense. They are closer to a body whose organs have been scattered across a continent and taught to wait.

The most conspicuous of them are the long-bodied walkers.

They hatch, if hatching is the right word, from warm seams beneath the gravel. For years they are small enough to pass through the galleries under the plateau, feeding on mineral films and the pale growths that form around pressure faults. Their bodies are soft then, nearly transparent, and divided into many short segments. They navigate by touch and vibration. A distant footfall is not merely a disturbance to them; it is information. They know the weight of a creature before they know its presence, and can tell from the rhythm of pressure whether the passing body is hungry, injured, gravid, or carrying water.

As they mature, their outer skin incorporates what it encounters. Silicates become plates. Iron darkens the joints. Flakes of silver-gray ore settle into the seams along their backs, where they conduct the faint electrical charges produced by their muscles. No two adults are the same color. One may be blue-black as deep ice; another may be the yellow-gray of weathered brass; another may bear a skin of almost white stone, so dull that it resembles snow hardened by wind.

They do not grow upward so much as outward. An old walker can be longer than a village, though most of its body remains hidden behind the shape of the land. The visible portion may be only a head and several raised plates, with the rest of it descending into a valley and reappearing far away. It is difficult to say where one ends. In some seasons, what seems to be a single animal is revealed as several joined individuals moving in sequence, each following the heat and pressure carried through the body before it.

Their heads are blunt wedges fitted with grinding surfaces. They eat exposed stone, frozen soil, dead plant matter, and the metallic crusts that form around underground water. The material is sorted inside them. Useful minerals pass into the bloodlike fluids that circulate beneath their plates. Waste is expelled as dark nodules, warm at first, then quickly cooling into glassy black beads. Smaller animals gather these beads and build with them. Nothing is wasted, though nothing is deliberately conserved. The walkers do not possess the concept of waste. Everything that leaves one body becomes available to another.

Their mouths are quiet. They do not bite so much as lean.

A walker settles its head against a slope and remains there for several hours, perhaps several years. Tiny grinding plates move beneath its jaw. Gravel trembles down the incline. A seam of ore is exposed. Water that has been sealed beneath the stone for centuries finds a new path upward. The animal drinks from the seep, then withdraws, leaving behind a basin in which moss and jointed plants may establish themselves.

These animals have no leaders. They have no need of them. Their decisions are made by pressure, warmth, mineral concentration, and the memory held in their bodies. When one changes direction, the change travels through the ground. Others feel it and alter their own routes, sometimes after a delay of months. A migration is therefore less an organized movement than a continent gradually changing its mind.

The great walkers reproduce during the deep cold.

Several gather around a basin where water remains unfrozen beneath a roof of stone. They stand without touching. Their plates open and close in sequence, releasing heat into the air. Beneath the basin, eggs or spores—or something between the two—are deposited in the sediment. The young are not raised. They are surrounded by the accumulated warmth of generations, by tunnels shaped long before their arrival, by food stored in the walls and by signals traveling through the ground.

Most do not survive. The plateau is too large and too poor for abundance without loss. The young that remain underground are fed by the smaller inhabitants, who do not appear to distinguish between their own offspring and the young of the walkers. In return, the growing animals press new passages into the soil and widen the old ones. By the time they emerge, they are already part of the infrastructure.

Below the walkers live the tunnel populations.

They are small, pale, and numerous, with bodies like flattened cords and limbs too fine to be seen except under magnification. Their eyes have vanished, if they ever possessed them. In their place are fields of sensory pits along the head, sides, and underside of the body. Through these they read the plateau: the density of the gravel, the temperature of buried water, the vibration of approaching bodies, the chemical traces left by other animals.

They live in networks, but not in fixed nests. Their galleries are continually altered. Walls are polished by the passage of bodies until they become smooth as ceramic. Chambers are opened around warm stones and sealed when the stone cools. Some tunnels are used for generations; others are created and abandoned within a single night.

They are the first to respond when the plateau changes.

When the deep vibration comes, they rise toward it. Thousands of them gather beneath the surface, orienting themselves along the spreading wave. They do not flee. They listen with their entire bodies. Some migrate toward the source, while others retreat from it, and between these movements new channels are formed. The vibration is translated into action: a spring opened, a ridge avoided, a layer of mineral brought nearer the surface.

They also tend the plants.

The pale jointed growths in the wet basins are not passive vegetation. Their roots—or rootlike tissues—reach through the tunnel systems, exchanging sugars and salts with the animals below. At night, the plants close themselves over the smallest burrows, creating pockets of warmth. The tunnel creatures feed on the outer tissues and carry pieces of them elsewhere. In this way, a plant may exist as a scattered population of root chambers separated by kilometers of barren rock, while the animals that tend it move constantly between its parts.

The mosses are older still. They spread over stone in rust-colored skins and tolerate conditions that would kill most life. Their cells trap water vapor, metallic dust, and the faint organic residue carried on the feet of passing animals. A moss field is not so much a carpet as a memory of traffic. Where the large bodies have traveled repeatedly, the stone becomes darkened, softened, and eventually alive.

Nothing on the plateau is solitary, though solitude is common.

An individual may pass years without seeing another of its kind. It does not experience this as abandonment. Contact is carried through the ground. A distant animal may send a pulse that arrives faintly beneath the feet. The message is not specific in the manner of speech. It is more like a change in condition: water available, route blocked, mineral exposed, young present, storm approaching.

At night these signals become visible.

The bodies of the larger animals contain thin chambers of living material that glow when activated. A walker turns its head, and a line of green light moves beneath its plates. Far away, another answers with amber. Smaller animals flash cold white from the edges of their bodies as they cross a basin. No signal lasts long. The lights do not illuminate the ground; they are meant only to be recognized by others already looking.

From above, the plateau can appear inhabited by a network of failing lamps.

From within it, the lights are not messages so much as acknowledgments. I am here. I have felt you. The route continues.

There are predators, though predation is not the dominant relation. Some narrow animals follow the warm trails left by the walkers and enter the soft spaces between their plates. They feed on parasites, injured tissue, and mineral-rich secretions. Others consume the small tunnel creatures in seasons when the buried water retreats. Their bodies are quick and nearly boneless, built for sudden movement through galleries rather than sustained travel.

The largest of these predators are rarely seen. They inhabit the deep fractures, where pressure makes the stone itself warm. They possess long sensory tendrils and mouths that open sideways. When they emerge, the smaller animals flatten themselves into the gravel. The walkers do not flee. Some turn toward the predators; others continue on. The outcome is not always a hunt. Often the great animals meet, touch their plates, and remain together for a time, exchanging fluids or organisms before separating.

Death is slow there.

A dead walker does not decay all at once. Its body remains warm for decades, perhaps centuries, while smaller animals enter it and gradually convert its tissues into galleries, nests, and mineral beds. The plates crack only after much of the inner body has been consumed. Then they are carried away piece by piece. Some become roofing for tunnel chambers. Some are ground into soil. Some are buried beneath new routes and become part of the pressure memory of the land.

An animal may therefore continue after death as a change in the behavior of others.

Its warmth alters a migration. Its bones redirect water. Its minerals feed a generation of plants. Its absence leaves a depression where snow will melt early for hundreds of years. The plateau does not preserve bodies. It preserves consequences.

There may be intelligence among them, though it would be difficult to recognize.

No single creature plans the opening of a spring or the movement of a herd. No one decides to create a road. Yet routes persist. Basins recur. Mineral bands appear with regularity across distances too great for one body to comprehend. The animals perform operations whose scale exceeds their lifetimes, and their instincts have been shaped by processes too old to remember.

They do not build toward a future. They inherit one.

Each generation is born into passages already made, water already sorted, routes already warmed by former bodies. It modifies these things slightly, and the next generation modifies them again. Over time, the plateau becomes a work performed without a worker: excavated, filtered, heated, seeded, and abandoned in cycles that have no final product.

The beings that live there do not look upon the emptiness and mistake it for emptiness.

They feel the buried water moving beneath the stone. They know where the snow will loosen before it falls. They sense the presence of distant bodies through the slow language of pressure. They recognize a living basin by the warmth in its roots and a dead one by the absence of answering motion.

Their world has no center. There is only a continuous exchange between exposed rock and hidden passage, between cold air and stored heat, between the individual body and the larger system that carries it.

When the plateau speaks, every living thing receives the message.

The moss closes. The plants fold. The tunnel populations rise. The great animals turn.

Then the vibration passes, and the inhabitants resume their work—not because they have been commanded, but because there is always some part of the world beneath them that has shifted slightly out of balance, and something living must move to meet it.
