They live by touch, temperature, and the memory held in stone.

The smallest are born already pale. They hatch in the shallow basins, in films of water no thicker than a thumb, and spend their first days drifting among mineral flakes. A touch makes them shine. The light is not meant to reveal them; it is a reflex, an alarm passed from body to body. When one larva is disturbed, the pool trembles with brief green pulses, and the others sink into the dark.

Some will remain in the water. Some will grow legs, or hooked limbs, or mouths suited to scraping the walls. Some will harden into things that look like pebbles until they move. Their lives are short by the mountain’s measure, but the hollow does not distinguish between generations. What dies is eaten, dissolved, or carried into a crack, and what emerges afterward bears the same blind knowledge: where the water gathers, where the warm air rises, where the larger bodies wait.

The roosting creatures know the upper walls as a body might know its own skin. They sleep in layers, each one gripping the stone and the one beneath it. Their wings are thin, many-jointed, and soft at the edges from constant contact. They do not fly for pleasure. Flight is an expenditure reserved for panic, mating, escape, or the sudden arrival of rain. Most of the time they hang motionless, their hearts slowing until they seem to be fragments of the mountain’s dark crust.

They feed on the pale insects in the seams, on fungus, on one another when the pools have failed. They recognize each other by warmth and odor. A mother may lose her young in the confusion of a disturbed roost and recover it hours later by the pattern of its breathing. There is no tenderness as creatures aboveground might understand it, but there is persistence. The young are kept beneath wings. Bodies make room. A living surface is never entirely one thing.

The squat animals on the ledges are the hollow’s heavier inhabitants. Their hands are broad and callused, their eyes nearly useless. They navigate by whisker, vibration, and the pressure of air moving through the cleft. Their faces are blunt because there is little need to look outward. Their world is close enough to touch.

They sleep in heaps around the warmest cracks. In the dry season they become sluggish and irritable, conserving everything: water, movement, breath. When the pools return, they drink until their sides swell and wash the mineral dust from their mouths. They know one another’s territories, though territory is never fixed. A shelf broadens when another collapses. A basin dries. A new trickle appears. Their boundaries alter with the mountain, and their quarrels alter with them.

When they fight, they do not fight to kill. They press forehead to forehead, shove shoulder against shoulder, and produce deep pulses that enter the rock. The sound travels farther than the animals can. Others answer from ledges above and below, not as witnesses but as participants in an old pattern. Eventually one retreats. The victor remains where it is, breathing hard, while the hollow absorbs the disturbance.

The hunters are quieter.

Some are small enough to hide beneath a stone. Some stretch across a pool with their limbs folded into the cracks, their bodies so still that larvae drift over them. They wait without impatience because impatience has never helped anything here. Their prey comes to them thirsty, careless, or born in the wrong place.

When they move, they move completely. A limb unfolds. A mouth opens. Water shivers. Then the hunter is still again, whether it has fed or failed. Nothing in the hollow is wasted on shame.

There are creatures that do not hunt at all but live from what hunting leaves behind. They enter the mouths of larger animals and clean them. They gather in the warm traces of sleeping bodies. They consume shed skin, old wings, softened fungus, the mineral-rich fluids that seep from wounds. Their entire existence is proximity. They are carried from ledge to ledge, pool to pool, without ever traveling under their own understanding.

The deepest basin draws all of them eventually.

They come to its margin in darkness layered upon darkness: the ledge-dwellers with their heavy hands, the blind wall-clingers, the winged bodies descending in brief flurries, the thread-finned animals rising beneath the surface. Some drink. Some lay eggs. Some wait for prey. Some wait for the rain to stop. Around the basin, no creature has a private life for long. A body is pressed between other bodies; a wound is found and investigated; a sleeping animal becomes a foothold.

At the center, beneath the black skin of water, something older than the visible creatures may live.

It is not large. It has no single recognizable form. It may be a colony of fine translucent threads spreading through the mud, or a mass of soft organisms joined by mineral growth, or simply the accumulated life of the basin behaving as one thing. The animals disturb it constantly, but it responds only to changes in water, warmth, and pressure. When the basin rises, it loosens. When the basin falls, it contracts. Its pale organs sometimes give off a dull glow beneath the surface, not enough to see by, only enough to suggest depth.

Everything depends on this hidden presence, though nothing knows it as a source. The larvae feed on its films. The armored grazers keep its surfaces open. Waste falls into it. Eggs are laid near it. Bodies return to it. The basin is not a home so much as a shared mouth, and the things around it are all, in different ways, entering and leaving its digestion.

They have no stories of the mountain. They do not remember its breaking, or the storms that shaped it, or the years before their kind occupied the ledges. Yet they carry its history in their bodies. Their blind routes follow ancient dampness. Their nesting cycles answer to underground water. Their pauses coincide with the deep movement far below. The rumble passes through them before it reaches any ear, and for a few breaths they become still—not from fear exactly, but from recognition.

Then the movement ends.

A wing opens. A claw scrapes. Something calls from the dark, and the walls return the call changed. The creatures resume their feeding, mating, sleeping, waiting, and dying. They do not know themselves as inhabitants of an enclosed world. They know only warmth beneath a ledge, water beneath the tongue, the pressure of bodies on either side, and the next safe place to put a hand.

They are not separate from the hollow.

They are its wetness made mobile, its mineral darkness given hunger and breath. When they crowd the basin, when they cling to the walls, when their dim lights pulse in the pools, the mountain seems briefly to possess a nervous system. Then the glimmers vanish, and everything settles back into the dark—alive, crowded, and continuing.
