What lives on the plateau has not yet decided whether it is an animal, a citizen, or a part of the weather.

At first it can be mistaken for a person walking alone between the slabs. It has the general proportions of one: a head, a trunk, four limbs, a forward inclination suited to wind. It wears a dark, close-fitted outer layer that reaches from throat to ankle, and over this a pale mantle of flexible plates. The plates do not clatter when it moves. They overlap like the scales of a seedpod and change position as the body bends, sealing against rain or opening to release heat.

But the thing is too tall when it stands still and too low when it walks. Its limbs divide at the joints more than once. The lower pair are broad and weight-bearing, spreading on the porous stone; the upper pair are narrow and many-fingered, used for touching walls, testing water, and reading the faint vibrations that travel through the mineral veins. It carries no visible tools. Anything it needs is already in its hands, beneath its skin, or in the ground.

The head is not quite a head. There is a face-like arrangement on the front, but no eyes as such. Instead, a dark horizontal surface receives light, pressure, temperature, and the minute electrical changes that precede weather. When it looks toward a distant city, the surface brightens in thin bands. When it looks down at a wet seam, it becomes cloudy and reflective. Its mouth is a narrow opening used mostly for speech, though speech is not its primary means of communication.

The plateau speaks through contact.

A resident standing on a translucent vein may know that a foundation has shifted two hundred kilometers away. They may feel a herd crossing a basin before the animals appear on the horizon. They may sense water rising beneath an abandoned settlement, or recognize the gradual tightening of a cliff face before the first fracture sounds. These impressions are not messages in the usual sense. They are more like weather inside the bones: changes that arrive without words and are understood because the body has been shaped to receive them.

The inhabitants call this listening.

They do not listen constantly. Constant attention would make individual life impossible. There are private rooms in the cities whose floors are insulated from the deep strata, where a person may sleep without dreaming of pressure and groundwater. Children are often raised there during their first years. Before they learn the ordinary civic signs, before they learn how to walk safely on wet ceramic shelves, they learn how to distinguish the pulse of a living animal from the pulse of a machine.

The distinction is not always clear.

Some of the city’s smallest residents are born. Others are assembled in warm chambers beneath the habitation fields, where mineral foams are coaxed into organs, membranes, and articulated frames. The two kinds grow up together. Neither is considered more complete. A child may have a breathing body, a printed body, or a body gradually revised over decades as it learns what the plateau requires of it. Identity is not attached to an original shape. It is attached to continuity: the pattern of attention that persists while the body changes.

An old inhabitant may begin life with soft, heat-holding limbs and end it as a distributed presence in the foundations of a city. Another may spend its whole existence in a compact animal form, tending herds and moving between remote shelters. Some choose to become thin, light-bodied things adapted for the escarpments, with membrane surfaces that catch the updrafts. Some enter the weather towers and remain there, reduced to a lattice of sensors, memory, and responsive material. From the ground, these beings look like buildings. From within, they still remember having once crossed the plateau under their own power.

There are humans among them, though the word has become imprecise.

The human-descended communities occupy the enclosed walkways and warm fields, maintaining meals, quarrels, rituals, schools, and private griefs. They are recognizable by their habits more than by their bodies. They gather at thresholds to smell the rain. They keep small fires despite having no practical need for them. They name animals that will never approach them and landmarks that shift before the names become old. They decorate their coats with strips of colored material, though the colors are muted by the perpetual cloud.

They are still inclined to build upward, even though the wind discourages it.

In the evenings, they leave the heated corridors and stand along the roofs while pale insects settle around them. Nobody brushes the insects away. The insects are part of the seasonal accounting of the city, and their thin bodies carry spores, mineral dust, and genetic material between distant wetlands. A person may stand beneath a tower with their face covered in living silver and speak to someone beside them as if nothing unusual were happening.

The people of the plateau do not own the land. They are not entirely certain that the land is capable of being owned.

They maintain agreements with it. A settlement may ask the deep veins for a stable foundation, but the request can be refused. A road may be laid across a migration route only if the ground confirms that the herds have another passage. Water can be drawn from beneath the stone, but not faster than the strata permit. When a city fails to observe these limits, the plateau does not punish it. It simply ceases to support the arrangement. Pipes clog with fresh mineral. Walkways soften. Doorways shift a few centimeters out of alignment. The ground beneath a tower begins to hum at a frequency that keeps everyone awake.

Eventually the inhabitants leave.

They do not leave ruins. The city folds itself inward. Walls become raw substrate. Heat drains away. The last residents carry their memories into neighboring settlements, while moss and glass-reeds occupy the empty seams. In a century, only a faint geometric regularity may remain, visible from above when the clouds thin.

For this reason, abandonment is not regarded as death. It is a return to provisionality.

Death itself is quieter. When one of the listeners can no longer maintain a body, its pattern is divided among those who knew it. A gesture is preserved in one person, a preference for certain weather in another, a memory of childhood in a building’s domestic system. The remainder is given to the plateau. It may persist as a tendency in a foundation, a slight warmth beneath a particular bench, a recurring brightness in a rain collector. No one claims that the dead are still conscious. No one insists that they are not.

There are also creatures that have never entered a city.

The six-legged grazers move in herds whose paths are older than the settlements. Their wool is dense with mineral dust, and their broad feet leave shallow hexagonal impressions in the mud. They graze not only the silver grass but the dark films growing on exposed stone. During cold periods they gather around warm structural pylons, pressing their bodies together until the whole herd steams.

The foxlike predators understand the cities better than the cities understand them. They know where construction drones pause, where food waste is released, where frightened ground birds seek shelter during electrical storms. They have learned to walk across enclosed transit bridges without triggering the alarms. Sometimes a person finds one sleeping beneath a heat vent, its narrow face turned toward the wall, listening to the infrastructure.

The birds are less tolerant of human interpretation. Their migrations follow atmospheric currents too high and too subtle to be seen. They land on the plateau only when the stone gives off the right combination of warmth and moisture. One species nests in the hollow crowns of the rare trees; another lays its eggs directly on translucent mineral bands, where the low vibration keeps them from freezing. Their calls are sparse. A single note can mean food, danger, weather, or the arrival of a familiar flock from beyond the visible horizon.

At night, the plateau’s inhabitants become difficult to separate from their surroundings.

A maintenance worker pauses with one hand against a tower and feels the pressure shifting below. A herd moves through the darkness, six-legged shadows breathing steam. Insects silver the roofs. A fox slips beneath a transit line. Far below, machines extrude a new foundation without sound. And under all of them, through the raw stone and the young mud, the continent continues its slow internal labor.

Those who live there understand that they are not finished either.

They are becoming suited to the place: learning its distances, its silences, its permissions. Their bodies are changing. Their cities are changing. Some of them are turning into weather instruments, some into animals, some into memories held by stone. They do not regard this as a loss of humanity, because humanity, on the plateau, was never a fixed shape.

It was the first temporary arrangement.
