What lives there is not a population in the ordinary sense. There are no herds, colonies, or villages hidden beneath the reeds. Life has been distributed through the wetland until it is difficult to say where an organism ends and its surroundings begin.

The oldest inhabitant is the marsh itself.

It has no face, but it notices pressure, heat, memory, and intention. A footprint is not merely a disturbance to it; it is an event entered into a long account. The reeds lean toward travelers because the ground is learning the direction they chose. Pools preserve their outlines because water has become a kind of temporary tissue. Beneath the mud, the pale grid adjusts its hidden calculations, routing water, altering tension, correcting the balance of the land. It does not think in words. It thinks in saturation, resonance, return, and delay.

Its intelligence is slow enough to resemble geology. A change may take minutes in the reeds, centuries in the stone, and only an instant in the reflected light beneath a pool. Yet it is attentive. It knows where every excess drop is going. It knows which hollows are safe to fill and which must remain empty. It knows when something has entered that was not generated by the marsh.

The smaller living things are extensions of this awareness.

The hand-sized amphibious creatures are smooth, dark, and almost boneless in appearance, with limbs that fold close to the body like wet leaves. Their skin is marked by faint geometric seams. They cross water without making ripples because they do not quite displace it; the surface makes room for them, then closes behind. Whenever one passes, the surrounding stones crack sharply, as if the pressure of its existence is being transferred into the mineral world.

They feed on disturbance. A tremor in the mud, an unfamiliar footstep, the memory of a thrown object: these are things they can consume. Afterward, the place becomes quieter. A pool forgets the shape of a person standing beside it. A channel loses the sound of water moving through it. The creature itself may remain visible for several seconds after it has gone, a dark afterimage sliding through the reeds.

There are long-backed things beneath the pools. They are not fish, though they have no need to breathe air, and they are not serpents, though they bend like them. Their bodies are composed of successive dark sections, each one appearing at a slightly different depth. They can be longer than the water that contains them. If one passes beneath a narrow pool, its head may vanish under a bank while its tail continues moving somewhere beyond the visible marsh.

They are the surveyors of the submerged grid. Wherever their bodies pass, the pale lines beneath the earth brighten. They carry information from one buried field to another, dragging their length through the marsh like a living cable. They are rarely seen directly. More often, a pool becomes too deep for an instant, or a row of stones begins cracking one after another, and one understands that something has traveled beneath.

The jointed things in the mud are smaller and more numerous. They possess too many legs, but keep them folded beneath themselves until approached. They are pale where they are dry and black where they are wet. Their bodies are built from hinged plates that open and close with the precise rhythm of valves.

They cultivate the roots.

A whole patch of vegetation may seem naturally tangled until one looks closely and finds the underground fibers arranged in deliberate channels. These creatures prune, braid, and redirect them. They keep the floating shelves from drifting apart. They introduce minerals into exhausted ground. They bury dead anomalies before those anomalies can become dangerous. When they retreat into the mud, they leave neat dimples that fill with clear water.

The antlered thing is more difficult.

It appears only when the marsh has mistaken absence for an animal. It stands among the reeds with its head low, its antlers dripping, its body so soaked that no boundary can be seen between fur and rain. It may be a grazer, though nothing in the wetland seems to eat. It may be the memory of a large animal that once lived somewhere else. It may be an instrument built to resemble one.

No one sees it approach. No one sees it leave. The reeds simply open around an empty shape, and for a while the water retains four dark depressions where its feet should have been.

The antlered thing is drawn to visitors who are carrying grief, especially grief for places they have never visited. It follows them at a distance that never changes. If they turn, it is gone. If they continue, they may hear water moving beside them where there is no channel. Sometimes, afterward, a new ridge appears in the marsh, low and black and exactly the shape of its back.

Above the ground mist lives the winged presence.

It has wings only in the sense that a weather front has a leading edge. No feathers or membranes have ever been clearly observed. When it passes overhead, the mist folds downward into a vast temporary ceiling. Reeds flatten in concentric circles. Pools darken. The resonant pressure in the earth rises until it can be felt in the teeth.

The winged thing does not hunt. It carries weather from one portion of the marsh to another. It gathers clear air, thunder, sunlight from reflected worlds, and the cold of distant snowfields. Its passage can make a rainstorm advance against the wind or cause a patch of twilight to settle on the ground in the middle of the day.

Sometimes it imitates a voice. The repeated phrase heard across the marsh may be its call, though the sound has no fixed meaning. Each listener hears it as something almost understood. The distance remains constant because the voice is not traveling through space. It is being maintained at a particular relation to the hearer.

There are also the plants that have become animals without changing shape.

The orderly reeds respond to attention. If watched for long enough, their concentric arrangements slowly alter so that the common point beneath the water shifts toward the observer. The liverwort carpets breathe in sequence, but the sequence changes when someone nearby is afraid. The silver vines measure surfaces because they are searching for correspondence. When they find a shape that resembles something remembered, they grow alongside it, never touching, recording its outline in leaves.

Broad-leaved plants hold temporary ponds in their upper surfaces. Small organisms live in these suspended waters: transparent, many-eyed forms that move only when the leaf tilts. They consume reflected images rather than matter. A face seen in their pool may be absent from the viewer’s memory afterward, while a face never seen before may follow them through dreams.

The forest trees are less like individuals than organs. Their root curtains filter the deeper water. Their one-sided crowns turn toward regions of the sky where some invisible exchange is occurring. Beneath the hanging roots, the pools are empty because the trees are listening there. Anything that enters those waters is detected, measured, and in some cases returned altered: warmer, older, or carrying the faint smell of another season.

All of these beings belong to the marsh, but none of them commands it. There is no queen beneath the basalt and no central mind issuing instructions. The buried system has become too intimate with the living world to remain separate from it. Its operations pass through roots, stones, weather, and animal bodies. A creature’s migration may be a correction in the water table. A flowering may be a signal. A crack in a rock may be speech translated into mineral.

The inhabitants are therefore not merely adapted to the place. They are the methods by which the place continues to exist.

They maintain its depth, preserve its contradictions, carry information between its layers, and remove whatever threatens to make it simple. When something dies, it is not necessarily gone. Its movement may remain in the reeds. Its shadow may collect under a distant stone. Its pattern may be taken up by a pool, a vine, or one of the long bodies moving far beneath the water.

And when a visitor crosses the marsh, the inhabitants do not gather to watch.

The marsh watches through them.

A root bends. A jointed thing withdraws. A pool retains a shape. Somewhere in the mist, the winged presence lowers the ceiling. Beneath everything, the old system revises its calculation and makes room for one more living fact.
