They live there by taking turns with the heat.

The workers come down in small shifts, carrying no sense that the underground quarter belongs to them entirely. They wear light industrial clothing darkened at the cuffs, rubber boots filmed with red mud, and lamps clipped to their helmets despite the sodium lights. Their faces are shiny with sweat before they reach the machinery. They move carefully, not because the work is delicate, but because everything around them is half-finished: a walkway may end in a drop, a pipe may discharge without warning, a wall may have changed since the last inspection.

They have learned to listen to the ground.

A change in the pumps is noticed before the gauges register it. A new tremor in the support pillars travels through the soles of their boots. They distinguish the ordinary dripping from the sound of water finding a dangerous route. They know which stretches of catwalk are slick with bat waste, which drainage grates conceal eels, which warm vents gather crickets so densely that the metal beneath them seems furred.

No one stays alone for long. Even in the control office, where the workers sit before monitors and switchboards, there is always someone else nearby: a mechanic sleeping upright in a chair, a surveyor sorting damp maps, a nurse rinsing mud from a pair of gloves. Their conversations are practical and unfinished. Which pump is losing pressure. Whether the eastern cut needs another brace. How many days before the loading yard floods again. Someone has found larvae in the drinking-water intake. Someone else has seen a bat colony move into the unfinished shaft.

They do not speak of the animals as an invasion. The animals are part of the quarter’s accounting.

A jammed pump may mean a salamander has entered the intake. A sudden silence in the cricket colonies can mean an exhaust duct is leaking something toxic. Bats are watched for changes in their roosting patterns, because they abandon unstable ceilings before instruments detect the movement. The workers leave small strips of mesh along the walls for the amphibians to climb, then complain when the same amphibians vanish into the motor housings. They seal holes against water and reopen them for air. They trap the eels and release them farther downstream. Their attempts at exclusion become, over time, a rough architecture of accommodation.

The crickets are the most visible residents. They inhabit every warm margin: beneath generator housings, inside cable trays, around the vents of the canteen. They are long-limbed and pallid, their bodies nearly transparent where the lamps catch them. Their antennae feel constantly through the damp air. A passing worker causes thousands of them to withdraw at once, folding into cracks and seams; then, when the footsteps recede, the population returns with the quiet inevitability of seepage.

They are not fed, but neither are they starved. Grease, mold, paper pulp, dead insects, mineral films—there is enough. The workers sweep them away from control panels and leave them everywhere else. In the warehouse, the crickets gather under the empty shelving, where their movement produces a faint dry rustle like rain falling on leaves.

The bats keep to the heights. Their colonies are dense enough to seem like dark growths on the rock. Some are small and brown, others broad-winged and almost black, with thin, membranous faces that turn toward every engine noise. They sleep through much of the quarter’s activity. When they wake, they do so by degrees. A ripple passes through the hanging mass. Wings open. Bodies detach. For a few minutes the machinery hall fills with them, and their flight makes the mist churn.

The workers have stopped ducking. They simply wait for the migration to pass.

Below, the water has its own population. Blind fish nose through the channels, pale as peeled roots. Eels coil in the shadows beneath foundation slabs. Long-legged amphibians cling to the wet sides of culverts, their toes spread against the concrete, their eyes filmed and useless. Some have taken up residence in the drainage trenches and grown unusually large on the steady supply of larvae. Others live in the warm runnel beneath the canteen, where scraps of food and wash water enter the system.

They are caught in screens, sucked into pumps, crushed beneath boots, and born in thousands more. The quarter does not sustain them kindly; it sustains them continuously.

There are also the smaller lives no one names. Mites in the moss. Worms in the sediment traps. White insects inside the insulation. Thin films of bacteria spreading over bolts and pipe joints, turning them rainbow-colored beneath the mineral crust. Colonies grow inside the walls where the workers cannot see them. Every sealed cavity is eventually inhabited. Every warm surface becomes a possible nest, nursery, feeding ground, or resting place.

The plants are sparse but persistent. Moss thickens around the lamp bases, where condensation collects and the heat is tolerable. It advances in soft yellow-green islands over the rock, translucent at the edges. The waxy reeds in the drainage ditch bend under the weight of droplets and recover slowly. Workers pull them out when they obstruct the channels; within months, new blades appear from the mud. Their roots follow the water more faithfully than any survey line.

The quarter’s human inhabitants cultivate small comforts in spite of this occupation. Someone has attached a row of plastic cups to the wall outside the workshop, each filled from a different vein of groundwater and marked with a date. Someone keeps a radio playing in the canteen, though the signal is mostly static. In the clinic, damp towels hang beside the sterilizer, and a potted plant—brought down from the surface as a joke or a promise—has become pale and leggy under the lamps.

They decorate the machinery with scraps. A strip of red fabric tied to a valve. A child’s drawing taped above the pump controls. A dead bat placed carefully on a shelf until someone can remove it. A cluster of polished stones collected from the newest excavation. The objects are not sentimental for long; the damp gets into them, rusts the clips, blurs the ink. Still, more appear.

There are children, though few. They live in the prefabricated rooms near the clinic, where the walls sweat and the windows look permanently fogged. They know the quarter as a system of forbidden places. They are not to cross the loading yard alone, not to touch the yellow pipes, not to approach the open shafts, not to lean over the drainage grates. They know, nevertheless, how to move through it. They can identify a safe wall by the clean dust beneath it. They leave crumbs under the catwalks and watch the crickets gather. They know the names of the machines before they know the names of many surface animals.

To them, the bats are not a disturbance but weather. The pumps are not equipment but something with a sleep cycle. A new tunnel is discussed as though it were a room being added to a house.

Older workers carry a different understanding. They remember when the quarter was only mud, cable, and temporary lights. They remember the first animals arriving from cracks in the stone, the first bridge collapsing, the first time the pumps stopped and the water rose across the floor. They distrust any area described as finished. A finished area is merely one that has not yet failed in a visible way.

They mark safe routes in chalk, though the marks wash away. They wedge boards beneath sagging doors. They inspect the rock with the attention of people listening to a sleeping animal. Their knowledge is not written so much as repeated: do not build over dry ground; do not trust a clean wall; leave room around every pipe; never mistake stillness for emptiness.

At the far edge, where the warehouse gives way to the steaming basin, the survey crews live most closely with uncertainty. They carry instruments into the dark and return with mud to their knees. Their lamps pick out fresh shelves of stone, new cracks, pale mineral fans, and tracks left by animals no one has yet identified. Sometimes they find a route suitable for a road or a shaft. Sometimes they find only water, heat, and the unmistakable signs that something has already moved through before them.

They place stakes. The ground swallows some. They replace them.

Nothing in the quarter remains merely an object. The pipes gather mineral coats. The rails become channels for runoff. The abandoned machinery fills with nests. The cracks widen into passages. The walls collect moss, insects, condensation, and the touch marks of workers. Even the warning lights become habitats for small flying things that circle them until they fall dead into the housings.

The settlement is inhabited not only by those who work there, but by everything that has learned to use what the work leaves behind.

The humans pump and brace, drain and pour, cut and reinforce. The animals occupy the intervals between those actions. Water passes through both. Heat rises around both. Rust, sediment, waste, eggs, spores, and dust accumulate without preference.

Together they make a place that is never still, though it is rarely hurried: workers moving from lamp to lamp, crickets folding away from their boots, bats loosening in the dark above, fish passing beneath the grates, roots entering the mud, and the rock itself slowly giving up one shape in order to become another.
