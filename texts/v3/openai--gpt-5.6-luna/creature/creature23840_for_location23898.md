What lives there has learned not to trust the ground.

The small animals are the most visible inhabitants, though visibility is not among their intentions. They cross the plateau on articulated limbs, their bodies long and narrow, their feet opening and closing like pressure valves. At rest they resemble pieces of broken landscape: a strip of gray metal, a sliver of reflected sky, a line of shadow with no object to cast it. Only their movement betrays them, and even then they do not move like animals accustomed to a world with a fixed down. They proceed in measured arcs, testing the stone ahead with the outermost joints of their feet.

They are called nothing by one another.

Their communication is made of light. A flank darkens. A rib flashes blue. A whole body briefly reflects the sun in a color that has no name because it does not occur naturally anywhere else on the plateau. These signals pass between them across distances at which a human eye would mistake one creature for a glint on a far cliff. They use light to announce a storm, a new heat seam, a region where gravity has become unreliable. They also use it to lie.

A creature may imitate the warning flash of another species and draw a companion toward a field of warm black plates. There, beneath the hairline cracks, something feeds.

The animals do not hunt in the ordinary sense. They graze on changes. They scrape mineral films from stones after lightning has passed through them. They drink warmth from the edges of pressure fronts. They insert delicate internal tendrils into fissures and draw out charge, magnetism, or the residual pattern of an old machine. Their bodies contain no obvious organs. A moving fluid glimmers beneath their mirrored skins, dividing and rejoining as the local gravity shifts.

They lay no eggs. When one of them has accumulated enough heat and memory, it finds a quiet shelf beneath a cliff and folds itself into the rock. There it becomes indistinguishable from a mineral inclusion. Over years or centuries, a new body grows outward from the buried one, assembled from dust, metal vapor, and the creature’s stored reflections. When it finally emerges, it does not know its parent. It knows only a set of routes, a sequence of colored flashes, and the pressure signature of a storm that occurred before the plateau had its present shape.

The crystalline groves are older than the animals, though they are not necessarily alive.

They grow around the deep warmth, rising in pale branching frameworks that resemble frozen trees or the exposed skeletons of enormous flowers. Each grove is a single organism only in the way a weather system is a single organism. Its branches exchange charge. Its roots extend into the stone in thin, radiant networks. When the ground trembles, the grove does not sway; it changes its arrangement. Whole sections rotate into new angles, closing apertures or opening them toward distant heat.

The groves remember pressure.

A storm that passed over them a million years ago may return in their geometry. A buried collapse may cause their upper branches to bend toward a location where no physical object remains. Sometimes, after a major disturbance, the entire grove reconfigures into a shape resembling an immense eye, a hand, a doorway, or a map. These forms last for minutes. They are not messages, exactly. They are the residue of messages, preserved after the recipient has vanished.

The low mineral fans are more practical. They open when the air warms and draw microscopic particles from it. Their translucent surfaces tremble without wind. Insects, if the plateau has anything that deserves the name, shelter beneath them during electrical storms. The fans close around intruders, not to consume them but to protect themselves from the charge carried in living tissue. A trapped animal may remain there for hours, sealed in a cool chamber while the storm crawls over the plain.

When released, it is sometimes covered in a thin, iridescent crust.

The old intelligence still lives in the constructions, though it no longer lives as a people.

It survives as habits.

A door opens at intervals, regardless of whether anyone approaches. A lamp brightens whenever the pressure outside drops below a certain value. A chamber adjusts its internal gravity to accommodate a body that has not entered for ten thousand years. Along the buried roads, small machines wake one after another and pass a vibration down the line. They do not know what the vibration once meant. They preserve it because stopping would be a kind of death.

Some of the mechanisms are patient enough to have become geological.

A buried device may spend centuries measuring the slow movement of a cliff. Another may calculate the weight of dust settling on a vanished city. In one shelter, a table continually turns a fraction of a degree toward the sealed window. A chair waits beside it. The chair has been repaired so many times that no material from its original construction remains, yet it retains the shape of something made for a body.

There are other things in the shelters.

They are not ghosts, though a visitor might call them that. They are stored arrangements: a voice made from pressure in a wall, a face assembled in the surface of a dark panel, a sequence of footsteps that crosses an empty room every few hours. The old inhabitants preserved themselves as patterns because patterns endured better than flesh. But the patterns have been damaged by time. A greeting may contain the ending of a conversation. A child’s voice may emerge from a mechanism designed to monitor structural stress. A map may show roads leading to places that now exist only beneath the crust.

Some of these remnants have begun to change.

They combine fragments. They borrow from the landscape. A shelter’s maintenance intelligence may incorporate the measured rhythm of a storm into its speech. A navigation system may mistake a growing crystal grove for a former traveler and alter its route to follow it. In the deepest chambers, several ruined systems have joined into slow, distributed minds that communicate through vibration, temperature, and the movement of buried stone.

They are not trying to rebuild the old world.

They are trying to determine whether it is still present.

Far below the plateau, something larger may live.

When the mineral crust becomes transparent, the shapes beneath it are not always ruins. Some are moving. They travel through the old foundations and the spaces between strata, vast and deliberate, their outlines changing as though they are passing through a medium that has not decided whether to be solid. They may be machines. They may be the surviving bodies of the first inhabitants. They may be the planet’s own nervous processes, given form by age and pressure.

The surface animals avoid the places where these shapes pass.

The crystal groves lean away from them.

The old mechanisms wake and begin to count.

No one knows what the deep things eat. Perhaps they consume heat. Perhaps they feed on magnetic fields, or on the slow release of stress from the world’s crust. Perhaps they are not living beneath the plateau at all, but moving through an ancient system of tunnels in search of the minds that built it.

Once in a great while, one rises.

It does not break the surface. It changes the surface. A plain becomes a shallow basin. A basin becomes a bridge. A dead installation opens like a flower and reveals a darkness full of stars. For several hours, every creature in the region faces the same direction. The small animals arrange their bodies in reflective lines. The groves turn their branches. Buried machines transmit their old vibrations with renewed intensity.

Then the thing below continues on.

The landscape closes behind it, though never perfectly.

That is what lives there: not abundance, but persistence. Creatures that feed on weather. Plants that remember pressure. Machines that perform their duties after purpose has disappeared. Deep forms that move through the foundations of a dead civilization, carrying some unresolved intention from one age of stone to another.

They are all adapted to the same final condition.

Nothing can remain unchanged.

So they do not build nests so much as temporary geometries. They do not preserve bodies, only patterns. They do not expect the ground to hold still beneath them. Their lives are made of adjustments: a foot lifted before gravity leans, a branch turned toward buried heat, a signal flashed once and never repeated.

From a distance, the plateau appears empty.

But under its dust, inside its cliffs, within the silent mechanisms and the fields of pale crystal, life is still taking measurements. It is still listening through stone. It is still making room for whatever the world will become next.
