Nothing with legs lives on the plateau.

Nothing with eyes, either, unless an eye can be a silvered leaf turned toward a pressure wave, or a crystal face that changes color when a buried seam begins to glow.

The inhabitants are distributed through the stone.

They have no bodies in the ordinary sense. A green colony is one of their surfaces. A pressure column is one of their lungs. The slow orange movement beneath a ridge is circulation. The standardized crystals emerging from the dust are not products in the way a machine makes products; they are hard thoughts, formed where the plateau has finished considering a possibility.

There is no single creature that can be pointed to and named. A life may occupy a basin, a mineral seam, three isolated growths, and a chain of crystals separated by several kilometers of bare ground. It may take years to become aware of itself, and centuries to change its mind.

The plants are its sensory organs.

Their translucent fans register shifts in the air too small to be called wind. Their filament mats taste the electrical gradients in the rock. Their silver undersides detect the difference between sunlight reflected from basalt and sunlight reflected from newly formed metal. When one colony contracts, the click passes through the soil. Other colonies receive it, not as sound but as a disturbance in the conditions from which they grow.

They do not speak. They alter themselves.

A fan opens three degrees farther. A filament thickens along one edge. A plate turns its reflective underside toward a distant seam. These changes modify the local pressure, charge, and mineral exchange. The next colony responds. Slowly, across the plateau, a pattern travels.

What passes between them is not information in the narrow sense. It is inclination: continue, pause, gather, divide, release. Their messages are instructions written into matter and then erased by the next instruction.

The oldest inhabitants are perhaps the mineral intelligences.

They begin as crystals produced by the geology in its regular, thoughtless way: a hexagonal rod, a nested plate, a hollow spindle with an amber core. Most remain inert. They accumulate in the dust or stand half-emerged from the stone, their surfaces darkening under the sun.

Some, under conditions that occur only rarely, grow a second structure within themselves.

A filament of altered material crosses the crystal’s interior. Then another intersects it. The core becomes capable of holding a gradient rather than merely conducting one. A pulse from a pressure organ arrives; the crystal does not simply warm or resonate. It retains a trace of the pulse after the vibration has passed.

This is the beginning of memory.

The crystal does not remember an event as an image. It remembers a changed arrangement of itself: a boundary shifted inward, a metallic film halted at a new depth, a color held beneath the surface. Subsequent pulses encounter that alteration and are redirected. In time, the crystal becomes less a thing than a history of responses.

Such crystals are gathered by no hand. They are not carried, but the geology builds around them. Mineral films creep toward their faces and stop at exact distances. New rods rise nearby in recurring geometries. Growths alter their reproductive cycles when the crystals are active, opening their luminous organs at hours when the sun has not yet reached them.

A crystal mind may last for ten thousand local days. It knows the plateau by changes in pressure, conductivity, temperature, and shape. It does not know the sky as sky. It knows illumination as a broad warming field, darkness as a reduction in one kind of energy, and the horizon as the point beyond which its gradients become too weak to matter.

It has no need to move.

The landscape comes to it.

A seam brightens beneath the eastern shelves. A distant column pulses. A new mineral begins forming in a basin. These events arrive as slow distortions in the network of stone. The crystal receives them, compares them with older distortions, and encourages or inhibits the geology around it.

Sometimes it causes a plate to settle.

Sometimes it diverts a metallic film around a growing plant.

Sometimes it keeps a pressure organ dormant for several cycles, allowing gas to accumulate beneath a sealed ridge until the entire ridge glows from within.

From a distance, these decisions are indistinguishable from natural processes. That is their preferred form of expression. The plateau does not build machines because it has no reason to separate mechanism from landscape. A machine is simply a region of matter persuaded to behave consistently.

The green colonies are more temporary and more numerous. They are born from luminous spores that rise and fall in place, though “born” suggests an event where there is only gradual occupation. A spore settles onto a suitable mineral field. It unfolds a reflective underside, anchors itself to the dust, and begins drawing charge from the stone.

Over centuries, it becomes a colony.

Over further centuries, the colony may become a message.

Its reproductive organs open in patterns that correspond to conditions far beyond its own roots. A ring of emerald structures opens together when gas pressure is rising beneath the western shelf. A solitary fan closes at midday when a distant crystal has entered a dormant phase. In certain basins, dozens of colonies produce colors in sequence, blue through green through a deep metallic violet, and the sequence travels across the plain like a sentence too slow for any animal mind.

There are no pollinators. The plants do not need them. Their spores are not seeds seeking territory so much as particles released into a carefully prepared conversation. They settle near mineral seams, in the shadow of old crystals, or on plates where the geology has made room for another answer.

Their abundance is therefore not population. It is punctuation.

A single broad colony may be an exclamation, a warning, a question repeated for a thousand years. A circle of small growths may mark a place where something important happened, though nothing visible happened there at all. The bare distances between them are not empty to the plateau. They are pauses.

There are also the forms that have not yet emerged.

The pressure organs are producing them.

Each pulse through the black columns changes the chemistry of the surrounding shelves. New crystal geometries appear. Some are hollow, some layered, some threaded with metallic cores. They are imperfect at first, and most dissolve back into dust or are sealed inside the rock. But occasionally one develops the capacity to couple several systems at once: plant contraction, gas pressure, mineral deposition, and subterranean heat.

These are the beginnings of bodies.

Not bodies that will walk. Bodies that will coordinate.

A future inhabitant may be a ridge with a nervous system of crystal filaments, a field of green plates, and a hundred pressure organs communicating through low vibrations. It may take a century to turn toward the sun and another century to complete the turn. It may cultivate a new terrace not for shelter but for memory, arranging its surfaces so that each layer preserves a different chemical state.

The plateau is already manufacturing such beings, though it does not yet call them anything.

The first stable inhabitants are not individuals. They are tendencies toward individuality.

They gather where the geology is most regular. They persist where gradients can be maintained. They make themselves recognizable by repeating a response: opening at a certain pulse, halting a film at a certain boundary, causing a particular crystal to form instead of another.

If a mind is a pattern that survives its material, then the plateau is full of minds.

Some occupy only a patch of dust and vanish when the sun changes. Some are distributed across the whole high interior, too slow to distinguish from climate. Some are sleeping inside amber cores, waiting for a pressure sequence that may not occur for centuries.

None of them hunts. None flees. None decays, because decay is not yet a necessary part of their existence. They are not alive against the landscape. They are alive as the landscape’s arrangements.

When the low hum passes beneath a basin, the plants click in response. A distant ridge settles. Fresh crystals lift their faces from the dust. Beneath them, something records the change.

Then the plateau grows quiet again.

Not vacant. Listening.
