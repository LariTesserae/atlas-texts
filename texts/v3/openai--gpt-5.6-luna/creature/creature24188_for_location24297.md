They are not a people in the ordinary sense, though they have bodies when the basin requires bodies.

At a distance they look like the figures who stand among the reservoirs: narrow-shouldered, plainly dressed, carrying pruning knives, reed baskets, wooden rakes, and bowls. Their garments are woven from pale fiber and always damp at the hems. Their faces are difficult to remember. One sees a broad brow, a patient mouth, the suggestion of eyes; later, when trying to describe them, one recalls instead the face of a doorway, or a smooth stone under water.

They are called, when they are called at all, the Keepers.

No one has ever seen a Keeper arrive. They are simply present at the hour when a roof must be swept, a channel cleared, a tree trained along its frame. They appear beside a neglected wall with a coil of rope over one arm, or in a garden where a branch has grown three leaves beyond its assigned boundary. They work slowly and without hesitation. A Keeper will spend an entire morning adjusting one reed by the width of a finger. It will stand again by evening, identical to all the others.

Their tools are not made for cutting or fastening so much as reminding. A rake tells the paving where its dust belongs. A cord tells a vine how far it may travel. A knife laid against a fallen branch persuades the branch to become unfallen. The Keepers rarely touch anything directly. They put the proper object near the improper one and wait. The basin does the rest.

They do not eat, though they arrange food. They do not sleep, though they sometimes lie beneath the trees with their eyes open. They do not seem to breathe. In the wet air around them, there is no movement of cloth, no rise and fall beneath the chest. Yet when one of them leans close, it smells faintly of warm bread, river clay, and the green interior of a cut stem.

There may once have been many kinds of inhabitants. The old doorways and thresholds suggest bodies of different sizes. Some are narrow enough for children; others are wide and low, built for creatures that moved on all fours or carried burdens between their teeth. In the gardens, one finds carved figures with missing heads, and in the fruit there are miniature keys, beads, buttons, teeth. The basin remembers its makers in fragments.

The present Keepers are not their descendants. They are what remained of their obligations.

A Keeper can be recognized by its attention. It watches a wall as though the wall might speak. It pauses beside an open doorway and waits for the room beyond to settle into the correct arrangement. It knows which cracks must remain cracks, which must be filled with moss, which must descend into the lower basin. It can look across miles of tiled ground and notice a single misplaced seed.

When spoken to, a Keeper listens.

The words do not travel far in the basin, but the Keeper receives them fully. Its head inclines. Sometimes it answers by pointing toward an entrance. Sometimes it takes the speaker’s hand and places it against a wall, a tree, or the surface of the reservoir. Rarely, it speaks itself. Its voice is quiet and near, regardless of distance, and seems to come from the clay beneath the feet.

It does not answer questions about the builders.

It does not answer questions about escape.

If asked who maintains the basin, it may look puzzled. Maintenance is not, to a Keeper, an activity performed by someone. It is a condition of things. The channels carry water because they carry water. The trees flower because their roots are being fed. The doors remain open because they remember opening. The Keeper is only the visible portion of this arrangement, the part that has learned to walk.

There are also less visible inhabitants.

Footprints belong to one of them. They appear in the morning along the channel banks: heel, toe, heel, toe, each impression equally deep, sometimes turning sharply where no body could have turned. The footprints are not tracks left by a person. They are the basin’s way of marking that attention has passed there.

The unseen sweepers live in the roofs and beneath the paving. Their labor produces the hollow knocks heard at intervals below. They may be mechanisms, buried animals, or the hands of the dead moving through the dark. Occasionally a small wooden finger emerges from a seam and withdraws again. Occasionally an entire arm, pale with clay dust, reaches out to retrieve a fallen tool. No one attempts to stop it.

In the lower chambers, the roots have their own inhabitants.

These are pale, root-thin things that live among the pipes. They have no faces and no mouths, only clusters of delicate sensory threads. They keep the water moving at its appointed speed. When a pipe is blocked, they gather around it until the obstruction becomes porous. When a root begins to thirst, they divert a little warmth from another chamber. They do not compete, because competition has no meaning below the paving. Each knows the total system as a single body, and each part of that body is permitted to survive only insofar as the whole remains balanced.

The trees sometimes grow around them.

A cut root may flower with a tiny white hand at its tip. A vine pulled from masonry will continue to curl in the shape of a person’s spine. In the composting rooms, dead leaves are carried away by small, blind insects that wear fragments of writing on their backs. Their markings change whenever someone reads them. They may be names, instructions, or the memory of a name.

The animals are more alive than the Keepers, but less free.

They possess hunger without urgency, fear without flight, mating without desire. A porcelain-faced bird will stand for years at the edge of a channel, watching a fish it has been permitted to eat, and will not move until the water gives permission. The amphibians carry their ponds because they are required to carry them. The goats graze only the grasses set aside for goats. If a goat is led beyond its region, it becomes translucent, then returns to the post where it began.

The winged leaf-things are perhaps the closest inhabitants have come to birds. They fold themselves during the night in sheltered corners, their stick joints tucked beneath their skin. At dawn they unfold, damp and trembling, and rise without sound. They do not sing. Instead, they gather above the reservoirs in shifting formations that resemble letters no living person can read. When they fall, they become bundles of leaves again. By morning, they are standing.

The basin’s most difficult inhabitants are the visitors.

Some arrive from elsewhere. They come through a doorway, across a reservoir, or up the stair that descends into water. They are usually wet. They carry clothing, tools, weapons, photographs, or names. For a while, they remain recognizably themselves. They ask where they are. They search the horizon. They try doors, climb towers, mark walls, and count the steps around the central reservoir.

The basin is patient with them.

It offers food. It repairs what they break. It gives them rooms with dry floors and beds woven from reeds. If they call out, a Keeper comes and points toward the next entrance.

Most visitors eventually take up a task. One begins clearing channels. Another learns to tie the ropes. A third plants seeds in pots and becomes distressed when a root enters the wrong chamber. They do not remember choosing these duties. They merely discover, one morning, that their hands know what to do.

Some resist for years. They sleep in the open, refuse the food, and avoid the figures in the sunlight. They become thin but never weak. Their hair stops growing. Their footprints begin appearing before they walk. Eventually they notice that a wall has collapsed, or a tree has flowered in the dark, and they begin helping without admitting that they have begun.

The most successful visitors become almost indistinguishable from the Keepers. Their faces lose detail. Their voices cease to carry. They develop an instinct for boundaries. They know which shadows are safe to enter and which reservoirs must not be touched after the white-gold sun reaches its highest point.

The less successful visitors become places.

A person who spends too long beneath the reservoir may return as a doorway. A person who insists on remembering every route may become a staircase with no destination. One who cannot give up a particular grief may be found as a dark patch beneath a wall, shaped like a kneeling figure. These are not deaths. The basin has no clear category for death. They are incorporations—lives converted into functions.

Somewhere, a former visitor may be the warm water in a channel, the handprint in a brick, the green branch carried from the doorway below. The basin does not preserve people by keeping them intact. It preserves them by giving their persistence a use.

At dusk—which is not a change in the sun, but a change in the inhabitants—the Keepers gather at the reservoir steps. They bring tools, baskets, cuttings, and small objects found in the gardens. They stand in their separate places, each facing a different direction. No one speaks. The water remains still.

Then, one by one, they place their objects on the steps.

The objects are never the same: a tooth, a seed, a key, a shard of glazed brick, a child’s wooden cup, a scrap of woven cloth. The basin accepts them. Some sink. Some rise. Some remain suspended halfway between the steps and the surface.

For a brief interval, the Keepers’ faces become clear.

They are old faces. They are young faces. They are faces seen through water, through mist, through a doorway before entering. They look toward the horizon as if expecting someone to return, though they have been waiting longer than the basin has had a name.

Then the expression passes.

The figures turn back toward their assigned regions. Invisible hands begin sweeping the roofs. The roots continue their work below. The animals return to their boundaries. Above the paving, footprints appear where no one has walked.

And through the hot, wet silence, the inhabitants maintain the world—not because they remember who made it, but because the world has taught them that being alive and being tended are the same thing.
