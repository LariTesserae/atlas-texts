What lives there is not a single species, though the basin has given its inhabitants the habits of one.

They are called—if a name must be used—the **Gathered**.

No individual Gathered is large. Some are the narrow translucent bodies that lie along the wet stones, shedding color as they drift. Some are the black birds, each eye attending to a different part of the world. Some are the six-jointed grazers that fold themselves into shapes when threatened. The pale ribbons in the pond belong to them as well, and the motes that erase insects, and the many-elbowed thing that waits in the trees. Even the luminous root tips and the mineral beads rising through the hills are involved.

The Gathered are not a colony in the usual sense. They do not share a nest, a queen, or a visible language. Their unity is slower and less personal. They share conditions. They share water, pressure, heat, and recollection. A change in one body travels through the basin as surely as a ripple, though it may take a season to arrive.

The translucent creatures are the youngest and most numerous expression of them. They hatch from the mud inside thin, colorless shells, already able to sense one another through warmth. At first they remain near the place of their emergence, feeding on films of mineral and the residue of older bodies. When the sun strikes them, they lift from the ground—not by wings, but by altering the density of the water within themselves—and drift in slow, coordinated currents.

They do not travel to find food.

They travel to become a pattern.

Where one settles, others gradually gather. A ring forms around a warm stone. A line collects along a hidden watercourse. A loose cloud hangs beneath a tree whose roots have begun to glow. These arrangements last for hours, years, or entire geological moods. The creatures do not seem to know why they assemble. Yet when they have completed some invisible figure, the hills often answer with their deep descending tone.

During the tone, the Gathered become still.

The birds close their wings. The grazers fold inward. The ribbons in the pond suspend themselves among the stems. The translucent bodies descend onto the stones and flatten until they resemble skins discarded by something larger. Beneath the basin, pressure moves through the old mineral. The black-glass plate brightens with maps of vanished water and unborn meadows. The frame at the southern shore gathers the sound through its dark, jointless material.

Perhaps this is prayer.

Perhaps it is digestion.

The Gathered do not distinguish between the two.

They possess no chiefs, but they have elders: bodies that have survived so long they are difficult to separate from the basin itself. An elder may be mistaken for a patch of damp stone, a black root, or a fold in the pond’s shadow. It moves only when necessary. It remembers the former height of the hills, the first opening of the pond, the extinct colors of the sky. Its memory is not held in thought but in arrangement. It changes the route of a trickle by the width of a finger. It settles beneath a root. It permits a certain moss to grow and prevents another from taking hold.

The basin remembers through these small permissions.

The six-jointed grazers are the most visible of its larger creatures. They move in scattered herds, though no two stand close enough to touch. Their reflective coats make them difficult to follow. A grazer may appear to be a wet slab of stone until it turns its head, and sometimes the head is only a change in the reflected light.

They feed on red growth, but this is not their true purpose. As they cross the open spaces, they press the thin soil into patterns that guide moisture toward the pond. Their feet carry spores from one channel to another. Their bodies cast brief shadows in places where new plants will later rise. When they fold themselves into compact geometries, they are not merely hiding. They are waiting for the landscape to finish speaking through them.

The black birds are listeners.

Their two independent gazes allow them to attend to the immediate and the distant at once. One eye follows a mote above the reeds; the other watches the light changing on a far hill. They do not call to one another. Their communication is made of absences: a wing lifted, a stem released, a gap appearing in the brightness. When they all vanish at once, something is approaching. When they all become visible, something has passed.

No one knows what the many-elbowed predator looks like. This is not because it is especially well hidden. It is because its shape is distributed among the shadows it occupies. In one season it may have a long pale torso. In another, a cluster of jointed limbs. It feeds rarely and without anger. The creatures it takes are not mourned. Their matter returns to the pond, and the pond returns it elsewhere.

Death among the Gathered is not an ending but a change in grammar.

The old machines are part of them, though the living creatures do not treat them as sacred. They sleep inside the southern frame because its warmth is useful. They crawl over the black glass because its surface is dry. Yet sometimes a bird will stand before the frame for an entire day, watching the points of light where rain should have fallen. Sometimes the translucent young gather along the edge of the map and alter their colors in response to landscapes that no longer exist.

The machines may once have been built to study the Gathered.

After so much time, the difference has disappeared.

The inhabitants do not defend the basin. They do not need to. Anything that enters from the plain is gradually included. Its heat is recorded. Its tracks become channels. Its remains feed the reeds. If it stays long enough, the Gathered learn its shape and make room for it.

If it leaves, the basin continues without resentment.

But it does not forget.
