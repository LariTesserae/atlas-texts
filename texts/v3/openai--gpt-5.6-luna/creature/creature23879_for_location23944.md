What lives there is difficult to separate from what has grown there.

In the warmest valleys, beneath the mats and the curtains of hanging vegetation, there are bodies that have learned to become terrain. They are called nothing by themselves. They possess no language, no face, no fixed outline. A pale ridge beneath the green crust may be the back of one. A branching mineral seam may be its exposed skeleton. A forest of jointed stalks may be rooted through its tissues, drawing heat and dissolved metals from it as much as from the stone.

They begin small: a clear, many-limbed thing no larger than a hand, released from a vent or split from the underside of an older body. It has no eyes. Light means little to it except as warmth and direction. Along its translucent edges run organs sensitive to pressure, salinity, vibration, and the minute chemical differences between one crack and another. It settles where several gradients meet—warmth below, light above, moving water through the stone—and anchors itself.

Then it waits.

Its first roots are not roots but threads of living material that enter every available fissure. They spread through sediment, across mineral faces, and into the underground mats of vegetation. The creature feeds by filtering the water that passes through it. It takes almost nothing at once: a trace of iron, a dissolved protein, a drifting cell, the waste of a plant, the slow warmth of a vent. In return, it exudes compounds that cause minerals to crystallize around its threads. The rock thickens. The cracks become ridged. A shelter forms without any act that could be called building.

After a century, it may be the size of a fallen tree.

After a thousand years, it may occupy an entire ledge.

From a distance, one of these creatures resembles a low, pale mound under a forest. Its body is flattened against the ground, broad as a valley floor, with a central mass buried beneath layers of growth. The visible parts are only its sensory organs: slender fans rising among the plants, translucent folds that open and close with the pressure fronts, and long tendrils hanging over cliffs. When the water changes, the tendrils move first. The forests bend. The mineral crusts tighten. The creature feels the approaching disturbance through every part of itself.

It does not flee from falling stone. It receives the fall.

A boulder striking its upper crust may be absorbed over decades. A collapsing wall may break it into several living regions, each carrying on independently. The new fragments remain connected by filaments buried beneath the sediment, exchanging nutrients and electrical impulses so faintly that the connection appears to be only another branching pattern in the rock.

Sometimes, however, the whole body moves.

This happens only when a valley becomes too cold, too dark, or too poor in dissolved minerals. The change begins beneath the ground. Roots withdraw from some cracks and thicken in others. The pale ridges along the creature’s back soften. Forests leaning over its body are not uprooted; they are carried. For years, perhaps for generations, the living mound creeps down the slope by a distance no greater than a person might walk in an afternoon.

Its passage leaves a shallow trough.

The plants close over it. Mineral dust settles into the marks. Before long, there is no sign that anything has moved except a new line of raised crust crossing the valley floor.

The oldest of them are almost indistinguishable from the country. Their bodies have mineralized into shelves and terraces. Vents pass through them. Their tissues occupy the roots of entire forests and the walls of hidden ravines. If one were to cut through such a region, there would be no clean boundary between creature, plant, and stone. Layers of living material would alternate with porous mineral, warm channels, old sediment, and chambers full of slow-moving fluid.

They do not think as a single animal thinks. There is no central awareness waiting behind the landscape. Sensation travels through them as weather travels through water: a pressure received here, a chemical change registered there, a response distributed over time. They remember injury as altered growth. They remember abundance as density. They remember the direction of light through the angle of their fronds.

Their young are released during long periods of stillness.

A mature body opens narrow pores along its warmest ridges and sends out thousands of nearly weightless membranes. These do not drift freely like spores. They cling to the water in translucent veils, held together by invisible threads. The veils may remain suspended for years, moving slowly through the submerged valleys. Most settle in places too cold, too bright, too barren, or too deep. They disappear into the sediment and become food for other growth.

A few find the right conjunction of heat and pressure.

Those become the small clear things that anchor beneath the green.

There are other inhabitants as well: the long ribbon-bodied swimmers that pass far above the ridges, following invisible currents between the upper forests and the blue descent. They are the mature, mobile phase of the same life. Their bodies are thin and enormously long, with no obvious front or rear. Along their length, membranes open and close in sequence, turning the pressure of the water into slow motion. They graze on suspended colonies and carry reproductive veils from one region to another.

When one passes overhead, the valley darkens.

The rooted creatures below register the shadow not as absence of light but as a brief cooling along their upper surfaces. Their fans close. Their fronds turn. A few bubbles detach from the hanging gardens and drift sideways instead of upward. Then the swimmer is gone, continuing through the green-gold water toward some distant country where the mountains descend beyond sight.

If it dies, it does not fall all at once.

Its body settles across a ridge or into a ravine, and the plants begin to enter it immediately. White filaments lace through the translucent tissues. Small transparent animals gather, not in a swarm but one by one. The mineral skeleton fractures under its own weight. Warm fluids escape from the interior and nourish the growth around it. Over centuries, the carcass becomes a terrace, then a garden, then an unnamed part of the mountain.

The world is full of such remains.

Some are the pale shelves beneath the forests. Some are the raised seams around the vents. Some are the broad, nearly level ledges where the vegetation grows thickest. The land has not merely been colonized by life. It has been incorporated into life so gradually that stone and organism have forgotten which of them arrived first.

And beneath the impossible ocean, where sunlight reaches the young ridges in softened gold, the living country continues to settle.

It senses the pressure.

It grows toward the warmth.

It waits for the next stone to fall.
