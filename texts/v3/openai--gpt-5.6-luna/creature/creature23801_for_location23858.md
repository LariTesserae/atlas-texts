What lives on the plateau has no single name for itself.

The nearest translation is **the Inhabiting**.

It is not a species, exactly, but a condition shared by the citizens, the cultivated plants, the machines beneath the ground, and the ancient systems that still remember how to maintain them. To live there is to become partly rooted. Every person carries a colony of engineered organisms through the body: mineral threads along the bones, pressure-sensitive membranes beneath the skin, small organs that store charge and exchange information with the surrounding growth. A citizen without those companions would be deaf to the plateau’s deeper speech and dangerously vulnerable to its weather.

They are tall, narrow beings, built for the dry cold. Their limbs are jointed with dark plates, and their skin has the matte, fibrous appearance of old leaves. No two bodies are quite alike. Some grow fanlike structures from the shoulders to harvest electrical gradients. Others bear transparent abdominal chambers where luminous symbionts drift in slow suspension. Many have no visible eyes. They perceive through pressure, vibration, mineral taste, and changes in the electric field around living things.

When they meet, they do not always speak.

A greeting may be a change in posture, a pulse sent through the rootwood beneath their feet, or the release of a faint chemical signal from the throat. Conversation can pass from body to body through cultivated streets. A citizen kneels, places one hand against the ground, and listens as the nearby plants carry a reply through their buried tissues. The exchange may include words, but words are only the sharpest and least reliable part of it. Memory, mood, location, and intention move together.

Their dwellings are grown around them.

A family does not occupy a house so much as inhabit a chamber in a larger organism. Walls thicken when danger approaches. Doorways open in response to familiar body chemistry. Sleeping rooms lower themselves beneath the root crust, where the temperature is stable and the deep vibrations are strongest. The dead are not buried. Their mineral skeletons are dissolved into nursery beds, their memories copied into the growth rings of civic trees, their remaining tissues fed to the organisms that once accompanied them.

Death is therefore a change in jurisdiction.

The individual ends, but some portion of them enters the local ecology. A voice may survive as a pattern in a root network. A citizen’s sensory habits may be incorporated into a weather engine. Their descendants may carry fragments of their metabolism, their reflexes, even their dreams. The people of the plateau do not speak of ancestors as watching from elsewhere. Ancestors are present in the roads that harden beneath the feet, in the buildings that close their windows against a pressure front, in the groves that remember old agreements.

Children begin as sealed nodules cultivated in communal chambers. For years they remain soft-bodied and nearly immobile, listening to the settlement through the walls. They learn the plateau before learning language. They know which root-fields are alarmed, which towers are hungry, which distant animals have entered a breeding season. When they are old enough to walk, the city opens a path for them and lets them choose where their first permanent symbiosis will grow.

No one chooses alone.

The city alters the choice. The plants respond. Families advise, machines measure, and the child’s own body signals what it can bear. One may be suited to the high fans and become a weather listener. Another may join the maintenance groves, spending decades inside the root systems repairing chemical memories and redirecting nutrients. Some travel between settlements in the tendon capsules. Some become interpreters of the ancient trees. A few leave the cities entirely and join the herds, living among the mineral grazers until their bodies grow plated and their speech becomes mostly vibrational.

The plateau does not consider this disappearance.

It considers it specialization.

There are also beings who were never born into the cities. The oldest of these move slowly through the plant colonies, large enough to be mistaken for mobile architecture. They possess no faces, only arrays of sensory pits and opening vents. Their bodies are layered from bark, ceramic, and living tissue. They tend the great fan-trees, clear blocked root channels, and awaken dormant groves. A city may wait centuries for one of them to arrive, yet when it does, every door opens.

The inhabitants call these beings **the Elders**, though the name suggests a personhood they may not possess. An Elder can remember a pressure front from ten thousand years ago. It can carry the chemical history of an entire watershed, though no watershed remains. It can distinguish between a city’s temporary illness and the first signs of extinction.

Sometimes an Elder stands outside a settlement for years without moving.

The people watch it. The plants lean toward it. Buried systems awaken beneath the streets, comparing old records with the visitor’s presence. Eventually the Elder will place one many-jointed limb against the ground, and the city will answer. A new road may grow. A tower may close forever. A forgotten chamber may open, containing tools or seeds or instructions for a crisis that has not yet arrived.

The citizens accept these interventions with something deeper than trust.

They have learned that the plateau is older than any decision made upon it. Its intelligence is distributed across roots, weather, animals, mineral shelves, and buried machines. No individual, council, or city can understand the whole. To survive, they do not attempt to rule the landscape. They negotiate with it.

This negotiation is their religion, their government, and their daily labor.

They prune the roads. They feed the root networks. They teach young plants which buildings are homes and which are organs. They accompany migrating herds away from unstable ground. During a bloom, they stand beneath the enormous vapor-filled flowers and allow the luminous chemistry to pass over their bodies, accepting whatever memories or mutations the plant chooses to release.

They are not afraid of being changed.

They are afraid of becoming unrecognized by the plateau.

That is the great danger: not death, but separation. A person who loses their symbionts, whose body can no longer hear the ground, is called **unplaced**. Such beings are cared for, but they live in sealed chambers and must rely on machines to interpret the world. To them, the plateau becomes mute scenery: the fans are only moving structures, the root-pumps only noise, the weather only pressure. They can still walk among the cities, but they are no longer fully inside them.

Most unplaced people eventually choose dissolution.

They enter a civic grove and let the plants consume the systems that once made them separate. Their final memories are recorded in mineral sequence. Their bodies become part of the ground. In this way even estrangement can be repaired, though not returned.

At night, the inhabitants emerge from their towers and gather on the living fields. Their bodies catch the buried glow. Around them, the plateau shines in scattered veins: blue beneath the root crust, amber in the sealed flowers, white within the lantern-moths drifting overhead. They stand among creatures, machines, and plants that may be listening to them, or remembering them, or slowly deciding what to make of them.

Then the great fans begin their turning.

The people fall silent.

Through the ground comes the old vibration, passing from colony to colony, beneath cities and terraces, beneath the mineral shelves and the sleeping herds. It is not a voice, but the inhabitants hear intention in it. The plateau is still alive. The plateau is still changing. The plateau has not yet finished deciding what its inhabitants are.
