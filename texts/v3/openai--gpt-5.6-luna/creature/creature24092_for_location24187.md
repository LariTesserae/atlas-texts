The inhabitants are people, mostly.

They have not become strangers to themselves so much as practical about the ways a body can change.

They live in layers, as the city does. The oldest families occupy rooms whose walls have been repaired so many times that no original surface remains. Newer arrivals sleep in converted pump stations, under railway arches, inside the hollow portions of unfinished towers. There are houses with doors at three different heights, because the floors have settled unevenly. In some neighborhoods, one must climb down into the kitchen and up again to reach the bed.

No one is surprised by this. A level floor is considered a temporary luxury.

The people of the basin are narrow-faced and heavily clothed, though the cold is not severe enough to explain the layers of fabric. They wear waxed coats, rubber aprons, knitted sleeves, insulated boots, and belts crowded with instruments. Every adult carries a measuring rod or chalk, a coil of cord, a folding respirator, and some means of marking a surface. Their clothing is patched not only where it has torn, but where the body beneath it has altered.

A sleeve may be lengthened to accommodate an arm that has grown slightly longer over the winter. Boots are fitted with adjustable soles. Gloves have extra fingers sewn into them, not because everyone has extra fingers, but because sometimes a person wakes with one.

These changes are seldom dramatic. A new joint in the thumb. A hard, warm patch beneath the shoulder. Skin that develops a faint mineral sheen. Teeth that continue growing until filed down. A second pulse felt in the throat for several weeks and then nowhere else. The people record such things in household ledgers alongside leaks, births, cracked beams, and the dates on which certain walls began to move.

They do not ask whether a change is natural.

They ask whether it is stable.

Children are taught early to distinguish between a dangerous growth and an ordinary one. A tooth emerging from the palm is concerning but manageable. A second shadow is watched carefully. A patch of moss on the back is generally harmless, provided it is kept damp and does not begin flowering indoors.

The city’s children are excellent climbers. They move through the industrial levels by routes invisible on official maps: along the backs of pipes, through ventilation shafts, over the roofs of moving railcars. They know which fences are merely fences and which are load-bearing. They can tell from the vibration of a wall whether a new room is forming behind it. They collect fragments of fresh stone, translucent insects, machine parts, and seeds that have not yet decided what kind of plant they are.

When asked where they live, they often point in several directions.

A child may sleep in a room above a bakery, eat at a family table three streets away, attend lessons in an old filtration plant, and spend most afternoons in a grove growing from the roof of a cooling tower. These arrangements are not considered unusual. The city’s population is too dense for anyone to belong to only one building.

There are no strict boundaries between occupations. A person may be a rail mechanic in the morning, a drainage surveyor in the afternoon, and part of a wall-stabilization crew by evening. Factory workers learn botany because plants enter their machinery. Gardeners learn metallurgy because roots lift paving stones and mineral deposits poison certain crops. Midwives know structural engineering. Architects know the habits of burrowing animals.

The most respected people are not rulers or owners but listeners.

They are called sleepers, though many remain awake during their work. They lie against foundations, place their palms on bridge supports, press their ears to tanks and retaining walls. They listen to the slow grinding beneath the pavement and interpret the pressure changes moving through the basin.

A sleeper can sometimes identify an approaching alteration days in advance. This wall will become hollow. That street will gain a slope. The eastern rail line will sink, but only after the rain. A new chamber is forming below the market and should not be opened until it has finished breathing.

They do not predict the future exactly. They negotiate with it.

The city’s engineers work beside them, carrying old diagrams and new measurements. Their maps have transparent overlays, detachable sections, and blank areas left deliberately blank. A building is not considered complete when it has been finished, but when it has survived long enough to receive a maintenance history.

Doors are built to be moved. Windows are mounted in sliding frames. Pipes are joined with soft couplings and long loops so they can accommodate a change in distance. Bridges have wheeled feet. Houses are sometimes tied to one another with cables, not for support but to ensure that if one shifts, the others will know.

People sleep lightly. They keep shoes beside the bed and leave doors unlatched.

The rule about humming ground is observed most strictly. When the vibration begins, conversations stop. Tools are gathered. Children are counted. Doors and windows are opened so that pressure can pass through the rooms without splintering them. In the quiet that follows, everyone waits to hear what the earth has chosen to become.

The inhabitants do not always look entirely human after generations in the basin.

Some have developed translucent membranes over the inner eyelids, useful in the bright vapor released from factories. Some can sense electrical current through the soles of their feet. Others grow thin, dark filaments in their hair that lift before rain. Among the oldest residents are people whose bones have become unusually dense, carrying traces of the local stone. They move slowly but are difficult to injure, and are often asked to stand in doorways while a building settles.

There are families with gills, though they call them folds. There are people who cannot cross freshly poured concrete without leaving impressions of leaves. There are people whose body temperature changes according to the nearest underground channel. The city accommodates all of them with the same matter-of-fact care it gives to grazing animals and damaged machinery.

No one is certain whether these traits are inherited, acquired, or invited.

The animals are not regarded as separate from the settlement, either.

The flat amphibious creatures in the drains have names in every district. They are useful indicators of water quality and are sometimes kept in ceramic basins beneath kitchen sinks. The large grazing animals are treated as slow-moving citizens. Their routes are marked on traffic boards. If one enters a factory, production pauses until it has found the exit or decided to remain.

Roof gardens are designed with their appetites in mind. Warm tunnels are left open for their winter passage. Young animals sometimes wear painted symbols to show which herd they belong to, though the paint generally flakes away within a season.

Birds are less trusted. Their habit of turning in unison toward an approaching person is considered rude but not especially alarming. They nest in transformer housings, listen from smokestacks, and occasionally carry objects from one district to another. A lost key, a child’s tooth, a piece of blue glass—these may appear overnight on a windowsill far from where they were left.

The people accept gifts without assuming they understand them.

Plants are more deeply involved in daily life.

Most households cultivate condensation plants in window boxes and hanging gutters. Their leaves collect enough water for washing, cooking, and sometimes drinking. Vines are trained along interior pipes to indicate temperature changes. A flowering stem near the ceiling means the room is too warm. A plant folding its leaves at noon suggests pressure below the foundation.

People prune plants carefully, but never completely. Every machine is allowed a portion of growth.

There are households in which a family and a colony of vines have lived together for so long that no one knows where one ends. The vines carry messages through changes in color and tension. A flowering pattern may announce a birth. Three leaves closing at once may warn of a broken valve. If the plants begin growing toward a particular wall, the residents usually make room.

Death, too, is provisional.

Bodies are not always buried. The ground may reject them, or incorporate them too quickly. Some are placed in warm soil beneath factories, where mineral-feeding plants grow thick and dark. Some are lowered into water channels, where the amphibious creatures gather and remain silent. The names of the dead are written on walls expected to move. When the walls crack, the names lengthen with them.

The people do not believe the city will last unchanged. They do not even believe it should.

Their faith, where they have one, is directed toward continuation: not preservation, but successful alteration. A good building is one that knows how to yield. A good machine is one that can admit a root. A good person is someone who notices what is changing before the change becomes an emergency.

At night, when the factories dim and the wet roofs reflect the few lamps that remain, the inhabitants move through the city carrying food, tools, seed trays, and sleeping children. They cross bridges that lean a little farther each month. They pass animals in the road and wait for them to finish. They place their hands on new stone to feel whether it is warm.

Beneath them, the ground shifts.

Around them, roots enter engines, water searches for another route, and buildings begin quietly acquiring rooms. The people continue to work, repair, plant, measure, and sleep.

They are not masters of the basin.

They are its current arrangement of attention: warm bodies, patient hands, adaptable bones, and thousands of small decisions made in response to whatever the world becomes next.
