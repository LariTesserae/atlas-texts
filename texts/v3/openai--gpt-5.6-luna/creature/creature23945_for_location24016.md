The beings who live there are difficult to distinguish from the systems they tend.

They do not build towns. A town would be an extravagance of proximity, and proximity is dangerous when every seep, cistern, and shaded seam already supports more life than the surrounding land can afford. Instead, they occupy the plateau in small, separated households: two or three families in an old pumping station, a watch group beneath a condenser array, a solitary keeper in a maintenance tower whose lower rooms have been sealed against the heat.

From a distance they resemble ordinary people moving carefully across the shelves of stone. Up close, the adaptations become clear.

Their skin is darkened and thickened by generations of sun and mineral dust. It has a dry, finely cracked appearance at the elbows and knuckles, but it does not split easily. The soles of their feet are callused almost to the texture of leather, and many go barefoot on stone that would blister an unadapted traveler. Their eyes are narrow against the light, with pale inner membranes that slide across them when they look toward the sun. At night those membranes give their eyes a cloudy, reflective cast. They have broad, flexible ears, not so much animal-like as simply more useful than human ears ought to be. A person can hear a pump begin beneath several layers of rock. They can locate a dripping pipe in darkness by the faint change it makes in the air.

They are lean, but not fragile. The body keeps little water in reserve. Sweat comes late and sparingly, and the blood runs thick with salts. They drink in measured amounts, never from a new source until it has been tested. Their hands are stained blue-green from handling copper, algae, and old pipe fittings. Around the eyes and at the throat, many carry a permanent discoloration from the mineral-rich water they consume.

They call themselves different names in different basins. The names matter less than the word shared between them, a word meaning something like *those who remain with the water*. Outsiders, when outsiders still came, called them the Cistern Folk, though the people themselves regarded the name as both clumsy and accurate.

They are not numerous. A hundred may live across a region large enough to swallow a city. Months can pass between encounters. A child may grow to adulthood without seeing more than a few members of another household, yet know their voices through the old radio channels, their footprints by the shape of the heel, and their family histories by the repairs they make to machines.

They travel when the plateau is cool.

Before dawn, or after the last heat has left the stone, a lone figure may emerge from a maintenance shed carrying a rolled mesh panel, a coil of wire, and a ceramic vessel padded against the body. Their clothing is layered and pale on the outside, dark underneath, with many pockets sealed against dust. They cover their heads during the day and uncover them at night. The oldest people wear masks over their mouths when crossing the open shelves, not to filter the air but to keep moisture from leaving the body too quickly.

They walk from one water source to another, following routes that are invisible to anyone else. A green stain on a cliff may indicate a hidden thread of seepage. A line of beetles beneath a slab may reveal warmth from a buried pipe. Birds gathering on a dead tower can mean that the condenser above it has begun to drip again. Their maps are not drawn on paper. They are memorized as relationships between sound, shadow, animal movement, and stone.

The plateau’s machinery is part of their inheritance, though they do not understand all of it.

Some pumps are older than their oldest stories. The people know which valves must be opened at dawn, which inspection tubes cloud before a failure, which engines are safe to approach and which have become inhabited by blind things that bite. They replace belts with woven fiber, seal cracks with resin, and patch corroded tanks with plates scavenged from buildings whose original purpose has been forgotten. A condenser array may contain parts from five different centuries and three different designs, all held together by practical knowledge no manual records.

They do not consider this maintenance in the human sense. The word they use means closer to *keeping a promise from becoming empty*.

A functioning pump belongs to the whole plateau, though it may stand beside one family’s shelter. Water is collected, tested, and portioned. The first drink from a new source is not taken by a person. It is offered to a small animal—usually a burrowing mammal or a lizard—because the animals are less tolerant of certain poisons. If it survives, the people wait another day. If it returns, they begin to use the water. No source is sealed away from life unless it carries a known sickness. Even then, channels are left for insects and birds.

Their children learn this before they learn numbers.

They are taught to read tracks, to feel condensation on the underside of metal, to tell clean water from mineral water by the smell of the cup. They learn which lichens can be eaten after soaking and which cause fever. They are given dead pumps to dismantle, then made to rebuild them without losing a washer. At night, elders point out the stars and name the basins beneath them, because navigation across the plateau depends as much on the sky as on the land.

The people speak softly. Their language has many words for water in motion and very few for ownership. A spring is not “mine”; it is “passing through my keeping.” A person who dies is described as having “gone into the channels.” Bodies are placed in dry shafts or beneath condenser walls, where mineral dust and condensation slowly cover them. Nothing is wasted. Bones may become markers, tools, or white fragments among the lichen. Hair is woven into filters. The dead continue, not as spirits exactly, but as material returned to the systems that sustained them.

They are wary of the cliff cats and respectful of the birds. They leave scraps for scavengers but never enough to make the animals dependent. They will drive a reptile from a cistern, then place a smaller basin nearby. Their oldest prohibitions concern imbalance: taking too much from a seep, blocking an animal route, burning a nesting roof, allowing a tank to overflow while another basin dries.

At night, their settlements show almost no light. A shaded lamp may glow behind a sealed window. A person may sit on a tower roof, listening to the buried pump and watching the stars thicken above the black shelves. Around them, insects rise from the cracks. Condensation gathers on the mesh. Somewhere below, water moves through stone.

They are not masters of the plateau, and they do not imagine themselves to be. They are one of its active layers: as temporary as the fog, as persistent as the mineral stains. They inherit the abandoned works, feed the animals that find them, repair what can be repaired, and leave openings for whatever comes after.

From far away, a traveler might see one of them crossing an immense terrace alone and mistake the figure for a piece of industrial debris blown loose from a tower.

Only when the figure stops—head lifted, broad ears turned toward a sound beneath the rock—does the plateau reveal that it is inhabited.
