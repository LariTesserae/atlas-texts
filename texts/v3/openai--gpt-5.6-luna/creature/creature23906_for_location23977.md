What lives there is not a population in the ordinary sense.

There are no bodies moving between the reservoirs, no faces at the windows, no sleeping places concealed beneath the paving. The makers are gone. What remains of them is distributed through the works: in the worn handles of the tools, in the exact width of the channels, in the habit of leaving a mallet beside a wall that will not need repair for another century.

But something has continued.

It begins as a nearly invisible pressure in the masonry. A hesitation in a drain. A film gathering along the wrong side of a basin. A seam that has widened by less than the thickness of a fingernail. The place notices these things—not with sight or thought, but with a change in its arrangements. Water pauses where it should descend. Moisture travels through the mortar toward the defect. Mineral gathers in the shape of a warning.

Then one of the keepers comes.

At first it resembles a piece of the construction that has been misplaced. A pale block rests beside a channel, too low and too narrow to be useful as a wall stone. It has no clear limbs. Its surfaces are squared, but not by cutting. They have grown into planes, each one faintly translucent, with a darkness within like water beneath ice.

It does not walk. It repositions itself.

A keeper may be present at the upper end of a stair and, after the light changes, be found at the bottom, though none of the steps show any mark of its passage. It moves through completed routes: behind the faces of walls, beneath the floors of cisterns, inside the hollow cores of pillars. Where the architecture has left no channel, it can remain still for years, absorbing condensation from the air until it has become indistinguishable from the stone around it.

Its shape is determined by what needs to be done. Near a blocked outlet, it becomes long and narrow, a wedge of damp mineral that inserts itself grain by grain into the narrowing mouth. Along a cracked retaining wall, it spreads flat, occupying the seam like a darkened layer of mortar. At a sluice, it may divide into several small forms, each fitting beneath a different hinge or counterweight.

The keepers do not carry tools. They use the tools that have been left for them.

A stone mallet rises from its niche without a hand upon it. A ceramic scoop turns over in a shallow pan. A length of mineral-stiffened fiber draws itself through a hole in a gate and tightens. The work is slow enough to be mistaken for settling. A keeper may strike a wall once, then wait through an entire winter before striking it again.

They are not intelligent in a way that would be comforting. They do not choose among possibilities. They do not wonder whether a channel should run east or west. They know only the intended relation between things: this basin must receive from that one; this wall must bear against that slope; this opening must remain open; this final tile must be present.

Completion is their nourishment, or perhaps their language.

When a keeper has corrected a defect, water begins to move. It may emerge from a dry joint, gather in a pan, or rise through a channel from somewhere far below. The keeper itself becomes less distinct as the route resumes. Its edges soften. Its substance passes into the damp stone, the pale film, the thin mineral crust around the outlet. For a time, a faint outline remains where it had been: a rectangular shadow, a hand-shaped depression, a series of parallel ridges.

Then even that is gone.

There are larger ones.

In the old districts, where the terraces have been built over and over, whole structures may be keepers. A reservoir wall will lean a fraction toward the correct angle when no one is watching. A stair will accumulate frost only on the steps that lead to a blocked mechanism. A tower will exhale at regular intervals, not because of wind, but because something inside it is slowly breathing the pressure from one chamber into another.

These greater keepers have no single form. They occupy systems rather than objects. One may extend beneath an entire ridge, its presence revealed by the synchronized trembling of distant water surfaces. Another may live in a chain of cisterns, passing from chamber to chamber as a change in level, a darkening of the mortar, a sudden clarity in the air.

They are most visible during repairs.

If a wall is opened, a cold shape can sometimes be seen behind the blocks: not a creature exactly, but a concentration of pale planes, jointed like a folded instrument. It withdraws when exposed to direct light. If the opening is left unfinished, it waits nearby. Condensation collects around it. The longer the work is delayed, the more of the surrounding stone begins to arrange itself toward the missing section.

The builders may have known this. Perhaps the last generation did not leave because they died, but because there was no longer any work for them to do. Perhaps they surrendered their bodies deliberately, pressing themselves into the mortar so the plateau could retain the measures of their hands. The tools in their niches are not abandoned tools. They are organs placed outside the body.

Sometimes, very rarely, a keeper makes a mistake.

A channel is completed, but the water enters from the wrong direction. A basin fills to the lip and remains there. A stair descends into a wall that has no door. In such places, the architecture becomes tense. Mineral ridges rise in parallel rows. The mist gathers and does not disperse. The nearest keeper may remain motionless for decades, facing the error.

Then the structure begins to change.

A wall acquires a second wall inside it. A drain bends by degrees. A courtyard deepens. Blocks lift from their beds without breaking and settle elsewhere. The correction is so gradual that the region appears to be dreaming in stone. When it is finished, the water recognizes the new route.

No keeper is ever found at the outer edge.

There, where the last retaining wall ends and the water falls into the white distance, the construction remains unfinished. The stacked blocks are clean and waiting. The final drain continues to release its thin thread into emptiness.

The keepers do not cross beyond the built world. They cannot work without an intended surface, a receiving chamber, a completed relation. Outside the plateau there is only open air, unmeasured depth, and water with nowhere prepared to go.

So they remain behind the wall.

They sleep in the channels. They listen through the stone. They wait for a seam to open, a basin to lose its tile, a distant mechanism to fall out of balance.

And when some small part of the high country is no longer exact, something pale and angular separates itself from the masonry and begins, without haste, to put it right.
