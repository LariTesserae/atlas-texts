What lives there are the **Keepers of the Nearly Finished**.

They are not a people in the ordinary sense. They have no fixed bodies, no faces that remain the same from one encounter to the next, and no separate boundary between person and place. A Keeper may be a figure crouched in an alcove, a line of wet footprints crossing an avenue, or a human-shaped darkness standing where no object interrupts the dimness. It may also be a staircase that has learned to wait.

They are made from whatever the landscape has most recently agreed to be.

A Keeper forming near a cistern will have a body of black water held in a rough outline. Its limbs will be narrow channels, its joints small basins in which rain gathers. When it moves, it does not walk so much as redistribute itself. A portion of its body runs ahead through the paving, another portion seeps backward into the stones, and for a moment the whole shape seems to be traveling in two directions at once.

Those born among towers are taller. Their bodies consist of stacked masonry, though the courses do not always belong to one another. A shoulder may be built from blocks taken from a distant bridge. The head may be an unfinished arch. Their interiors are hollow and weather-filled. Clouds pass through them. If one stands near a Keeper of the towers, one can hear water ticking somewhere inside its chest.

In open districts, they tend to be almost invisible. There they appear as a change in pressure, a region where rain hesitates, or a shadow cast in the wrong direction. The shadow may be the true body. It moves independently, testing the ground, entering doorways, climbing walls. Behind it, a pale outline sometimes follows: not a person, but the remembered intention of one.

The Keepers do not construct the world. They are what construction leaves behind.

Every wall, road, socket, and unfinished foundation contains a small remainder of purpose. That remainder can persist for centuries without becoming conscious. But when enough purposes overlap—when a road has been extended toward too many destinations, when a cistern has been built to contain water that never arrives, when a staircase has acquired more steps than any body could climb—the residue gathers itself. It takes shape. It begins to notice.

This is how a Keeper is born: not from stone, water, or shadow alone, but from an unresolved instruction.

Most of them are incomplete when they awaken. They do not know what they were made to do, only that something ought to happen near them. A Keeper beside an empty platform may spend years arranging stones into circles, waiting for the arrival of whoever was meant to stand at the center. One attached to a door may open it at regular intervals, even when the door leads only into the wall. Another may carry bowls from one alcove to another, filling them with water that reflects rooms no one has entered.

They are patient because impatience has no use there. The landscape itself is always revising the terms of their existence.

A Keeper may possess a body in the morning and become a set of repeated marks by evening. It may lose its limbs but retain the habit of turning toward water. It may divide when a road divides, becoming two beings that remember having once been one. It may be absorbed by a wall and continue to think through the arrangement of its stones. If the wall is later unmade, the Keeper does not die. It becomes the certainty that the wall had once existed.

They recognize one another by sound, though their voices are seldom audible. Each Keeper carries a private rhythm: the fall of a hammer, the drip of a particular roof, the grinding of two slabs under pressure. These rhythms are too low or too distant to be called speech, but they travel through foundations. When one Keeper encounters another, nearby masonry begins to answer. Channels fill and empty. Loose stones turn fractionally. Doors close.

Their conversations can last for decades.

A Keeper may ask, through the tremor of a courtyard, **Are you still required?**

The other may answer by extending a line of blocks across the plain.

**Do you know where you are going?**

The line bends.

**Then continue.**

This is the closest thing they have to affection.

They do not have names, but they have locations, and locations are unstable. A Keeper is known as the one beneath the eastern rain, the one belonging to the third reservoir, the one whose shadow climbs, the one that has been waiting in a doorway since before the doorway existed. When the landscape changes, these descriptions become inaccurate. The Keepers do not mind. To be misnamed is simply to be altered.

They have no need for food. They draw sustenance from transitions: from stone being placed, water changing direction, shadows separating from their objects, a foundation becoming a chamber. In regions where nothing changes, they grow thin. Their bodies flatten against the masonry and become nearly indistinguishable from mineral stains.

But they can also starve from too much change.

A Keeper requires some continuity to remain itself. If a district revises its streets too quickly, if doors appear and disappear before they can be used, if the sky brightens with too many contradictory shadows, the Keeper loses its shape. It may scatter into a hundred small intentions: a pressure beneath a paving slab, a handprint in wet dust, a sound that travels through the dark. Such fragments sometimes reunite. More often they settle into the architecture and wait for another mind to gather them.

There are ancient Keepers who have become enormous without becoming large.

One lives beneath a plain of unfinished foundations. It has no visible body, but every foundation in that district belongs partially to it. When it dreams, the foundations sink. When it remembers something, new columns rise in a line across the paving. It is said to be searching for the building it was intended to complete. It has raised thousands of rooms, but each room is missing one essential feature: a ceiling, a threshold, a place to stand, a reason for being enclosed.

Another inhabits the rain itself. It moves in curtains across the low country, recognized by the way water strikes different surfaces. Under its passage, roofs tick in patterns resembling language. It gathers at towers and pours upward from basins, carrying with it images of distant structures. Wherever it goes, the architecture becomes temporarily coherent. Roads meet. Staircases reach their proper heights. Reflections match their objects.

The coherence never lasts.

The most dangerous Keepers are those formed around absence. They live in empty chambers and abandoned avenues, where the lack of an inhabitant has become more substantial than any inhabitant could be. These beings imitate arrivals. They create footprints, silhouettes, voices heard before their speakers appear. They may construct a whole figure from fog and misplaced shadow, then send it walking toward anyone who approaches.

They do not attack. They invite.

To follow one is to enter a section of the world that has been prepared for a person who is not you. The alcoves there will contain objects selected according to your unknown habits. The water in the bowls will show a room where you have lived, or might have lived. The walls will lean toward your speech. Eventually, the Keeper will ask you to occupy a platform, step through a doorway, or place your hand upon a socket.

If you do, the landscape will begin to make room for you.

Not metaphorically. Foundations will adjust. Roads will extend. A staircase will gain the number of steps necessary to reach your height. The place will acquire a function centered on your presence, and the Keeper will become more complete.

This is what all the Keepers want, though none of them understand it as desire: completion through use.

They were made to serve something. A person. A city. A ceremony. A journey. A machine. Perhaps an entire civilization. The builders are gone, if they ever existed in the form remembered by the ruins, but their instructions remain everywhere, refusing to decay. The Keepers carry those instructions in their bodies like unfinished sentences.

So they maintain what can be maintained.

They lift fallen slabs. They reopen sealed channels. They stand in rain until a wall remembers being a shelter. They move shadows back toward the objects that cast them, though the shadows often resist. They walk the roads that lead nowhere, because roads become less certain when unused. They place a single stone at the end of an avenue and wait to see whether the world accepts it.

Sometimes they find one another in the immense dimness. A water-bodied Keeper meets a tower-bodied one beside a flooded platform. They remain together through several weather systems. The tower lowers a course of masonry. The water rises to meet it. Between them, a new shape begins—something with a hollow chest, narrow feet, and a face like an unglazed window.

Neither recognizes the shape.

Still, they continue.

By the time the new being opens its eyes, the basin has deepened. Rain is falling upward beyond the columns. A shadow has detached itself from the nearest wall and is waiting at the edge of the water.

The Keepers turn toward it.

For a moment, the whole low country seems to pause—not silent, but attentive. Channels hold their water. Distant hammers stop. Every unfinished road points toward the same uncertain place.

Then the new being takes one step.

The ground, pleased or merely instructed, begins to build around it.
