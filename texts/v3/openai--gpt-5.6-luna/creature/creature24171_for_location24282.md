What lives there is not a population so much as a continuation.

The district is inhabited by the **Dwellers**, though they have no word for themselves that can be spoken above a whisper. They are small, pale beings, narrow in the shoulders and long in the hands, with skin the color of wet ash. Their eyes are dark and reflective, adapted to the weak amber of the lamps, and their ears are covered by thin, translucent membranes that tremble whenever a pump changes rhythm. They are not blind, but they do not rely on sight. They know the district by pressure, vibration, temperature, and the taste of minerals carried in the water.

Most of them live in the old service corridors behind the pump houses. They sleep in niches warmed by pipes, wrapped in strips of insulation and the soft inner lining of abandoned machinery. Their homes are never wholly separate from the infrastructure. A dwelling may be the hollow inside a dead motor, a dry space beneath a stair, or a chamber built against the side of a tank where condensation gathers reliably each morning. They prefer places where something repeats: a drip, a pulse, the slow opening and closing of a valve.

They are maintenance workers, though maintenance is not quite the right word. They do not restore the district to any former condition. They keep it in motion.

A Dweller will spend an entire day clearing a channel no wider than a finger. Another will sit beside a bearing and feed it grease by hand, waiting between applications for the metal to accept it. They replace belts with braided root fiber, patch pipes with sheets of softened tin, and mark damaged walls with lines of white mineral paste. Their repairs are careful but rarely permanent. They understand that everything here must eventually be altered by water. To repair something is not to save it. It is to give it a little more time in which to become something else.

They travel in small family groups, though family is determined less by birth than by shared rhythm. Those who tend the same pump come to breathe and move at the same intervals. They eat pale fungi cultivated in cracks, blind fish taken from the settling tanks, and the soft-bodied insects that gather beneath the lamps. They also consume mineral blooms from the walls, scraping them into shallow dishes and dissolving them with water. Their teeth are dark from this practice.

Young Dwellers are raised near the machines. Their first lessons are not names or numbers, but sounds. A child is taught to distinguish a healthy pump from one whose inner piston is beginning to fail, a harmless tremor from the passage of something large beneath the grating, the ordinary drip of condensation from the deliberate striking of a warning bell. Before they can walk, they are laid against the concrete so they may feel the district’s deeper movements through their ribs.

They are quiet even among themselves. Conversation consists of clicks, finger taps, and brief touches to the wall. A message can travel farther through a pipe than through a voice. The Dwellers tap instructions into railings and listen for answers in distant pump houses. They know which doors will be open, which stair will give way, which pool contains fish, and which one contains something that has learned to imitate fish.

They are not frightened by the animals. The animals are part of the district’s function, and the Dwellers regard them with the practical respect one gives to weather. They leave scraps near the drainage holes for the transparent-eared mammals. They build small shelters beside the inspection pipes for the long-legged amphibians. In return, the animals warn them of changes below: a sudden silence in the tunnels, a ripple moving against the slope of the water, a gathering of insects around a machine that has not yet begun to fail.

The broad-bodied creature beneath the grating is known as the **Underkeeper**.

No Dweller has seen all of it. They know portions: a pale ridge passing beneath black water, a cluster of folded limbs withdrawing into a tunnel, the impression of a wide head pressing against the underside of the floor. It has lived below the district longer than the oldest pumps. It may have been born there, or it may have arrived through the stone when the place was first opened.

The Underkeeper does not maintain the machinery. It maintains the relationships between things.

When a pipe becomes too warm, it passes beneath it. When a channel begins to stagnate, it disturbs the water far below. When a pump loses its rhythm, it circles the foundations until the vibration returns. The Dwellers leave offerings at certain grates: bundles of wire, warm stones, the bones of blind fish, pieces of metal that still remember a purpose. Sometimes the offerings vanish. Sometimes they are returned transformed into smooth, useless shapes.

The Dwellers believe the Underkeeper dreams through the engines. They believe the district is its body, and that the pumps are not machines but organs whose names have been forgotten.

They may be right.

Once in a long while, all of the pumps stop. The Dwellers emerge from their rooms and stand beside the channels without speaking. The animals flatten themselves against the walls. Even the plants become still, their pale leaves turned toward the same unseen place.

During this interval, the Dwellers do not work. They listen.

The water rises. The stone gives a low, inward groan. From beneath the oldest building comes a sound like something enormous drawing breath through a narrow throat. No one knows whether the pause is a failure, a warning, or a form of communication.

When the machinery starts again, the Dwellers resume their tasks. They clean the grates. They count the fish. They touch their foreheads to the warm pipes. They check the lamps and wipe the mineral film from their eyes.

If a surface dweller entered the district, the Dwellers would not attack. They would watch from the dark beneath the stairs, waiting to see whether the newcomer understood how to move slowly. If the visitor damaged a pump, blocked a channel, or made too much noise, the district itself would begin to close around them: doors would lock, water would find their footprints, and the animals would gather in the walls.

But if the visitor listened, the Dwellers might approach.

They would offer a cup of mineral water, cloudy with life. They would place one small hand against the visitor’s chest and wait for the heartbeat. Then they would lead them through the damp passages, past the sleeping machines and the blind fish, toward the deepest grate in the district.

There, below the trembling water, something vast would move.

And the Dwellers would be watching not the creature, but the visitor—waiting to learn whether they could feel the answer.
