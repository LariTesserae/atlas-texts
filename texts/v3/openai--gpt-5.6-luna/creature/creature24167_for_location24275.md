What lives there does not arrive from elsewhere.

It comes up through the seams.

At first it resembles a flaw in the stone: a darkening no wider than a hair, trembling along the edge of a plate. Then the flaw thickens. Powder gathers around it. The mineral pales, buckles, and opens—not into a hole, but into a joint. A second joint forms above it, and a third. Something unfolds itself from the fracture in the manner of a diagram being remembered.

The newly risen beings are narrow and many-limbed. Their bodies have no clear front or back. They are made of overlapping plates, translucent at the edges and dark around the center, where a slow internal pressure pulses. They may stand upright for a while, though standing is not their natural posture. More often they cling to walls, spread across floors, or flatten themselves into the shallow depressions between plates.

They are not born alone. A fracture may release a single one, but it is usually followed by others: smaller, unfinished forms that gather beneath it and touch their jointed bodies to its sides. They remain connected until the stone around them has settled. Then they separate without ceremony and vanish into the city.

They do not have faces. What might be taken for eyes are black knots embedded beneath their outer membranes, distributed through the body rather than concentrated in the head. These knots respond to movement, warmth, and the opening of cracks. When one of the beings turns toward something, it does not look at it. It redistributes itself around the thing’s presence.

They know the city by pressure.

A footfall hundreds of meters away is not a sound to them but a change in the weight of the ground. A breath is a warm disturbance that pulls them from concealment. The slow contraction of the basin is felt as a summons. During it, they gather in the streets and along the walls, aligning themselves with the fractures as if reading a message written beneath the mineral.

When the city tightens, they become still.

When it releases, they move.

Their movement is nearly invisible. One moment a creature lies beneath a stair; the next, it has spread itself over the wall above, its limbs inserted into seams that were not there before. They travel through the thin spaces inside the architecture, not through rooms or corridors. The city’s visible passages are incidental surfaces. The true roads are the pressures below them.

They feed on change.

Where a wall is fresh and warm, they press their bodies against it. Their dark knots sink into the pale surface and draw out something that cannot be seen: perhaps heat, perhaps tension, perhaps the force by which stone becomes architecture. In return, the wall hardens around them. Small hollows remain when they pull free, each one shaped like a joint, a mouth, or a listening organ.

The colorless vegetation is part of this exchange. The threadlike stalks drink from the new mineral, and the flat creatures graze along the roots. Neither destroys the other. The animals crop the filaments, and the filaments grow back in altered patterns, opening their translucent fans toward whatever disturbance is approaching. Together they form a kind of nervous system spread across the city.

When the fans unfold, the beings come.

They gather in places where the ground is about to move. Sometimes dozens cover a courtyard, arranged in widening rings around a point that looks no different from any other point. Sometimes they climb the columns and fit themselves into the hollow openings at the top. When the columns begin to sing, the creatures vibrate with them. Their bodies blur. For a moment they seem less like animals than shadows cast by an underground mechanism.

The song changes them.

Afterward, some are shorter. Some have acquired extra limbs, or lost them. Some become almost flat and transparent, able to pass beneath doors and through hairline fractures. Others grow tall enough to reach the lower ribs of the buildings. These larger forms are rarely seen in the open. They remain folded inside towers, arches, and sealed chambers, waiting for the city to produce a structure suitable for their bodies.

They are not builders, exactly.

They do not decide what a wall should become. They attend the wall while it becomes. They cluster around new arches, brace themselves against rising ridges, and press their limbs into soft stone to guide its hardening. A building may be their nest for only a few hours before the city changes and leaves them exposed. They do not resist this. They withdraw, divide, and follow the new geometry.

Their homes are temporary arrangements of pressure.

A creature may inhabit a stair until the stair turns into a pillar. It may sleep within a hollow column until the column’s opening seals. It may spend a cycle beneath a roof, then emerge to find that the roof has become ground and the ground has become a wall. Nothing in the city is lost to them by such transformations. They remember spaces not as locations but as patterns: the angle of a passage, the depth of a chamber, the direction in which the stone once leaned.

They have no need for light. The gray radiance is not illumination to them, and darkness is not concealment. They detect the difference between old and new stone through their bodies. Fresh surfaces call to them with warmth. Ancient surfaces are nearly dead. They avoid the oldest chambers unless something below is moving.

They are wary of attention.

Direct observation causes them to flatten and become motionless. This is not fear. Attention is a kind of pressure they cannot interpret, and the creatures respond by removing every sign of themselves. Their limbs draw inward. Their dark knots dim. Their bodies take on the exact pallor of the mineral beneath them.

If watched for long enough, they seem to disappear.

Yet they are not gone. They have merely become part of the wall, waiting for the observer to turn away.

There are larger inhabitants beneath the settlement.

No one sees them whole. Their presence is known by the behavior of the city: by the widening of a seam, the simultaneous bending of the threadlike plants, the movement of a shadow where no open space exists. At times, one of these hidden bodies passes beneath a district and the buildings rise slightly above it, as though lifted by the back of something enormous.

The smaller creatures respond to these passages with submission. They lie flat. Their dark knots open. Some enter the fractures and do not return. Others gather in the central enclosure, where the line in the floor marks the path of whatever moves below.

The line is a mouth, though it never opens.

When the thing beneath it passes, the city builds around the event. The inhabitants do not worship it, because worship would imply distance and choice. They are organs of its awareness. They carry its pressure through their bodies and into the walls. The low columns sing because something below them is communicating. The creatures vibrate because they are receiving. The architecture changes because the message has entered the mineral.

Perhaps the beings above are not inhabitants at all, but temporary nerves.

Perhaps the city is not their dwelling but their body, and the creatures are its thoughts made visible: small, jointed impulses moving through a structure that has not yet learned what it means to be alive.

Still, they possess something like preference.

They return to certain chambers. They gather beneath particular arches. They leave the oldest, darkest walls untouched except when the pressure below becomes unbearable. When one of them finds another injured by a shifting plate, the others surround it and press their bodies against the damage. They do not repair it. They redistribute the creature’s shape until it can move again.

There is no tenderness in the gesture as humans would understand it.

There is recognition.

The injured one is a pattern that has not yet finished. The others hold it in place.

They reproduce when the city does. During a contraction, the animals retreat into the seams, where their bodies become indistinguishable from the dark below. After the release, new forms emerge among the fresh cracks. Some are small enough to pass through a needle’s eye. Others unfold in the height of a room, already bearing the marks of limbs they will later shed.

They do not age. They are worn away, revised, or absorbed.

A creature that has spent too long in old stone becomes opaque. Its dark knots spread until the whole body is nearly black. It moves less and less, eventually fastening itself to a wall. The city grows over it. In time, the creature becomes one of the hollows in the architecture, a chamber no larger than a hand, waiting for another body to recognize it.

That is how the inhabitants remain.

Not as individuals, and not as a population, but as recurring forms in the stone’s unfinished thought.

The city is waiting for them to become more numerous. Or for something beneath the city to finish arriving.

Until then, they move through the pale streets without footsteps, feeding on new walls, listening with their entire bodies, and gathering whenever the hidden pressure rises. They know that every building is temporary. They know that every passage may close behind them. They know that the central line leads somewhere below, though none of them has crossed it.

When the next contraction begins, they will all turn toward it.

The plants will fold.

The columns will sing.

And from the seams, more of them will unfold—quietly, patiently, already accustomed to a city that has not yet decided what they are for.
