What lives in the basin is not one kind of thing.

The smallest inhabitants are the most numerous. They live in the mud, in the warm seams beneath the concrete, inside the reed roots and cable housings. They are jointed, pale, and almost transparent, with bodies like strings of folded wire. Some resemble worms until they unfold too many legs. Others resemble insects until they open a second set of mouths along their backs.

They feed on films of mineral, dead skin, the faint organic residue left by passing animals. They also feed on machinery. A cluster may gather around a valve and worry at the metal for days, not eating it exactly, but removing some invisible quality from it. Afterward the valve turns more easily. The creatures become darker. Sometimes they vanish into a crack and do not emerge again.

They travel in lines because the ground remembers lines. A single animal crossing the mud leaves a weak impression. Ten crossing together make a path strong enough to remain for years. The oldest routes are crowded with their descendants, each following the pale, water-filled trace of those that came before. When the basin revises itself, the creatures do not panic. They stop, raise their front segments, and wait until the new ground has finished deciding what it is.

The reed beds belong to another kind of life. The reeds are rooted, but their roots move through the mud at night—or during whatever passes for night beneath the black ceiling. They exchange water, minerals, and impressions. A reed that has been touched carries the touch through the whole stand. A reed cut in one place will sometimes bleed from another.

Small animals nest among them, especially the black amphibious things that float in the channels. They are smooth-headed and nearly eyeless, with mouths hidden under their throats. They feed by opening themselves to the current. Whatever passes through them—water, insects, reflections, memories of drowned things—is strained and sorted.

Their young are born as pale knots attached to the underside of the leaves. For several weeks they do not move. Then, all at once, they drop into the water and disappear below the surface. When they return, they are larger and have learned the names of channels that have not yet been made.

The larger animals are called, if they are called anything, the patient ones.

They stand in the mist with their ridged backs exposed and their feet sunk deep into the mud. Their hides are thick, gray, and wet, though in places they appear to be made of layered stone. They have no obvious eyes. Their faces are long and blunt, with narrow openings that close whenever the deeper noise begins.

They do not graze. They drink from the shallow pools and absorb heat through their feet. Occasionally one lowers its head to a geometric mat and remains there for hours. Perhaps it feeds on the pale growth. Perhaps it is listening to the buried machinery beneath it.

They gather near newly altered terrain. If a ridge has become a wall, or a pool has deepened without losing its surface, one of the patient ones will come and stand beside it. After a while the animal moves on, leaving behind a dark impression that resembles its body but is too tall and too narrow. These impressions fill with clear water. Small creatures breed in them.

The patient ones are not harmless. Nothing in the basin is entirely harmless. When threatened, they do not charge or cry out. They simply continue toward whatever has disturbed them. Their reflections usually arrive first.

There are also things that have no bodies unless observed indirectly.

They occupy the water, the mist, and the polished faces of old machines. A worker near a pumping station may see a second figure moving at the edge of vision: taller, thinner, bent at the wrong angle. If the worker turns, it is gone. If the worker looks down, it may be standing in the water beside their own reflection.

These beings seem to be made from retained shapes. The basin preserves everything that enters it, but preservation is not the same as survival. A drowned animal leaves behind an outline. A hand leaves an impression. A person who has walked the same route often enough may leave a posture, a rhythm, a turn of the head. In time, these remnants begin to move without the original.

They are most common in channels where the water is too deep to see the bottom. They climb slowly out of the surface, though “climb” is not quite right. They arrive in pieces: a shoulder at one point, a face farther away, a footprint appearing several meters ahead. They do not speak. They imitate actions. A reflection may bend over a pool long after its owner has gone. Another may operate a rustless valve, one quarter-turn at a time, repeating the movement forever.

The machinery has its own inhabitants.

Inside the dead generators, behind control panels, and within the sealed shafts of the pumping stations are soft, warm colonies that resemble organs more than animals. They are threaded through the old systems, drinking condensation and minute electrical charges. They do not repair the machines. They keep them from becoming fully dead.

When an engine warms itself, these colonies are waking. When a cable tightens, they have passed a signal along its length. The translucent growths on the cables are not separate organisms but exposed portions of the same buried body. Their pulses travel from station to station, sometimes taking days to cross the basin.

No one knows whether the machines built the organisms or the organisms learned to inhabit the machines. The distinction has become less useful with time.

There are larger presences beneath the plain.

The animals react to them before the water rises. The reeds fold inward. The reflections turn their faces toward the ground. Even the small crawling things leave the channels and gather on the hard geometric mats.

Then the basin inhales.

Something below draws water through the earth. The channels lift without overflowing. The cables strain upward. The scattered engines wake together, not to perform separate tasks but to participate in one operation whose beginning and end lie outside the visible world.

During this event, shapes sometimes appear in the mist: tall frames, many-legged silhouettes, forms like scaffolds walking with deliberate care. They may be workers. They may be tools. They may be the bodies of creatures too large for the basin, visible only where the darkness allows them to intersect the ground.

They collect things.

A dead animal. A loosened bolt. A handful of black reeds. The pale imprint left by a footprint. Once, perhaps, a person.

The collected objects are carried toward the orderly lanes in the mist. They are not taken by force. The basin itself seems to slope toward the unseen factory, though the ground remains level. Those who resist find that the distance has altered. The machinery they came from is suddenly farther away. The water around their feet rises without deepening. Their own reflection begins walking in the opposite direction.

At the far edge of the plain, the factory continues its patient work.

Perhaps it produces machines. Perhaps it produces animals. Perhaps it produces revisions: new channels, new distances, new laws by which the basin can continue to exist.

The creatures here do not worship it. They do not understand it as a god. Their lives are too old and too practical for that. They feed, mate, migrate, shed, listen, and wait. When the buried system calls, they become still because stillness is part of their inheritance.

And when the call is over, they resume.

The small things return to the mud. The patient ones begin their crossing. The reflections settle into the water. The reeds straighten, though not always in the same places.

Something has been made in the dark.

Something has been consumed.

The basin keeps both records.
