The people of the shelf are small in number and difficult to distinguish from one another at a distance. They wear layered garments in colors taken from the settlement itself: ash-gray, brown-black, faded clay, the pale green of old moss. Their outer cloaks are close-woven and hooded, fastened at the throat with bone, wood, or a piece of dull metal. Nothing hangs loose enough to catch the wind. Nothing is bright enough to be seen easily from the slopes below.

They are not frail, though they are lean. The mountain has given them narrow shoulders, strong hands, and a way of standing that keeps the body close to the ground. They walk with their feet placed carefully, not because the paving is treacherous, but because care has become the settlement’s common motion. A person carrying water does not hurry. A child crossing the square does not run without looking first. Even the oldest people descend the worn stairs with a measured confidence, one hand sometimes touching the wall as if greeting it.

Their faces are weathered but not harsh. The cold has reddened some cheeks and roughened the skin around the eyes. Most wear their hair cut short or braided tightly beneath a cap, though a few older women allow silver-black lengths to fall down their backs. Their eyes are accustomed to distances that cannot be crossed: cloud, stone, pale air, the unbroken shoulders of neighboring mountains. They look outward often, but rarely with longing. The valleys are known to them as a fact, not a promise.

They live in households arranged around work rather than kinship. A house may contain several generations, or several unrelated adults who have chosen to share a cistern allocation, a hearth, and the maintenance of one roof. Children belong to the settlement before they belong to any single family. They are taught the names of the channels and the depth of the wells almost as soon as they can speak. They learn which roofs collect clean water, which walls hold condensation longest, which stones become slick after rain, and which paths should never be taken alone in fog.

There is little formal authority. The settlement is governed by whoever remembers most accurately.

An old woman knows how many stones make up the eastern retaining wall and which three were replaced after the last great frost. A broad-shouldered man can tell, by the taste of water, whether a cistern has cracked below its lining. Someone else keeps the measure of grain, salt, dried roots, lamp oil, and the small stores of metal that cannot be replenished. Their authority does not come from command. It comes from being difficult to contradict.

When disagreements arise, they are carried into the square and spoken plainly. No one raises their voice. Anger seems wasteful here, an expenditure of breath and warmth. The people listen until the matter has been reduced to something practical: a roof that needs weight, a path that should be closed, a family that has drawn too much water, a wall that must be repointed before the next rain. Personal grievance is not absent, but it is expected to survive without damaging the structures on which everyone depends.

Their days begin before sunrise. A few people climb onto the roofs to inspect the stones and clear dust from the channels. Others uncover the cisterns, measure the water, and wipe the lids clean of condensation. Hearths are stirred from ash. Clay pots are placed near the northern walls to catch the morning beads before they vanish. The first meal is usually plain: softened grain, bitter roots, dried goat meat, or a thin broth thickened with ground seed.

Work then disperses through the settlement. Stone is dressed, clay is mixed, reeds are sorted, clothing is patched, and old tools are reshaped. The people do not build much, but they are always repairing something. A broken bowl is not discarded until it has been used as a drain cover, a step, a weight, or a fragment in a wall. Children gather chips of slate and arrange them into little enclosures that adults pretend not to notice until they become useful.

They speak softly, but not from shyness. Sound is treated like water: something to be directed and conserved. Conversations happen close to doorways or beneath low overhangs. At night, families sit near their hearths and tell stories in voices that barely rise above the settling of ash. The stories are rarely about heroes conquering the mountain. They concern errors avoided, promises kept, wells found under old paving, and people who mistook the cloudline for a road.

The dead remain present in practical ways. They are buried in narrow chambers cut into the stone beyond the upper ridge, where the wind is strongest and the ground least likely to shift. Their names are carved on small, unadorned slabs, though many stones have worn nearly smooth. After a burial, the dead person’s tools are distributed among the living. A hammer passes to a niece. A spindle goes to a neighbor. A water frame is repaired and returned to service. The people do not believe this diminishes the dead. To leave a useful object idle would be a more serious insult.

There are celebrations, though they do not resemble festivals elsewhere. When the cisterns fill after a long dry interval, the town gathers in the square. Someone brings out a better meal. Lamps are uncovered. Children are allowed to stay awake late. The people sing, but the songs are low and repetitive, built to fit within the walls. Their music has no grand climax. It circles familiar phrases until the singers fall silent together.

They possess few possessions, but they are deeply attached to particular things: a pot whose rim has been repaired seven times, a doorway whose threshold was laid by a vanished ancestor, a certain patch of moss that is never scraped away. The sparse plants beside some houses are tended with extraordinary attention. A person may spend an hour loosening soil around a bitter shrub that will never grow taller than the hand. They do not call this hope. They call it keeping.

No one in the settlement speaks of leaving as though it were a simple choice. The descending path is known, and a few have taken it in the past. Some returned after months or years, altered by noise, abundance, and the presence of too many horizons. Others never came back. Their absence is acknowledged but not dramatized. The people keep a place for them in certain stories, and sometimes a repaired tool is set aside until someone decides it may be used.

The inhabitants are not imprisoned by the mountain, nor do they imagine themselves its masters. They have made an agreement with a narrow piece of the world. They take water when it comes, heat when they can, stone when they must. In return, they maintain the channels, keep the roofs weighted, preserve the paths, and disturb as little as possible.

From the cloudline, they might seem like moving shadows among the stones: a figure crossing the square, two people bent over a cistern, a child carrying a bundle of reeds, an old man standing motionless beside a wall. Then the vapor closes over the shelf, and they disappear.

When it lifts again, they are still there.
