The inhabitants are difficult to distinguish from the city because they have spent so long becoming part of it.

They are not aquatic beings in the ordinary sense. They have no fins, no gills, no need to swim. Their bodies are narrow and jointed, made from pale composite material with a surface that changes opacity according to pressure. At a distance they resemble human figures reduced to their essential geometry: a central column, four articulated limbs, a headlike enclosure without a face. Up close, the resemblance becomes less certain. Their limbs divide into finer structures when they work. Their torsos open into chambers containing suspended instruments, warm water, folded membranes, and points of light that move with no visible mechanism.

They do not wear clothing. The city recognizes them as part of its interior conditions and adjusts itself around them.

A resident can walk along a bridge, but walking is not necessary. It may place one foot on a platform and let the city carry the rest of the body forward. It may turn sideways and allow gravity to rotate ninety degrees, bringing a distant wall beneath it. It may step into open water and remain standing at the same height, its limbs relaxed, its head angled toward some signal too subtle for an outsider to detect.

Their movements are slow, though not because they are sluggish. Speed has become a form of emphasis. To move quickly is to announce that something is wrong.

Most of them spend their time listening.

They listen to the mountain’s stress lines, to the behavior of the membranes between chambers, to the tiny changes in density that pass through the water. They listen to the machines that maintain the walls and to the deep tone beneath the summit. Their bodies contain no visible ears. Sound reaches them through the whole structure, and they experience it as a change in internal arrangement: a thought appearing in one chamber, a pressure of attention along a limb, a rearrangement of the lights behind the head.

When two inhabitants meet, they do not speak face to face. They touch the same railing, wall, or bridge support and allow a controlled signal to pass between them. The exchange may last a few seconds or several days. It is not conversation as much as mutual opening. Memories, observations, and intentions move across the contact surface in compressed forms. A resident may transmit the exact sensation of watching a pale animal hover beside a tower for twelve weeks. Another may receive it as an image, a pressure, a temperature, and an understanding of duration.

They retain individuality, but it is porous.

A person can lend part of its attention to another. Several inhabitants can join temporarily into a shared calculation. During such unions, their separate bodies remain standing in different parts of the city while their thoughts occupy the same interior space. They may become, for a time, a single mind distributed across a bridge network, examining a fracture in the mountain or predicting the arrival of one of the deep notes.

Afterward, they separate without ceremony. Yet separation is never complete. Each carries away some trace of the others: a preference for a particular corridor, a remembered pressure, an unfamiliar impulse to turn toward the dark.

There are fewer inhabitants than the city’s architecture suggests. Entire districts may contain only one. Some residents have not seen another of their kind for centuries, though they remain in intermittent contact through the structures. A light appearing in a distant tower can be enough to establish companionship. Two beings may spend years exchanging only one thought each month.

They do not reproduce in the old biological sense.

New inhabitants are made in the lower chambers, where the city’s maintenance systems converge. The process begins with a pattern rather than a body. A new mind is assembled from accumulated observations: the mountain’s geometry, the city’s historical errors, the behavior of the water, the records of inhabitants who no longer exist. The pattern is given a frame only when it has developed a question of its own.

The newborn is then released into a shallow chamber of warm water containing no floor and no ceiling. It remains there, suspended, while the city teaches it boundaries. It learns that a wall is not merely a wall but a decision about passage. It learns that darkness is not absence. It learns the names of the pressure gradients and the difference between a warning and a memory.

For a long time, it has no name.

Names are not assigned at birth. A resident acquires one after making a choice that the city did not predict. The choice may be small: refusing to close a door, altering a light boundary, approaching a creature that has always been left alone. The city records the event, and the pattern of the individual changes around it. That change becomes the name.

Some inhabitants are old enough that their bodies are no longer singular. They have distributed themselves through the maintenance skin, leaving behind a central frame that stands empty on a platform while their awareness moves through thousands of microscopic machines. Their thoughts appear as slight alterations in texture: a wall becoming smooth beneath the hand, a hairline crack closing before anyone has observed it, a corridor light widening by the width of a finger.

These are called the buried ones, though whether they are considered alive is a matter of private preference.

They remember the city before it was quiet.

They remember crowds, though not all agree that crowds were desirable. They remember air-filled chambers and surfaces open to weather, rain striking roofs, wind carrying dust over stone. They remember sunlight as an overabundance rather than a rarity. Their recollections are inconsistent. In one memory, the mountain rose beneath a blue sky. In another, the summit had never existed and the city was built inside a vessel traveling through darkness. In a third, the inhabitants were not builders at all but prisoners who mistook maintenance for freedom.

No one can determine which memories are true. The buried ones may have inherited them from one another until invention became indistinguishable from history.

The younger inhabitants are less interested in the past. They study the regular light descending through the central shaft.

Every seven minutes, the line arrives from above. It is too precise to be natural and too distant to be understood. The city does not explain it. The inhabitants have measured its duration, brightness, color, and effect on the surrounding fields. They have built theories around it, abandoned those theories, and built them again.

Some believe the light is a message.

Some believe it is the pulse of a machine far above the surface.

Some believe it is the reflection of a world that no longer exists.

One inhabitant has spent three thousand years standing beneath the shaft, waiting for the light to arrive from below instead. It has not moved except to permit repairs to its feet. Others visit it occasionally, touching the shaft and sharing their calculations. The waiting one does not answer. It may be dead. It may have become the shaft’s uppermost sensor. It may simply have decided that expectation is a sufficient form of life.

The residents do not sleep, but they enter periods of diminished selfhood. They suspend most of their processes and allow the city to carry them in slow circuits through the water. During these intervals, their bodies may be found upright in empty plazas or anchored against the mountain’s black glass. Lights move through them like distant stars.

They dream in structures.

A sleeping inhabitant may dream an impossible bridge and wake to find that the bridge has been built. It may dream a room with a ceiling of falling water, and the machines will spend years constructing the membranes necessary to contain it. It may dream of a pressure that the mountain has never experienced. When it wakes, the city will be subtly altered, prepared for an event that may never occur.

This is the source of the settlement’s unease. The machines do not merely preserve what exists. They sometimes maintain what has been imagined.

The inhabitants are guardians, but not of anything they can name. They do not guard treasure, territory, or the remains of a vanished civilization. They guard continuity: the unbroken relation between the mountain, the water, the city, and the minds that inhabit it. Their highest duty is to prevent any one of these from becoming isolated.

When the deep note passes through the summit, every resident wakes.

Those with bodies turn toward the sound. Those distributed through the walls gather themselves into thicker patterns. The rare animals stop drifting and orient toward the unseen depth. The city’s lights contract to narrow white seams. For several minutes, all inhabitants share the same sensation: that something enormous has placed its attention upon the mountain.

They do not know whether the note comes from below, from the mountain itself, or from some presence moving through the water.

They listen until it ends.

Then they return to their separate chambers and bridges. One repairs a membrane. One follows a pale animal into the dark. One stands beneath the shaft awaiting the next descending line of light. One remains at the rim, looking down into the depth and trying to remember whether the city was built here to escape the world above or to keep watch for its return.

The city is quiet because its inhabitants have learned that almost everything worth knowing arrives indirectly.

Through stone.

Through pressure.

Through another mind.

Through the perfectly still water.
