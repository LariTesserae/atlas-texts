What lives there is not a single kind of creature.

The plateau has grazers, burrowers, hunters, pollinators, scavengers, and things that have never needed names because they are too closely related to the ground to be distinguished from it. But above all these lives a population that does not occupy the plateau so much as participate in its circulation.

They are called, by the few travelers who have encountered them, the **Rootwalkers**.

The name is misleading. They do walk, but walking is only one of their ways of moving. A Rootwalker may spend several days crossing a basin without once appearing above the moss. It will pass beneath the living surface through channels that are not tunnels but temporary loosenings in the root-fabric, parting the soil with its hands and letting it close behind. At other times it travels openly, slowly, bent beneath the mist, its body almost indistinguishable from a wet black tree trunk.

They are tall when standing, though they rarely stand fully upright. Their limbs are long and jointed, with broad, flexible hands that spread their weight over the ground. The feet are not feet exactly, but layered pads of dark tissue, each one capable of opening into a fringe of fine sensory roots. These roots taste the moisture beneath the soil. They tell the Rootwalker where the mineral water is rising, where a warm seam has shifted, where a buried chamber is filling too quickly.

Their bodies are narrow through the waist and heavy across the back. They are covered in a dense, short coat that seems at first to be fur, though it grows in mats and takes on the colors of whatever has settled into it: green from moss, gray from ashless stone, orange from iron-rich pools. In the oldest individuals, lichen hangs from the shoulders and spine in long cords. Small flowers sometimes root in the coat. The Rootwalkers do not remove them.

Their heads are low and wedge-shaped, with no visible ears. Instead, each side of the skull contains a series of shallow, vibrating membranes. These quiver when water moves beneath stone. A distant pressure shift, inaudible to other animals, can make a Rootwalker stop in the middle of a step and turn toward a ridge. Their eyes are small and pale, protected by translucent inner lids. They see adequately in the gray light, but most of what matters to them is learned through touch, pressure, scent, and the minute changes in temperature carried through the wet ground.

They have mouths, but they do not speak as other creatures do.

A Rootwalker can produce a low note through the chest, a sound almost identical to the call of the great amphibious stream-dwellers. It is felt more than heard. Another answer may come from a reed bed half a mile away, or from beneath the moss at the edge of a basin. Their communication travels through water, roots, and stone. A message can be a warning, a direction, a declaration of need, or simply the presence of one living body acknowledging another.

When several Rootwalkers gather, the surrounding ground begins to tremble faintly. Not from their weight. Their bodies resonate with one another, and the resonance passes into the wetland. The reeds hum. Pools quiver. The moss lifts and settles in small waves.

They are not builders. They make no shelters, tools, roads, or boundaries. A place of rest is chosen where the ground is warm and the roots are strong. The Rootwalker lowers itself into the moss and allows the vegetation to close over its back. By morning, it may be difficult to tell whether a body lies there at all. During long periods of rain, a whole family can disappear into a slope and remain beneath it for weeks, feeding on stored minerals and the soft bulbs that grow around warm seams.

Their young are not born in the manner of the grazing animals.

A mother chooses a basin where the water is clean and the metallic deposits are thin. She enters the pool and remains there until the translucent growths beneath the surface begin to pulse in response to her presence. Over several days, a pale, folded form develops among the roots. It is not yet shaped like a Rootwalker. It resembles a knot of wet limbs and gill-fronds, blind and clinging. The mother carries it against her chest until it learns the rhythms of the plateau: the rise of heat, the downward pull of water, the tremors of settling stone.

A young Rootwalker spends its first years underground. It follows root channels, eats fungus, drinks mineral seepage, and learns to distinguish living pressure from geological pressure. When it first emerges, the surrounding animals often come to watch. Grazers pause at the edge of the clearing. Small black-faced scavengers gather in the ferns. Birds settle silently on the low branches.

The Rootwalkers do not herd the animals, though the animals frequently follow them.

Where Rootwalkers travel, damaged ground tends to recover. They press their hands into collapsed channels and hold them open until water finds a new course. They break apart metallic crusts with their fingers and bury the fragments beneath filtering moss. They uproot diseased growths, not by tearing them away but by tracing the infection through the root-fabric and redirecting the flow that feeds it. Around failing bulbous plants, they may remain motionless for days, their bodies connected to the underground network while they coax clean water through the tissue.

No one knows whether they understand the plateau as an individual understands a machine.

They do not seem to worship it. They do not fear it. The deep throb beneath the ridges is not, to them, an omen or a voice. It is a condition of existence, as necessary as breath. A shift in pressure changes their posture. A mineral failure changes their routes. If a ridge settles, they adjust. If a stream runs warm, they gather there. If a basin becomes poisonous, they leave it, and the vegetation follows slowly behind.

Their movements are patient but not slow in the way of helpless things. They can cross a stretch of open meadow with startling speed when necessary, their bodies folding and unfolding over the moss without leaving more than temporary impressions. They climb wet cliffs by pressing their palms and feet into the stone, finding holds too small for any other creature. They can remain underwater for hours. In deep winter—or what passes for winter beneath the cloud—they bury themselves in groups beneath the roots, linked together by thin tendrils that grow from the backs of their hands.

Predators avoid them. Not because the Rootwalkers are invulnerable, but because their flesh carries an unpleasant concentration of iron and bitter minerals. A young one might be taken by a catlike hunter, though the hunter would likely abandon it. The great amphibious animals tolerate Rootwalkers in their streams. Sometimes one will approach and place its broad, wet head against a Rootwalker’s legs. The two remain that way for a long time, exchanging vibrations through the water.

The six-legged grazers are less wary. They pass around Rootwalkers as they would around stones or old trees. Flocks of seed-pickers perch on their shoulders. Burrowers use the warmth of their resting places. When a Rootwalker dies, its body is not defended or hidden. The moss enters it first. Then fungi, worms, beetles, and the pale root-feeders. The body becomes a mound of dark soil, unusually rich, and for several seasons the plants above it grow taller and denser than anywhere nearby.

Other creatures are less visible but equally important.

There are ribbon-bodied animals that live in the water films beneath leaves, transparent except for their red digestive organs. There are insects that spend their entire adult lives inside the hollow reeds, emerging only to mate when the reed’s internal hum reaches a particular pitch. There are pale, many-fingered things in the peat that consume fallen roots and exhale warmth. There are colonies of soft-bodied filter feeders attached to the underside of stones, turning cloudy seepage clear.

The plateau’s birds are small, mostly gray or blue-black, and rarely sing for pleasure. They give short calls to mark safe crossings through the mist, the location of fruiting shelves, or the approach of one of the unseen cats. Some mimic the clacking of underground stone. Others imitate the pulsing notes of the reed beds so perfectly that a listener cannot tell whether the sound comes from throat or stem.

The predators are nearly all ambush hunters. They do not chase so much as wait for the living country to bring something close. The catlike animals lie beneath fern curtains with their paws folded under them, eyes reflecting no light. Their coats are mottled with pale mineral streaks, and when they remain still, condensation gathers on their backs. They kill quickly and leave little behind. By the time their prey is discovered, the insects have already begun their work.

The largest animals belong to the streams.

They are broad, low creatures with thick limbs and soft, dark skin. Their eyes are nearly blind, their hearing poor, but their bodies are exquisitely sensitive to pressure. A footstep on the bank can be detected through the water. Their resonant call is used to claim warm pools, though territorial conflicts are rare. Most of the time they lie half-submerged among reeds, with only the crown of the head visible. Their backs support moss, small plants, and sometimes sleeping birds.

When the plateau’s buried machinery shifts, these animals know first. They rise from the water and face the source. The Rootwalkers emerge from the ground. Herds abandon the low meadows. Birds fall silent. For a few moments, every living thing seems to listen through the same body.

Then the pressure passes.

Water resumes its descent. Heat moves upward through stone. A metallic taste enters one stream and is filtered away in another. The reeds begin to hum again. The grazers return to the grass. Somewhere beneath a ridge, the Rootwalkers put their hands into the earth and begin the quiet work of making the damage temporary.

Nothing there lives apart.

The plants are filters, reservoirs, scaffolds, and food. The animals are distributors of seed, heat, mineral, and memory. The Rootwalkers are neither rulers nor caretakers in any deliberate human sense. They are one of the plateau’s organs: mobile enough to respond, intelligent enough to recognize patterns, and ancient enough to have forgotten where the landscape ends and their own bodies begin.

If there is a mind in the high country, it does not reside in them alone.

It is in the root-fabric under the moss, in the water changing course through the dark stone, in the reeds translating pressure into music, in the grazers carrying one valley’s life into another. It is in the innumerable small acts by which every living thing receives something, alters it, and passes it on.

The plateau is inhabited by creatures.

But more truly, it is inhabited by circulation.
