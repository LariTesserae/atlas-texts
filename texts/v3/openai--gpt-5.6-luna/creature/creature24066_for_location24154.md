They live by contact.

Nothing in the pocket possesses a complete life of its own. Each creature is pressed against another creature, or against stone, or against the old machinery. The smallest animals are almost indistinguishable from movement itself: translucent legs flickering in seams, pale bodies folding and unfolding through the grit, mouths working constantly at salt, corrosion, shed armor, and the soft residues left by things that died before they could become still.

They are called nothing, because there is no one here to name them. Their distinctions are practical. Some are burrowers. Some are scrapers. Some are warm-seekers. Some are crushers. Some wait beneath the hoppers with their mouths open, while others make the mistake of passing over them. The names, if they could have names, would be verbs.

The smallest have no eyes, though many carry clusters of light-sensitive pits beneath their shells. They know the ceiling break only as brightness and danger. They retreat from the daylight not because it hurts them, but because anything that makes a shadow is large enough to eat them. Their bodies are nearly colorless where they live among saltstone, darkening to iron-brown where they feed against the rusted machinery. Some have learned to carry the taste of metal in their shells. Some are plated with mineral dust so thoroughly that they resemble splinters of the floor until they move.

They breed wherever a crack can hold a body still.

Their nurseries are not nests but temporary concentrations: a seam newly opened by pressure, the hollow beneath a displaced gear, the warm underside of a pump casing. Eggs are deposited in clusters that look like grains of fused salt. The young emerge already armored and immediately begin consuming whatever lies nearest. There is no parental care. A parent may pass over its own offspring and eat them if the young are slow enough. This does not make the population cruel. Cruelty requires choice. Here, hunger is simply the most reliable form of attention.

Above them move the grazers, though they graze on nothing living.

They are broad, low animals with many jointed supports and heavy backs. Their bodies have adapted to the pocket’s narrow height. They cannot rear up. They cannot leap. They advance by spreading their weight across the floor, their feet tapping ahead of them in overlapping patterns. Their undersides are soft and crowded with sensory filaments, while their backs are armored in dull plates that collect dust until each creature becomes a moving piece of the chamber.

They scrape mineral from the walls with circular mouths. They rasp corrosion from bolts. They worry at old belts, insulation, wire sheathing, and the hard pale films left by chemical deposits. Their feeding sounds like stone being filed. When they meet, they do not fight unless one is carrying a particularly rich patch of softened metal. Usually they simply turn, slowly and without alarm, and let the other pass.

Some of the largest are not hunters in the usual sense. They are reservoirs of appetite. They lie anchored beneath the wall bands, swollen and gray, with only their mouths and sensory whiskers visible. Smaller animals pass over their backs without noticing the slight inward drag of the skin. Then a circular mouth opens, and the traffic dips into it. A brief eddy forms. Shells click against teeth. The mouth closes. The surface becomes still again.

These stationary feeders are ancient by the standards of the chamber. They have grown around particular places: a warm fracture, a chemically rich trough, the vibrating foot of the conveyor. They have become part of the geography. Other animals use them as landmarks. The small ones shelter in their folds. The medium ones avoid the slow sweep of their mouths. When one finally dies, the chamber changes around it for years.

There are hunters that move with purpose.

They are longer and darker, with flexible bodies built to enter seams after fleeing prey. Their sensory whiskers spread across the floor in fans. They do not smell food so much as feel the disturbances left by it: a tremor in the grit, a faint repeating tap beneath a salt plate, the warmth of a body hidden under metal. They can remain motionless for hours while the lesser animals pour around them. Then they strike forward, not quickly but with terrible accuracy, and the carpet opens.

Their young are born knowing how to be still.

The hunters do not dominate the pocket. They are too expensive to maintain, too large to feed constantly. They vanish for long periods into recesses and emerge when the machinery begins to hum, because warmth brings the small animals out and makes them careless. Their bodies are scarred by the old machines. Some carry bolts embedded in their armor. One has grown a tooth around a length of copper wire. Another drags a broken chain that has become part of its movement, announcing itself with a faint metallic clatter.

There are climbers everywhere.

They ascend the abandoned ladder in streams, using the polished rungs as a route between the basin and the wall cavities. They gather inside the dead pump, in the hoppers, along the conveyor rollers. Some live their entire lives on the machinery and never touch bare stone. Their bodies have flattened to fit between housings and casings. They feed on the residues of electricity: not electricity itself, perhaps, but the compounds and heat and charged dust left by its passage. When the pump hums, they gather around it in a dense trembling skin.

The pump is not their parent, but they treat it with the persistence of worship.

When it falls quiet, they disperse. When it wakes, they return from every direction. They climb over one another to reach the warmest seams in the casing, and the casing becomes furred with pale bodies. The pump’s uneven vibration passes through them. In response, they pulse. For a while, the machine and the animals seem to breathe together.

No creature understands the machines. Understanding would be unnecessary. The machines provide heat, vibration, hollows, sharp edges, trapped dust, and occasional chemical richness. That is enough. A machine is not a tool here. It is a weather system with metal bones.

The conveyor is a road, though none of those who travel it could say where it leads. Its rollers turn whenever distant pressure moves through the shelf, and this intermittent motion draws whole populations toward it. Small animals ride the rollers briefly, clinging to rust, then fall away into the grit. Larger ones use the beltless frame as a boundary, sleeping beneath it or waiting along its edge. When a roller jams, the creatures gather around the obstruction and feed on the newly exposed corrosion. When it turns again, they scatter.

The hoppers are chambers of many purposes. One contains eggs. Another is filled with shed plates and the pale bodies of the dead. A third has become a feeding basin where several large animals rest nose to nose, opening and closing their mouths into the stream of smaller life. The hoppers are also refuges during disturbance. At the first deep vibration, they fill from below. Bodies pour into them until the metal seems to boil.

The inhabitants do not flee danger in the same way. The smallest vanish. The medium-sized flatten themselves against the floor, becoming nearly impossible to distinguish from the saltstone. The great plated animals turn their backs toward the vibration and wait. The anchored feeders close. The hunters move toward it.

Those hunters are not brave. They are responding to opportunity.

A disturbance drives prey from places where prey is usually safe. It exposes bodies, cracks, nests, and forgotten concentrations of food. The hunters know this without knowing anything. Their whiskers rise. Their bodies angle toward the source. For a few minutes, the chamber becomes predatory in a more visible way: pursuit crossing retreat, armor breaking the flow of smaller bodies, mouths opening wherever panic has made the animals dense.

But the disturbance also exposes the hunters. A larger thing may come from a recess. A section of ceiling may settle. The old machinery may jerk to life. The animals that advance toward danger are often the first to be crushed by it. Their remains enter the same economy as everything else.

Nothing is mourned. Yet nothing is lost.

A carcass is found by the smallest within minutes. They enter through the joints, behind the plates, beneath the mouth. They strip the soft interiors, then worry at the armor until it becomes thin enough to crack. Larger animals take what remains. The fragments are carried through the basin on countless feet. Some are dissolved into grit. Some are incorporated into shells. Some are pressed into the seams and become the pale substrate of a future generation.

The dead return as floor.

The inhabitants have no season except the cycles of warmth, light, vibration, and exhaustion. They gather under the white shaft when its illumination is weakest, not for warmth but because the dust raised there reveals movement. They spread outward when the engines below wake. They retreat from the walls when old chemical deposits begin to crumble. They follow no leader. The pocket has no center, only gradients: warmer, darker, richer, safer, more dangerous.

And yet, seen from above, they seem coordinated.

A pressure wave passes through the mineral shelf, and millions of bodies stop at once. The stillness travels outward in rings. Then one small creature moves, and another follows. A seam begins to pour. A plated back emerges. A hunter turns. The whole population resumes itself, not as a herd but as a single body made from incompatible parts.

They do not possess the factory. They have outlived its purpose.

They inhabit its hollows, digest its failures, polish its edges, and carry its dust from one generation to the next. Their ancestors entered through cracks long ago, when the machinery was still warm and the chamber may have smelled of oil, wet stone, and human labor. They have never seen the factory as abandoned. To them, its knocks are natural sounds. Its rust is a crop. Its buried wires are roots. Its dead conveyor is a migration route. Its sealed pump is a season that returns.

The pocket is not a ruin full of animals.

It is their body: salt for bone, machinery for skeleton, vibration for nerve, and innumerable moving mouths for metabolism. When the daylight shifts, they shift with it. When the mineral settles, they adapt to the new shape. When a machine dies completely, the population does not mourn the silence. It enters the silence, feeds there, breeds there, and makes the dead mechanism part of the living floor.
