The oldest inhabitants are called, if they are called anything at all, the **Pressure-Born**.

They are difficult to mistake for people, though they possess a kind of posture, a kind of attention, and—at a distance—the suggestion of faces. Their bodies are long and jointed, built less for swimming than for moving through architecture. They have six narrow limbs, each ending in a broad, flexible membrane that can flatten against a wall or catch a current like a sail. Between the limbs hangs a translucent body, pale as old ice, containing a dark cord of organs and a slow green pulse.

They do not have eyes. They read the world through pressure.

A change in current tells them that a door has opened three districts away. A vibration through a supporting arch informs them of a collapse before the masonry begins to move. They can distinguish one animal from another by the shape of its wake, and one chamber from another by the way water presses against its walls. To them, the city is not primarily a collection of rooms and towers. It is an immense instrument, perpetually sounding through their bodies.

They cling to the sides of submerged buildings in groups, arranged along seams and ledges. When the current is mild, they remain almost motionless, their limbs folded close, appearing to be mineral growths beneath the gray crust. When the water changes, they unfold all at once and move with startling speed. They do not swim through the city so much as pass from surface to surface: wall to ceiling, ceiling to arch, arch to the underside of a bridge. Their bodies become briefly indistinguishable from the water between each contact.

The young are born in sealed chambers deep within the oldest foundations. No one knows whether these chambers were made for them. They are warm only by comparison with the surrounding water, and their walls are lined with hundreds of shallow recesses. There the young cling in rows, nearly transparent, feeding on mineral films and whatever minute life the current carries through the cracks.

They remain there until they can feel the architecture.

The first sensation is usually a distant fracture: a small disturbance moving through the walls, too weak for any other creature to notice. The young respond by turning toward it. Later they learn the difference between a collapse and a settling, between an animal passing nearby and water forced through a narrowing shaft. By the time they leave the nursery, they can sense the hidden hollows beneath a floor and the pressure gathering behind a sealed wall.

They do not speak in air, and perhaps they do not speak in water either. They communicate by altering their position. A body pressed flat against an arch means danger below. Three bodies aligned along a seam may indicate a navigable passage. A sudden opening of all six limbs is warning, invitation, or mourning, depending on the direction of the current.

Their gatherings are quiet and formal. Dozens may assemble in a flooded plaza, clinging to the columns while a pressure front travels through the district. They hold their places as the water shifts around them. One by one, they release themselves and move toward the disturbance. From far away, the congregation resembles a cloud of pale leaves being drawn into a drain.

They do not repair the ruins. They cannot restore a fallen bridge or close a breached avenue. But they influence what survives.

When a wall begins to fail, they gather in its cavities, filling the channels through which water is cutting. Their bodies are flexible enough to occupy narrow cracks, and their secretions harden into a thin mineral film. This does not stop a collapse. It delays it. It changes the angle at which the blocks fall, redirects the current, preserves a chamber for another century or another thousand years.

Whether they do this deliberately is uncertain. The Pressure-Born may have no idea of preservation. They may simply respond to pain in the architecture as animals respond to injury in their own bodies. A failing arch produces a disturbance; the disturbance summons them; their presence alters the flow. The result resembles care, though resemblance is all that can be proved.

They travel along ancestral routes through the city. Some are so old that the routes have become tunnels of polished masonry, their walls worn smooth by generations of bodies. Others cross open water, where the travelers move in lines between towers, using invisible pressure gradients to carry them across distances too vast to swim against.

The lines sometimes contain hundreds of individuals. They are most visible at night—or what passes for night beneath the ocean—when the lantern organs in their chests begin to pulse. The lights do not illuminate anything. They are soft green contractions, appearing and vanishing in sequence. A procession may therefore look like a string of stars sinking through the ruins, one light after another extinguished by depth.

They have enemies, though predation is rarely violent here. The blind stairwell creatures consume the young when they can catch them. The broad raylike animals brush them from walls and swallow them incidentally with the mineral film beneath them. The largest creatures do not hunt them at all. They pass through their districts like moving geography, and the Pressure-Born scatter from the advance of their wakes.

When one of the great animals appears, the inhabitants do not flee randomly. They retreat into the architecture according to a practiced pattern, each body entering a different opening. They seem to know the entire shape of a building from the pressure on its outer face. A hundred creatures can vanish into a wall in a few seconds, leaving only their green pulses fading inside the cracks.

They are long-lived, but not immortal. An old Pressure-Born becomes increasingly mineralized. Its limbs stiffen. Its translucent skin clouds with deposits until it resembles the stone around it. Eventually it chooses a quiet place—a recessed stair, a drained chamber, the underside of a dome—and anchors itself there. It continues to sense the currents for years after it can no longer move.

Others gather around it.

They press their bodies against the old one and remain still while the water passes. The dying creature’s last sensations travel through them: the map of a collapsing district, the opening of a forgotten shaft, the approach of a current that will not arrive for centuries. Whether these impressions are memory or merely transmitted pressure is impossible to know. Afterward, the body is left in place. Mineral grows over it. The architecture takes it back.

Some buildings are crowded with such remains. Whole walls are furred with the pale outlines of former inhabitants, limbs and torsos preserved beneath layers of precipitate. The living move among them without distinction. An ancestral body is not a monument. It is another surface, another contour in the water, another part of the city’s reading.

The Pressure-Born have no rulers and no visible possessions. They do not mark territory except through continued presence. A chamber belongs to those who can remain in its currents. A route belongs to those who remember how to cross it. When two groups meet, they may cling to opposite walls for days, sensing one another through the intervening water. Then the current changes, and the division ends without agreement.

They may once have been made here.

Their bodies bear faint internal geometries: repeating chambers, articulated membranes, organs arranged with a precision unlike that of the other animals. In certain light, the bones—or whatever serves as bones—show straight angles and regular spacing. Some of the oldest sealed rooms contain impressions shaped like them, though the impressions may be accidental, or may belong to something that preceded them.

If they were designed, they have forgotten their purpose.

Perhaps they were intended to maintain the city. Perhaps they were inhabitants engineered to survive its drowning. Perhaps they are descendants of the builders, altered beyond recognition by pressure, darkness, and time. The Pressure-Born themselves offer no answer. Their oldest individuals spend their final centuries listening to the walls, and the walls speak only in collapse, flow, and distance.

From the surface islands, one sometimes sees them below.

A pale procession moves beneath the dark water, crossing the drowned terraces. It follows no visible road, but bends around towers and descends through open shafts. Green lights pulse within the line. Far beneath, another procession answers. Between them, the ancient city shifts by a fraction of its width.

The inhabitants pause.

Then, together, they change direction.
