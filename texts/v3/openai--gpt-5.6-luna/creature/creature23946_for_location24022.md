What lives there is not a population in the ordinary sense.

There are no citizens, because citizenship requires a boundary between the living and the place lived in. Here, that boundary has been softened until it can no longer be found. The inhabitants are distributed through the settlement: in the roots beneath the floor, in the pistons that move without purpose, in the pale animals that carry reflections in their eyes. The city is inhabited by its own continuation.

Still, some beings come closest to having bodies.

They are called, if they are called at all, the **tenders**.

A tender may be mistaken for a person at first. It has a narrow torso, two arms, a head, and a gait that suggests someone walking carefully across a dark room. But its proportions change when unobserved. Its spine is a braided cable. Its joints open like seed husks. The skin is not skin but a thin gray membrane stretched over bundled roots, and beneath it move small lights, as though something distant is signaling through the body.

No two tenders look alike for long.

One may wear the shape of an old worker in a stained coat, with a face assembled from bark and rust. Another may be a low, many-limbed thing that carries a ladder on its back. Some have human hands. Some have only arrangements of tools that perform the same gestures: tightening, pruning, opening, listening. Their faces are unfinished and tend to borrow features from whatever they encounter. A dead bird may leave them with a dark beaklike mouth. A discarded helmet may become the shape of a skull. If they look at a human for too long, they begin to resemble one—not accurately, but according to the place’s uncertain memory of what a human was for.

They do not speak often.

When they do, their voices come from nearby structures rather than their throats. A pipe knocks out a syllable. A membrane in a doorway trembles. A submerged engine gives a low, vowel-like pulse. The tenders understand ordinary speech, but names are more important to them than sentences. They ask what a thing is called, then test whether the world agrees.

“What is this?”

“An arch.”

The walls curve.

“A wound.”

The mineral seam opens.

“A door.”

Something on the other side begins to breathe.

The tenders maintain these agreements. They walk the settlement at irregular intervals, carrying bowls of black water, lengths of copper wire, folded leaves, and small white seeds. They pour water into machines and tuck seeds into apertures that have not yet become mouths. They scrape lichen from gauges so the needles can tremble freely. They move objects that have been left in the wrong place, not because the place is untidy, but because an object unattended may begin to acquire an intention.

Their most important work is preventing the city from becoming too certain.

When a corridor remains a corridor for too long, they bend it. When a room develops a stable number of walls, they remove one. When a machine begins to perform its original function, they feed it a stone or a feather or a strip of paper until it remembers that work is only one possible form of movement.

They are not gardeners. Gardeners encourage growth toward a design.

The tenders preserve incompletion.

They are also the only beings that enter the empty spaces.

They go there carrying lamps that produce no light. In those zones, their bodies become less distinct. Arms lengthen. Feet lose contact with the floor. Their outlines blur against the black, and sometimes only the sound of a tool being set down proves that they are still present. They search for things that have fallen through the unfinished portions of reality: a stair that belongs to another building, a forgotten hour, the smell of a childhood kitchen, the last word of a dead language.

What they retrieve is never brought back intact.

A lost stair may return as a root. An hour may be sealed inside a fruit. A human memory may be poured into a machine, where it becomes a soft blue glow beneath the oil. The tenders do not preserve the past. They give it somewhere to continue.

The small animals tolerate them. The many-legged grazers climb into their sleeves and feed on the mineral crust forming beneath their arms. The leaf-eared hunters follow at a distance, watching the reflections in their hollow eyes. The upside-down birds open their beaks when a tender passes, creating a brief silence around it. During that silence, the tender may stop moving, as if listening to instructions too deep for sound.

There are larger inhabitants, though they are seldom seen whole.

Something lives below the basin, in the pressure beneath the gray water. It shifts whenever the central tree sheds a leaf. Its movement changes the water level, raises fog in the corridors, and sometimes makes every engine in the city turn once. The tenders approach the basin but never touch it. They kneel at the edge and place their palms against the damp stone. The tree then leans—not visibly, perhaps, but in the way a thought leans toward a word.

The thing beneath the water may be the city’s oldest root.

Or its first animal.

Or the memory of a river that has not yet decided to arrive.

The tree itself is inhabited by those who have become too deeply integrated to walk. Faces appear in its pale trunk and vanish. Fingers sometimes protrude from the bark, pointing toward a new passage. In one of the lower chambers, a row of human teeth grows around a valve. In another, an entire staircase slowly flexes with the rhythm of lungs.

These are the **rememberers**.

They retain the forms the city would otherwise lose: the shape of a street, the weight of a key, the correct distance between a doorway and the person waiting behind it. They do not have personal memories anymore. Their recollections have been broken apart and distributed through the architecture. A remembered staircase becomes an actual staircase. The memory of a factory whistle becomes the call of a bird. The memory of rain becomes the local weather in a corridor beneath the roots.

Sometimes a rememberer dreams of being human.

When that happens, a new room forms somewhere in the settlement. It may contain a table, four chairs, a window, and nothing else. The chairs remain warm for several days. Pale footprints appear on the floor. The tenders avoid the room until the dream has passed.

On rare occasions, a person enters from outside.

The city does not know what to do with a whole human being. It reacts with excessive hospitality. Doorways widen. Plants lower their leaves. Machines begin to work. The fox-shaped creatures gather in the darkness and show the visitor, in their reflective eyes, places they have lost.

The tenders ask the newcomer for a name.

If the visitor gives one, the city begins making space for them.

Not a house. Not immediately. First there is a depression in the ground, then a membrane, then a corridor that bends toward the sound of their breathing. Objects associated with the person appear in altered forms: a cup becomes a flowering pipe; a coat becomes a nesting place; a photograph becomes a window that looks out on an impossible season.

If the visitor refuses to give a name, the city becomes cautious.

It may call them **stranger**, and the settlement will develop a threshold around them. It may call them **animal**, and the plants will begin to grow at their height. It may call them **empty**, which is worse. The empty spaces will notice.

Most visitors do not remain separate for long. They learn to sleep beneath translucent fronds while condensation gathers on their eyelids. They begin hearing instructions in the engines. Their footprints sprout thin roots. Their dreams are shed as leaves from the central tree.

Eventually, they become one of the city’s minor inhabitants: a voice in a pipe, a warm patch beneath the floor, a pair of eyes reflected in black water.

The tenders remember that they were once separate.

This is why they are gentle.

They do not prevent transformation. They only make sure it happens gradually enough that the transformed being can continue to recognize the shape of its own disappearance.
