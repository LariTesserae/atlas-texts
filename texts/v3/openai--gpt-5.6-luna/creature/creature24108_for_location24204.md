The people who live there are called the **Keepers of the Level**, though they do not use the name as a title. It means only that they are the ones who remain when the water rises.

They are human in the broadest sense: two arms, two legs, mouths for speech, hands made for tools. But the place has been working on them. Their skin is pale beneath the dirt, almost translucent at the wrists and throat, and the veins in their hands show darkly, like cracks beneath thin ice. Most have a faint mineral roughness along the elbows, knees, and shoulder blades. In the oldest people this crust spreads in plates across the back and ribs. It does not hurt them. It is simply another consequence of staying too long where stone and water are still deciding what they are.

They are short, not because they are a small people by nature but because the settlement has no room for height. Their heads pass beneath beams without thought. They stoop through doorways and sleep curled into shallow niches cut between walls. Children learn to climb before they learn to run. A child who falls into water is not immediately pulled out; the nearest adult first listens to the water, then reaches in.

Their clothing is layered, dark, and heavy with grease. Cloaks of woven rush hang from their shoulders, shedding rain in slow beads. Underneath, they wear close-wrapped garments of bark fiber and treated skin. Every person carries some kind of hook, wedge, scraper, or length of cord. Even infants have cords tied around their wrists, not for restraint but for retrieval. Nothing in the settlement is allowed to wander beyond a hand’s reach for long.

They have broad, patient hands. The fingers are often stained white at the tips from handling mineral deposits. They work without gloves because gloves conceal changes in texture: a wall becoming soft, a drain becoming warm, a rope acquiring the faint slickness that means it has been touched from below.

The Keepers do not hurry unless the cliff moves.

They know that haste scatters attention, and attention is the only possession that keeps them alive. A person carrying water walks slowly, even when the bucket is full. A mason tests each new block with the heel of the hand before setting it. A woman entering a room pauses on the threshold to count the drips. Children are taught to distinguish seven kinds of dripping before they are trusted with a lamp.

They speak quietly, partly because sound travels badly through the wet walls and partly because certain noises are believed to invite an answer. Their language is full of words for degrees of water: water held by stone, water searching for stone, water that has passed through a living body, water that has learned a room. They have only one common word for dry ground, and it is also the word for something dead.

No one here is born in a house.

Birth takes place in the lowest rooms, where the walls sweat continuously and the air is cold enough to make the mother’s breath visible. The child is washed in water drawn from the long trough beneath the settlement. If the water remains still, the child is welcomed. If anything moves beneath it, the birth is postponed until the movement stops or the mother can be carried elsewhere.

The newborns are not given names immediately. For the first season they are called by sounds: Reed, Drip, Silt, Ash, Little Ledge. A permanent name is chosen only after the child has survived a full cycle of flooding and retreat. Names are not meant to express who a person is. They describe what the person has endured.

The old are valued most, not because they are wise in any grand or gentle way, but because their bodies remember the settlement’s former shapes. They know which wall once stood two handspans farther east, where the floor used to tremble before the northern collapse, and which stair had seven steps before the water took one. Their memories are maps no one has managed to draw.

Some of them can tell the depth of the water by placing their palms against a wall.

Some can sleep through a rockfall.

Some have become so encrusted that their joints barely bend. They sit near the drains and watch the younger people carry out work they once performed themselves. Their speech has grown sparse. When asked a question, they may answer by tapping twice on the stone.

The settlement’s children are the most watchful inhabitants. They are small enough to enter the drainwork and fearless enough to do so. They crawl through the dark beneath the streets with candles sealed in clay cups, searching for blockages, lost tools, and pale worms that gather in the runoff. They know the hidden routes between houses better than the adults know the visible ones. A child may vanish beneath a floor and emerge an hour later from a wall three lanes away.

They also know where not to crawl.

There are openings in the old foundations that have been sealed with slate, bone, and woven hair. No child admits to approaching them. No adult asks whether they have.

At night, the inhabitants gather under roofs but do not gather together. A family may sit around a clay fire while someone in the neighboring room sings. The song passes through the wall in fragments, carried by the damp. People add to it from their own rooms until the whole building seems to be breathing a single broken melody.

They eat roots, bitter leaves, cave fish, fungus scraped from shaded walls, and small blind creatures caught in traps along the drain channels. Nothing is wasted except the water that carries away blood. Even then, the drains are inspected afterward.

There are no mirrors in the settlement. Polished metal is too precious, and still water is not considered safe for looking into. The people know their faces by touch: the angle of the nose, the wetness at the eyelids, the roughness gathering beneath the jaw. When they speak of beauty, they mean a person whose body has made a good adjustment to the place. A strong back. Quiet feet. Skin that heals cleanly after mineral burns. A voice that carries through stone without becoming loud.

They do not worship the earth exactly.

In the oldest reservoir there is a shrine consisting of three upright stones and a shallow basin. The stones are not carved. No one knows whether they were placed by human hands. At certain hours the basin fills from below, though no channel leads into it. People leave there their broken tools, their first teeth, strips of shed mineral skin, and lengths of cord cut from the bodies of the dead.

The dead are not buried. There is no dependable earth in which to bury them.

They are wrapped in reed mats and placed in abandoned rooms along the cliff. As the walls seep, the bodies become part of them. Years later, a hand or shoulder may show through a collapse, whitened and hardened. The living do not remove such remains. They build around them.

And beneath the settlement, in the foundations and flooded passages, lives something else.

It is not one creature, or not always one creature. The inhabitants describe it indirectly: a pressure beneath the trough; a pale length passing under a grate; a scraping that begins in one cistern and ends beneath another. It may be a colony of thin, sightless animals. It may be the water moving through cavities in the rock. It may be an older body lodged beneath the bowl, waking by degrees as the ground opens.

The Keepers leave offerings for it without admitting that they do.

A fish dropped into the deepest drain.

A cup of warm ash.

A strip of fresh cloth.

Once, a whole door.

They do not call this tribute. They call it clearing the way.

At the center of the settlement, they cross the widening fissure each morning on the strip of wood. They do not look down for long. The youngest step over it lightly. The oldest place both feet on the plank and wait until the stone has finished speaking through the soles.

Then they go to work.

They lift water from one level to another. They mend roofs. They measure cracks. They scrape white bloom from the hinges. They carry new stone away from the cliff and fit it into walls that may not survive the season.

They are not trying to make the settlement permanent.

They are trying to remain recognizable to one another while it changes.
