What lives there is not a population so much as a succession of accommodations.

The first inhabitants are the films.

They lie over brick, iron, glass, and bone-colored concrete in thin, iridescent skins. In the weak plant-light they appear gray, but when a current passes over them they show colors that have no business surviving in that darkness: bruised violet, oil-green, a brief metallic gold. They eat what the city gives them—rust, machine grease, old paint, the residue of batteries, the faint heat of electrical fields. They spread most rapidly along cables and around the bases of lamps, where the stone is warmest.

They do not grow at random. Their edges advance in branching patterns, and their branches sometimes meet. Where they touch, they exchange something. A wall becomes a map of their decisions: broad blank patches where nothing can live, narrow roads along which the tiny jointed organisms travel.

Those small white organisms are the district’s scavengers.

They move in files, never hurried, each one following the disturbance left by the one before it. They have many minute legs and bodies so thin that they vanish when viewed from the side. In darkness they are almost invisible, but the pale mineral coats their backs, making their lines resemble writing across the walls. They clean the films, loosen the rust, and carry heat from one warm surface to another. When a pump begins, they turn toward it. When a lamp fails, they gather beneath the dead fixture and remain there for several days, as if waiting for its return.

They have no queen, no nest, and no visible means of communication. Yet they respond together. A pressure change in the factory can turn an entire colony. A distant impact will send them flowing into cracks. They do not flee from danger so much as redistribute themselves around it.

The black reeds are more deliberate.

They grow wherever warmth leaks from the machinery, thin as wires and translucent as frozen blood. Their roots occupy concrete seams, valve housings, and the spaces beneath floor plates. They feed on heat, but heat alone does not satisfy them. They also drink the small electrical differences that gather around the old equipment. When a motor starts, the reeds bend toward it. When the current weakens, they loosen and drift upright again.

In the warmest chambers they grow tall enough to form thickets. The organisms pass among them without touching. The reeds close around anything that brushes them, not violently, but with the slow certainty of a hand remembering how to hold. Afterward they reopen, leaving a faint mineral dust on whatever has passed.

The seal-shaped animal belongs to the streets.

It comes from the upper darkness, from the broader lake beyond the district’s walls, though no one knows how it enters. It may use cracks in the foundations. It may follow the pressure of the pumps. It may simply know a way that does not exist for anything else.

Its body is nearly transparent and soft-looking, but it is not fragile. Old scars run through its sides like cloudy threads. Its ribs show only when it turns beneath a lamp. The whiskers around its face are long, jointed, and sensitive enough to find a door handle beneath silt or distinguish a living current from the dead movement of a pipe.

It feeds slowly. First it presses its face to a wall and waits. Then the small organisms emerge from their films, and the mineral mats tremble. The animal takes them in without biting. Its mouth opens and closes like a valve.

It does not fear the machines. It has learned their schedules. When the stamping press descends, it leaves the central streets and waits beneath an apartment block until the vibration has passed. When the pumps reverse, it follows the new current through alleys too narrow for its body, folding itself around corners. It knows where air remains. It knows which doors open into water, which into rain, and which into rooms that have held the same trapped breath for decades.

Sometimes it lies on a submerged roof and listens with its entire body.

Then it may rise and swim directly toward something no lamp can reveal.

In the warm tanks beneath the factory live the sparks.

They gather by the hundreds in dense layers, though each creature is small enough to fit in a hand. Their bodies are dark until the blue light reaches them. Then each one produces a brief internal flare, and the tank fills with synchronized stars. The flashes travel through the school in waves, not from front to back but everywhere at once, as if the whole mass shares one nervous system.

They are not fish. They have no fins, only folded membranes that open and close in the warm chemical water. They feed on the black nodules carried by the conveyors and on whatever leaks from the machinery. Their bodies change according to the tank: some become broad and slow, some threadlike, some hard-shelled and nearly motionless. When the factory’s pumps disturb the water, they arrange themselves around the intake grilles, shielding the smallest among them.

No one knows whether the lights are language, warning, or digestion.

The factory responds to them.

A pump that has cycled at the same interval for a century occasionally pauses for no mechanical reason. A tank valve opens before pressure requires it. The stamping press waits. The robotic arms turn toward the glass and remain still while the sparks pass through their synchronized display.

The factory is not alive in any simple sense. It has no appetite except the appetites built into it, no intention except the continuation of its routines. Yet its routines have become a body. Its pipes are vessels. Its pumps are organs. Its chambers hold different pressures like different moods. The old production schedule persists not because anyone commands it, but because every abandoned component has settled into dependence on the others.

The factory makes conditions.

The conditions make life.

The life alters the conditions.

No creature rules the district. There is no central mind beneath the tanks, no survivor in a sealed shelter, no hidden colony waiting to emerge. But the district has developed a kind of distributed awareness, spread through vibration, pressure, current, and growth. A door opening in the eastern blocks can change the behavior of organisms in the plant. A crack in the ice can make the reeds flatten three streets away. When the blind animal enters, the mineral films withdraw from certain walls before it arrives.

The city does not think in thoughts. It notices through consequence.

There are also the remnants that never became anything else.

The coats behind the shelter door remain coats. The chairs remain chairs. The empty tram remains a tram, though silt has filled its seats and the organisms have built pale roads beneath its floor. The statues remain turned away from the plant. Yet these objects have acquired a place in the circulation. Currents move around them. Creatures shelter beneath them. Their surfaces hold the history of pressure and sediment.

The coats sometimes stir when the impossible rain falls.

No animal lives among them. No current seems strong enough to move them. Still, one sleeve will lift, then another, the fabric turning as if someone inside has shifted their weight. The motion ends before it becomes convincing.

In the square, the statues gather mineral along their backs. Their faces are hidden from the plant, their stone hands closed or hanging at their sides. The blind animal avoids them. The small white organisms cross their feet but do not climb. When the ice groans overhead, all the statues shed a fine veil of dust at once.

Perhaps they are only statues.

Perhaps the district has kept them facing outward because something in the streets still expects to be watched from beyond the walls.

What lives beneath the frozen sea is therefore not merely what moves.

It is the factory’s patience. It is the reeds bending toward a motor. It is the pale traffic of scavengers under a dead lamp. It is the tankful of creatures flashing together without making a sound. It is the blind animal tracing its ancient circuit, carrying the shape of the city inside its nerves.

And it is the darkness itself, thickened by all these small continuities.

When the ice cracks, everything living becomes still. The sparks go dark. The reeds fold. The white organisms disappear into the masonry. The animal presses itself flat against the nearest foundation. Even the films seem to contract.

For a moment, the city is reduced to pressure.

Then the ice settles.

The tanks begin to glow again. The pumps turn. A mineral fan opens beside a leaking valve. Somewhere in the upper streets, the transparent animal lifts its head and resumes its route.

The district continues—not inhabited, exactly, but inhabited enough.
