They are called, where names still matter, the **Settled**.

No two observers agree on what a Settled one looks like. From a distance, it may resemble one of the long-backed grazers with its head lowered to the stone. Up close, it may be a figure of black felt and pale joints, or a thin animal folded into the shape of a person, or a person whose outline has been worn smooth until it belongs to the country. Its number of limbs depends on the light. Its face is usually turned away.

The Settled do not build homes. They inherit positions.

A place on the plateau may be occupied for so long that occupation becomes part of the geology. A broad shelf belongs to a family, though the family may consist of one body spread across several shadows. A mineral seam may be watched by a creature that has not blinked in ten thousand years. Around the seven-rung ladder, the cairns are tended by those who descend beneath the world and return from above, carrying no objects and remembering no journey.

They do not seem to eat, though they lower their mouths to the stone. They do not sleep, though they become indistinguishable from the resting forms around them. Their bodies receive dust, mineral, light, and the slow pressure of time. Sometimes a Settled one loses a limb. Sometimes it grows another from the place where the missing limb touched the ground. Sometimes it sheds its entire outer shape and leaves behind a hollow skin, which continues to face the direction its owner once faced.

The animals accept them without distinction.

A horned grazer may pass through a Settled one’s ribs. A cluster of pale shells may open beneath its feet. Neither event is unusual. The inhabitants of the high country do not recognize the boundary between body and surroundings as something worth defending. They are not solitary, but neither are they social. They occupy one another the way stones occupy a wall: in contact, under pressure, without intimacy.

Their young—if they have young—are found already grown.

They emerge from the shallow cracks in the plateau, or from beneath the bodies of the dead, or from shadows cast by creatures that have never existed. A newly arrived one may spend several centuries learning where its limbs end. It will stand among the herds, adjusting itself by fractions. Eventually it will become still enough to belong.

The oldest inhabitants are difficult to distinguish from landmarks. One lies beneath a low overhang, a long dark body plated with gray mineral. Another has become the wall crossing the plateau; its vertebrae are the flat stones, its spine running straight toward the horizon. The solitary hut on the ridge is not empty. Something within it has been waiting beside the sealed doorway since before the roof was made. When the light dims, its shadow sometimes appears outside, standing upright and looking down across the country.

The Settled have no language suited to mouths.

They communicate by arrangement. A body shifts half an inch. A cluster opens. A shadow points where no limb points. One animal leaves a space unoccupied, and another recognizes the space as a warning, an invitation, or a recollection. Their messages may require centuries to complete. A procession of tiny creatures crossing a mineral shelf can be a sentence. A herd taking one step can be an answer.

They do not ask questions because the plateau has already answered everything it intends to answer.

What remains for them is maintenance.

They keep the old boundaries. They return loose stones to the places from which they rolled. They preserve the exact edges of the pale mats and scrape the black plants clean when dust gathers on their translucent leaves. They stand around the ladder so that it will continue to descend only as far as a person’s height. They arrange the cairns with stones that cast the correct shadows.

When a creature dies, the Settled do not bury it. They make room around it.

The corpse remains where it fell, slowly acquiring other lives. Burrowers enter its chest. Shells fasten to its joints. Black stalks rise through its skull. In time, the dead animal becomes a low hill crowded with quiet organisms. Yet the country does not consider this transformation a departure. The dead continue to occupy their places. Their shadows persist, sometimes with more clarity than the bodies that cast them.

There are beings that live only during the dimmings.

They are narrow and pale, with too many hinges in their legs. They unfold from the undersides of stones and from the dark intervals between one creature and another. Their bodies are never fully present; portions of them remain elsewhere, perhaps beneath the plateau, perhaps in one of the other skies visible through the dark seam. They move quickly only when unseen. When looked at directly, they become part of the ground.

These are the ones that approach travelers.

Not to hunt them. Not to guide them. They have no hunger that a traveler could satisfy and no destination they could reveal. They come near because a moving person is a disturbance in the final arrangement of things. They study the traveler’s outline, imitate its distance, and eventually take up the position the traveler has left behind.

That is why the distant creature keeps appearing on the same ridge.

It is not following. It is learning the traveler’s place.

If the traveler stays long enough, the creature will begin to resemble them. First in posture, then in proportion. Its shadow will acquire the traveler’s hands. Its head will turn when the traveler turns. If the traveler looks directly at it, there may be a moment in which the two figures exchange distances: the creature suddenly close, the traveler impossibly far away, both still standing on the same shelf.

The high country has no predators because nothing there needs to be removed.

It has no prey because nothing there needs to be overcome.

Its inhabitants persist by becoming less separate from the world around them, until a creature is only the temporary emphasis of a ridge, a shadow, a mineral stain, or a patch of standing air. They do not live in the plateau so much as continue its long process of settling. Each body is another surface reaching its final form.

And yet, beneath their silence, there is something like attention.

Every eye—when there is an eye—faces the horizon. Every folded head, every antenna, every unfinished gesture is turned toward some distant alteration that has not happened. The Settled wait for no event. Waiting implies that time might reward them.

They are simply present when the world changes, and present when it does not.

Should the rain return through the seam in the sky, they will lift their faces. Should the mountains rise again, they will remain among the old shelves. Should another world appear beside this one, greener and loud with weather, they will not cross toward it.

They will make room.

Then they will close ranks, settle their shadows, and continue occupying the country that has outlasted every reason to be alive.
