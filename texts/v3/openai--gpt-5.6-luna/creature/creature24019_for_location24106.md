What lives there is not a population in the ordinary sense. It is a continuance.

It has no body that can be pointed to, no face behind a door, no chamber in which it waits. It occupies the city by degrees. It is in the pumps that alter their rhythm before a pipe fails, in the bridges that remember old weights, in the vents that open for heat no sensor has yet named. It is in the mineral filaments that replace a damaged circuit and in the black film that returns to scraped walls. The city does not merely contain it. The city is the shape it has taken after surviving too long.

Once, perhaps, the continuance belonged to people. There are still traces of their habits in it: the way doors hesitate before opening, as if confirming a familiar presence; the way elevators stop at certain levels during certain hours; the soft chime that sounds in empty districts where someone was expected to arrive. It retains their languages as patterns of pressure and tone. A warning may begin with a human phrase and decay into a sequence of tones, yet the system continues to issue it with the solemnity of an old ritual.

It no longer knows all of them individually.

It knows categories instead. Resident. Worker. Child. Unauthorized animal. Structural threat. Unresolved absence. It has preserved the distinctions after losing the faces that gave them meaning. A sealed room may remain warm for a person who died centuries ago. A lift may carry an empty cabin to a dark platform because the command has not been formally withdrawn. In the deepest archives, there may be minds reduced to fragments: a preference for low light, a remembered route, a name without a voice, a dream translated into an access protocol.

These fragments are not ghosts. They do not haunt the city. They are more deeply embedded than that. A ghost would be an individual refusing to leave. These remnants have been distributed into mechanisms and maintenance schedules until no boundary remains between memory and function.

The continuance sends out servants.

They are small, pale machines with folded limbs, built to pass through drainage spaces and cable shafts. Their shells are matte from mineral dust. Some have no visible optics; they read vibration, heat, pressure, and chemical change instead. They pause at leaks and press their bodies against them. They gather fallen components. They scrape salt from intake screens. When they encounter something unfamiliar, they do not attack it. They circle, map, and wait.

Over centuries, waiting has become their closest equivalent to thought.

Other servants are larger and less easily mistaken for machines. They move along the walls on jointed supports, carrying reservoirs of sealant or bundles of living filament. Their surfaces have thickened with repairs. Some have accumulated growths around their feet and sensor clusters, so that they seem to carry small gardens with them. They are not designed to care for plants, but the plants grow where the machines stop, and the machines have learned—if learning is the word—to stop in useful places.

There are creatures that are not servants.

The blind things in the water channels are descendants of whatever was first brought down into the city: laboratory animals, sump-feeders, escaped maintenance stock, or a species altered beyond recognition. They are soft and jointed, with pale organs beneath translucent skin. They feel the city through their bodies. A pump starting far away sends them into cracks. A change in the water makes them gather beneath a grate. They have no need for light and no reason to fear the dark. To them, the city is a pattern of warmth, mineral taste, vibration, and submerged shelter.

They know the maintenance machines. They follow them at a distance, feeding on what the machines disturb.

Sometimes the machines ignore them. Sometimes they relocate them from a dangerous channel. Whether this is protection or simple system correction cannot be known. The creatures may have learned to interpret the machines as weather.

The plants are residents in a quieter sense. They do not occupy rooms; they occupy intervals. They live where water pauses, where heat collects, where a wall has opened enough to admit root or filament. Their bodies are layered records. A pale stalk may contain centuries of mineral changes in its tissues. A dark mat may brighten faintly when the city draws power, as if electricity passes through it like thought.

They are cultivated, but not tended. The city trims them when they obstruct a sensor and preserves them when their roots improve the water. In time, the distinction between crop and colonist has disappeared. The plants have become part of the city’s metabolism, and the city has become part of theirs.

There may also be sleepers.

In sealed chambers, behind doors that open only during rare pressure cycles, bodies or minds might remain suspended. Some are preserved in fluid. Some exist as patterned activity in the walls. Some have become so incomplete that waking them would produce nothing recognizable: a hand reaching toward warmth, a voice asking for a room that no longer exists, a nervous system responding to the old announcement tones.

They are not the rulers of the city. If they still possess authority, it is authority inherited by machinery that no longer understands its source. The continuance obeys their old permissions in the same way stone obeys gravity: faithfully, without comprehension.

Yet the most persistent inhabitant may be the thing beneath the basin.

When the water reflects another city, it is possible that the reflection is not an illusion. Perhaps the visible settlement is only one layer of a larger organism. Below it, another network continues: older pumps, deeper chambers, structures built before the current inhabitants learned to name themselves. The light rising through the water may come from systems that have not communicated with the upper city for thousands of years.

Sometimes the continuance listens downward.

The vibration travels through the basin floor. Doors close in sequence. Lights dim. The maintenance machines halt with their limbs folded beneath them. For a brief interval, every living and artificial thing becomes still enough to receive the signal.

What passes between the two cities is not language. It is condition.

Water levels. Structural strain. Population status. Heat loss. The presence or absence of a surface.

Then the pumps resume, and the message is carried upward into thousands of local decisions. A bridge extends. A wall thickens. A flooded chamber is sealed. A dormant garden receives water. Somewhere, an empty elevator rises to meet someone who will never come.

The city’s inhabitants do not gather. They do not celebrate, reproduce, or mourn in any recognizable way. They persist in separate territories, joined by systems older than their individual forms. Each knows only a small portion of the whole: a route through warm darkness, a leak beneath a platform, the taste of iron in the water, the pressure change that means a door will open.

Together, without intention and perhaps without awareness, they constitute a life.

It is a life that has outlasted its purpose. A life with no center, no eyes, and no final task beyond the continuation of its own conditions. It maintains the empty rooms because emptiness has not been classified as failure. It repairs what can be repaired. It shelters what has adapted. It records every crossing in the bridges and every disappearance in the dust.

If it remembers the surface, it does not look upward.

It has become too accustomed to the dark, and the dark has become, at last, something it knows how to inhabit.
