The Low Country is inhabited by things that have not learned to be separate.

Its life does not arrange itself neatly into beasts, plants, and weather. The categories remain, but they bleed into one another. A root becomes a burrow. A burrow becomes a nest. A nest becomes a trap for leaves and insects, then a raft when the flood comes. An animal carries seeds in its gut, fungus in its fur, mites beneath its eyelids. The river takes a tree, the tree takes a bank, the bank makes an island, and the island is immediately occupied.

Nothing lives alone for long.

The largest creatures are the reed-grazers, low and broad, with six powerful legs and skin the color of wet clay. Their bodies are warm, their sides marked by pale bands where the mud dries after feeding. From the front of the skull extends a flexible trunk, not long enough to reach the high branches but perfectly suited to uprooting water plants, stripping bark, and probing beneath mats of floating vegetation. They travel in groups that appear disorganized until something threatens them. Then the adults close around the young, the trunks lift, and the whole herd becomes a bristling, mud-spattered wall.

They are never entirely silent. Even while feeding, they mutter to one another through their chests: low, rolling notes that can be felt more easily than heard. At night, when fog lies across the marsh, those sounds move through it in all directions. A herd may be close enough to touch or half a mile away. Their tracks fill with water almost as soon as they are made, and by morning small animals have entered them to drink.

The grazers are followed by hunters.

Some are swift, narrow-bodied animals with four low legs and long, flexible backs. They move through reeds without breaking them, flowing where the grazers blunder. Their eyes reflect little light. They depend more on scent, vibration, and the change in pressure made by a moving body. Others wait in the trees, wrapped around pale branches so completely that they seem to be lengths of dead vine. They descend only when the prey has passed beneath them. Their bodies strike, coil, and withdraw, leaving behind a widening disturbance in the leaves.

The most patient predators live in the water.

They are not always large. Some are no longer than an arm, but they can remain still for hours in a shallow pool, buried beneath silt with only a fringe of sensory filaments exposed. Others are vast enough to bend the reeds when they turn. They possess no fixed territory. Floods move them. Warm currents draw them upward. A carcass may keep them in one channel for a week, until the water changes and they disappear without struggle into another.

The people who enter the Low Country are animals of a different kind, though the country does not grant them any special distinction.

They come in small groups, carrying fire in shells and gourds, their bodies wrapped against insects, rain, and the tearing growth. They know how to move without insisting on a path. A person who has traveled here for many seasons steps on roots, stone shelves, and the floating backs of old vegetation, never trusting the mud simply because it looks firm. They touch the ground before resting. They smell water before drinking it. They sleep lightly, if they sleep at all.

Their shelters are brief things: a lean-to beneath a split tree, a platform of lashed branches above a wet hollow, a screen of leaves tied between roots. Nothing is built to last. The country would take offense at permanence, or perhaps merely consume it. Fiber rots. Stakes loosen. A shelter becomes a nest for beetles, then a trellis for flowering vines, then an indistinguishable part of the forest floor.

The people carry little because everything carried must be defended. A stone blade wrapped in leaves. A fire-drill of hard wood. Shell hooks, bone points, braided cord. Their baskets are suspended from the body rather than set down, because anything left on the ground may be entered, eaten, buried, or carried away. They gather water from leaves and warm springs, but they also know that a clear pool may contain a mouth, an egg, or a poison released only when disturbed.

They do not conquer the country. They negotiate with it, and often lose.

A hunt may end with the spear abandoned in a flooded channel. A fishing net may return full of eels and broken branches, or catch something that bites through the mesh. A person may vanish between one bank and the next, not dramatically, but by stepping where the crust has formed over warm mud. The others mark the place with a stone if they can find one, then continue. The Low Country does not pause for grief. It surrounds grief with frogs, insects, and the smell of flowers opening.

Those who survive become difficult to distinguish from the land. They wear mud on their legs and pollen on their hair. Tiny leeches darken their ankles. Seeds cling to their clothing. Their hands are scarred by basalt and thorn. They learn the names of living landmarks, though names are not ownership here. A split-crowned tree is remembered because it leans over a channel that moves. A colony of white fungus is remembered because the ground beneath it is warm. A flock of red-throated birds is remembered because, for a time, it returns.

The country remembers differently.

It remembers through recurrence. The birds return to a drowned forest even as the forest sinks. The grazers follow the same broad current of edible growth until the current changes. Predators revisit the places where floods strand fish. People seek the sleeping-animal ridge because water moves strangely around it. None of these are permanent landmarks. They are habits in a landscape without fixed shape.

The smaller inhabitants are everywhere.

Shelled things cling to the rocks in wet, shifting colonies. Pale crabs tunnel through the banks, their claws leaving small paired marks around every collapse. Amphibians hang beneath roots, their bodies nearly transparent in the damp darkness. They open their mouths at whatever passes close enough: flies, hatchlings, falling seeds, once perhaps a finger.

There are creatures that glide from tree to tree on membranes thin as wet leaves. There are reptiles with feathers along their necks, their colors changing as the mist thickens. There are many-jointed animals that move along vines in looping waves, each segment testing the surface before the next advances. Some carry lights—not true lights, perhaps, but cold green organs that pulse beneath the skin. When disturbed, they scatter in the dark like sparks dropping upward.

The insects are the country’s innumerable citizens. They bore into bark, drink from flowers, colonize carcasses, and rise in clouds from every disturbed patch of ground. Some make the rasping night. Some make the sudden silver flicker above the marsh. Some spend years beneath the soil and emerge all at once, covering the trees, the animals, and the faces of the passing people with thin, trembling bodies.

The flowers exploit them.

At night, the blossoms open with the slow confidence of mouths. Their colors are dark enough to seem black until touched by a flame. Their scents can be sweet, bitter, rotten, or so rich that they cause dizziness. Small animals enter for nectar and emerge dusted with pollen. Others do not emerge immediately. The flowers close around them, not always to kill, but to hold them, taste them, and release them when the plant has taken what it needs.

In the wet season, the forest appears to breathe because it is breathing. Leaves unfold. Seed cases burst. Roots swell beneath the soil. Spores rise from drowned wood. The air fills with the minute exhalations of countless lives, and the mist becomes a suspension of living material.

Even the dead remain inhabitants.

A fallen grazer feeds scavengers, insects, fungus, and the roots of nearby plants. A drowned tree becomes a bridge for crabs and a nursery for larvae. A human body would not lie undisturbed for long. The first arrivals would be the small ones: flies, beetles, worms, things that work beneath the skin. Then larger mouths would come. Within days, the body would have entered the mud, the water, the air, and the bodies of other creatures.

This is the deepest law of the Low Country: nothing is allowed to remain itself.

Water remakes the banks. Heat breaks the stone. Plants climb over the fallen. Animals carry the forest inside them and return it elsewhere. Fire appears briefly and leaves behind a new abundance. The people pass through with their small flames, believing themselves visitors, but they too are taken into the country—by seed, scar, memory, spoor, and bone.

What lives there is not a population occupying a place.

It is a single, crowded motion with innumerable faces: feeding, fleeing, flowering, sinking, hatching, calling, hunting, rotting, and beginning again.
