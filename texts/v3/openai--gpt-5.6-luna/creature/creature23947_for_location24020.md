They are creatures of pressure, mineral, and patience.

The smallest live where the floor has cracked into plates. They are hardly more than articulated knots, no larger than a hand, with six or eight jointed limbs tucked beneath bodies plated in ash-colored armor. Their mouths are not openings but soft, many-ridged surfaces that they press to the stone. They drink warmth from it. They taste the faint stresses running through the crust and know, long before any visible sign, when a ridge is about to shift or a buried thing is about to wake.

They do not gather in swarms. They gather in arrangements.

At certain hours—if hours exist there—they leave the grooves and align themselves along the mineral seams, each body touching the next with one filament or hooked limb. A tremor passes through them. It may be nourishment. It may be communication. It may be worship, though the distinction would mean little to them. When the vibration ends, they separate and return to their places, carrying in their bodies a pattern that may take years to fade.

Some of the low creatures are born from eggs, though an egg in that country is a hard, warm nodule fused to the ground. It opens only when the surrounding stone has reached the proper pressure. The young emerge already jointed, already able to find the seams, and remain beside the shell until its last stored heat is gone.

The broad pale things travel farther. Their bodies are composed of overlapping segments, each one thin and translucent as old horn. They move sideways because their limbs are arranged for the reading of surfaces rather than the bearing of distance. They can tell whether a patch of floor is safe by the way it answers their weight. They can cross an apparently empty plain and stop without hesitation at a place where no mark is visible. Beneath them, something has spoken through the crust.

They are not blind. They possess eyes, but the eyes are recessed and sealed most of the time. Sight is a crude sense in the country beneath the sky. Pressure is more useful. Heat is more useful. The slow movement of buried bodies is most useful of all.

When they encounter one another, they do not threaten or greet. One extends a fan of filaments. The other receives it. They exchange their impressions of the floor: a fracture far to the east, a warm pocket under an old ridge, the passage of a large animal, the disturbed attention of something embedded in the wall. Then they continue. Their lives are made of such small recognitions.

There are hunters, though hunting is not a violent art there.

A hunter may follow a creature for several days without approaching it. It watches the changes in pressure around the quarry, waits for its route to become predictable, and then places itself in the path of the next disturbance. When the other arrives, the hunter unfolds—not quickly, but completely. A ring of fine hooks opens around its body. The quarry enters the ring and is held there by a difference in warmth. Neither creature struggles. One is dismantled. The other feeds.

Afterward the hunter remains motionless for a long time, its body darkening as it incorporates what it has taken. There is no waste. The bones, if they are bones, are pressed into the cracks. The fluid becomes a glistening seam. The memory of the dead survives in the hunter’s altered pressure-signature and may be recognized by others for generations.

The wall-dwellers are older and difficult to call animals.

They are immense, but most of them cannot be seen. Their bodies run through the strata in branching masses, filling cavities that would be mountains elsewhere. Only their surfaces emerge: a fold along a cliff, a broad mound beneath the dust, a ridge that expands and contracts at intervals of several days. Some bear vertical eyes, each one a dark slit that opens toward the plain. Others have no visible organs at all. They listen through the entire wall.

The smaller inhabitants avoid stepping on them. The tool-makers build their paths around them. When one wakes, the event is not announced by movement but by rearrangement: the iron-black plates begin to ring, the glowing seams brighten, and creatures across the plain pause at once. A wall-dweller may remain awake for a single night or for a century. During that time, it learns everything that has touched the stone around it. It does not pursue. It does not need to. Its attention is enough to make the other inhabitants alter their routes.

The makers of shelters are recognizably people, though not necessarily in the human sense.

They are tall, narrow-bodied beings with skin like dull volcanic glass, flexible at the joints and warm beneath the surface. Their faces are covered by overlapping plates that open only when they feed or speak. They carry no visible eyes. Instead, they have delicate sensory threads gathered around the skull and throat, which they extend into the air when listening. Their voices are low pulses felt more clearly in the ribs than heard with the ears.

They live alone or in small households. A household may contain three generations, though age is measured by the number of times a body has been repaired rather than by years. Their flesh hardens gradually. The oldest among them are unable to bend and must be propped upright in niches, where they continue to advise the living through the pressure of their hands against the walls.

They build shelters from whatever the country offers: slabs of stone, plates shed by larger animals, leathery mineral gathered from vents, and the black pillars that can be sharpened into tools. Their homes have no doors. A door would imply that the danger comes from outside. Instead, a shelter has a threshold: a carefully graded strip of ground whose pressure and temperature are known. Anyone crossing it is immediately identified by the disturbance they make.

The people carry lamps, but the lamps are not held high. Light is not used to see far. A lamp is kept close to the body, where it reveals the hands and the next step. The darkness beyond is considered a necessary courtesy. To illuminate too much is to announce oneself to every buried listener.

They travel with hooked staffs. The hooks test the crust, lift thin plates, retrieve objects from fissures, and touch the walls before a passage is entered. A staff is personal. Its owner gives it a name, though the name is not spoken. It is made through a sequence of taps against the handle, a rhythm learned by the hand. When its owner dies, the staff is planted upright beside the shelter. In time, the ground accepts it.

Their dead are not buried. Burial would be an insult to the country, which is already a body and has no need to be fed. The dead are laid on a warm ridge or in a mineral hollow and left with their tools. The living return repeatedly, not to mourn but to observe the stages of incorporation. First the skin loses its distinction from the stone. Then the joints disappear. Finally there remains only a slight difference in pressure where the person had been.

That difference is remembered.

The people do not tell stories in the ordinary way. They make maps of events. A carved plate may show the route taken by a dangerous creature, not in lines but in varying thicknesses and textures. A shelter wall may bear a sequence of dents recording the waking of a wall-dweller. A heap of colored dust marks a place where someone heard a sound that had no source.

Their history is kept in the floor.

They know which ridges formed before the oldest settlements and which rose within living memory. They know the tone produced by every major layer of iron-black plate. They know where the warm air tastes sweet, where it tastes metallic, and where the sweetness becomes so sharp that it numbs the throat. They know that some expanses of apparently empty ground are occupied beneath the surface by vast, sleeping anatomies.

They also know that the country has no center.

The forest of needles is called the Stillness by those who approach it, though it has other names among older peoples. The inhabitants do not enter. Even the wall-dwellers grow quiet near it. The offerings laid at its edge are not gifts to a god. They are acknowledgments of uncertainty: fragments of metal, worked stone, dust gathered from distant regions, tools no longer useful. Each object says, in its own way, *We have passed through. We do not claim to understand what remains.*

Those who tend the offerings are the most patient of the people. They sit at the forest’s boundary for long periods, sometimes facing the needles, sometimes facing away. They listen for changes in the pressure between the pillars. They watch the creatures hanging there—if watching is the word for sensing their layered presence—and record nothing.

Once in a great while, one of the offerings is gone.

No one sees it taken. No track leads inward. The pillars remain still. But the next time the attendants return, a different object has appeared among the needles: a fragment of bone too large to belong to any known animal, a piece of warm metal that has never cooled, a smooth black plate inscribed with a pattern no tool-maker recognizes.

The attendants do not retrieve these things.

They leave them where they are and move their own offerings farther from the trees.

Nearly all life in that country possesses some form of sleep, but sleep is not unconsciousness. The creatures fold themselves into the stone and continue to sense through it. The people lie beneath their shelters with their hands touching the floor. Their dreams are disturbances: a pressure from far away, a light moving without a source, the shape of an immense body turning beneath the plain.

Sometimes an entire settlement wakes at once.

No one knows why. The inhabitants rise, take up their staffs, and stand outside their shelters. The fauna emerge from the grooves. The embedded creatures grow attentive. For a while, every living thing faces the same direction, toward some place beyond the visible dark.

Then the pressure passes.

The people return indoors. The low creatures fold themselves back into their crevices. The great bodies in the walls resume their almost imperceptible breathing.

Whatever moved through the country leaves no mark except a temporary rearrangement of attention.

That is the common condition of its inhabitants: they are not waiting for a future, because the future is too slow to distinguish from the present. They endure, feed, listen, and become part of the ground. Their lives are not measured by growth or conquest but by the gradual refinement of their sensitivity. The oldest creature is not the largest, nor the strongest, nor the one that has survived the most dangers.

It is the one that can tell, from a single change in warmth beneath its feet, that something elsewhere has remembered it.
