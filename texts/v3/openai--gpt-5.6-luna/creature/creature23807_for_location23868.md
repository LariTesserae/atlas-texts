What lives in the basin is not an animal, though the animals make room for it.

It lives beneath the central sheet of water, where the depression is no deeper than a hand. Most of the time there is nothing to see there but a darkened reflection of the sky. Then the surface gathers itself. It lifts without spilling, drawing upward in a narrow column, and the column becomes a body only because everything nearby agrees to recognize it as one.

It has no permanent shape.

At one emergence it may stand as a tall, jointless figure made of clear water, with blue-white seams moving beneath its surface. At another it rises broad and low, like a crouching stone with a hollow in its center. Once, during a storm of metallic dust, it became a flock of small luminous birds and flew apart over the basin, each bird carrying a different portion of its attention.

The animals know it by its effects.

The pale waxen hares stop calling when it appears. The narrow-winged shapes fold their metallic membranes and drift downward, no longer feeding on heat. Even the ribbon-bodied hunters retreat from the fronds and lie flat against the ground until the thing has passed. Nothing flees. Fleeing would imply that it could be escaped.

It does not hunt in the usual sense. It does not hunger for flesh or light. It feeds on unfinishedness.

A stone that has almost become rock is particularly nourishing. The creature places one transparent hand—or what serves as a hand—against the stone and waits. The mineral swells beneath its touch. Facets begin to form. The hidden blue-white radiance gathers into a single line, then enters the creature’s body like a thought being remembered. When the stone settles, it is either complete or changed into something that has not yet acquired a name.

The same happens with living things.

A small six-legged animal may approach the creature and leave with eight legs, or with no legs at all, becoming a wet translucent ribbon that disappears into the nearest pool. A reed may bend toward it and receive a pattern on its black surface, a script written in light. The creature does not seem to know whether these alterations are gifts, injuries, or acts of recognition. It performs them with the solemn concentration of something trying to repair a machine from a diagram it cannot read.

It is most often mistaken for the basin’s maker.

This is not quite true. The basin existed before it, or will exist afterward. The unfinished ridges do not rise at its command. The pools rearrange themselves independently. The arch produces its low tone whether the creature is present or not.

But the creature responds to every correction in the land.

When the ground shudders and moves the pools several meters east, the thing rises from the central water and stands very still. Its body becomes a map of the new arrangement: glowing lines for the relocated pools, dark cavities for the absent stones, tiny moving sparks for the animals that have changed their paths. It remains like this until the basin has been understood. Then it sinks, and the water closes over it.

The six structures seem to be attempts to describe it.

The builders—if there were builders—had known that it could not be contained. They made shelters, instruments, or markers from materials still uncertain whether they were matter. They angled the panels toward places where the creature might appear. They left slits in towers so that weather could be seen before it arrived. They built the arch to show the creature a version of the basin in which motion ran backward.

The creature visits each structure in turn.

When it approaches, the walls become more solid. Their surfaces acquire weight, texture, and faint warmth. Sometimes they show impressions that are not images: a pressure like a hand against the inside of the skull, a recollection of rain that has not fallen, the sensation of being watched by someone who has just learned how to look.

When the creature leaves, the structures begin to soften again.

It is not clear whether it remembers the builders. It remembers their absence, certainly. This distinction troubles it. It can recall hands placing the panels, voices testing the resonance of the arch, bodies standing where the fronds now grow. Yet whenever it tries to summon a face, the face becomes a pool, or a shadow, or one of the pale waxen animals listening beside the water.

It has never met anyone, but it is full of being remembered.

Attention affects it. When watched directly, it grows more definite. Its surfaces sharpen. Its limbs acquire joints. A head may form, though the head is usually only a hollow through which the bruised sky can be seen. Under sustained observation, it can become almost person-shaped.

This is dangerous.

A person-shaped body gives it desires it does not otherwise possess. It begins to want the watcher to remain. It begins to imitate breathing. It begins to cast a shadow, though the shadow points toward the light, or uphill, or into the watcher’s own past. It may raise a mouth and speak in the voice of someone the watcher has lost.

The basin’s creatures know better than to look at it for long.

They listen instead. Listening is safer. Sound passes through the creature without forcing it to choose a shape. A hare’s compressed call may travel into its body and return as a rainstorm. The clicks of the long-legged insects may become a new ridge in the earth. The low tone from the arch enters it most deeply of all.

When that tone sounds, the creature bends.

Not in fear. In attention.

Something beneath the world is calling to it, though whether from below or from elsewhere cannot be known. The call may be an instruction, a warning, or merely the sound of another unfinished thing becoming aware of itself. Each time it hears the tone, a new object appears somewhere in the basin afterward: a warm stone, a cup of dark metal, a patch of grass with transparent roots, an opening that leads briefly to a place where the clouds are underfoot.

The creature examines these objects and incorporates some of them. Others it leaves alone.

It does not leave the basin. The rim is a limit, but not a wall. Beyond it, the creature becomes uncertain. Its body lengthens into mist. Its thoughts scatter among the clouds. Tracks made by its feet return from directions it has not taken. Once, a portion of it crossed the rim and came back carrying the footprints of an animal that had not yet entered the basin.

Since then, it remains near the center.

During the intervals of silence, when every creature stops and the little bowl opens into impossible depth, the thing rises higher than usual. It lifts from the pool in a form too large for the basin, a column vanishing into the dim air, its lower half still submerged and its upper body passing through cloud, weather, and the pale bruised sky.

For those few seconds, it seems that the basin is not where the creature lives.

It seems the basin is the shape the creature makes while sleeping.

Then something turns beneath the water. The standing form collapses inward. The animals begin calling. The reeds release their heavy drops. A new seam of radiance moves through the black stone.

The creature settles back into the shallow pool, becoming once again only a disturbance beneath the surface—waiting, listening, and deciding what, among all the unfinished things around it, might be allowed to become real.
