What lives there is not a population but a body too large to have a body.

The plants are individuals only in the briefest, most local sense. A stalk may separate from its neighbors, raise its leaves, and maintain a chamber of its own, but beneath the living floor its roots are already involved in other lives. Water passes through several species before reaching the place where it is needed. Signals travel as pressure along runners, as changes in sap, as pulses of warmth and electrical agitation. A wall knows when a bridge is bearing weight. A district knows when one of its towers has opened. A distant grove may alter its shape because somewhere else, beyond sight, a root has been bruised.

They do not speak, but they are never silent.

The smallest inhabitants are films and threads: translucent things spread over bark, bright fungal veils, fine rootlets that explore cracks in stone. They taste moisture, minerals, vibration, and attention. They are the settlement’s nerves, though no single one understands what it serves. When a traveler steps onto a path, these minute lives register the pressure and pass it outward. Within moments, the route may thicken beneath the foot, soften ahead, or divide into several possible directions.

Larger plants appear more deliberate. They form around useful shapes. A cluster of stems may become a doorway because something has repeatedly passed between them. A mat of leaves may raise itself into steps where a route has been remembered. These structures are not built by planners. They are the accumulated preferences of living matter: a tendency to arch here, to open there, to preserve a dry chamber, to gather around a warm stone.

The plants have no names for one another, but they possess distinctions. A tower recognizes its own offshoots by the rhythm of their sap. A grove can tell whether a neighboring growth is kin, rival, continuation, or opportunity. Some plants are generous, opening their tissues to whatever approaches. Others are defensive, closing corridors and raising ridges of thornless, densely packed growth. Some tolerate being inhabited. Others slowly absorb their visitors into the structure, not violently, but with the patient insistence of roots finding a seam.

What seems like civic life is the movement of these tendencies through the whole region.

The great columns are its elders, though age does not make them still. Their trunks contain chambers of stored water, mineral, memory, and seed. They lean over districts like patient authorities, their hollow interiors resonating with the activity of lesser plants. When one opens, an entire neighborhood may reorganize around it. When one closes, paths vanish and new walls rise elsewhere. Their pulses are not commands exactly. They are conditions to which everything nearby responds.

There are also transient inhabitants: districts that exist for only a day, sometimes less. They emerge from spores, from ruptured roots, from the collapse of older structures. They arrive already formed, carrying no history except the pattern encoded in their tissues. A new grove may begin as a green cloud and settle as a line of identical trees. By evening it has become a corridor. By morning, the corridor has fused into a single immense organism with many trunks and one thirst.

The young are not born into homes. They are born as homes.

A seed may become a room before it becomes a plant. A folded leaf may harden into a roof, then send roots downward and become a household, then a street, then a reservoir. Growth is not a private process. Every new body immediately offers itself as structure for others. Small vines occupy the hollows of larger ones. Moss seals exposed surfaces. Pale reeds rise through cracks and turn them into ventilated passages. No plant grows alone for long. Solitude is simply an early stage of being colonized.

Reproduction is communal and often indistinguishable from construction. Two plants may lean together until their tissues join, their separate circulations becoming one. They may remain recognizable afterward, but their memories mingle. One may inherit the other’s habit of opening at dusk; another may retain only a preference for wet stone or a tendency to form arches. In the deep districts, whole neighborhoods fuse and reproduce as a single spore-bearing mass. When it releases itself, the spores do not scatter randomly. They rise toward warmth, settle where attention has lingered, and begin rebuilding a shape that may not yet exist.

The settlement remembers through recurrence.

A path that has carried many travelers becomes hard and woody. A chamber used for shelter develops thicker walls. A tower repeatedly observed from a distance draws itself nearer, as if visibility were a form of nourishment. Conversely, an abandoned district loosens. Its floors become runners, its windows close with membranes, and its rooms open into soil. Forgetting is not destruction. It is redistribution.

The inhabitants have no concept of death separate from change. When a stalk collapses, neighboring tissues drink from it. When a tower breaks, its chambers become pools, then wetlands, then new roots. Some structures remain recognizable for years after they have ceased to function, like old bones beneath a forest. Others vanish so completely that their former existence can only be sensed in the shape of a recurring bend or the stubborn persistence of a certain kind of bridge.

They do not fear collapse. They are made of collapse.

What they may fear—if fear is the right word—is disconnection. A dry patch beneath the living floor is not merely barren; it is an interruption. A severed root mat causes tremors far beyond the wound. Plants crowd toward the break, sending down pale exploratory strands, attempting to close the distance. If they cannot, the surrounding district begins to alter. Roads bend toward one another. Pools migrate. Towers open more frequently. The region spends itself trying to restore continuity.

Attention is another kind of contact.

The plants respond to being watched, though not with shyness. Observation gives form. A traveler staring at an empty plaza may cause shoots to appear there. A structure imagined as a house may grow a threshold. A feared passage may lengthen, branch, or acquire walls. The plants do not read thoughts as animals might. They sense the pressure of expectation and use it as a pattern.

An intruder would therefore become, almost immediately, a participant.

The ground would thicken beneath them. Leaves would turn their faces toward them. Hidden roots would trace their route. The settlement would begin making accommodations: a roof against the rain, a stairway over a fissure, a chamber with a warm interior. But accommodation would not remain separate from incorporation. The walls would learn the traveler’s habits. The paths would anticipate them. Doorways would appear before they were sought.

Eventually, the question would become difficult to answer: was the city making a place for the traveler, or making the traveler into a place?

At sunset, the towers open because the whole settlement enters one of its recurring exchanges. Vines loosen. Reservoirs empty into roots. Flowers unfold without needing to be visited. Spores rise through the canopy in green, luminous veils. Far below, the vascular columns pull water from the dark mineral shelves, and their great suction passes through every connected plant.

The sound resembles breathing, though there is no mouth, no chest, no single creature to own it.

The city-forest lives by becoming more of itself. Every wall is a stem learning to bear weight. Every road is a root remembering pressure. Every ruin is a beginning. And when rain starts inside the leaves, the inhabitants turn—not individually, but together, across the entire plain—toward water.
