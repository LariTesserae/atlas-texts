What lives in the settlement does not announce itself as life.

At first there are only signs: a line of stones newly turned beside a blocked channel; a square of lichen scraped clean from a threshold; a door that has been left open against the weather, though no wind has entered it. In one of the upper districts, rainwater disappears through a grate that was clogged the day before. Somewhere below, a shutter closes without a sound.

The people who remain are few, and they are difficult to distinguish from the city at a distance. They wear layered garments of felt, hide, and coarse woven fiber, all in colors taken from wet stone: charcoal, green-gray, dull brown, the pale gray of fog. Their outer coverings are broad and hooded, designed less to preserve warmth than to divert water. Rain runs from their shoulders in narrow streams. Their boots are wrapped in treated hide and bound close around the ankle, so that they make little sound on the slick paving.

They have adapted to the cold by becoming economical. They do not hurry unless stone is falling or water is rising. They stand for long periods beneath archways, watching a channel fill. They kneel beside walls with their hands pressed to the masonry, feeling for changes that would be invisible to the eye: a hollow behind a facing stone, a new seepage, the faint vibration of saturated earth. Children, when there are children, are taught to read these changes before they are taught letters.

Their faces are narrow and wind-burned. Skin at the hands and cheeks is darkened by weather, though the rest of the body remains pale from the dimness of the covered rooms. Their eyes are accustomed to low light and often seem black beneath their hoods. They blink rarely. In fog, they can see farther than an outsider would expect, not because their sight is extraordinary, but because they are practiced in noticing the slight disruptions that reveal a wall, a stair, a person standing still.

They do not build fires in the open. Smoke would be wasteful, and warmth is not permitted to escape merely for comfort. Heat is kept in stone-lined chambers beneath the oldest halls, where slow-burning fuel is used for cooking, drying, and the treatment of fibers. These rooms are entered through narrow passages and closed behind thick hanging screens. Even there, a fire is small and watched continuously. The people gather around it without speaking, with their hands held close but never too close, as though warmth were a shy animal that might retreat if approached carelessly.

Most of their work concerns water.

They clear channels by hand. They reset paving stones that have shifted. They descend into cisterns with ropes around their waists and scrape silt from the mouths of underground passages. They build temporary walls of fitted rubble when a marsh begins to enter a house. They know which lower streets are safe in a long rain and which should be abandoned before dawn. A house may be left to the weather, but its watercourse is not. Even ruins have to drain properly.

Their tools are made from stone, wood, fired clay, horn, and bone. A smooth length of black wood may be more valuable than a room. Clay lamps are set into niches, though they are lit only when necessary. The people repair things in ways that preserve their age. They do not replace a wall if it can be braced; they do not empty a courtyard if it can be redirected; they do not remove moss unless the moss is holding too much water. Their idea of maintenance is not to keep the city as it was, but to make its next alteration survivable.

They live in the upper floors and the protected interiors, above the level where standing water can remain for months. The lowest rooms are used as reservoirs, workshops, burial chambers, or left to the marsh. Some houses are occupied only on their eastern sides because the western walls have begun to lean. In older districts, a family may sleep in a sequence of rooms connected by raised planks, moving their bedding whenever the floor becomes damp. They know the city not as a map but as a set of conditions: dry enough, unsafe, echoing, breathing, hollow, newly settled.

They speak, but rarely and quietly.

Conversation is practical in the open air. A hand raised beneath a hood may mean that a ledge has shifted. Two fingers pressed to the wrist may indicate time, or depth, or the expected arrival of a storm. In enclosed courts, where sound lingers, they use low voices that seem less like speech than the continuation of water under stone. Names are short. Many are borrowed from places, channels, weather events, or materials: Silt, East Wall, First Thaw, Black Tarn. A person may acquire a new name after surviving a collapse or discovering a dry route beneath a flooded district.

They sing only underground.

The songs are not meant for celebration. They are used to measure distance and shape. A person at one end of a tunnel sings a sustained note while another answers from beyond a bend. By the return of the sound, they learn whether a passage is open, flooded, newly blocked, or occupied by something that should not be there. The melodies are low and narrow, with long spaces between them. Aboveground, they would be mistaken for wind moving through a shaft.

The dead are not buried in the open soil. There is too little of it, and the plateau does not keep what is given to it. Bodies are wrapped in waxed cloth and placed in dry chambers high within the retaining walls, where the air moves through carved vents. Over time, the names are worn into lintels, then softened by rain and handling. When a chamber is needed for another purpose, the remains are moved deeper into the masonry. Nothing is thought to remain separate forever. A wall will contain a person as readily as it contains a roof beam or a layer of silt.

The inhabitants are not afraid of the city’s collapse. They are afraid of collapse occurring without being noticed.

A fallen roof is ordinary. A new crack that has not yet widened is a warning. A courtyard filling with reeds is not a tragedy if the water has found its rightful basin. But a channel that runs clear one day and stops the next can cause an entire district to be evacuated. They listen for changes in the settlement’s hidden circulation. At night, some of them place their palms against the floors and lie awake, feeling the distant movement of water through the foundations.

They have no stories of an earlier, better age. The ruins around them are not ruins in the usual sense. They are former arrangements of the same place, and the people regard them with the calm attention given to an elderly body: with knowledge of its weaknesses, gratitude for what remains, and no expectation that it will recover.

From the highest terraces, they sometimes gather to watch the weather cross the basin. They stand in small groups, wrapped in dark coverings, while districts vanish beneath fog and reappear under a passing strip of light. In those moments they seem almost motionless enough to be mistaken for statues.

But one will turn its head.

Another will step down into the mist.

Below, in the drowned streets, pale amphibians cling to the stones. Water moves through the old channels. A hand reaches beneath an arch and clears a grate. The city takes the water in, and those who live there listen to make sure it has gone where it should.
