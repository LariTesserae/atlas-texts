The oldest inhabitant of the chamber is almost never seen whole.

It lives beneath the basin, where the clay remains cool and faintly damp, folded through the earth like a root that learned to move. What appears at the surface—a hooked foot, a pale feeler, the curve of a smooth back—is only the exposed edge of it. The rest extends downward into the dark soil, divided into many soft-armored segments that overlap one another so closely that the creature seems less like an animal than a piece of buried anatomy belonging to the chamber itself.

Its body is white, but not the white of bone. It is the color of milk held beneath water, translucent at the margins. Veins of gray-blue run through its limbs. Along its back are shallow plates, each plate dusted with powdered limestone. When the mineral shelves brighten, the creature’s armor catches the light and gives it back weakly, so that it seems to be carrying the chamber’s glow under its skin.

It has no eyes.

The light reaches it through the clay, and that is enough. It knows where the luminous shelves are by the warmth they press into the stone. It knows where the pools lie by the changing pressure of moisture. It knows the creatures above by their weight, their shadows, and the small tremors made when they cross the basin.

Most of all, it knows the drops.

Long before one falls, the creature becomes attentive. A thread of water begins its descent through the rock; the ceiling tightens around it; some minute alteration passes through the basin. One of the feelers rises from the clay. Then another. The creature waits beneath the place where the drop will land, though it cannot see the drop and may have chosen the place many hours earlier.

When the water strikes, the sound travels down through the basin.

The creature opens.

Not quickly. Nothing it does is quick. Its limbs unfold in a widening flower, each one pressing into the damp clay. The impact has loosened insects from their cracks, and the creature feels their movements above it. It may send one feeler after them. It may remain still and drink the water that has seeped downward. Sometimes it gathers the gray-green growth around the basin, drawing the rootlike threads into the dark with a patience that is almost tenderness and almost feeding.

It is difficult to tell the difference.

The other inhabitants avoid it without seeming to fear it. The glassy insects cross above its buried body and occasionally disappear between its plates. The drifting animals settle when one of its feelers emerges, their membranes trembling in the warm air. The pale stalks grow around it, and their roots follow the creature’s old paths through the clay. Its movements make shallow furrows that collect condensation. In this way, the animal destroys and prepares the chamber at once. It eats what grows, and its passage leaves behind places where new growth can take hold.

It does not hunt often. Hunger, for it, is a long condition rather than an emergency. When another white creature surfaces nearby, the two may remain beside one another for an hour, motionless except for the slow testing of their feelers. Then one closes around the other. The victim does not cry out. There is no chase, no violence recognizable as such—only the gradual disappearance of one pale form inside another.

Afterward, the survivor sinks beneath the basin and leaves the clay slightly raised.

The creature has endured many cycles of the chamber. It has watched the pools migrate, though “watched” is not the right word. It has felt them migrate. It knows which corner will dry first. It knows when the mineral shelves are beginning their long brightening and when the rock below has started to move.

When the great vibration comes from beyond the walls, it becomes utterly still.

Its limbs fold inward. Its feelers lie flat. The clay settles over its back until it resembles an old stone. The creature cannot know what makes the pressure, nor whether the presence beyond the fissure is approaching or merely turning in its sleep. But it remembers the vibration. Perhaps it remembers many such vibrations. Perhaps the chamber’s rounded corners and worn basin are partly the record of its response: a body withdrawing, surfacing, withdrawing again, over centuries.

When the pressure fades, it does not emerge at once.

It waits through the silence that follows. Then one pale limb presses upward, displacing a little dust. Another joins it. Somewhere above, a stalk opens its tiny cup. A glassy insect begins to move. The pools remain unbroken.

The creature rises just far enough to feel the air.

Then it resumes its slow work beneath the bright stone, carrying the chamber forward by increments too small for any living thing to notice.
