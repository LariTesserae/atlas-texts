What lives there has no single shape.

It begins as a film no thicker than breath, spread across the underside of a pipe or the black margin of a pool. It feeds on metals, warmth, and the dissolved remains of older things. Left undisturbed, it thickens into a translucent skin. The skin gathers grit. The grit becomes a crust. The crust develops pores, hinges, channels, and minute movements of its own. In time, what seemed to be mineral begins to flex when water passes over it.

The smallest inhabitants live within this growing skin.

They are pale enough to disappear in condensation and dark enough to vanish against wet stone. Some have many legs, though the legs are too fine to see except when they move across a lit surface. Some are little hinged bodies that open and close themselves in the current, filtering what the pumps and pipes have failed to remove. Others are soft, transparent threads that bore through the mineral layers and leave narrow tunnels behind them. The tunnels collapse slowly, but never completely. If one watches a deposit long enough, it seems to breathe.

These small creatures do not make nests. They make conditions. Their feeding clears passages through the crust. Their waste becomes new crust. Their dead bodies are dissolved, sorted, and incorporated into the same pale ridges that shelter them. Nothing in the basin is separate for long. A creature becomes sediment; sediment becomes a wall; the wall becomes a feeding ground.

The larger animals are assembled for the channels.

They have low, plated bodies that press close to the water, broad limbs for gripping slick surfaces, and trailing organs that taste vibration and chemical change. Their backs open and close at the vents, drawing in the basin’s damp air. They do not seem to possess eyes, but they know when light has changed, when a pump has begun, when something unfamiliar has stepped onto the grates.

They gather beneath the warm runoff in dense, overlapping heaps. One body lies partly beneath another, and another beneath that, until the whole colony resembles a single armored creature sleeping in the channel. The individuals are not still. Plates adjust. Limbs test the current. Vents pulse at different rhythms. A disturbance travels through them as a slow wave.

When the mass moves, it does not scatter. It turns.

For a moment, every creature faces the same direction. Then the pile loosens, slides, and flows into the nearest crack, leaving only a few pale plates and a trembling skin of water behind.

There are predators among them, though predation is almost indistinguishable from the ordinary work of the place. Long animals wait under shelves of mineral, folded into the dark like lengths of wet cable. Their feeding tendrils rest in the current. When something small touches them, they draw it in without lunging. The water closes. The ledge remains empty.

Larger things move beneath the settling tanks. They are rarely seen whole. A back may rise beneath the opaque surface, or a row of vents may open in the silt. Their presence is known by the sudden withdrawal of everything else. The filtering colonies close. The transparent burrowers retreat. Even the mineral films seem to stiffen around the pool’s edge.

The creatures respond to the machinery as if it were weather, though it is more intimate than weather. They know the factory’s cycle. They know which pipes will warm, which drains will flood, which chambers will briefly become rich with loosened matter. When the pumps stop, the basin’s life draws inward. Animals abandon the runoff and descend toward the buried conduits, where the water is colder and the vibrations are deep. When the machinery resumes, they return in streams, following heat through cracks too narrow for anything else to enter.

The factory feeds them without intending to. It sheds rust, oil, scale, salts, and traces of whatever substance it once processed. It gives them warm water and endless surfaces. In return, they keep the filters from sealing, open the mineral pores around the pipes, and carry dissolved material from one chamber to another. Their bodies are part of the plant’s circulation. The plant is part of theirs.

The mineral growths may be alive in a slower fashion.

They thicken around sound. They spread toward standing water and recede from swift currents. They form smooth halos around bolts, sheath cables, and preserve the impressions of broken machinery. If a body rests in one place long enough, the deposit rises around it—not violently, not with hunger, but with the patient certainty of silt accumulating in a drowned room.

The inhabitants do not defend the basin. They do not understand it as territory. They simply remain within its temperatures, pressures, and vibrations. Bright light stills them. Sudden heat gathers them. Running water carries them. The plant’s pulse organizes them more completely than any instinct of fear or hunger.

When the fine rain begins, they all emerge.

The smallest rise first, turning the mineral crust into a field of minute silver motions. Then the plated animals leave their channels. They come from beneath grates, from flooded seams, from the walls and the settling tanks. They climb over one another in silent currents toward the factory, toward the warm pipes and breathing vents.

For those few minutes, it is impossible to tell where the basin ends and its inhabitants begin.

The ground moves. The water moves. The deposits flex and close. The machines labor below it all, and the creatures gather around that labor as if around a buried heart.

Then the rain stops, and the population sinks again into its layers.

What lives there is not merely an animal community, nor merely a contaminated factory left to decay. It is a slow composite thing: bodies, mineral, water, rust, and machinery joined by repetition. It feeds, filters, hunts, condenses, and builds itself around whatever remains.

It has no face, but it notices.

It has no voice, but every vibration passes through it.

And it is still becoming.
