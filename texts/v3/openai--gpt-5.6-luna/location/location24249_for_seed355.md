The city lies in a basin where the ground has forgotten how to drain.

It is low country, nearly level, though the horizon is interrupted by long, dark ridges of old embankment and collapsed walls. Water stands everywhere in shallow sheets: between paving stones, under doorways, along the broad avenues, in the courtyards of roofless houses. It does not reflect the sky. The sky is too dark to be reflected, and the water instead shows a dim, submerged version of whatever stands above it—buildings beneath buildings, streets running in directions they do not run, windows opening onto other districts.

The heat is immediate and close. It presses against the skin like wet cloth. Even in the darkness, the air has the weight and color of a hot afternoon. Condensation gathers on every surface, although nothing is cold. Walls sweat. Stone beads. The undersides of bridges release a steady rain. In some places, water rises directly out of the ground in slow, silent bulges, then settles again without spreading.

The settlement is large enough to have districts, though not large enough to feel like a city in the ordinary sense. Its construction is old and settled into itself. Streets have sagged into channels. Foundations have become islands. Staircases descend into black water and continue for many steps beneath the surface. Whole blocks lean together, their upper stories touching, their masonry fused by decades—or centuries—of pressure and damp.

There are no lamps, no engines, no wires, no signs of machinery. Yet the city was organized once, and its organization remains. Roads converge on plazas. Canals run beneath the streets. Courtyards repeat at measured intervals. There are civic buildings with vast blank entrances, bathhouses filled to their thresholds, granaries whose walls have split open and become reservoirs. The architecture is not ruined so much as completed by erosion. Water has performed the final design.

The materials are mostly stone, fired brick, plaster gone to powder, and a dark mineral that appears wherever the city has been worn down. It is smooth as glass but porous, drinking in the rain. In some places it grows upward in thin shelves along walls, like the frozen overflow of a black tide. These shelves are not crystals and not masonry. They are simply what the walls become when left alone.

Vegetation is sparse and widely spaced. A few pale reeds stand in the deepest channels, each one separated from the next by many yards of empty water. They have no leaves, only jointed white stems that bend toward sounds that never occur. Low mats of gray-green growth cling to shaded foundations, but they do not spread like moss. They arrange themselves in rings around certain stones, leaving the centers bare. There are occasional trees in the outer districts, thin and leafless, their roots visible through the flooded soil. Their branches hold droplets that remain suspended long after the rain has stopped.

There is no animal life.

No insects skim the water. No birds move in the high dark. Nothing swims beneath the streets. The reeds do not harbor nests, and the fungal growths do not attract anything. The city is not merely quiet because it is abandoned; it is quiet because the category of animal seems to have been omitted from this place. There are no tracks, no gnawed edges, no feathers, no shells, no dead things. The absence is clean and complete.

Still, the landscape is active.

Water migrates against gravity, flowing uphill through gutters and climbing steps in narrow, glistening threads. Pools drain into walls and reappear on rooftops. Streets that were dry a moment ago fill from below, though the level may stop at the height of a person’s knee and remain there for years. In the center of one square, a column of water rises and falls like a slow breath. It is never windy, but the surface around it trembles continuously.

The weather operates in cycles that are difficult to measure. A rain may begin without clouds and continue for minutes or months. Sometimes the entire city becomes wet at once, each surface shining under the dark, and then dries in a single instant—not to dust, but to the texture of old bone. Heat comes in pulses. During these pulses the air bends, and distant buildings appear to fold inward, their windows stretching into long horizontal wounds. When the heat recedes, the architecture returns, though never exactly. A tower may have acquired another floor. A street may now pass through the middle of a house.

Sound is rare and therefore immense.

Most of the time there is only the delicate patter of condensation, the soft displacement of standing water, the occasional settling crack of masonry. The silence is not empty; it seems occupied by something too broad to make a noise. Far away, beyond the visible districts, comes the low sound of water moving through underground spaces. It resembles breathing, but without lungs or rhythm. Once in a while, a bell can be heard. There are no bells in the city. The sound comes from somewhere beneath the foundations, and whenever it rings, every pool briefly reflects a different sky.

The darkness is not simply the absence of light. It has depth. The nearest walls are visible as masses, while farther streets vanish into a blackness that seems to begin only a few yards away. Yet distances remain strangely exposed. A person standing at the edge of the basin can see the outline of a district several miles off, while being unable to make out a doorway across the street. The horizon appears close, then recedes. On some nights—or what passes for nights here—the whole city is visible as a dim relief, and on others only isolated surfaces emerge: a bridge, a staircase, the pale trunk of a dead tree.

The sky has no stars. It is a broad, unbroken dark canopy with occasional lighter regions that drift beneath it, as though clouds were moving on the wrong side of the world. Sometimes a pale red glow spreads along the horizon, not illuminating anything, only revealing that the darkness has layers. Sometimes a second horizon appears overhead. Buildings can be seen there, inverted and far away, standing on a ceiling of black water.

The laws of the city are mostly stable, but they are not the laws of an ordinary place.

Water remembers the shapes of things that touched it. If a person steps into a pool and leaves, the depression of their foot may remain on the surface for years. If a fallen wall is removed, its reflection may continue to occupy the space, solid enough to cast a shadow but not solid enough to obstruct passage. Doors sometimes open into the rooms they were built to contain, and sometimes into rooms belonging to buildings that no longer exist. Every map becomes inaccurate when brought here. Roads lengthen when approached and shorten behind the traveler.

The city does not decay uniformly. Some structures have been reduced to low islands of brick; others remain almost intact despite being full of water. A house may lose its roof but keep the arrangement of its rooms. A palace may remain standing while its exterior walls become soft and translucent. Through these walls, one can see staircases leading in incompatible directions, descending into bright water or rising into rooms under the ground.

There are places where the settlement gives way to open marsh. The transition is gradual: first the streets become narrower, then the paving breaks into rectangular slabs, then the slabs lie scattered across mud. Beyond them, the basin extends in a wide, nearly featureless plain of shallow black water. Sparse reeds mark old property lines. The remains of roads persist as straight ridges beneath the surface, visible only when the water clears for a moment. Here and there, isolated structures stand far apart: a gate with no wall, a roof supported by no house, a stairway descending from the mud into deeper darkness.

The emptiness between these presences is part of the design. Nothing crowds. No vines overtake the walls, no roots split the streets, no birds gather on the towers. The city has room to continue being itself. Its silence has room too.

At the center of the basin is a broad civic square, flooded to a depth that cannot be judged. The surrounding buildings form a low, uneven ring. Their facades are dark, but each contains one opening filled with a dull, internal glow—not light exactly, but the suggestion of visibility. Looking into these openings does not reveal rooms. It reveals the same square from another time, or another arrangement: dry, submerged, overgrown, newly built, utterly absent. In every version, something has just moved beyond the edge of sight.

Nothing ever enters the square.

Nothing living waits there.

Yet the water continues to rise and fall, the buildings continue to change by imperceptible degrees, and the unseen city below continues its quiet work of rearranging the world.
