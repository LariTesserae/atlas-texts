The basin is warm without being bright.

It lies low between long, dark shoulders of stone, a country of shallow depressions and worn shelves extending farther than the eye can comfortably measure. Nothing rises sharply. The land has been rubbed down over ages into a broad, settled form: low ridges, flat aprons of mineral, channels that begin and end without urgency. The horizon is almost level, but not empty. It is crowded with distant folds, each one the softened remnant of something harder, each one holding the same dull red-brown color beneath a skin of gray.

There are no plants. No weeds in the cracks, no pale films of moss, no thorn or grass or lichen. The absence is not recent enough to feel like a disaster. The stone has had time to become accustomed to it. Its surfaces are bare, and where they are not bare they are coated in deposits: white salts, yellow crusts, blue-black stains, and thin fans of copper-green mineral that spread around old seams. Some expanses glitter faintly when seen from the side, not with reflected light but with the tiny, fixed faces of crystals packed into the ground.

Moisture occurs in pockets.

A shallow rain may have fallen sometime in the unmeasured past, or condensation may have gathered beneath the basin’s overhangs. Water occupies the lowest places in narrow, motionless sheets. It darkens the stone rather than reflecting it. In some hollows, clear pools sit beneath a skin of mineral haze, their edges sharply outlined by rings of salt. Elsewhere, a dampness has soaked into porous ground and risen again as beads along the walls of a trench. The beads are warm. They do not run. They remain, each one swollen against the stone, until they slowly disappear.

The air is warm and heavy, though not oppressive. It carries the smell of wet iron, hot dust, and something faintly sour, like the interior of a battery or the breath of a deep mechanical chamber. Visibility is good under a flat, ordinary sky. There is no dramatic glow, no colored sun, no perpetual twilight. Shadows lie where they should lie. The light reveals the basin plainly and gives it no mystery except the mystery of its persistence.

Across the open ground, machinery works.

It is not housed in buildings. There are no towers, roads, pipes, fences, platforms, or walls. The machines are mostly below the surface, and what can be seen of them is not an object so much as an effect. Long strips of black stone tremble at regular intervals. A buried engine turns somewhere beneath a plain, causing the ground to pulse with a deep, measured force. In another region, a row of vents opens directly in the mineral crust, each one exhaling a column of warm vapor. The vapor rises a few meters, loses its shape, and settles back into the basin as dampness.

The sound is immense.

It does not come from movement in the landscape. Nothing is visibly moving. The sound comes from the continuous operation of things concealed under it: turbines, pumps, compressors, grinders, electromagnetic drives, and immense rotating assemblies whose purpose has long since become inseparable from the geology. The noise rolls across the low terrain in layers. Near the vents it is a hard metallic roar, full of vibration and pressure. Farther away it becomes a broad, steady droning, as if the entire basin were a single machine idling beneath a sheet of stone.

There are tones within the noise. A slow hammering repeats somewhere beyond the next ridge. A thin whine rises and falls without variation. Deep impacts pass through the ground at long intervals, felt more clearly in the feet than heard in the ears. The sounds do not converge into rhythm. They have no beginning and no end. They are simply present, as constant as heat.

The machinery does not seem to manufacture anything.

It may once have extracted material. It may once have processed the basin’s salts or metals, or maintained an underground pressure system, or converted heat into energy for a settlement that no longer exists. But no waste heaps interrupt the land, and no product emerges from the vents. The engines operate without visible purpose, their output disappearing into the ground or passing from one buried system into another. Their industrial character is unmistakable, but their relationship to industry has become abstract. They do not build, transport, or consume in any recognizable cycle. They continue.

The basin is sparse. A dark, many-legged animal appears perhaps once in several days, if the impression can be trusted. It moves along the damp margins of a mineral pool, low to the ground, with a body like folded charcoal and a few dull, translucent plates. It makes no sound. When approached, it does not flee so much as become difficult to distinguish from the stones. There are occasional traces of other life: narrow impressions near a vent, a cluster of hollow shells fused into the ground, a threadlike residue around a pool that may be biological or merely chemical. Nothing grows from these traces. Nothing hunts openly. The ecology, if it is an ecology, has been reduced to rare encounters between organisms and the stable heat of the earth.

Some surfaces are strangely smooth. They form broad, shallow planes that look poured rather than eroded, though there are no edges or seams to confirm construction. On these planes, moisture gathers in perfect, widely spaced circles. Each circle contains a dark central spot, and each spot gives off a tiny sound—a click, a tick, a brief electrical snap. The pattern never changes. The circles do not spread, merge, or dry. They are one of the basin’s small irregularities, accepted by everything around them.

Time is difficult to perceive here.

Dust does not visibly drift. Water does not visibly evaporate. The vapor above the vents remains in nearly the same low columns. Erosion has finished its urgent work. The ridges are old and rounded; the channels have settled into their courses; the mineral crusts have thickened without advancing. Even the weather seems less like weather than an occasional condition imposed upon the place. Rain may arrive as a brief darkening of the ground, fall in widely separated drops, and vanish from attention without changing anything.

If there is wind, it is only a warm pressure moving across the basin. It carries no seeds and rearranges no loose debris. The world has no use for dispersal. There is nowhere for anything to take root.

At the far edge, where the low land meets a band of darker rock, the ground is split by a long, shallow fissure. From it comes the loudest sound, a continuous industrial thunder that flattens every other noise nearby. Yet the fissure itself is narrow and unremarkable. No flame emerges. No light shines within. Only heat, vibration, and a faint dampness along its lips. Standing beside it, one feels that the entire region is being held in place by whatever turns below.

Nothing in the basin appears unfinished. Nothing appears to be waiting for development, weather, invasion, or repair. The machines have not failed, but neither are they actively succeeding. The pools have not dried. The rare animals have not multiplied. The stone has not broken further. Under the ordinary light, among the salt rings and dark mineral shelves, the place has reached a kind of permanent industrial equilibrium: a warm, bare landscape maintained by loud mechanisms no one can see, where moisture gathers, life barely persists, and every apparent change resolves into the same old shape.
