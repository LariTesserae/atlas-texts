The place is a high basin cut into a mountain that no longer belongs entirely to geology.

It is small enough to cross in an hour, if crossing were possible, but its boundaries are severe: on every side, the land falls away into a white mineral emptiness, and the basin itself hangs above a greater absence. There is no river, no pond, no seep, no glimmering thread in the stone. The hollows are filled with pale dust and fields of salt-like crystal, though the crystals are not salt and the dust is not quite dust. Both are residues of old atmospheric reactions, laid down over ages by winds that have never carried rain.

The air is cold and extraordinarily clear. It has the thin, sharpened quality of altitude, but beneath that is a pressure one feels in the teeth and behind the eyes. The sky is a hard blue-black at the zenith, paling toward the horizon where the mountain’s edge cuts across it. Light falls normally—cold sunlight, clean shadows—but everything seems overexposed at the margins, as if the world is being rendered by an instrument with too much resolution.

The basin’s floor is covered in vegetation.

Not green vegetation. Not mostly.

There are mats of blue-black fronds, forests of jointed silver stalks, low cushions that shine like tarnished copper, and tall hollow reeds whose surfaces are veined with slow-moving amber light. They grow densely in patches, then stop abruptly, leaving hundreds of meters of bare ground between one living concentration and the next. From a distance these patches resemble islands in a dry sea. Up close they are more like organs laid out beneath the sky: every plant has a function, a posture, a direction of attention.

Their roots do not seek water. They anchor themselves in seams of warm mineral and draw nourishment from the electrical gradients that pass through the mountain. Many are rooted only in appearance. Some cling to the ground with lattices of filament, while others suspend their lower ends a finger’s breadth above the stone, feeding on the invisible turbulence there. Their leaves are rigid planes, overlapping like scales or solar panels. Their flowers are apertures, polished mouths, rotating slowly toward the sun.

The vegetation is lush because it is hungry for energy, not because the land is wet.

When the wind comes—and the wind is almost always coming—it passes through the growth in a violent chorus. The tall reeds do not sway; they flex in synchronized waves, each one emitting a deep metallic note. The low mats chatter with millions of tiny impacts as their scales open and close. The copper cushions produce a grinding hum. In the densest groves, these sounds combine into a deafening chord that seems to come from below the ground as much as from the plants themselves.

There are moments when the whole basin sings one immense, sustained tone.

The tone is so loud that it erases thought. It enters the body as pressure. Loose mineral slides from the cliffs. The air in the lungs trembles. Creatures flatten themselves against the ground, and the scattered structures on the ridges unfold protective skins like flowers closing at night.

The fauna is abundant, though rarely visible for long.

Things move through the vacant spaces between the plant colonies: long, many-legged grazers made from matte ceramic and translucent tissue; thin-winged shapes that ride the pressure fronts; flocks of small bright animals that appear as handfuls of thrown needles. Some creatures are nearly weightless, rising on the basin’s electromagnetic currents and descending only to feed. Others tunnel through the mineral crust, leaving behind temporary lines of blue fire.

The largest animals are slow and cathedral-like. They have no obvious heads. Their bodies are arched frames of bone, chitin, or engineered alloy, with vegetation growing through them in deliberate symbiosis. A single one may stand for centuries in the center of a grove, its limbs sunk into the stone, while smaller organisms nest in its cavities. When it moves, the movement is sudden and catastrophic. The ground breaks open beneath it. The sound of its joints resembles distant artillery.

The fauna does not appear to fear the few structures scattered across the basin. It feeds on them, incorporates them, or uses them as shelter.

One structure stands near the basin’s highest rim: a narrow tower with no visible entrance. Its walls are black and perfectly smooth except where the wind has exposed layers of luminous circuitry beneath the surface. The tower is old enough to have acquired a weathering pattern, but the weathering is not decay. Its outer skin grows back in branching sheets whenever struck by dust. Each repair is slightly different from the last. The tower is slowly becoming something else.

Farther off are smaller remnants: three silver arches half-buried in mineral powder; a low platform held above the ground by a field of invisible force; a cluster of glassy stakes arranged around nothing. They may once have formed a settlement, or a machine, or a single device that was dismantled by time. No path connects them. The inhabitants, if they were inhabitants, traveled in the air or through the stone.

At irregular intervals, one of the artifacts wakes.

A line of light runs across the basin. The plants fold toward it. The animals scatter—not away, but sideways, as though obeying a geometry not shared by the ground. Then the empty air fills with transparent architecture: bridges, lattices, impossible stairways, brief rooms whose walls show places elsewhere. These forms persist for seconds or centuries. Some collapse harmlessly. Others strike the landscape with tremendous force, rearranging the mineral crust and flinging entire stands of vegetation into the air.

The basin is in constant crisis, but not in decline.

Nothing settles for long. A grove may erupt into a forest overnight, its stalks punching upward with enough force to crack the plateau. A slope may turn inside out, exposing a buried machine or a colony of pale burrowing animals. Wind arrives from below. Shadows detach from their objects and race across the ground. A field of crystals can suddenly liquefy into a crawling foam, then harden again around whatever has crossed it.

Yet the place has existed in this state for a very long time. Its violence is established. Its turbulence is an equilibrium.

The cliffs are smooth from ancient abrasion, their faces carved into long, horizontal shelves. In sheltered hollows, the rock has been polished almost black. On exposed ridges, it is pale and porous, covered with scars that may have been made by weather, machinery, or enormous living things dragging themselves across the mountain. There are no fresh landslides. Even the broken ground looks old, as though destruction here is immediately accepted into the shape of the terrain.

At the basin’s center is a ring of bare mineral surrounding a single low plant.

It has no leaves, only hundreds of thin vertical blades arranged in a perfect circle. Each blade is different: some transparent, some dark, some glowing faintly from within. They bend toward events before those events occur. When a storm of debris forms on the horizon, they lean. When an animal is born under the ground, they lean. When one of the ancient machines is about to activate, all of them point toward it.

Nothing grows inside the ring. Nothing crosses it.

The plant’s sound is almost inaudible beneath the general roar, but if one stands close enough, one can hear a rapid sequence of clicks—too fast to count, too regular to be natural. The clicks are not language exactly. They are adjustments. Measurements. The plant is keeping the basin in balance, or perhaps merely recording the ways in which balance fails.

Above it all, the high atmosphere flashes without thunder.

Vast discharges move horizontally through the thin air, illuminating the clouds from within. There are no rain clouds. The clouds are made of mineral vapor, charged dust, and drifting organisms. They form and unravel in seconds, casting enormous shadows over the small basin. Under their passage, the vegetation changes color. The silver stalks become violet. The copper mats turn white. The amber veins in the reeds flare bright enough to cast their own shadows.

The wind rises again.

Every plant in the nearest grove snaps upright. The animals vanish into the ground or launch themselves into the empty spaces. The tower’s skin opens in a thousand narrow seams. Something deep beneath the basin begins to rotate.

Then the landscape becomes sound: a tremendous, layered, deafening roar of living systems, weather, machinery, and stone, all changing at once without becoming confused. The mountain does not merely endure it.

The mountain is doing it.
