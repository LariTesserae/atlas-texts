The plateau begins where the ascent ends.

There is no summit, no dramatic ridge to announce arrival—only a long, cool rising through darkness until the land loses its inclination and becomes an immense upper plain. It stands high above the lower world, though the lower world is rarely visible. The horizon is usually cut off by curtains of black-green growth, by terraces of ancient plant matter, or by the pale ceiling of the atmosphere itself. On clear days, if such days can be said to exist here, the sky is a dim metallic blue, almost colorless near the horizon and deepening overhead into a bruised violet. Light comes weakly from a sun that never seems to warm anything. At night the plateau does not become dark so much as redistribute its darkness: vast leaves close, root-towers go dormant, and thousands of buried organs begin to glow beneath the ground.

The plain is entirely occupied by vegetation.

Not covered by it. Occupied.

The soil, where there is soil, is only a thin residue between living structures. Broad fields of low, leathery plants have spread over the old stone until they resemble roofing, paving, or frozen surf. Their surfaces are matte and gray-green, ribbed with channels that do not carry water but gradients of pressure, heat, and chemical vapor. Underfoot, the vegetation is firm enough to walk on, though it gives faintly with each step, like dense muscle beneath a crust. In some places a foot sinks through a membrane and comes up dusted with silver spores. In others the ground rings like ceramic because the roots have mineralized into interlocking plates.

The oldest plants have forgotten the distinction between trunk, wall, and terrain.

They rise in scattered colonies across the plateau: immense vertical boles with faceted sides, each one several hundred feet tall, their bark compressed into black layers like the pages of a book written in stone. Their upper branches do not bear leaves. Instead they support vast suspended fans, translucent and finely veined, which turn slowly in the thin air. They harvest pressure differentials, trace gases, electrical charge. When the fans move, the whole plateau hears them.

The sound is not the sound of a forest.

It is a continuous, physical roar.

The great fans beat at different intervals, producing long waves of thunder that travel across the open ground. Beneath them, root-pumps knock in the dark. Seed capsules snap open with reports like rifles. Stiff grasslands hiss and clatter as their blades fold against one another. Far off, a colony of hollow-stemmed plants emits a descending chord, each stalk opening and closing in sequence until the note seems to pass through the earth. The noise rises and falls without ever ceasing. Even in a quiet interval there is a vibration under the soles, a deep vegetative machinery working through the plateau.

The air is dry enough to hurt the mouth. Nothing here runs. There are no streams, ponds, marshes, or beads of condensation. The plants have evolved around the absence of liquid water so completely that the idea of wetness seems primitive. Their tissues are filled with gels, pressurized oils, reactive slurries, and dense clouds of suspended particles. Some absorb minerals directly from the air. Some draw charge from the rocks. Others feed on the heat differential between their shaded roots and their cold upper surfaces. A few maintain internal atmospheres in sealed chambers, opening only when a passing creature triggers them.

In the broad spaces between the plant colonies, the ancient stone shows through. It is pale, flat, and heavily fractured, an elevated shield of basalt and salt-colored mineral. Erosion has worked for so long that it has nearly finished its work. There are no sharp escarpments here, only low ramps, shallow basins, and terraces worn down to their final geometry. The land appears less destroyed than settled. Every ridge has become a shelf. Every canyon has become a trough just deep enough to retain dust and roots.

Some of the troughs are lined with cities.

They are not dense cities. They occupy the gaps between old growths, leaving enormous fields of empty ground around them. From a distance, a settlement looks like a scattering of dark vertical marks and low geometric embankments, as if someone had placed the remains of a town across a quarry. Towers stand apart from one another, joined by narrow bridges and buried conduits. Their surfaces are covered in climbing plants that have been trained, or perhaps persuaded, into precise layers. The living architecture holds the buildings together. It seals fractures. It changes opacity in response to the dim light. It grows new balconies when populations increase and closes whole corridors when the local ecology shifts.

The streets are not paved. They are cultivated.

A traveler follows a strip of pale rootwood that hardens beneath the feet and softens behind them, leaving no path except a temporary memory in the colony. Transit lines run as elevated tendons between the towers, carrying small, silent capsules through the air. The capsules do not seem to accelerate. They simply vanish from one station and appear at another, their passage marked by a brief distortion in the surrounding plants. A network of silver needles extends from the buildings into the plateau, measuring pressures too subtle for ordinary instruments. Every settlement is therefore partly an observatory, partly a greenhouse, and partly an organ of the landscape itself.

The technology is everywhere, but almost none of it looks manufactured.

A black tree may be a weather engine. A field of squat red leaves may be a distributed computer. A cliff face may unfold into a communications array only during certain atmospheric conditions. Beneath the ground, root systems have been modified over centuries into information networks, their chemical exchanges carrying memories across the region. The oldest civic records are not stored in archives but in groves whose growth rings contain altered sequences of mineral. To read one, a machine lowers a probe into the trunk and listens to the changes in resonance.

What the people call infrastructure is indistinguishable from ecology.

What the ecology calls people is less clear.

Fauna is abundant, though most animals are not immediately recognizable as animals. There are long, jointed grazers that move across the plant-fields on three pairs of narrow legs, stripping mineral-rich films from the ground with rasping mouths. Their bodies are wrapped in folded plates that open and close to regulate temperature. They travel in loose, widely spaced herds, and their footfalls produce a hard, synchronized ticking.

Above them drift the lantern-moths: large, nearly weightless creatures with sail-like wings and luminous organs suspended inside their rib cages. They feed on the airborne spores of the fan-trees. When disturbed, they release clouds of shining dust that make the air look full of slow stars. The dust is harmless to most life, though settlements close their vents when the moths pass.

There are predators, too. Some move with the plants rather than through them. A line of dark leaves may suddenly lift itself from the ground and reveal a many-limbed body beneath. Thin hunting organisms imitate the sound of root-pumps, drawing small animals toward hollows where the soil opens. Along the edges of the cities, pale things with glassy heads wait motionless among the cultivated roots, taking readings from the feet of anyone who passes. They may be domesticated. They may be surveillance devices. They may be both.

Every creature leaves a trace of sound.

The grazers tick. The moths hum. The burrowing predators produce subsonic pulses that make the sternum ache. At certain hours, thousands of small animals emerge from the crust and begin calling at once, creating a shrill, layered noise like metal being torn in the distance. Their breeding cycles are synchronized with the flowering of the old plants, and when the plateau blooms, the sound becomes almost unbearable.

The blooms themselves are rare and enormous.

A single plant may open one flower in a century, raising it from a sealed shaft in the ground. It unfolds slowly over several days, petal by petal, until it forms a basin wider than a city square. Nothing moistens the basin. Instead, it fills with a dense, pale vapor drawn from deep chemical reservoirs. The vapor catches the weak light and becomes a luminous pool without liquid, a suspended brightness in which insects, machines, and small animals lose their orientation. The flower’s opening calls fauna from miles away. For a week, the surrounding plateau is crowded with feeding creatures, mating swarms, maintenance drones, and wandering citizens who come to witness the event. Then the petals close, harden, and remain as a stone-colored monument for another hundred years.

The weather is active despite the dryness.

Pressure fronts travel over the plateau as invisible walls. When one arrives, all the plants bend in the same direction, not from wind but from a brief alteration in the atmosphere’s electrical structure. The fan-trees accelerate. Root systems flash with blue fire. Loose mineral dust rises in vertical columns and hangs suspended above the ground. Sometimes the front passes harmlessly. Sometimes it causes every sealed plant within a region to open at once, releasing a roar, a cloud of spores, and enough static charge to make the city towers sing.

There are no storms of rain. There are storms of reaction.

The sky may turn green with ionized dust. A dry lightning can crawl along the surface of a root-field for miles. Ancient mineral shelves crack open and expose buried growths that have been waiting in darkness since before the settlements were founded. The inhabitants respond with practiced calm. Towers fold their upper sections inward. Transit tendons retract. Cities lower themselves into the plant crust while the fauna gathers beneath the broadest leaves.

When the weather ends, the plateau is always slightly different.

A new grove may have risen overnight from a fracture. A road may have closed itself. An old building may have been absorbed up to its windows. The landscape changes slowly by ordinary measures, but its timescale is not entirely human. It is an active, thinking succession of organisms and machines, constantly repairing, testing, extending, and discarding itself.

Still, it feels ancient.

The trees are older than the cities. The mineral shelves were worn smooth before the first engineered species arrived. Even the modified plants contain dormant structures whose functions no living intelligence remembers. There is a pervasive sense that the plateau is not developing toward anything. It has already passed through its age of invention. The cities, the transit lines, the cultivated roads, and the impossible biological instruments are not signs of a young civilization expanding into the world. They are the maintenance systems of something that has endured almost to the end of history.

Beyond the settlements, the open ground stretches toward more plant colonies, more low terraces, more distant towers half-hidden in the dimness. Sparse fires of bioluminescence mark the roots. Vast animals move between them like pieces on a board too large to understand. Above, the translucent fans turn in the thin cold air, harvesting the invisible elements of a world without water.

The plateau does not feel barren.

It feels overgrown past the point where barrenness was possible.
