The plateau is high enough to see the curvature of the land, but not so high that the air feels thin. It lies beneath a warm, white-gold sky, a broad country of ochre stone and pale mineral soil extending in long shelves toward distant ridges. The ridges are not sharp. They have been worn down over immense spans of time into deliberate-looking forms: tables, ramps, shallow bowls, and terraces where rainwater gathers for a few days before vanishing into the rock.

Moisture arrives irregularly. There may be no rain for weeks, then a storm crosses the plateau in silence, leaving dark patches scattered across the ground. Water collects in hollows lined with blue-black clay, in cracks beneath overhanging shelves, and in shallow basins where condensation beads every morning. At dawn, the stones sweat. By midday, most of the water is gone, drawn into the porous ground or lifted invisibly into the heated air.

The vegetation is sparse but not frail. Low silver grasses grow in separated cushions, each one rooted around a buried seam of moisture. There are small, thick-leaved plants with translucent skins, and black-stemmed shrubs that open only during the brief coolness before sunrise. Here and there, a solitary tree rises from a depression, its trunk twisted by ancient winds, its canopy narrow and high. The trees do not make a forest. They mark water.

Between them, the land is open and brightly exposed. Sunlight catches on mica in the stone and flashes from thousands of tiny mineral facets. Farther off, whole slopes appear to shimmer—not from heat haze, but from fine reflective dust moving over the ground in slow, wind-driven sheets. The effect makes the plateau seem to breathe.

Animals are everywhere once the eye learns how to find them. Long-legged grazers move in small, widely spaced groups, their bodies the color of the stone until they turn and reveal soft green undersides. They drink from condensation wells with narrow mouths and stand motionless for long periods, listening. Small burrowing creatures leave branching patterns of fresh soil around the bases of shrubs. Bright-winged insects pass between flowers too widely separated for any ordinary meadow, traveling in straight lines as if following invisible cords.

Above the plateau, pale shapes drift on thermal currents. Some are birds; others are not easily classified. One kind resembles a manta ray made of skin-thin membranes, folding and unfolding itself without flapping. It casts a moving shadow over the ground and occasionally descends to skim moisture from the backs of animals. Nothing reacts with alarm. The relationship seems old.

The quiet is unusually complete. There is wind, but it moves high above the surface, making a remote rushing sound along the ridges. Near the ground, the air is almost still. Footsteps produce dry clicks. Insects give off brief, delicate pulses rather than a continuous hum. At intervals, something underground emits a low tone—too soft to be called a vibration, more an alteration in the silence. The animals pause when it happens. Then they resume feeding.

Scattered across the plateau are remnants of construction, though “remnants” may be the wrong word. A narrow tower stands alone on a rise, its surface seamless and pale, without doors, seams, or visible support. It leans at an impossible angle but has not fallen. Once every few minutes, a faint line of violet light travels from its base to its tip and disappears into the sky.

Elsewhere, dark arches emerge from the soil. They are too thin to be bridges and too large to be frames. Nothing passes through them, yet dust avoids their interiors, and rain does not fall within their shadows. A half-buried platform occupies one of the larger hollows. Its surface is covered in shallow depressions that fill with water after storms. When an animal steps into one, the water briefly reflects not the animal, but a different position: the same body several paces away, or standing beneath another angle of sun.

There are no roads. Still, the scattered devices seem arranged according to a pattern that becomes visible only from high ground. Lines connect the tower, the arches, the water basins, and the solitary trees. They may be boundaries, measurements, or the remains of a machine whose operation has been reduced to environmental effects. The plateau does not feel abandoned. It feels maintained by processes too slow, too distributed, or too subtle to identify as maintenance.

The weather changes with little warning. Clouds gather in a clear sky, not above the land but at its level, forming thin horizontal veils that slide through the gullies. They leave behind beads of warm water on every surface. During these passages, dormant plants unfold, animals emerge from burrows, and hidden panels in the old structures briefly become visible beneath their mineral coating. Tiny motes of light rise from the damp ground and drift toward the highest ridges.

By afternoon, the clouds are gone. The land brightens again, and the plateau settles into its spacious balance: stone, distance, isolated growth, abundant life moving quietly through the gaps. Nothing crowds the view. Every presence seems to have been given exactly enough room to endure.
