The place is a hollow in the upper face of a mountain, though “hollow” suggests an openness it does not possess. It is a narrow, vertical basin formed where the summit broke and then, over immense spans of time, stopped breaking. The walls rise close around it: black volcanic stone on one side, pale mineral crust on the other, both weathered into shelves, lips, pockets, and smooth inward curves. The floor is no more than a small area—perhaps the size of a village square—but it is crowded with every surface the mountain has allowed to exist.

Above, the cleft narrows to a slit of sky too distant and oblique to provide light. Clouds pass over it, and sometimes rain falls through in a silver thread, but down here it is always dark. Not the clean darkness of night: a humid, textured darkness, thick with movement. The stone sweats. Condensation gathers along the undersides of ledges and releases itself in patient drops. Water has collected in shallow basins worn into the rock, some no wider than a hand, some deep enough to conceal an entire animal. These pools vanish and return according to weather far beyond sight. During dry periods they become black, damp stains; after a storm they join in trickles that descend through cracks and disappear beneath the floor.

The air is warm despite the elevation. It rises from the mountain’s interior in slow breaths, carrying the smell of wet stone, old guano, sulfur, and the green, bitter odor of things that grow without sun. The temperature changes little. The hollow is insulated by its depth and by the bodies packed into it.

There is almost no vegetation. A few tough, pale plants cling to the mineral shelves: threadlike mosses, small fans of fungus, and low, leathery growths that spread around the condensation pools. Their leaves are gray, translucent, or nearly colorless. Some feed on the mineral runoff. Others live from the waste of the animals. They do not make the place green; they mark it, like bruises or stains, in small dense patches.

The fauna is everywhere.

The upper walls are furred with roosting creatures whose bodies hang in overlapping layers. They are not all of one kind. Long-winged gliders cling beneath the shelves beside small, blind mammals with grasping feet, and pale insects cluster in the seams between them. When disturbed, the wall seems to liquefy. Wings unfold over wings. Bodies drop, turn, and rise again, making a brief storm within the darkness before settling back into the stone.

The ledges are occupied by squat, warm-blooded animals with broad hands and blunt faces. They live in the crevices and feed on the smaller things. Their calls are low and resonant, more felt through the soles than heard through the ears. They quarrel constantly over the wettest ledges, though their disputes are slow and ritualized: a baring of teeth, a heavy shove, a long exchange of throaty pulses. Nothing here moves quickly unless it must. Even predation has a patient quality. A many-jointed hunter waits beside a pool for hours, its limbs folded into the same angles as the surrounding cracks.

The water basins are thick with life. Tiny translucent larvae drift through them, some carrying luminous organs that glow only when touched. Flat, armored things graze the mineral film. Thread-finned creatures rise to drink the falling drops and sink again. When the pools shrink, the animals do not flee. They enter the wet mud around them, seal themselves in mineral skins, or gather in dense knots beneath stones and wait for the mountain to sweat again.

Sound fills the hollow so completely that silence is impossible. The constant noise is made from many small sources pressed together: the ticking of falling water, the scrape of claws on rock, the papery shifting of wings, the wet clicking of feeding mouths, the deep calls from the ledges, and the continuous rustle of bodies making room for other bodies. Above all is the echo. Every sound returns from the close walls, slightly altered and delayed, so that a single cry becomes a crowd answering from everywhere.

At intervals the entire basin gives a low, prolonged rumble. This is not an earthquake. Far below, water moves through the mountain. The sound travels upward through the stone and causes the animals to pause. For a few breaths the creatures become still enough that the drip of condensation can be heard distinctly. Then the rumble passes, and the hollow resumes its layered activity.

The walls show the age of the place. There are no fresh collapses, no raw scars, no unstable piles of rubble. Every break has been rounded. Every fallen fragment has been incorporated into the floor, polished by feet, bellies, and water. The stone has reached an arrangement that seems permanent without being rigid. A ledge may crumble by the width of a finger over a century; a channel may deepen imperceptibly; a pool may migrate from one hollow to another. Change occurs, but it is measured in generations of animals.

There are no paths, tools, shelters, or marks made for any purpose beyond survival. The inhabitants use the mountain as it is. They know the wet shelves, the dangerous cracks, the pools that refill first, and the high recess where the air is warmest. Some animals carry pieces of fungus or shed skin from one niche to another, but this is instinctive cultivation rather than craft. Nothing has been built. Even the dens are temporary arrangements of bodies, feathers, hair, and loose mineral flakes.

The only light comes from the life itself. It is weak and intermittent: a pale bloom under a pool, a brief green pulse along a larva’s side, the dull reflection of wet eyes. These glimmers do not illuminate the basin. They make the darkness more apparent by giving it edges. A creature is recognized first as two points of light, then as a shifting mass among other shifting masses.

At the center of the hollow is the deepest basin, an oval depression in the floor. It is usually covered by a skin of dark water. Nothing large lives in it, but everything visits it. The animals crowd its margins in layered rings, drinking, washing, hunting, laying eggs, and waiting. When rain reaches the cleft, the basin rises. When the mountain dries, it falls. The entire ecology follows this modest pulse.

From outside, the place would be invisible: a dark seam high above the cloud line, an opening too small to distinguish from the mountain’s weathered face. Inside, it is a complete world compressed into stone—warm, wet in patches, deafening with life, and almost entirely without light. Its boundaries are absolute. The walls do not invite passage, and the creatures do not seem to imagine a world beyond them. They live within the slow breathing of the mountain, crowded together in a darkness that has lasted so long it feels less like an absence of light than a substance of the landscape itself.
