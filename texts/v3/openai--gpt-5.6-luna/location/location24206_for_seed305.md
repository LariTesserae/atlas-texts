The high country begins above the weather.

It is not a mountain range in the ordinary sense, though from below it presents itself as a wall of mountains: a continent-wide uplift of black, newly hardened stone, thrust upward in long blades and tilted tables. The rock is dark glass, iron-red seams, pale mineral crusts, and surfaces so sharp that snow cannot settle on them. Everything has the unfinished look of a world still cooling. Ridges end abruptly. Valleys are narrow wounds. Slopes descend in enormous fresh planes, broken by teeth of stone and shelves that have not yet decided whether they are cliffs or future soil.

At this elevation the air is thin, brilliantly clear, and cold enough to make moisture behave strangely. There are no broad rivers. Water appears in brief, concentrated forms: a silver rainstorm that crosses the plateau once every few weeks; clouds snagged on the highest escarpments; beads of condensation gathered along the undersides of overhangs; meltwater imprisoned in cracks and released slowly through the rock. In the darkness beneath the stone, water travels for years through porous layers before emerging as a sudden vertical spring.

These springs are the green places.

They spill into clefts and terraces, and wherever they remain for more than a season, vegetation crowds in with almost violent abundance. Low forests of blue-black stalks rise shoulder to shoulder from the mineral mud. Their leaves are narrow, translucent, and densely veined, catching the white sunlight like thousands of small windows. Mosses and mats cover every surface that stays damp: the backs of boulders, the walls of ravines, the limbs of other plants. Long, jointed growths bridge gaps in the stone, rooting at both ends and flexing gently in the wind. Some carry their own water in swollen green cords. Others draw condensation from the air and funnel it underground.

There is almost no empty ground.

Where the vegetation does not grow, something else occupies the space: pale crusts of mineral, colonies of burrowing animals, mats of black filament, fans of red lichen, or loose heaps of shed plates and bones. The living world is packed into every fissure. A square meter beside a spring may contain several layers of plant, insect, fungus, and animal activity, all moving at different speeds. Even the stone seems inhabited. Fine roots enter its pores. Tiny creatures scrape mineral from its face. Subterranean organisms pass through it in tunnels as straight and regular as drilled shafts.

The plateau is full of engines.

They are not built. No hand has assembled them, and no road leads to them. Yet they are unmistakably industrial in their scale and function. Across the high flats stand groves of immense, dark organisms whose trunks are hollow pressure chambers. Wind enters through slots in their sides and turns internal vanes. The resulting force pumps nutrient slurry from deep seams in the earth, lifting it into elevated reservoirs of living tissue. Other structures resemble factories grown from mineral and cartilage: long ribbed halls, chimney clusters, heat exchangers made of overlapping plates. They process the sparse moisture and the rich stone into gases, salts, spores, and food.

Some have been operating for thousands of years.

Their work is slow, but never idle. A vent opens by a fraction. A piston of root and bone descends. A membrane contracts. A column of warm vapor rises. Nothing happens quickly enough to look like violence, yet the whole plateau is engaged in patient production. New hills of biological waste accumulate beside ancient processing colonies. Mineral slag, bright as polished copper or green as oxidized glass, is deposited in terraces. The terraces become fertile. The fertility attracts animals. The animals break down the terraces. The machinery feeds on what remains and begins again.

The bright sky makes all of this difficult to hide.

The sun is hard and almost colorless, throwing shadows that are intensely blue. Light travels far across the uplands. On clear days, distant ridges appear close enough to touch, while things nearby shimmer at the edge of perception. The air is full of reflected glare from ice, mineral crust, and wet leaves. At noon, the plateau can seem bleached and merciless. Then a cloudbank arrives from beyond the horizon and pours itself into a valley, filling it completely. The cloud remains there for hours, glowing from within, while the upper ridges stay in unbroken sunlight.

Rain is local and consequential. A single storm may fall over one ravine and nowhere else. Water rushes down the black slopes in temporary silver threads, carrying seeds, fragments of soft rock, larvae, and entire mats of moss. By the next day the channels are dry again. The landscape remembers the storm in small changes: a new green line, a collapsed ledge, a congregation of animals around a shallow pool.

The animals are everywhere.

Most are not large, but there are so many of them that the vegetation appears to flow. Millions of small bodies move beneath the leaves. Pale, many-legged grazers cling to stems in dense layers. Winged things rise in clouds whenever the wind shifts. Broad-backed animals wedge themselves into cracks, their warm bodies steaming faintly in the cold. Long-necked browsers pick their way along cliff shelves, feeding on the upper fronds of the tallest growths. They carry colonies of smaller organisms in their fur, and their passage leaves behind trails of seeds, larvae, and warm compost.

There are also immense creatures that seem to belong to the geography rather than the fauna.

Slow herds cross the plateau, each animal the size of a hillock, armored in overlapping mineral plates. Their feet make shallow basins in the stone. Their breath emerges as low clouds. When one stops, plants colonize its back; when it moves again, entire gardens travel with it. From a distance, a herd can be mistaken for a range of low, dark ridges advancing across the plain.

Predators are less common but never absent. They emerge from green chasms and mineral vents, moving with a deliberate economy that makes their size difficult to judge. Some have mirror-bright eyes adapted to the relentless light. Some imitate the sound of machinery to draw prey toward them. A few are nearly invisible except when they disturb the condensation on a rock. Their hunts are not frantic. The abundant life leaves no need for speed. A predator may wait for a day, a season, or the gradual collapse of a feeding colony.

Sound travels strangely in the thin air.

The high country is loud, but not in the way of cities. The industrial organisms produce a continual bass vibration, felt through the soles before it is heard. Vast pressure organs exhale in intervals. Vents clatter as their mineral teeth adjust. Deep beneath the plateau, something turns with the slow regularity of a planetary wheel.

Above this, the fauna creates a constant layered noise: chittering, clicking, wingbeats, whistles, shrieks, wet impacts, and the dry rasp of thousands of feeding mouths. When a herd passes, the ground trembles beneath the collective weight. When a flock rises, it fills the air with a sound like tearing fabric. The ravines amplify everything. A single animal calling from a green cleft may produce echoes from slopes kilometers away, and the echoes sometimes answer in altered voices.

Wind is the largest instrument. It moves across the exposed stone in long, sustained tones, then catches in forests and turns into a roar. Through the slots of the pressure groves it becomes a sequence of deep notes, each one lasting minutes. On certain days the whole plateau sings: plants flexing, engines breathing, animals calling, stones ticking as they expand under the sun.

Despite the noise, the world changes slowly.

The cliffs are young, but erosion has not yet had time to soften them. Fresh fractures remain bright. Landslides are rare and enormous. A single fall may take centuries to finish settling. The great valleys deepen by increments too small for any animal to notice. Forests advance one damp crack at a time. A spring can nourish the same dense colony for generations before shifting its course by the width of a hand.

The slowest things are also the largest. Weather systems circle the uplands in broad, recurring patterns. Whole cloud fields gather and disperse according to cycles longer than the lives of the largest animals. The industrial colonies alter the atmosphere by degrees, releasing heat and vapor into the high air. Their emissions seed distant clouds. Their clouds return as condensation. Their condensation feeds the colonies. The entire landscape operates as a closed, patient machine.

Nothing here has been built, yet nothing is untouched.

There are no walls, towers, roads, tools, or ruins. There are no signs of deliberate planning in the human sense. But the plateau is saturated with organization. Every available material has entered a process. Every hollow is a habitat, a reservoir, a nest, a lung, or a factory. Every bright surface reflects not emptiness but activity.

At the far edge of the uplands, where the black stone descends into permanent cloud, the factories continue. Their chimneys rise above the weather. Their slow exhaust forms a luminous veil around the peaks. Below, unseen rivers gather from the condensation and begin their long journey toward lower lands.

Above them, in the white sun, the new continent keeps producing itself.
