The plain begins below the last rise of dry country, where the ground loses its slope and spreads for many miles beneath the heat. It is not a marsh in the continuous sense. Water comes and goes here. In the wet season it gathers in long, dark channels and shallow basins; in the dry months it retreats into cracked hollows, root chambers, and the deep shade beneath the vegetation. Rain may pass overhead without reaching the soil. It breaks apart on the roof of the forest, dripping for hours afterward from leaf to leaf, while the ground remains powder-dry in some places and black with standing water in others.

At night, the landscape is almost entirely dark.

There is no moonlight strong enough to separate the trees. The vegetation rises in dense layers: low thorny shrubs, broad-leaved plants with waxy surfaces, mats of grass, vines, reeds, and tall trees whose trunks stand close together as if the forest had been packed into place over centuries. Nothing grows in clean intervals. Every opening is occupied by another stalk, another fan of leaves, another rooted mass lifting itself above the mud. The plants have adapted to the alternating extremes—heat that bakes the upper ground and sudden, soaking water—and many of them hold their own moisture in thick stems or folded leaves.

The air is hot even after sunset. It lies heavily over the plain, carrying the smell of wet earth, bruised greenery, stagnant pools, bitter sap, and the mineral tang of old mud. In some places there is a faint oily scent, not strong enough to identify its source. It may come from distant industrial country beyond the horizon, where pumps and processing towers operate unseen, or from natural deposits rising through the sediment. Occasionally the wind brings a dry metallic taste with it, a reminder that the wider world has machinery in it, though none of that machinery stands here.

The terrain is low and nearly level, but not simple. Old channels wind through it, their banks softened and rounded by long erosion. Some are filled with water no deeper than a handspan; others plunge suddenly into dark pools beneath overhanging roots. Raised islands of firmer ground run between them, broad enough for a thicket or a stand of trees. In the dry season the channels become pale scars, lined with salt, fish scales, and the small polished bones of things that could not leave in time. The landscape has settled into these forms. Nothing looks freshly broken. Even fallen trees have been absorbed by the ground, their trunks converted into ridges of moss and fungus, their cavities occupied before the wood has fully softened.

The silence is deceptive.

At first there is only the faintest movement: leaves touching leaves, water making a small sound in a hidden channel, the soft descent of condensation from the canopy. Then the living presence becomes apparent. It is everywhere. Insects press against the darkness in vast numbers, though most remain unseen. Their wings make occasional dry tremors in the air. Beetles move through the leaf litter. Small animals pass through the undergrowth without showing themselves. Something large wades in a pool and stops. The water settles.

The fauna is overwhelming not because it is constantly loud, but because there is no unoccupied place. Burrows enter the banks. Nests hang beneath leaves and inside hollow trunks. Mud is punctured by tracks, feeding marks, and the narrow entrances of creatures that wait below the surface. Birds roost in the upper branches, silent except when one shifts its weight. Reptiles lie along the warm edges of the channels, so still that their outlines are indistinguishable from roots and fallen branches. Herds or colonies may be moving through the darkness in the distance, but their passage is slow and muted: a pressure through the vegetation, a succession of small disturbances, then stillness again.

Now and then the plain produces a single sound with startling clarity—a deep splash, a crack of wood, the rush of something taking flight. These events do not develop into a sustained commotion. The forest closes behind them. Its general quiet is not emptiness but restraint, as though every creature has learned to conserve motion and sound in the heat.

The ground is warm underfoot and often soft without being unstable. Black silt gives way to clay, clay to sand, sand to shelves of old stone. In exposed places, the soil has baked into plates that break under pressure. Around the pools, it remains dark and greasy, retaining footprints for several days. The roots form much of the actual architecture. They rise in walls, arches, and low buttresses, making passages that are narrower than the open plain suggests. A person moving through the vegetation would rarely see more than a few body lengths ahead, but beyond the thickets the land opens again into reed beds, shallow water, and long corridors of grass.

There are no paths, fences, buildings, poles, bridges, or discarded objects. No human arrangement has been imposed on the place. The nearest industrial systems—if they are indeed the source of the distant smell and intermittent vibration—belong to another region. Sometimes, beneath the ordinary sounds, the ground seems to carry a low mechanical murmur: too steady to be an animal, too distant to locate. It fades with the wind. The landscape itself remains unlit, unmarked, and physically unchanged by it.

The rules here are old and uncomplicated. Water arrives in pulses, and life gathers wherever it pauses. Heat slows everything but does not stop it. Decay is not an ending but a change of occupation: one body becomes shelter, then soil, then food. The channels shift only slightly from year to year. Trees fall, pools fill, thickets close, and the same broad balance returns.

By day the plain would appear green, crowded, and exposed beneath a hard white sun. By night it becomes a single continuous presence—hot, breathing, densely inhabited, and almost impossible to read. The darkness does not conceal a wilderness so much as reveal how little of it is meant to be seen.
