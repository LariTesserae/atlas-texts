The plateau begins above the last crooked shrubs, though there is no clear line where the shrubs end. They simply become fewer, lower, and more reluctant to remain alive. At a certain height they are reduced to black-green pads pressed into the gravel, each one holding a small private climate beneath its leaves. Beyond them lies an immense upland of stone, snow, and old weather.

It is not a mountain range in the ordinary sense. It is a country raised up and left there: a broad, tilted continent of slate-colored rock, extending farther than the eye can measure. Ridges pass into ridges beneath a pale, dim sky. Valleys are so wide that their opposite walls appear to be the horizon. The ground has been exposed for an age, and erosion has done its work with patience rather than violence. Every surface is settled into its final-looking form—rounded shoulders of rock, long shelves, shallow basins, scree slopes that have almost ceased to move. Nothing appears freshly broken. Even the cliffs seem less fallen than worn down to a decision.

The cold is not sharp. It is comprehensive.

It occupies the stones, the air, the thin soil, the hollows beneath the ice. Wind moves over the plateau with such low force that it is difficult to tell whether it is wind at all, or merely the slow exchange of air between one vast basin and another. Snow does not gather evenly. It lies in long gray ribbons behind ridges and in the lee of solitary boulders, while the exposed flats remain mostly bare, scoured down to a skin of frost and iron-colored dust. In summer—if this place has a summer—the snow retreats a few meters into the same sheltered hollows it has occupied for centuries.

Water exists in intervals.

A dark seam may appear in a field of stone, a narrow runnel descending from a snowbank, flowing for an hour or a day before vanishing into gravel. There are meltwater pans cupped in the rock, each no larger than a room, their surfaces filmed with ice. In the deepest basins, condensation gathers at night and freezes in translucent layers, so that by morning the ground holds small, clear plates like broken glass. Somewhere beneath the plateau, water travels through the stone in slow, pressurized veins. It emerges only where the old fractures allow it, and around those emergence points life collects with startling density.

These are the wet places.

A basin no wider than a village may contain more living bodies than a hundred kilometers of dry upland. Low mats of rust-colored moss spread over the stone. Pale, jointed plants lift themselves from the damp, their leaves narrow as wire and dark as tarnished copper. The air above the water trembles with minute wings. Beneath the ice, blind things move in crowded layers. Their motion is almost imperceptible until the surface breaks in a dozen places at once.

Elsewhere, life is scattered across the emptiness.

The plateau supports little vegetation, but it is full of animals. They are not seen continuously; they are encountered as events. A distant slope may suddenly become patterned with movement. A dark thread crossing the snow resolves, after several minutes, into a procession of long-bodied grazers, each one separated from the next by hundreds of meters. Their backs are plated in dull mineral colors—blue-black, ash, old brass—and their legs are thin, many-jointed, designed for distances rather than speed. They travel without apparent destination, following pressure changes in the buried water or the faint warmth released from the stone.

Some are enormous. Their bodies pass behind ridges and reappear much later, making it difficult to judge whether they are single creatures or moving portions of a herd. Their heads are low and wedge-shaped. Along their flanks, shutters of skin open and close in sequence, exchanging heat with the air. When they stop, they become almost indistinguishable from outcrops.

There are smaller animals everywhere, though most of them remain beneath the surface. They tunnel through the gravel in coordinated networks, compressing loose stone into smooth-walled galleries. They possess no visible eyes, only rows of sensory pits and delicate feelers that register vibration across the ground. A passing herd causes these underground populations to rise toward the surface, not in panic but in attendance. The soil lifts and settles around the animals’ feet as thousands of hidden bodies adjust themselves to the pressure.

The dominant species are not merely numerous. They are infrastructural.

Over generations, their migration routes have become channels. Their resting grounds are broad circular depressions where the snow melts first. Their droppings harden into dark, glassy nodules that retain warmth and form stepping-stones over wet ground. They maintain the plateau without intention, as machines maintain a factory floor: opening passages, sorting minerals, pumping water, grinding stone, and distributing heat. Industrial function exists here, but not as construction. It is an ecology of scaled production, powered by metabolism, pressure, friction, and time.

The largest creatures perform stranger work. On the horizon, a line of them may stand motionless for days beside a ridge. Their bodies slowly flex. Plates along their backs rotate by degrees. Beneath them, the ground begins to shift—not dramatically, but measurably. Gravel drains into a hollow. A buried spring is uncovered. A sheet of ice is broken into regular slabs and carried away on the backs of smaller animals. The process resembles quarrying, excavation, and transport, though no one has built a tool and no product is ever stored. The plateau is continually processed and released back into itself.

For all this abundance, the landscape is quiet.

There are no forests to rustle, no rivers to carry a constant voice, no insects forming a summer haze. The animals are padded, low-slung, and careful. Their feet settle into the ground without impact. Even the great herds move with a nearly inaudible sliding pressure. At close range one may hear a dry clicking as their joints align, or the faint rasp of plates adjusting against one another. A wet basin makes small sounds: the soft collapse of bubbles beneath ice, the delicate ticking of freezing water, the tremor of wings under a shelf of stone.

Most of the time, the only sound is the body’s own blood.

Then, perhaps once in several days, the plateau speaks.

A deep vibration comes through the soles of the feet. It is not loud, and it does not travel through the air so much as through the ground. Every animal pauses. Snow loosens from a distant ledge. A skin of ice cracks across a basin in a pattern like branching metal. The vibration continues for minutes, sometimes hours, while subterranean populations rearrange themselves and the great migratory bodies turn almost imperceptibly toward new routes.

Nothing visibly explodes. Nothing collapses. The world simply changes its operating condition.

There are metallic traces in the stone: seams of black ore, flakes of silver-gray material, occasional surfaces that reflect the dim sky with an oily sheen. Some animals consume these deposits. Their teeth or grinding plates wear down the exposed seams, and their bodies excrete sorted fragments in long, deliberate bands. Other species gather the fragments into warm nests beneath the scree. The resulting concentrations alter the local weather. A thin fog forms above them. Frost arrives early. Small storms may circle one valley for a week, fed by heat drawn from below.

The sky is vast enough to have moods of its own. Most days it hangs low and colorless, the sun a blurred presence rather than a visible disk. Light pools on the snow without seeming to come from anywhere. Shadows are weak, blue-gray, and longer than they should be. During clear intervals, the stars appear with startling density, and the plateau’s creatures become more active. Their bodies emit small points of green, amber, or cold white light—signals too faint to illuminate anything, but sufficient to stitch distant individuals into temporary networks across the dark.

From a height, these signals might look like a scattered industrial grid: isolated lamps, moving indicators, a production system operating across an unbuilt continent.

But there are no towers, roads, cables, fences, or walls. No abandoned stations hide in the valleys. No machine has been left behind. The regularity belongs to living systems, and the systems have had so long to mature that they no longer resemble nature in any familiar sense. They are patient, efficient, and almost entirely without noise.

The plateau does not feel untouched. It feels fully occupied by processes that do not require witnesses.

A herd crosses the far flats, too distant to identify. Beneath the ground, water passes through iron-dark chambers. In a shallow depression, pale plants fold themselves shut against the cold, while hundreds of small bodies gather under their roots. Somewhere beyond the visible ridges, a population of enormous animals is turning the land by fractions of an inch, preparing a route that will not be used until the next season, or the next century.

The landscape remains open between these presences—immense fields of stone and frost, long empty intervals, silence extending in every direction.

And yet the emptiness is never vacant. It is only the space between operations.
