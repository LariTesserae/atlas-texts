The basin has no horizon.

It is too large for that. The land continues past the reach of any eye, and the darkness between one sound and another is not empty distance but a substance—thick, warm, and faintly wet, pressing against the skin. The basin may be a valley, or a crater, or the inside of something much larger than a mountain range. Its upper walls are never seen. Somewhere above, rain falls through the black for hours, sometimes days, striking leaves, metal roofs, pools, and the broad skin of the earth with a constant, layered percussion.

Water is everywhere, but seldom in clean or simple forms. It gathers beneath the soil, rises through cracks, threads through curtains of moss, and spills from the rock in slow, muscular weeping. The ground gives underfoot. In some places it is peat; in others, a pavement of smooth dark stone polished by centuries of runoff. Shallow channels wind through the vegetation, full of warm water and pale things that flex when touched. Larger streams move with surprising force, carrying branches, bones, rusted drums, and entire mats of root downstream. Their banks are not banks so much as negotiated edges: reeds, machinery, mud, and the footholds of animals all crowded together.

The elevation is felt more than seen. The air is warm but thin, and every exposed surface sweats. Water condenses on the hanging cables and drops from them in steady intervals. The stone holds the cold of depth beneath its wet outer skin, while the open air carries the breath of hot vents and distant engines. Storms form within the basin itself. You can hear them approaching long before the rain arrives: a deep rolling pressure moving through the dark, followed by wind in the canopy and a sudden rattling of loose metal.

The vegetation is lush without being soft. It grows in layers, each layer occupying whatever space the one below has failed to seal. Tall blade-leaves rise in dense stands from the mud, their edges hard enough to cut cloth. Vines hang from unseen heights and descend in bundles thick as pillars. Moss fills the seams in old concrete. Ferns push through the floors of abandoned sheds. There are trees, though their trunks are mostly hidden by other growth: immense, ridged columns with roots that travel aboveground for hundreds of meters before plunging back into the soil. Their crowns form a second landscape overhead, a tangled ceiling that catches rain and releases it in delayed avalanches.

Some plants have adapted to the permanent dark by becoming tactile. Their leaves close around passing bodies. Their stems tremble when footsteps approach. Broad fungal shelves cling to walls and machinery, warm to the touch, breathing out clouds of fine spores whenever the air pressure changes. Along the waterways, low fields of translucent bladder-plants pulse in synchronized waves, making the mud seem to inhale.

The darkness is absolute, but it is not featureless. There are colors in it that cannot be seen so much as inferred: the greenish cold of algae beneath water, the dull red heat of buried pipes, the blue-white flicker of insects moving through the reeds. Here and there, organisms carry their own dim signs—spots, threads, milky membranes, brief sparks under the skin. These are not enough to illuminate the land. They only reveal that something is present, and then conceal it again.

Industrial structures stand scattered across the basin, though “scattered” is misleading at this scale. A tower may be kilometers from the next, yet between them runs a dense infrastructure of old works: rails sunk in mud, pressure lines crossing streams on concrete stilts, conveyor belts vanishing into vegetation, ladders leading up into blackness. The machinery is large enough to have its own weather. Condensation rains from the undersides of bridges. Steam escapes from ruptured seams and hangs low among the leaves. Entire walls of turbines turn somewhere beyond sight, their rotation communicated through the ground as a slow, persistent vibration.

The factories are not ruins exactly. Some have been dead for generations, their windows blind, their doors furred with roots. Others operate without human attendance. Pumps throb beneath the marsh. An automated dredge crawls along the edge of a reservoir, scooping black sediment into hoppers. A line of freight cars moves by itself through the forest at irregular intervals, wheels shrieking, doors open, carrying nothing anyone can identify. From deep inside the stone comes the report of presses, one after another, stamping parts in a rhythm too slow to be called mechanical and too regular to be natural.

There are occasional inhabited places: a maintenance hut raised above the floodline, a cluster of sheet-metal rooms around a warm generator, a narrow station built against the roots of a colossal tree. Their lights, if they have any, are covered or hooded. People here do not illuminate the landscape. They mark themselves with sound, heat, scent, and the careful placement of ropes. Doors are made to close quickly. Walkways have bells, not for visitors but for things that cross them unseen.

The fauna is abundant and difficult to separate from the environment. The water is crowded with small bodies. Mud bubbles where burrowing animals pass beneath it. Long-backed amphibians rest in the drainage channels with only their nostrils exposed, while blind fish strike at the vibrations of rain. Something with many jointed legs moves through the upper vegetation, shaking leaves in a line that advances against the wind. Large herbivores pass in herds through the reed fields, their weight producing a rolling disturbance in the ground. Their bodies are plated or furred or overgrown with moss; in the dark, the distinction is academic.

Predators announce themselves less often. A sudden silence travels through the smaller creatures. Insects stop ticking. Waterfowl lift from the marsh without calling. Then comes the sound of something heavy entering the channel, or something wet dragging itself across the roof. The ecosystem is crowded enough that every absence has meaning.

The basin has rules, though they are not written anywhere.

Do not follow a machine that is running quietly.

Do not stand beneath a dripping cable unless you know what it carries.

When the underground pumps stop, leave the low ground before the water rises. It will rise without rain.

Never answer a voice that comes from inside a closed tank.

And if the reeds begin to move in circles, rather than with the wind, make no attempt to cross them.

These rules are practical, but the world has stranger laws beneath them. Water remembers the shape of vessels long after the vessels are gone. Certain tunnels are longer on the way out. Heat travels upward through rock in narrow columns, and animals gather around these columns as if around invisible trees. Machinery left unattended does not always decay; sometimes it changes its purpose. A failed engine may become a nest, then a colony, then a kind of organ for the surrounding wetland. The boundary between factory and ecology has been eroding for so long that neither side can be said to have won.

Everything is established here. The stone has been worn into its final forms. The rivers have chosen their beds. The roots have found the foundations and split them according to patient geometries. Rust has become a mineral of the soil. The great works, even when broken, are part of the basin’s balance: they divert floods, warm pools, shelter insects, feed fungi, and provide elevated paths for creatures that have never known the structures as human things.

The loudness never ends. Rain drums on leaves and roofs. Water rushes under platforms. Ventilation shafts roar. Distant turbines pulse through the earth. Animals bark, click, boom, hiss, and call to one another across kilometers of dark. Metal expands with heat and contracts with the night, though there is no true day. Somewhere a warning siren gives one low note and falls silent. Somewhere else, a chain begins to move.

Above it all, unseen weather turns through the immense black. The canopy bends. Condensation falls. The ground exhales warm vapor.

And beneath the ground, deeper than the pipes and the roots, something vast continues to operate—slowly, efficiently, without light, producing enough heat to keep the whole dark world alive.
