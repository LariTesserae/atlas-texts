The place is a high hollow no larger than a village green, though no village has ever stood there. It lies in a fold of dark stone near the summit of a mountain, enclosed on three sides by weathered cliffs and on the fourth by a steep drop into cloud. The hollow is not a valley so much as a room in the mountain: oval, bounded, and complete. Its floor is level with peat, moss, and black water, while the walls lean inward beneath a ceiling of fog.

It is always night there.

Not the sharp, star-filled darkness of a clear alpine sky, but a dense, blue-black dimness that seems to gather beneath the overhanging rock. The air is cold enough to tighten the skin, yet not bitter. Water is everywhere. It beads along the cliffs, threads out from cracks, gathers in shallow basins, and trembles beneath mats of moss. Fine condensation hangs in the air. Every exposed surface is slick, softened, or furred over with growth.

The rock is old and dark—slate, basalt, perhaps something deeper and less easily named. Erosion has rounded it into settled forms. There are no fresh breaks, no unstable shelves, no raw scars. The cliffs have been worn into long shallow shelves and smooth hollows, their edges cushioned by centuries of moss. The stone seems to have arrived at its final shape long ago and has remained there, patiently accepting water.

Vegetation fills every available space.

Low shrubs with waxy leaves grow in black mats around the pools. Long grasses rise from the peat in tight, motionless ranks, their blades silvered by moisture. Moss hangs from the walls in thick curtains, not quite reaching the ground, and beneath it grow pale ferns with translucent stems. At the center of the hollow stands a cluster of stunted trees, each no taller than a person. Their trunks twist close to the ground before dividing, as if shaped by a wind that no longer blows. Their leaves are small, dark, and densely layered. Water rests on them without falling.

There is no bare earth. Where the plants do not grow, animals occupy the ground.

They are present in numbers that would make the hollow seem to move if they were not so still. Small, furred bodies nestle between roots. Pale beetles cover the wet stones in overlapping shells. Long-legged insects stand on the surface of the pools, touching one another without apparent conflict. Snails cling to the cliff in crowded spirals. Thick-bodied salamanders lie half-submerged in the water, their black eyes open. In the branches, folded birds press together so closely that they resemble knots of leaves.

Larger creatures are there too, though the darkness reveals them only in pieces: the pale underside of a grazing animal, a pair of reflective eyes behind fern fronds, the slow passage of something heavy through the moss. Nothing hunts. Nothing flees. Predators and prey occupy the same cramped spaces with the calm of neighboring stones. A small animal may sleep beneath the foreleg of a larger one. Insects crawl over the faces of resting beasts. No creature seems hungry in the urgent sense. Feeding happens, but gently and without pursuit, as though the entire ecology has agreed that there will always be enough.

The abundance is not noisy.

There is the steady, delicate sound of water: drops striking leaves, threads running through peat, hidden seepage passing beneath the hollow. Occasionally the pools make a soft internal click as air escapes from the mud. The animals contribute only slight sounds—a dry rustle, a wet swallow, the tremor of wings folded and refolded. No bird calls. No creature cries out. Even the insects seem to hum below audibility.

When something large moves, the vegetation absorbs the sound.

The hollow’s atmosphere is close and saturated. Breath appears briefly before dissolving into the fog. The smell is of wet stone, cold leaves, peat, and the faint mineral sweetness of water gathered from far above. Beneath these is an animal warmth, not unpleasant, held in the crowded spaces between bodies and roots.

There are primitive signs here, but none made by hands. Certain stones bear shallow parallel grooves, perhaps polished by generations of antlers, claws, or passing backs. A ring of pale mushrooms circles one pool with mathematical neatness, though nothing has planted them. At the highest point of the hollow, a narrow crack in the cliff emits a weak gray radiance—not enough to illuminate anything, only enough to distinguish the crack from the surrounding stone. It may be daylight from the world beyond, or the memory of daylight trapped in the mountain.

The rules of the place are simple and absolute.

Water does not run out. Moss does not die back. The pools never freeze, though the air is cold. The plants do not spread beyond their established boundaries, and no seedling ever grows where another plant already stands. Animals are born into the hollow and remain there, but the number of bodies does not increase beyond what the place can hold. A creature that dies is gradually covered by moss, then roots, then the dark growth of the floor. It does not decay so much as become part of the hollow’s permanent fabric.

Nothing arrives.

Nothing leaves.

Even the weather seems unable to enter. Snow may be visible beyond the rim, suspended in the distant darkness, but no flake falls inside. Wind may move the clouds far below, yet the grasses never bend. The fog thickens and thins without traveling. A drop of water hangs from a leaf for years, perhaps, without falling until some unseen balance permits it.

Time passes here only as an arrangement of things that remain.

The animals shift their weight. Water gathers. Leaves glisten. Roots hold the soil. The cliffs continue their ancient yielding, but so slowly that erosion is indistinguishable from permanence. The hollow is not frozen; it is finished. Its life is crowded, wet, and quietly immense, yet nothing in it seems to be becoming anything else.

At the center, beneath the stunted trees, there is one patch of open water no wider than a doorway. Its surface is perfectly black. Around it, every creature faces inward—not with fear or worship, but with the passive attention of things accustomed to a constant presence.

Sometimes, from beneath the water, there comes a single soft sound like a stone settling deep underground.

Then the hollow returns to silence.
