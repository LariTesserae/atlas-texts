The place is a city built in the hollow of a newly opened stone.

It is not large—perhaps the size of a village, perhaps smaller than that—but its boundaries are sharply defined. On every side, pale mineral walls rise only a few dozen meters before ending in a low, dark ceiling of fractured rock. The whole settlement seems to have been pressed into a shallow basin or grown inside a shell. There is no horizon. There is only the near wall, the near roof, the near ground, and the dim interval between them.

Nothing here is wet. The stone has never known rain.

The ground is a broad sheet of gray-white material, neither salt nor ash, though it resembles both. It has emerged recently from beneath the planet’s crust in long, clean plates. Their edges are sharp enough to cut cloth. Some plates have buckled upward into streets and stairs; others have split apart, leaving narrow black seams that descend into absolute darkness. The seams are too narrow to enter, yet from them comes a slow pressure, as though something deep below is breathing through the rock.

The city has been arranged around these fractures.

Its buildings are not made of brick, metal, or wood. They are made of the same pale mineral as the ground, but in forms too orderly to be natural: walls folded into arches, ribs laid parallel beneath ceilings, square towers with their corners softened by no age at all. They look newly made, but not freshly constructed. There are no tool marks. No scaffolds. No machines. The surfaces seem to have been persuaded into architecture while still soft, then hardened in place.

The buildings are mostly empty.

Doorways open onto rooms without floors. Balconies overlook shafts. Staircases rise a few levels and then terminate against seamless walls. Windows are narrow, dark, and placed at inconsistent heights, as if intended for creatures of several different sizes—or for things that do not stand upright. Some chambers contain rows of low stone seats. Others contain tall, hollow columns with openings at their tops. A few structures resemble houses, but their doors are too small for people and their roofs are too high for warmth.

There is no sign that anyone lives here. Yet the city is not abandoned.

The walls are slowly rearranging themselves.

At irregular intervals, a slab will flex inward with a faint, dry creak. A street will close as two curbs grow together. A new passage will open where there was only blank wall a moment before. The changes are not rapid enough to witness directly; one looks away, and when one looks back, the geometry has become more certain. The city is still forming, and its formation follows rules no observer can recognize.

A staircase may lead downward in the morning and upward by evening. A courtyard may become a sealed room. A tower that stood alone may find itself joined to another by a bridge of fresh stone, its surface pale and unblemished, its underside still warm from whatever process made it.

The temperature is low, but not brutally cold. The mineral holds a steady chill, and the air is thin and dry. Breath does not mist. Instead, in the dimness, exhaled warmth leaves a temporary distortion, a trembling blur that hangs close to the face before being drawn toward the nearest crack.

There is almost no light. A weak gray illumination comes from everywhere and nowhere, as if the stone itself were remembering daylight. It gathers most strongly along new edges: the rim of a recent fracture, the lip of a newly formed arch, the white face of a wall that has not yet darkened. Older surfaces recede into blue-black shadow. The result is a city of outlines—stairs, ribs, corners, and apertures—rather than a city of colors.

The sparse vegetation grows where it should not.

There are thin, colorless stalks emerging from the seams, each one no thicker than a thread. They have no leaves. At their tips are small translucent fans, folded shut most of the time. When the ground shifts, the fans open and catch the tremor. They are not plants in the ordinary sense, but they root themselves and draw something from the mineral. Their pale filaments spread across walls like frost, though they are warm to the touch.

In the deepest rooms, these growths gather into sparse curtains. They sway without wind, reacting to pressure changes below the city. Sometimes they all bend toward one wall at once. Sometimes they withdraw into the cracks, leaving only fine white roots behind.

A few low organisms move through the settlement. They are difficult to see against the stone: flat, many-jointed things with translucent bodies and dark internal knots. They shelter beneath stairs or in the unlit spaces behind walls. Their movement is not accompanied by footsteps. They glide through the powder that collects in the streets, leaving branching marks like the trails of ice crystals.

They do not seem to hunt one another. They feed, perhaps, on the threadlike vegetation—or on the changes in the stone itself. When a building shifts, they gather around it in silence, pressing their bodies to the fresh surface. Afterward, the new wall is marked with small, dark hollows.

There may be larger fauna, but none are often seen. Occasionally a shadow passes across the upper reaches of the city, though there is no open sky for it to cross. Occasionally one of the high arches contains a shape that was not there before: a folded limb, a cluster of reflective eyes, a pale membrane drawn tight between pillars. It vanishes when approached, not quickly but completely, as if the air has edited it out.

Sound is nearly absent.

The city absorbs ordinary noise. A stone dropped onto the ground makes no impact. A voice becomes a pressure in the throat and dies there. Even the movement of the sparse creatures is silent.

What can be heard comes from below.

There is the slow, granular settling of the mineral: a faint interior rubbing, like teeth moving against teeth. There are distant knocks, never repeated in the same rhythm. There is an occasional long tone from one of the hollow columns, though nothing visibly strikes them. The tone begins beneath hearing and rises only slightly above it, felt more in the ribs than received by the ears. When it ends, the walls have usually changed.

The silence is therefore not peaceful. It is attentive.

Nothing in this place appears designed for comfort, storage, travel, or habitation. The architecture has the outward grammar of an urban settlement but lacks its human purpose. Streets lead toward thresholds rather than destinations. Public squares are too narrow to gather in. Houses contain no warmth, no furniture, and no means of closing their openings. The city may be an organism’s skeleton, a ritual diagram made inhabitable, or the first draft of a settlement whose inhabitants have not yet arrived.

Its rules reveal themselves slowly.

Fresh stone is luminous and slightly warm. Old stone is dark and cold. Fractures widen when approached by living things, but close when left alone. The pale vegetation opens in response to vibration, while the flat animals become motionless beneath direct attention. No object remains exactly where it was placed if it is left unattended overnight. The city does not permit stillness for long.

Every few hours—or perhaps according to some cycle unrelated to time—the whole basin contracts.

The walls lean inward by a fraction. The floor plates rise. The black seams narrow. The column-tones sound together, low and almost inaudible, and every threadlike plant folds shut. For a brief period the city becomes denser, the empty spaces reduced, the buildings pressed toward one another like organs tightening around a pulse.

Then it releases.

New cracks race across the ground without sound. A roof splits cleanly in two. A white ridge thrusts upward through a courtyard. Powder lifts from the streets and hangs in the air, not falling but drifting toward the walls. In the aftermath, the city is never quite the same size. It may have gained a room, lost a tower, or rotated a whole district around a fracture that was not there before.

The settlement is small enough to cross in an hour, but it resists being crossed the same way twice.

At its center stands a single structure unlike the others: a circular enclosure open to the ceiling, though the ceiling above it is solid stone. Its walls are low and perfectly smooth. Inside is a plain floor marked by one dark line from edge to edge. No plant grows there. No animal enters. The line is not a crack, but the absence of material—as if a blade had removed a narrow segment of reality.

From time to time, something passes along the line beneath the floor.

The city responds by building.

A new arch rises in the eastern quarter. A staircase extends toward the central enclosure. Pale growths emerge in every seam. The hollow columns begin their low, scattered singing. Far below, the sealed depths shift against one another.

The place is empty, but it is not vacant.

It is waiting in the way a newly formed bone waits for weight: already shaped, not yet finished, changing under pressures that have not fully arrived.
