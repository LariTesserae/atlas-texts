The low country is built entirely of dark masonry, iron, and poured stone.

There is no soil. Where the ground ought to be, there are broad plates of basalt-colored concrete, fitted together so long ago and so precisely that their seams have become narrow black canals. Water travels through them constantly—not in streams, but in a thousand thin, deliberate movements. It seeps up through grates. It beads on vertical walls. It pours from the mouths of buried conduits and runs in silver films across the level ground before vanishing into slots. After rain, which is frequent but never dramatic, the whole plain shines as if varnished.

The landscape lies low and open beneath a sky that never quite becomes day. A lid of cloud presses down over it, bluish-black near the horizon and lighter only where distant industrial flares stain the underside. The light is insufficient for shadows. Instead, every object has a dim halo of reflected wetness: rails, towers, tanks, retaining walls, the skeletal frames of factories. At a distance, the land appears to be made of shallow water and interruptions.

It extends for many miles in every direction, but almost nothing is crowded. Great structures stand alone in the emptiness, separated by long causeways and drainage fields. A pumping hall here. A cooling tower there. A row of sealed reservoirs, each the size of a small lake. Between them, flat expanses of engineered pavement descend by imperceptible grades toward the central basin. The basin is not a natural hollow. It is the final floor of the entire region, manufactured to receive whatever cannot be allowed to remain elsewhere.

The oldest surfaces have softened. Not crumbled, exactly—there is little loose material anywhere—but worn into subtle, monumental curves. Corners have become radii. Stairways descend into shallow ramps. The foundations of immense buildings have been polished by centuries of runoff and abrasive dust until they resemble the exposed bones of something larger than a city. Water has etched pale mineral lines down every wall, marking former levels in layers: one line, then another above it, then hundreds more, each recording a season or a century of containment.

The factories are still operating.

Their lights are mostly internal, hidden behind blackened glass or narrow slits high in their walls. Their engines turn below ground. The sound rises through the pavement and fills the country without a clear source: a continuous, crushing vibration, crossed by deep pulses and sudden metallic impacts. Entire buildings tremble on their foundations. Long pipes knock from end to end as valves open somewhere beyond sight. Pressure escapes in white columns from distant stacks, but the vapour hangs low rather than rising, spreading in sheets across the causeways.

There are no birds above the towers. No insects collect around the lamps. No small things move in the drainage channels. The water is too cold, too treated, or too old for life. Even the moss that might normally gather in the cracks has been replaced by a manufactured black sealant, applied in thick ribbons wherever moisture attempts to enter. Nothing grows unless it has been installed.

In a few places, vegetation has been attempted. Pale grasses stand in straight, widely spaced rows within recessed plots of glass and concrete. They are thin as wire, colorless at the tips, and never bend in the wind because there is almost no wind at ground level. Their roots are not in earth. They descend into transparent tubes, where a dim green fluid circulates continuously. The plants do not form a meadow; they form a measurement. Their height, angle, and gradual decline are monitored by small lamps along the beds. When one falls, a machine removes it.

The trees are stranger. A handful rise beside the old administrative roads, each one made of laminated mineral, metal filaments, and something that may once have been wood. Their branches are fixed in a permanent spread. Rain gathers on them and falls in delayed drops from the tips. In the dark, their trunks give off a faint internal glow, as though the trees are remembering sunlight rather than receiving it.

No natural horizon remains. The distant ridges are flood barriers, slag embankments, and the backs of buried structures. Their slopes have been worn smooth by weather until they look geological, but their regularity gives them away. Here and there, enormous doors interrupt them—doors wide enough for a train, a refinery, or a district to pass through. Most are closed. Some breathe warm air into the cold plain, causing banks of condensation to form and drift knee-high across the pavement.

The water level changes according to a schedule no one can see.

At times the channels empty with a long, descending roar, leaving the surfaces coated in a dark skin that reflects the sky. At other times water emerges from hundreds of vents at once, spreading evenly over the plain until the causeways become islands. The buildings are designed for this. Their lower doors seal. Their bridges rise. Their machinery continues without pause beneath the flood. In the distance, whole sections of roadway may lift silently from the ground, carrying lamps, rails, and maintenance housings above the water, then settle again once the basin has drained.

The drainage system has no single direction. Water can run uphill here—not because gravity has failed, but because the land is under continuous pressure from below. Pumps force it through hidden gradients, and channels occasionally reverse themselves. A narrow stream may climb a ramp, flatten against a wall, and disappear sideways into a seam. If watched too long, the movement becomes difficult to trust. Drops gather on the underside of bridges. Condensation falls upward into vents. Mist collects in hollows that are higher than the surrounding plain.

The buildings are mostly faceless. Their walls are made for endurance, not habitation: thick slabs, armored windows, sealed access points. Some are ribbed with external pipes, their surfaces alive with slow flows of hot and cold fluids. Some have immense rotating drums embedded in them, turning behind grilles with the solemnity of planetary bodies. One complex, extending beyond the visible distance, consists only of vertical chimneys connected by catwalks. Its exhaust is cold and perfectly clear. It releases no smoke, only a sound like a thousand giant lungs inhaling at once.

At intervals, warning beacons activate in the dark. They do not flash red or amber but a dull, submerged blue. When they turn on, all nearby machinery changes rhythm. Pumps accelerate. Gates close. The shallow water on the ground shivers in parallel lines. For several minutes, the entire country seems to be responding to an event beyond its borders—some distant intake, rupture, pressure front, or transfer of mass. Then the beacons go dark, and the old rhythm resumes.

The air smells of wet iron, mineral scale, ozone, and the faint medicinal sharpness of processed water. Beneath these is a deeper odor, almost impossible to identify: the smell of stone that has been kept underwater for too long, or of a sealed room opened after centuries.

There are signs of occupation everywhere and evidence of occupants nowhere. Elevated walkways cross the empty spaces, but no one uses them. Inspection lamps move along rails by themselves. Doors open onto darkness and close again after counting an invisible interval. Loudspeakers announce conditions in a language worn smooth by repetition, their messages swallowed by the machinery before they can be understood. The industrial system is not abandoned. It is simply operating beyond the need for visible workers.

The place has settled into its final form, though the final form is still in motion.

Its ancientness is not expressed by ruins but by continuity. Nothing has fallen far enough to become a ruin. Every failure has been absorbed into another structure, every former landscape paved over, redirected, sealed, or made into a reservoir. The oldest walls remain because newer walls have been built around them. The oldest water remains because the system has no outlet, only transformations: rain into runoff, runoff into coolant, coolant into vapour, vapour into condensation, condensation back into the channels.

Beyond the last lit facility, the plain continues beneath the low darkness. Sparse towers mark the distance. Water moves between them. The ground hums underfoot with the work of pumps and engines, and the sound is so immense that silence has ceased to be a natural possibility.

Nothing lives here.

Everything is active.
