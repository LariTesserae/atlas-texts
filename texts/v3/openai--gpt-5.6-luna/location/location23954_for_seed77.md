The plain is called the **Unfinished Low Country**, though there is no one left to say why.

It extends farther than the eye can hold: a continent of level terraces, shallow ramps, broad courtyards, and immense unroofed halls whose walls have not yet been raised. There are no hills in the ordinary sense, only long interruptions in the flatness—ridges like the edges of buried foundations, low embankments, half-formed causeways. The land lies close to sea level, hot enough that the horizon trembles from morning onward, but the air is not dry. Moisture appears irregularly, as if the country is remembering rain.

Everything underfoot has been made.

The surface is composed of pale slabs fitted together with astonishing patience. Some are square, some many-sided, some long as a village street. They are not quarried stone; there are no quarries, no exposed bedrock, no natural soil. Each slab has the matte, mineral skin of something cast or grown in place. Their colors are subdued: bone white, ash gray, old ocher, the faint green of oxidized copper. Seams run between them in endless patterns. In some places the seams are tight and dry. In others, they have opened by the width of a finger, and dark moisture glistens in the gaps.

The construction is complete nowhere.

A wall may continue for a hundred kilometers and then end in a staircase leading up to empty air. A paved basin may contain a single pillar, perfectly centered, rising only three meters before becoming a blunt, unfinished stump. Long corridors cross one another without doors or roofs. There are courtyards too large to cross in a day, their surfaces gently sloped toward drains that lead nowhere visible. Every structure is monumental without quite becoming architecture. It feels less like a city than like the first, abandoned draft of a world.

There are no tools, machines, roads, or signs of labor. No marks of wheels. No debris from demolition. The place makes itself.

Very slowly, over hours or seasons, the slabs shift against one another with a pressure too gradual to witness directly. A seam closes. A corner rises. A stair gains one step. New material wells up from beneath the paving in smooth, soft-edged forms, cooling as it spreads. Sometimes a whole district of tiles settles by a few centimeters with a low, nearly inaudible shudder. The country is under construction by its own weight, heat, and hidden geometry. Its rules are simple but absolute: nothing grows unless it has first been shaped; nothing remains truly unfinished, though completion may take thousands of years.

The heat is strongest in the open courts. At midday, the slabs radiate a dry, white glare, and the air above them bends like clear fabric. There is no shade except where a wall, a column, or one of the enormous unfinished roofs casts it. In shaded corridors the temperature drops enough for condensation to gather. Water beads along the underside of ledges, collects in the grooves of decorative channels, and drips at long intervals into shallow basins.

Rain comes rarely and locally. A darkening may appear on the horizon, not as a cloudbank but as a low stain moving across the atmosphere. When it arrives, it releases narrow curtains of warm rain over particular sections of the plain. One courtyard may be drenched while the next remains bright and dustless. Water spreads over the paving in thin reflective sheets, finds the seams, and disappears below. For several days afterward, the country smells of wet stone and heated minerals.

In the lowest places, the water persists. These are not ponds so much as temporary rooms filled with sky. Their bottoms are tiled in blue-gray plates, and their margins are lined with shallow steps. Condensation gathers there at night, replenishing what the sun removes by day. A few sparse plants occupy these damp places: low mats of dark green growth, thin reedlike stalks, and pale crusts that resemble moss but have the stiffness of felt. They grow in deliberate-looking strips along the seams, never spreading into the open. Their roots, if they have roots, follow the joints in the construction.

There are no animals.

Nothing moves with intention across the plain. No wings pass overhead. No insects rise from the wet margins. The plants bend only under rain and heat. Their activity is slow but undeniable: a green line advances a few centimeters along a crack; a cluster of stalks opens toward dawn and folds shut beneath the noon glare; a damp patch darkens, brightens, and slowly migrates through the paving as hidden water travels beneath it.

The absence of animal sound makes the scale difficult to understand. There are no calls to measure distance by, no rustling bodies, no sudden departures. Sound arrives from the land itself.

A warm wind moves through the unfinished corridors, producing a soft, hollow breath. Water ticks into basins. Far away, a slab flexes and gives a single muted knock. Sometimes the vast plain emits a long vibration, too low to be called a rumble, as if the foundations are settling in sleep. During rain, every surface speaks at once: thousands of small impacts, then trickling, then silence returning in widening circles.

The atmosphere is clear under the normal sun, though the heat makes distant walls appear to float. At dawn, condensation lies in silver threads along the seams. By noon it is gone. Toward evening, the light turns the whole constructed continent the color of old ivory, and the unfinished forms lose their edges. A corridor seems to continue beyond itself. A staircase appears to descend into the horizon. The farthest walls become thin bands of rose and gray, until it is impossible to tell whether they are structures or mirages.

The land is sparse not because it is empty, but because its presences are widely separated. A solitary tower may stand a hundred kilometers from the next. A wet garden of mosslike growth may be enclosed by a square wall and surrounded by nothing but hot pavement for days of travel. Between these places is immense, quiet space: enough for weather to develop, enough for heat to accumulate, enough for the world’s slow construction to proceed unnoticed.

Erosion has barely begun. The edges are sharp, the corners mostly intact, the decorative channels still holding their intended lines. Rain has made only shallow stains. Wind has not yet rounded the walls. There is no sediment, no accumulated dust deep enough to become soil. The country has not had time to weather into ruins. It is raw in a subtler way: not broken, but still becoming.

At night the heat releases from the slabs in waves. The sky remains ordinary and crowded with stars, while below, isolated patches of condensation return to the seams. The plants close. The basins darken. Somewhere beneath the paving, pressure shifts through the immense foundations.

Then, from far across the low country, comes the quiet sound of something new being made.
