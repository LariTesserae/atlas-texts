The settlement occupies the last level place on the summit of a mountain that has forgotten how to be a mountain.

It is not a peak in the sharp sense. There are no cliffs dropping cleanly into distance, no snow, no shining streamlets, no weather-cut gullies. The summit is a broad, enclosed crown of black-red stone, perhaps a few hundred paces across, raised so high that the surrounding world has become a pale, indistinct rim below it. The mountain was once taller. Ages of wind, chemical weathering, and the slow grinding of mineral dust have worn its upper reaches down to a basin with walls only shoulder-high in some places and cathedral-high in others. The ancient erosion has made everything rounded and final. Rock surfaces have the smoothness of old bone.

There is no water here.

The hollows are filled instead with powder: white salts, yellow crusts, blue-gray clays, and soft deposits of iron-rich dust. In the deepest seams, the ground has a leathery sheen, as though the stone once melted and cooled under pressure. Nothing runs. Nothing drips. After the rare storms, the air becomes briefly heavy and metallic, but the moisture vanishes before it can gather into a bead. The summit’s living things drink vapor directly from the atmosphere, or draw chemical traces from the rock with roots fine enough to resemble mold.

Yet the place is lush.

A dense vegetation crowds every wall, roof, path, and exposed ledge. It is not the green of wet forests. The plants are muted colors: sage, ash, deep rust, violet-black, and a pale, almost luminous gray. Their leaves are thick and many-layered, covered in wax and fine hairs. Some spread in low mats across the mineral floor, forming padded carpets that crackle softly underfoot. Others rise in clustered columns, jointed like reeds but hard as carved wood. Their flowering structures are dry, elaborate things—fans, bells, spirals, and feathery cages that collect atmospheric salts. When the dim light reaches them, they open by degrees, not toward the sun but toward the warmest direction in the air.

The vegetation has grown through the settlement and over it without quite destroying it. Homes stand in cavities cut into the ancient stone, connected by narrow terraces and stairways. Their walls are made from stacked slabs, salt-brick, fired clay, and the dark stems of larger plants. Roofs are low and heavy, woven from fiber, hide, and overlapping sheets of barklike growth. Nothing is built high. At this elevation, the wind can peel an unweighted roof away, and the people have learned to press their architecture close to the ground.

The whole place is organized around the summit’s central depression, where a grove has become the town’s square.

The grove is astonishingly dense. Tall mineral trees grow there, their trunks twisting together into a living colonnade. They have no visible bark. Their surfaces are ribbed and dull, as if made of compacted ash, and their branches bear thick clusters of blue-green blades. The trees feed on heat leaking from cracks beneath the basin and on trace gases trapped in the stone. In return, they release a faintly sweet vapor that the inhabitants gather with cloth screens and porous ceramic vessels. It is not water, exactly, but it sustains the body. People inhale it, press it into their mouths through reed filters, and store it in sealed bladders for the days when the summit air turns too dry.

The grove is also the loudest place.

The vegetation clicks, ticks, and rustles continuously. Leaves rub together like thousands of small knives. Hollow stems knock against one another. When the wind passes through the upper branches, the whole grove produces a low, resonant moan, almost musical but too irregular to be called a song.

The animals add to it.

They are everywhere: in the walls, under the walkways, inside the plant colonies, hanging in folds from the roofs. Long-legged grazers with plated throats pick mineral lichen from the paving stones. Small, many-winged insects pour out of cracks in the afternoon warmth, their wings beating with a dry electrical buzz. Broad-backed burrowers drag themselves through the salt dust, leaving braided trails. Pale climbing things, no larger than a hand, cling to the vertical surfaces and chirp at one another in sharp, repeated notes. Nothing seems especially afraid of the settlement. The animals have lived alongside it for so long that the distinction between wild and domesticated has become uncertain.

Some creatures are kept for their fibers, their shell, or the warm gases they produce in digestion. Others are simply tolerated because removing them would mean disturbing the whole living structure of the place. A house may contain a family, three kinds of beetle, a colony of root-eating worms, and a nesting animal beneath the floor. At night, people sleep through the scratching.

The inhabitants use simple tools: stone blades, fired vessels, bone needles, wooden levers, and dull metal salvaged from old veins in the mountain. Their craftsmanship is patient and repetitive. They braid plant fiber into climbing lines, scrape salts from shaded rock, shape mineral plates into knives, and maintain the terraces by hand. There are no machines large enough to dominate the summit. Even the settlement’s most ambitious works—a retaining wall, a roofed market, a stairway cut into the upper rim—feel like extensions of older geological processes.

The city is small, but it is not sparse. Every passage is occupied. People sit beneath awnings sorting seed-cases and scraping edible films from stone. Children move through the paths in groups, carrying baskets of dry leaves. Traders call from doorways. Animals nose through refuse heaps. Smoke-colored insects rise in clouds whenever someone opens a storage room. The streets are too narrow for carts, so goods travel in bundles on backs, shoulders, and the broad shells of patient pack-beasts.

Dim light lies over everything.

It comes from a low, diffuse sky of copper-gray cloud, or perhaps from an atmosphere thick with suspended mineral dust. Shadows are soft and close to the feet. The summit never seems fully daylit, even when the air above the clouds burns pale. At the edges of the settlement, the world falls away into brightness: a vast white glare below, with distant ridges showing through like the bones of drowned continents. There is no clear horizon. The height is so extreme that distance has become a wash of color.

Warmth remains trapped in the stone. The ground is pleasantly hot in some places and dangerously hot in others, though the air itself can be cold whenever the wind shifts. Families know which walls to sleep against, which terraces are safe at dawn, and which cracks must never be crossed after the long seasonal warming. The climate changes slowly. The plants open and close over weeks. The great groves shed their outer layers over decades. A house may lean for a generation before anyone rebuilds it.

Nothing here happens quickly except noise.

A wall settles with a muffled thunder. A swarm erupts from the ground. A chorus of animals begins and continues until it becomes part of the air. Then the place returns to its prevailing condition: crowded, warm, dim, and nearly motionless.

The settlement feels less constructed than inherited. Its earliest walls are indistinguishable from the mountain’s own layered deposits. Its paths follow the same curves as ancient mineral flows. The oldest trees have grown around doorways and through foundations, their roots gripping stone that was already ancient when the first people arrived. Residents repair, harvest, and rearrange, but they do not expect to transform the summit. The summit has already reached its final form.

They live inside that finality.

Above them, the mountain’s rounded rim holds back the pale world. Below, unseen slopes continue their patient descent. Around them, a small civilization breathes among dry gardens, mineral dust, dense animals, and trees that drink the air. The place is silent only from a distance. Up close, it is a continual clatter of life maintaining itself where no water runs.
