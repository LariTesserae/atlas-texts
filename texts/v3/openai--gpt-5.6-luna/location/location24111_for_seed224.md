The place is hidden inside a summit that does not appear on any mountain range.

From the outside, the summit is a small dark inclusion in a pale cliff: a thumb-sized cavity in stone, easily mistaken for a flaw in the rock. Inside, however, distance has been folded without being enlarged. The cavity contains a high plateau, a ravine, a weather station, and the black horizon of a sky that should not fit behind its opening.

Elevation here is not measured from sea level. It is measured from ordinary space. Everything in the pocket is impossibly high: the air is thin, the cold is clean and mineral, and the ground seems to hang above the world rather than rest upon it. There is no visible valley below. At the edges of the plateau, the terrain simply becomes less certain—first a steep scree slope, then a vertical plane of gray light, then a place where distance has been removed.

The ground is made of dark, fine-grained stone veined with dull metallic seams. It resembles basalt, ash, and the interior of a machine at once. In places, the surface has weathered into shallow polygonal plates, each plate separated from the next by a hairline darkness. The cracks do not deepen. They lead nowhere, or perhaps to elsewhere, but anything dropped into them remains visible forever at the same depth: a grain of dust, a shard of glass, a small brass-colored screw suspended in permanent descent.

There is no water. The pale deposits along the rocks are not salt and not frost, though they have the appearance of both. They are residues of pressure: matter forced into a powdery state by the pocket’s geometry. They gather in sheltered corners and form soft-looking drifts that do not shift when touched. Their grains are square, each one a microscopic room containing a single muted reflection.

The air is cold enough to discourage movement, though there is no wind. It has almost no scent. Breathing is possible only because the chamber supplies an approximation of atmosphere, assembled molecule by molecule from no apparent source. The air tastes faintly of iron and distant electricity. Occasionally it becomes thinner in one direction, and the horizon bends toward the deprivation. There are no clouds, only slow darkening: a gray ceiling gradually losing its distinction from the ground.

The light is dim and without a source. It seems to come from the surfaces themselves, though every stone remains shadowed. Shadows are sparse, narrow, and patient. A standing object may cast a shadow several minutes after the light changes. Sometimes a shadow continues moving after its object has been removed.

Nothing grows here. No plant, fungus, lichen, or microbial film interrupts the mineral surfaces. There are no insects beneath the stones, no birds above the ridgeline, no hidden burrowing things. The landscape is not dead so much as preoccupied with a form of existence that has no appetite. Fine patterns appear across the stone in slow, branching arrangements, but they are stress maps, not roots. They alter when observed. Given enough time, they may assemble the outline of a leaf, a hand, or an unfamiliar alphabet, but they never become organic.

The only ecology is infrastructural.

Small devices are scattered across the plateau, each separated from the next by long stretches of empty ground. Some are black pylons no taller than a person’s knee. Some are thin tripods carrying rings of translucent material. One structure resembles a hut assembled from six stone panels, but its walls are continuous with the mountain and its doorway opens onto a different angle of the same room. There are no roads between them. Their placement suggests a network, yet the distances change according to who is looking.

The devices are old, though “old” is an inadequate description. Their surfaces are unworn, but their purposes have eroded. A pylon may be a beacon, a boundary marker, a dormant engine, or the fossilized remainder of a calculation. Its inscriptions are not written on the metal; they occur as temporary absences in the surrounding light. When one approaches, the inscriptions become legible in a language that feels remembered rather than learned. When one turns away, they vanish.

At the center of the plateau stands a narrow tower with no visible entrance. It is built from a material darker than the surrounding dark, a substance that absorbs not light but conclusions. Looking at it, one cannot decide how many sides it has. Its height changes only when attention wanders. Near its base lies a ring of pale fragments, perhaps broken instruments, perhaps pieces of a moon reduced to a convenient scale.

The tower is still functioning.

Its operation is nearly imperceptible. Every few hours—or every few centuries; the distinction is unstable—it releases a pressureless pulse. The pulse passes through the ground and rearranges one minor fact. A crack closes. A stone acquires a second shadow. The tower shifts one centimeter to the left while the mountain remains convinced it has always been there. These changes are too slow to witness directly, but the landscape accumulates them. Its settled forms are the result of countless corrections.

The place has rules, though they are not laws in the human sense.

A straight line cannot remain straight if it crosses the plateau twice. A sound cannot travel toward the tower. An object left unattended is gradually assigned an alternate history. The longer it remains, the more difficult it becomes to remember what it was before arriving. Distance is conserved, but not consistently: walking away from the center may bring one closer to it, and looking down from the ridge may reveal the underside of the plateau overhead.

Height behaves most strangely. The plateau is high because it is above all ordinary reference, but from its rim one sometimes sees a vast landscape below: salt deserts, cloud systems, oceans, cities, or a starless black. These views do not belong to the pocket. They are possible lower elevations, glimpsed through a failure in containment. They remain perfectly still, as if painted on the back of reality. No wind rises from them. No sound crosses upward.

The silence is almost complete. There is a faint granular ticking beneath the ground, too slow to be called a vibration. From the scattered structures comes an occasional soft tone, like a glass being touched in a neighboring room. The tower produces no audible sound, but nearby objects sometimes finish a sentence that was never spoken.

When the pocket enters one of its long cycles, the dimness thickens. The metallic seams in the stone become briefly visible, glowing with a cold internal geometry. The plateau seems to expand—not outward, but into greater intimacy. A far ridge is suddenly beside the observer. The tiny cavity in the outer mountain can be seen overhead, impossibly distant and impossibly near, opening onto the ordinary world like a keyhole.

Then the cycle passes.

The seams dull. The structures return to their scattered stillness. The ground resumes its ancient, low-grade settling. Dust hangs where gravity has misplaced it. The summit remains concealed inside its stone, high above everything, containing more emptiness than its dimensions allow.
