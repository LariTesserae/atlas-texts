The city occupies the summit of a mountain that has no visible base.

It rises from a pale mineral continent in a series of immense terraces, each one cut into the next as though the whole range were a stairway made for something larger than weather. Below, the lower slopes descend into dimness—not fog, not shadow, but a region where distance loses its agreement with itself. Ridges continue downward for a while, then appear overhead. Far-off walls are seen edge-on and face-on at once. The horizon is a narrow, irregular band of bruised gray, and beneath it there is no valley, only a depth too empty to reflect light.

The air is cold and exceptionally dry. It has the thin, flavorless quality of a sealed chamber, though the city is open to the sky. Breath leaves the body as a faint powder rather than vapor. There is no moisture anywhere: no frost, no snow, no ice, no dampness darkening the stone. The exposed surfaces are pale as ash, with occasional seams of blue-black glass and long metallic veins that have never known rust.

The settlement is called a city because it has streets, districts, gates, towers, courtyards, and a clear insistence on arrangement. It is not a city because anyone remembers its builders.

Its avenues run along the mountain in broad, level bands. Stairways climb between them, often too tall and steep to have been made for ordinary walking. Bridges cross gaps that are not always there. An arch may span an empty ravine in the morning and connect two neighboring towers by evening. Buildings stand in dense clusters near the central terraces, but the spaces between them are extraordinarily large. A single square may contain a dozen structures separated by hundreds of paces of bare stone. Their arrangement suggests a crowded plan viewed through a vast expansion: a city stretched thin until every building has room for its own silence.

The architecture is mineral rather than constructed. Walls have been quarried, but they also seem to have grown in place, layer by layer, under pressures no living mason could survive. Houses are narrow and windowless, with entrances cut as vertical slits. Their roofs curve inward like folded plates. Towers taper toward the summit and terminate in open chambers, each chamber containing only a circular floor and a view of the impossible sky.

Some structures are ruined, but ruin here is a slow and orderly thing. Corners have softened. Doorways have tilted. Whole facades have slid a few inches down their foundations and stopped. Nothing is collapsed in the ordinary sense. The city has settled into itself rather than fallen apart. Streets are buried under a skin of powdered stone, yet the powder gathers in precise channels, leaving the old paving visible as if the ground is remembering where people ought to walk.

There are no tools, machines, lamps, wires, or signs of fire. The built world has no technological residue. Every wall, road, buttress, and stair seems to have been shaped by hand—or by a kind of intention that took the place of hands. In some districts, one can see the fingerprints of the builders: shallow parallel grooves in the stone, too regular to be natural and too numerous to belong to any known tool. In others, construction becomes geological. A row of residences has been pressed out of one continuous slab, their interiors separated by translucent partitions. An entire plaza is a single black stone, polished in concentric rings.

The city’s highest quarter is a ring around the summit. The ring is broken in seven places, though the breaks do not always face the same directions. Inside it stands the mountain’s crown: a broad, flat expanse of white mineral with no buildings at all. At its center is a depression shaped like a basin, but the basin contains neither water nor dust. It contains distance.

Looking into it does not reveal a bottom. It reveals the same summit from another angle, then a different summit, then a low plain under a red sky. Occasionally something moves far below—too slowly to be an animal, too purposefully to be geology. The motion may be occurring in the basin. It may be occurring in the observer.

Nothing grows in the city. There are no plants, lichens, mosses, or fungal films. The cracks remain clean. Even the most sheltered courtyards contain only mineral powder, sharp flakes of glass, and pale nodules that resemble seeds without being seeds. These nodules are found in orderly clusters beneath staircases and along the inside corners of rooms. They are cold to the touch. Some slowly rotate when no one is watching them.

Animal life is scarce, but not absent. At long intervals, a shape crosses the open terraces: a six-legged thing with a narrow, jointed body and a skin like folded slate. It moves without making footfalls. Its legs seem to touch the ground only after the body has already passed over them. From a distance it resembles a crack traveling across the stone.

Another creature inhabits the towers. It is never seen directly, only inferred from disturbances: a door left open, a drift of powder drawn upward, a line of small black impressions appearing across a ceiling. Once, in a high room, there is a chance to find a translucent membrane stretched between two walls, trembling though the air is still. Behind it, something large has pressed its outline into the dust. The outline remains for several hours after the membrane has vanished.

The city is not dead. It is active in ways that do not require crowds.

The mountain changes by fractions. A stairway gains or loses a step. A wall lengthens through the night. The empty spaces between buildings widen and narrow in slow cycles, as if the settlement is breathing without lungs. Far below, entire ledges shear away with no sound and hang suspended in the open air. After a time they descend, though not necessarily toward the ground. They may settle against the underside of another terrace, becoming a new district overhead.

The atmosphere is dim even beneath the open sky. Light arrives as a diluted silver, without a clear source. There is no sun, but sometimes a brighter region moves across the clouds—or across whatever lies above the clouds—and the city’s shadows rotate independently of the light. At other times, every surface becomes faintly luminous from within, revealing veins and seams that vanish when touched.

The sky is a low, colorless vault during most of the cycle. Its gray is not uniform. It has depth, folds, and occasional seams through which a colder darkness shows. There may be stars beyond it, but they are visible only in the polished sides of buildings, and never in the same arrangement twice.

Sound almost does not exist. Footsteps are swallowed by the powdered streets. Voices seem to stop a short distance from the speaker. A stone struck against another stone produces only the sensation of an impact in the teeth.

Yet the city makes quiet sounds of its own.

From the lower terraces comes a faint, intermittent settling: not rumbling, but a sequence of soft internal knocks passing through the mountain. In the towers, empty chambers exhale a thin tone below hearing, felt more in the ribs than perceived by the ear. Bridges give off a dry clicking when their far ends shift position. At certain hours, the entire city produces a sound like a distant crowd holding its breath.

The rules are simple until they are not.

Stairs always lead upward, but “upward” may change during the ascent. A person who climbs without looking back will reach a higher place. A person who turns to check the distance may find the stair descending behind them into a district that did not exist below.

Doors open only when approached with a destination in mind. If the destination is a room, the door gives onto a room. If it is a person, the doorway may reveal a street, a childhood landscape, or the inside of a body. It is safer to want nothing specific.

The city preserves paths but not travelers. Any trail walked three times becomes permanent, cut into the stone by no visible process. Any place visited with a strong expectation becomes less real. The buildings there flatten, their interiors becoming painted suggestions. To keep a location, one must approach it without deciding what it is.

At the edge of the settlement, the streets stop abruptly. Beyond them lies the mountain’s open face, climbing toward no summit or dropping toward no floor. Sometimes the edge overlooks the pale mineral world from which the mountain rises. Sometimes it overlooks the underside of the city itself: foundations, buried plazas, and streets running through the rock like veins.

The most unsettling feature is the sense that the settlement was not built upon the mountain.

It may have been built through it.

The terraces, towers, and avenues could be the exposed remains of a larger structure whose hidden portions extend into the impossible depths. The summit may be only one roof. The empty basin may be an entrance. The quiet knocks beneath the streets may be movement from districts still being formed below.

In the far distance, across a gulf where the air has gone black, another line of buildings sometimes appears. It hangs vertically on the side of nothing. Its towers are inverted, pointing downward into the void. No path leads there. No bridge reaches across. But on certain cycles, a single pale figure can be seen walking along its streets.

It pauses.

It looks toward this city.

Then both settlements shift slightly out of alignment, and the distance between them becomes too large to measure.
