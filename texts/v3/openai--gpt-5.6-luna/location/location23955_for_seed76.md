The high country begins above the weather.

It is not a mountain range in the familiar sense, though its horizons rise and fall like mountains viewed from an even greater height. The land is an immense plateau, or a continent-sized crown of one: broad shelves of pale stone, tilted basins, ridges worn smooth by ages of wind. Its surfaces are gray, silver, green-black, and the color of old bone. Here and there the rock opens into vast shallow depressions, thousands of miles across, where the ground has been polished into faintly rippled planes.

There is no water on the plateau. No rivers cut it. No lakes shine in its hollows. The sky carries no rain.

Instead, the ground is alive with a different fluid: a cold mineral vapor that rises slowly through cracks in the stone. It is almost invisible except where the bright air catches it, making thin vertical veils above the darker ground. In the mornings—if morning can be called the long period when the sun stands low and white—the vapor gathers in hollows and moves in broad, deliberate sheets. It does not wet anything. It settles as a skin of salts, a faint bloom of reactive dust, and the rooted life drinks it through filaments buried deep in the rock.

The vegetation is lush, but not in the sense of crowded green. It is lush in reach, in persistence, in the extravagant abundance of a life-form that has had an age to occupy every possible surface.

Long mats of blue-green growth spread over the stone in branching fans. Their leaves are narrow, translucent blades, each one stiffened with mineral ribs. They lie flat where the wind is strongest and rise into low forests where the rock gives shelter. In sheltered basins, entire fields of them stand three or four meters high, pale stalks clustered so densely at their bases that the ground disappears. They have no flowers, no fruit, and no visible seeds. In place of flowers, they produce brief coronas of white filaments that open directly toward the sun. When the filaments close, they leave behind small black nodules embedded in the rock.

Other plants grow as towers: hollow columns plated in overlapping scales, colored rust-red at the base and greenish silver above. Some are no taller than a person; others rise hundreds of meters, in groves so widely spaced that each tower seems solitary. Their upper surfaces are broad and cupped, not to catch rain, but to gather the charged mineral haze. During periods of atmospheric activity, their crowns glow faintly from within.

Still others are low and immense. A single organism may extend beneath many square miles of stone, appearing at the surface as a mosaic of leathery leaves, ridged pads, and dark circular openings. These openings pulse slowly. They exhale warmth into the cold ground and draw the vapor downward. If one stands near them, the landscape seems to breathe—not with a rise and fall, but with a minute, geological alternation of pressure.

The plateau is high enough that the sky is nearly black above its blue-white glare. Light is abundant and merciless. It comes from a sun that appears smaller than expected, its brightness sharpened by the thin atmosphere. Shadows are deep, clean-edged, and blue. The air itself seems empty, yet the distance is never entirely clear. High ridges waver behind sheets of refracted brightness. Far-off forests appear to rise from the ground and float, inverted, in the sky.

There are no clouds in the ordinary sense. Instead, the upper atmosphere develops enormous curtains of suspended mineral particles. They form slowly over the continental interior, then drift for weeks toward the margins. From the ground they look like translucent walls, gray-violet or pearl-colored, passing across the sun without dimming it so much as changing its character. Under one of these curtains, every surface shines with a faint internal color. The plants lean toward it. The stone clicks as it contracts.

The cold is constant but not severe. It is a dry cold, without the softening presence of frost. Nothing freezes because there is nothing liquid to freeze. Exposed rock remains hard and bare. At night, when the sun passes beyond the horizon, the temperature falls toward the limits at which the living mats become rigid. Their tissues lock into place. The towers stop their slow turning. The entire plateau becomes still enough to resemble an abandoned world.

Then the ground begins to warm again.

The sounds are small. Wind moves across the open shelves in long, almost inaudible streams. It passes through the upright growth with a dry brushing noise, like thousands of paper strips being drawn across one another. The tall mineral plants produce hollow notes when they flex, though these tones are so low and widely spaced that they seem to come from beneath the ground. In the basins, the sound may continue for hours: one deep note, then another, separated by long silences.

The stone also speaks.

Temperature causes the broad slabs to shift by fractions of an inch, and the movement travels for miles as a sequence of distant cracks. Not sharp fractures, but slow reports—muted detonations rolling along the horizon. In the quietest places, a listener can hear the surface adjusting itself: clicks, sighs, faint grinding, the settling of a landscape that has been settling for millions of years.

Life is sparse despite the vegetation. The plants are spread over enormous distances, and between one colony and the next there may be a hundred miles of bare terrain. The bare ground is not dead. It is a mineral desert covered in old growth traces: branching impressions, low black crusts, pale shelves left behind by organisms that have withdrawn beneath the surface. But the living forms are widely spaced, each adapted to an immense territory and a slow rhythm.

Animal life is rarer still.

Occasionally, something crosses the open flats: a many-jointed grazer, low to the ground, with a body like folded slate and a fringe of flexible sensory tendrils. It moves for days without stopping, extracting minute nutrients from the mineral crusts. Its passage leaves no tracks, only a temporary darkening in the surface as the crust closes behind it.

There are larger predators, but seeing one is unlikely. They are nearly stationary creatures, rooted for long periods among the tower groves, their bodies disguised as damaged columns or slabs of uplifted stone. When they move, they move suddenly and with terrible economy. The plants around them bend away before the animal becomes visible, responding to pressure changes in the ground. A herd—or what passes for a herd—may alter course miles in advance of one.

Most of the plateau’s activity is not animal. It is the migration of roots through stone, the opening and closure of mineral pores, the slow advance of colonies over exposed shelves. Whole ecosystems shift by inches each year. A field of silver blades may gradually climb a ridge. A black undergrowth may retreat from a basin as the vapor beneath it changes composition. Once in several centuries, a tower forest may release its accumulated salts in a single synchronized shedding. The event lasts less than an hour. The air fills with glittering scales, and afterward the forest stands stripped and dark, waiting to rebuild itself.

The old forms remain everywhere.

Ridges have been rounded by wind and chemical abrasion until they resemble enormous sleeping animals. Cliffs are rare; where they exist, their faces are fluted and layered, marked by the repeated passage of mineral storms. Basins have been filled and emptied—not with water, but with drifting dusts and the growth of successive generations. On the flanks of the highest elevations, the ground rises in terraces, each one bounded by a low lip of darker stone. These are ancient shorelines of a vanished substance, perhaps a denser atmosphere, perhaps a phase of the planet that no living thing remembers.

Nothing here seems newly made. Even the active processes have settled into their mature forms. The wind has found its corridors. The plants have occupied their niches. The rock has been worn into its enduring shapes. There is change everywhere, but almost none of it feels like disturbance. The plateau does not struggle toward another state. It persists, adjusts, and continues.

At the far edges, the high country descends very gradually, over distances too large to read as slopes. The vegetation thins. The stone darkens. The mineral haze grows denser, and the bright sky becomes hazy with suspended matter. Beyond that, the land drops toward regions never seen from the plateau: lower atmospheric basins where the air may be thick, the light dim, and other forms of life gathered around chemistries this upper world cannot support.

But from the high shelves, those depths remain hidden beneath the curvature of the land.

There is only the immense pale ground, the scattered forests, the black sky, and the cold bright sun. A wind crosses the continent. Somewhere far away, a living tower gives one low, resonant note. Beneath the stone, roots continue their patient search through the mineral dark.
