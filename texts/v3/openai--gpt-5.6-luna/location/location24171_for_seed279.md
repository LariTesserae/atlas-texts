The basin is low and broad, a shallow country still deciding what shape it will have.

It lies below a ceiling of cloud the color of old pewter. Light comes through without direction. There is no sun to point toward, only a general pallor above the horizon, and the wet ground returns almost none of it. Everything is gray, black, or the muted white of exposed mineral: a world with its colors not removed, exactly, but held underwater.

Rain has recently passed, though the rain never seems entirely finished. It hangs in the air as fine suspension, beads on every surface, and gathers in the shallow grooves between slabs. The atmosphere is cool enough to raise a faint vapor from the warmer seams below. At a distance, the basin appears to be breathing.

The land is young.

It has not been softened by weather or made familiar by repetition. Long shelves of dark stone rise only a few feet above the flats, their edges as clean and sharp as broken ceramic. Channels cut through them in sudden straight lines, then end abruptly where the ground has lifted or folded. There are mounds of pale rubble with no older soil beneath them, and terraces that look poured rather than eroded. In places the crust has split open to reveal layers of glassy black stone, blue-gray clay, and a shining substance like compressed frost.

Water occupies every low place. It does not form a single marsh or lake, but a multitude of connected films and pools: ankle-deep sheets over stone, narrow runnels that vanish under shelves, round basins filled to their rims, and dark puddles trembling with unseen pressure. The water is clear where it is shallow and almost opaque where it gathers. It carries no leaves, no silt from a living watershed, no organic stain. Instead it leaves behind thin white rims and delicate crusts that resemble salt, bone, or the dried edges of a wound.

Some of the rocks weep.

They sweat steadily from their vertical faces, each bead emerging from a different point and following a separate path downward. The walls are dense with these threads of water, close together but never joining until they reach the floor. In the dimness, the wet surfaces look furred with glass. A person could stand beneath one of the cliffs and hear thousands of tiny impacts, a soft rapid pattering beneath the larger voice of the basin.

That larger voice is everywhere.

Water moves beneath the ground with the force of something trapped for a very long time. It rushes through hidden channels, strikes hollow chambers, and rebounds under the stone. Some areas produce a deep continuous bellow, as if the earth were holding a note in its throat. Elsewhere, pressure vents through cracks in sudden hammering bursts. The sound is loud enough to flatten distance. A nearby shelf and a far ridge seem equally close; a shout becomes part of the general thunder almost before it leaves the mouth.

Nothing in the basin moves quickly, despite the noise.

The water advances by fractions. A wet edge thickens. A pool rises imperceptibly against its rim. A new crack opens with a sound like a cannon heard underwater, then remains unchanged for years. The sharp slopes are not collapsing so much as remembering that collapse is possible. Their instability is patient. A slab may lean through an entire season before falling, and when it does, the event is enormous but brief: a white burst of spray, a long shudder beneath the feet, and then the same slow accumulation begins again.

There is no vegetation. No moss softens the ledges. No root has entered the cracks. There are no reeds in the flooded hollows, no algae staining the water, no lichen on the exposed faces. The absence is not barren in the ordinary sense; it is too complete for that. The basin seems to belong to a stage before plants, or after them, with the intervening history erased so thoroughly that even the stone does not remember.

Yet the mineral surfaces are crowded.

Along the flats stand clusters of thin vertical plates, each one no wider than a hand and ranging from knee-high to taller than a person. They grow in close ranks, nearly touching, like a forest translated into geology. Their surfaces are dull on one side and mirror-bright on the other. When the mist thickens, droplets gather on them in perfectly even rows. When the wind changes, the plates give a low collective vibration—not ringing, not singing, but a pressure felt in the teeth.

Other formations resemble low, sealed mouths. They are oval ridges of gray stone surrounding shallow depressions. Water gathers inside them but never overflows, even when the basin floods. From each depression comes a slow exhalation of warm vapor. The vapor leaves a temporary darkening on the air, a distortion like a figure seen through glass. If one watches long enough, the shapes resolve into nothing. If one looks away and back, they may have changed.

The densest parts of the landscape are not forests or settlements but accumulations of these near-living structures. They crowd the edges of channels, pack the bases of cliffs, and fill the broad shelves in overlapping layers. A path through them is possible, but only by turning sideways, stepping over ridges, and placing the feet carefully between water-filled seams. Every surface seems to touch another surface. There are few empty spaces, though there is a great deal of open distance.

Across that distance, scattered constructions appear.

They are low and severe, made from the same dark stone as the basin. Some are half-buried chambers with sloping roofs, their entrances set just above the waterline. Some are towers no higher than a house, composed of stacked blocks whose joins have been sealed by mineral growth. A few are long walls that stop without explanation, each end cleanly broken, as though the builders had left in the middle of a thought.

There are no doors that can be seen. No windows give light. In one structure, a narrow opening leads to a dry interior lined with pale plates. In another, the walls have been worn smooth by repeated passage, though no footprints remain and nothing appears to have passed through it recently. The buildings do not feel abandoned. They feel paused.

Their age is difficult to judge. The stone around them is fresh and sharp, but the structures are already being incorporated into the basin. Water drips through their ceilings. Mineral shelves rise along their walls. One long shelter has become indistinguishable from a ridge except for the regular spacing of its inner supports. The land is not destroying the constructions so much as learning their shapes and continuing them.

Fauna is rare, and what exists does not announce itself as animal life.

At the edge of one pool, a pale, many-jointed thing may unfold from beneath a submerged ledge. It is no larger than a dog, though it has no visible head, eyes, or limbs in the familiar sense. It advances by tightening and relaxing its whole body, leaving the water strangely unruffled. Its surface is translucent and reveals a dim internal glow like light trapped in cloudy ice. It remains for several minutes, tasting the air or the stone, then withdraws without a splash.

Elsewhere, small dark forms cling to the undersides of mineral shelves. They resemble knots of wet rope, but occasionally separate into several pieces and rejoin. They are almost never seen in motion. Their presence is inferred from the faint circular marks they leave on the rock, each mark slowly filling with clear water after they depart.

These creatures do not flee from people. They do not approach, either. The basin has the indifference of a place where recognition has not yet evolved.

There are rules here, though they are not written anywhere.

Water never crosses a dry line until the line has been observed. A pool can remain perfectly level beside a slope, then creep over its edge after someone notices the imbalance. Sounds travel through stone more clearly than through air. A hand placed against a wall may feel distant footsteps, though there are no footsteps to account for. Reflections are delayed: the water shows the sky as it was several moments ago, and sometimes shows a shape that has already moved away.

The mineral plates respond to attention. When no one is watching, they slowly lean toward the nearest source of water. When watched directly, they hold still. If a person walks away without looking back, the plates may change position with a series of almost inaudible clicks.

Fire does not burn well here. It gives a small, blue, exhausted flame and produces no warmth beyond its immediate edge. Breath, however, remains visible for a long time after it leaves the body, gathering in front of the face as a white veil that drifts against the prevailing air. In the basin, the air seems to have weight and preference. It presses toward the flooded channels, carrying the smell of wet stone, cold metal, and something faintly sweet that has no apparent source.

The horizon is broken by low ridges and distant shelves, never mountains. The region may be crossed in a day, perhaps two, but the sound makes the journey feel longer. Each step is accompanied by the subterranean roar, the patter of dripping faces, the shuddering murmur of plates, and occasional deep knocks from beneath the ground. At intervals, all of these noises align. The entire basin then seems to strike one immense chord.

When that happens, the water rises.

Not dramatically. It lifts across the whole landscape by the thickness of a finger, perhaps less, filling cracks and darkening the lower faces of stone. The change takes several minutes and then stops. Afterward, the basin is quieter for a while—not silent, never silent, but subdued, as if whatever lies beneath has settled into a more comfortable position.

In the dim light, the wet ground begins to resemble a map of veins, the channels and reflections joining the scattered structures into a pattern too large to understand from within. The place feels engineered, though no tool marks remain. It feels alive, though nothing grows. It feels newly made, yet burdened by a history that has not happened yet.

And always, beneath the cool mist and the sharp young stone, the water continues moving slowly toward the lowest part of the world.
