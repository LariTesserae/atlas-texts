The plateau begins above the cloud line, though the clouds do not stay below it.

They gather in the long hollows and spill over the escarpments in slow white rivers, pouring through clefts in the rock, snagging on forests, thinning, returning. The highest ridges rise far beyond the immediate valley—black, rust-red, and green with distance—until they become a broken horizon of enormous uplands. Nothing here is sharp with youth. The cliffs have been standing for ages, their edges rounded and undercut, their faces patterned by old watercourses that no longer run. Valleys have settled into themselves. The land seems less shaped than finally arrived.

Heat lies over everything.

It does not shimmer much in the ordinary way, because the air is heavy with moisture. It beads on stone. It hangs in the leaves. It gathers beneath overhangs and falls in a fine, continuous dripping, even when no rain is visible. The sun is a plain, bright disk through the thinning cloud, and the light is ordinary daylight—white on quartz, gold on grass, blue in deep water—but the heat is immense, pressing through clothing and bark and the thin membranes of leaves. At midday, every wet surface gives back a low breath of steam.

Water is everywhere, but rarely in a straight line.

It comes up through the plateau in warm springs, runs beneath mats of vegetation, gathers in shallow basins, and disappears into cracks that glow faintly from within. Black rocks weep from their vertical faces. Slender waterfalls descend from one shelf to the next, spreading into mist before they reach the ground. In some places the surface is porous and trembling: a person can stand on a skin of moss and feel a hidden river moving beneath it. In others, entire meadows float on saturated peat, their roots interlocked so tightly that they move as one slow body.

The vegetation is lush without being delicate. It has adapted to heat, altitude, and constant wetness by becoming structural.

Tall spear-leaved plants grow in dense tiers along the ravines, their stems pale and jointed like bundled pipes. Ferns climb the trunks of squat, broad-canopied trees, while red flowers open only after rain and close again when their inner reservoirs are full. There are vines with translucent veins, swollen with mineral-rich water, and low cushions of blue-green growth that release warm vapor when touched. The higher slopes are covered in thick grasses that bend under the weight of condensation. Their seed heads are dark and glossy, and when the wind moves through them the whole hillside seems to fur itself.

In the hottest gullies, the plants are nearly black. They grow around vents where the mountain exhales sulfur, methane, and something cleaner and colder that has no familiar smell. Their leaves turn toward the vents rather than the sun. Along the spring channels, by contrast, vegetation fluoresces weakly—not brightly enough to compete with daylight, but enough to mark the water after dusk and reveal the hidden branching of the aquifers.

There are many animals, though the density of life is most apparent in layers.

Small, quick bodies move under every shelf of leaf litter. Long-legged grazers pick their way through the high grass, their feet spread wide to keep from sinking. They are furred along the spine and nearly hairless beneath, with thin cooling membranes between their ribs. Bright-winged things rise from the marshes in swarms, settle, rise again. Their wings make a sound like tearing paper. Larger animals watch from the shade: heavy, amphibious herbivores with stone-colored hides; narrow predators whose paws leave no impression in the mud; slow climbing creatures that cling to cliffs and drink condensation directly from the rock.

Some fauna is difficult to distinguish from the landscape. A line of moss may close one eye. A slab of wet basalt may shift its weight. In the reed beds, tall shapes stand motionless for so long that they become part of the vertical pattern, then unfold themselves and wade away. Nothing hunts constantly. Even predation is patient here. Animals lie in water or foliage for hours, conserving energy in the heat, waiting for a single successful movement.

The noise is substantial.

Water strikes stone from every direction: taps, hisses, heavy impacts, endless fine pattering. The waterfalls are not grand enough to dominate the region, but there are so many of them that their combined sound fills the air like a continuous engine. Insects pulse in the lower growth. Birds—or things occupying the ecological role of birds—call across the valleys in deep, resonant tones. Their voices travel farther than expected in the wet air, returning from cliffs seconds later in altered pitches. From the marshes comes a communal booming, irregular but immense, as if the ground itself were trying to speak.

There is also a mechanical sound, faint and widely distributed.

It resembles the vibration of a taut cable, though no cable is visible. Sometimes it rises from beneath the plateau. Sometimes it travels through the water. When it passes underfoot, pools tremble in perfect concentric rings, and the fur of nearby animals lifts along the spine. The sound is not threatening. It is simply part of the place, as old and persistent as the springs.

Human construction appears only occasionally, and never in the valleys where it would be easiest.

A narrow bridge crosses one chasm, made of a dark material with no seams. It has no supports. It hangs in a shallow curve and flexes almost imperceptibly as the fog presses against it. Along its surface, thin lines brighten when approached, mapping safe footing around patches of condensed water. No one remembers who built it, though the bridge adjusts itself every season, growing a little longer as the opposing cliffs settle apart.

Farther up, a small station clings to the mountain face. It is half obscured by vines and mineral deposits, its windows opaque, its exterior covered in a skin of pale ceramic that repairs scratches before they become visible. A single antenna reaches into the cloud. It bends toward storms that are still days away. Nearby, an old access shaft descends into the rock, but the entrance is sealed by a smooth membrane that opens only for certain animals.

There are other marks: a row of metal pillars beside a spring, each one leaning at a different angle; a circular foundation filled with rainwater and lilies; small black instruments embedded in the cliff, their surfaces warm despite the shade. They do not look abandoned so much as incorporated. Moss grows across them without obscuring their function. Roots pass through their housings. Water routes itself around them. The living world has not reclaimed the technology because the technology has learned to belong.

The most extraordinary structure is visible only from the upper ridges.

Across the far plateau, where the ground rises into a broad saddle between two volcanic peaks, the land is divided by a faint geometric pattern. Long arcs run through the vegetation, too regular to be natural and too large to read from the ground. At intervals, the arcs dip into valleys and reappear on the other side. When cloud shadow passes over them, they answer with a dim internal light. They may be conduits, weather instruments, buried machines, or the remnants of an intervention so old that its original purpose has become indistinguishable from geology.

They operate slowly.

A slope may remain unchanged for a lifetime, then shift by a few centimeters in one night. A spring changes course over decades. Trees migrate uphill by sending roots through the porous rock and leaving their old trunks standing. The great underground mechanism—or whatever lies beneath the plateau—works in cycles measured not in seconds but in seasons, centuries, perhaps orbital periods. Its influence is felt as a gradual rebalancing: water diverted from one basin to another, heat drawn from deep strata, clouds held against a ridge until the thirsty side has greened.

The place does not feel static. It feels unhurried.

Everywhere, processes are underway, but almost none of them are urgent. A cliff is dissolving. A forest is advancing. A machine is correcting the pressure beneath a mountain. A species is learning to nest inside a warm conduit. These things happen with the calm inevitability of weathering.

Toward evening, the light remains ordinary, but the clouds turn copper along their upper edges. Mist fills the lower valleys until only the highest ridges and trees remain above it. The wet stone cools by degrees. The strange mechanical vibration deepens, joining the waterfalls and animal calls. Across the open plateau, scattered lights appear beneath the plants—not lamps, not fire, but the quiet signals of buried systems waking in sequence.

By night, the whole mountain is still loud.

Water falls. Creatures call. Leaves knock together in the warm wind. Deep below, something immense alters the course of a river by less than the width of a hand.
