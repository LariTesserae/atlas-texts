The chamber lies beneath the world like a seed caught in stone.

It is not large. A person could walk from one wall to the other in less than a minute, though the floor is broken into ridges and shelves, and the ceiling hangs low enough in places that the taller growths have learned to bend. The chamber may once have been a crack, newly opened in the deep rock. Its walls are raw and angular: black mineral planes, pale splinters of crystal, fresh faces that have not yet been softened by dust or time. There is no sand. Nothing has traveled far enough to become sand.

The air is warm and thick with the smell of iron, resin, and something faintly sweet. It does not feel wet. No beads gather on the stone, no trickles descend into cracks, no hidden pool answers the ear. The moisture that sustains the place is bound inside the living things themselves, drawn from the rock by roots, skins, and filaments, then held there with stubborn economy. Every surface appears dry until touched. Then it gives slightly, like the rind of a fruit.

A low ceiling seals the chamber overhead. In its center, a narrow vertical wound rises into darkness, perhaps toward the surface, perhaps into some other layer of the world. Dim amber light descends through it, but not in a beam. The glow is caught and spread by the vegetation, which has learned to turn darkness green.

The plants are lush without being crowded. They stand apart from one another in deliberate-looking intervals, each occupying a patch of mineral soil or a shelf of stone. Their forms are broad, fleshy, and unfamiliar. Some resemble ferns made from translucent leather. Some grow as jointed fans, their leaves angled like the fingers of an open hand. Others are low mats of dark green blades, each blade tipped with a tiny amber tooth. Pale cords descend from their roots and disappear into hairline cracks, where they drink the warmth and the metallic salts of the chamber.

Nothing grows in abundance for its own sake. The empty stone between the plants is part of the arrangement. It is an orchard with its trees removed, a garden designed around gaps.

Here and there, the rock has split into sharp terraces. At one such place stands a shelter assembled from slabs of black stone and the rib-like stems of dead plants. It has no door. Inside are a grinding hollow, a bundle of sharpened bone tools, and several shallow trays filled with red mineral flakes. A second structure crouches against the far wall, little more than a lean-to and a frame of lashed branches. The bindings are made from braided root fiber. There is no metal, no glass, no worked surface finer than a hand can make. The inhabitants, if they still come here, have left behind only the practical shapes of their presence.

Yet the chamber is full of animals.

They occupy the spaces between the plants. A six-legged thing with a plated back rests beneath a shelf of stone, its many nostrils opening and closing in time with the chamber’s pulse. Small white creatures cling to the walls, each no larger than a thumb, with soft bodies and long, bristling limbs. They do not scatter when approached. They turn their heads together, watching with black, wet-looking eyes that are not wet at all.

Near the central fissure, a herd of thin, upright animals stands in a loose ring. Their bodies are warm-colored and nearly hairless, marked with dark seams where their ribs show through. They have no visible mouths. From time to time, one bends its head to a plant and presses its face against the leaves, taking nourishment through the skin. The plants do not resist. Their leaves close around the animal’s muzzle, then open again.

There are insects, or things that occupy the role of insects: copper-colored beetles crawling in patient lines, winged forms that beat their wings without flying, and small translucent creatures that hide beneath the stone’s surface until a shadow passes over them. The fauna is abundant, but not busy. Each animal is placed in its niche like a piece in a game that has already ended. None hunts. None flees. Predators and prey coexist in a tense, permanent arrangement, as though the act of eating has been replaced by some older exchange of heat, minerals, or memory.

The noise is enormous.

It comes from the fissure, from the walls, from the bodies of the plants. A continuous subterranean roar fills the chamber—not the rush of water, but the grinding and resonant pressure of deep stone settling against deep stone. The sound has several layers. At the bottom is a broad, subsonic vibration felt in the teeth. Above it comes a harsh drumming, as if thousands of dry seeds are being shaken in a sealed vessel. Over all of this rises a chorus of clicks, shrieks, and hollow bell-tones made by the animals.

The sounds do not form a pattern that changes. They repeat with absolute fidelity.

A beetle strikes its shell against the rock: three clicks, a pause, three clicks.

A wall-creature gives a thin cry.

Somewhere beneath the floor, the chamber answers with a long metallic groan.

Then the sequence begins again.

The plants tremble, but never grow. Leaves fold and unfold, but always return to the same angles. The herd shifts its weight, yet remains in the same ring. Dust hangs in the air in fixed veils around the amber shaft of light. Even the falling fragments from the ceiling seem suspended just before impact, caught in a world where motion has been permitted only as repetition.

This is not stillness. It is a kind of permanent activity without consequence.

The chamber is young in its geology. Its edges are fresh, its fractures bright, its surfaces sharp enough to cut skin. But nothing here is weathered by age because nothing here is allowed to proceed. The place has emerged recently from the surrounding stone and then stopped at the instant of becoming itself. It has no ruins, only first attempts: the first shelter, the first tools, the first growths, the first animals to find the heat. The unfinished quality is everywhere. Roots end abruptly. Mineral columns rise only halfway before flattening. One wall contains a row of narrow cavities, each the beginning of a tunnel that never continued.

In the dimness, the green of the vegetation is almost black. The amber light touches only the highest leaves and the ridges of the animals’ backs. Below, the chamber is filled with pockets of dense shadow. Things are always visible at their edges: a pale hand of leaves, a jointed leg, the outline of a crouching body behind a rock. It is difficult to tell whether the shadows conceal movement or merely preserve the possibility of it.

At the center of the chamber stands the strangest growth.

It rises from a split in the floor, taller than a person but no thicker than a wrist. Its trunk is ivory, segmented, and completely smooth. From its uppermost joint hang seven long leaves, each pointing toward a different wall. They are not green. They are the color of old bone, with dark veins that pulse faintly beneath their surfaces.

The tree—if it is a tree—produces no fruit and casts no shadow. Every animal in the chamber is oriented toward it. Even the plants lean subtly in its direction, though their roots do not move. The roar beneath the floor seems loudest around its base.

Once, perhaps, it was a living thing.

Or perhaps it is the chamber’s instrument: a mineral organ, a tuning fork grown by the rock. The creatures may gather here because it sings to them. The plants may root around it because its vibrations break the stone into nourishment. The shelters may have been built by beings who believed the growth was sacred, or useful, or dangerous.

Nothing reveals which explanation is true. The place offers no history, only evidence held in suspension.

Warmth presses from every direction. The stone has the heat of a body that cannot cool. Breathing here feels unnecessary but not impossible. The air tastes faintly of pennies when inhaled. On the tongue, the mineral dust leaves a bitter green residue.

At the farthest edge of the chamber, behind a curtain of broad leaves, a narrow passage leads downward. It is no wider than a shoulder. The stone inside is smooth and dark, as if melted, though there is no sign of fire. From the passage comes the same endless noise, perfectly unchanged. No wind enters. No animal emerges. The passage simply continues into a depth that the pocket of the chamber seems too small to contain.

Around its mouth, someone has arranged seven flat stones in a circle.

They are not marked. They have not been carved. But each stone is warm, and each bears a faint smear of green.

The animals never cross the circle. The plants never root inside it. The repeated noise never falters.

Beyond the stones, the darkness remains open.
