The place is a reservoir, though “reservoir” suggests a greater scale than it possesses. It is a single chamber beneath the ground, no larger than a village square, sealed into the foundations of something above. You enter through a low arch and immediately pass below the waterline. There is no shore. The whole floor, the walls, the pillars, the steps, and the undersides of the overhead galleries are submerged in warm, clear water.

Everything here has been made.

The floor is laid in pale brick, fitted so tightly that no earth shows between it. Its surface rises and falls in shallow terraces, each edge sharp and recent, the mortar still clean and white. The walls are built from blocks of yellow limestone and dark fired clay, with broad bands of blue-green glazed tile marking the water levels. There are no rounded stones, no roots breaking through, no natural cave ceiling. Even the corners are deliberate: square buttresses, straight channels, narrow ledges for tools and lamps.

The chamber was built recently enough that its surfaces have not yet softened. Corners remain knife-like. Plaster flakes from the undersides of the arches in thin plates. The brick bears the marks of fingers, wooden paddles, and rough cutting tools. Here and there, a block has been replaced, but the replacement is obvious: newer, brighter, and set slightly crookedly among the older pieces.

The water is warm—not hot, but body-warm, with the heavy, enveloping comfort of a bath that has been kept moving. It fills most of the chamber and climbs into the upper galleries. In places it reaches the ceiling. In others, it falls away into vertical wells and stairwells, making the reservoir seem to possess several levels, though all are part of the same compact construction.

The water is remarkably bright. Sunlight enters through a row of narrow shafts in the roof, somewhere far above the chamber, and descends in broad trembling columns. The shafts are lined with polished white plaster and fitted with slabs of translucent stone. Their light spreads through the reservoir in pale gold. The farther walls are not dim so much as blue with depth. Every suspended grain of silt shines. The light catches the edges of steps and turns them into thin blades.

There are lamps too, fixed in ceramic niches above the waterline. Their flames burn behind hammered mica screens, small and wavering, their reflections stretched down into the water. The makers have not mastered illumination so much as multiplied it: daylight, flame, white plaster, glazed tile, and the steady glitter of the moving surface all work together until the enclosed chamber feels more exposed than a courtyard.

The reservoir is not silent. It is loud in the intimate way of enclosed water.

A sluice gate opens somewhere behind the eastern wall, and a thick current pushes through the chamber with a continuous, muscular rush. Water strikes the steps and breaks over them in sheets. Smaller channels chatter and click as they fill. A wooden wheel turns in an alcove, its paddles taking the force of a narrow fall and transferring it to a chain of lift-buckets. The wheel is crude, swollen from constant damp, and reinforced with strips of bronze-dark metal. It groans at every rotation.

Voices would not travel well here. A person speaking at one end becomes a blur of vowels at the other, swallowed by the roar and returned by the masonry as several delayed echoes. The builders have left no attempt at acoustic softness. Sound ricochets from tile to tile. Water striking water, wood striking stone, the creak of rope, and the occasional deep knock of a gate closing all become part of one continuous mechanical weather.

There is very little vegetation. A few rows of planted reeds stand in shallow ceramic troughs along the upper ledges, their green leaves sparse and yellow-tipped. They are not wild; each has been tied into place with cord. Their roots are packed into woven baskets filled with dark compost brought from above. Elsewhere, thin mats of green algae cling to the warmest bricks, but even these are scraped back regularly. The builders want the water clean and the mechanisms visible. Growth is allowed only where it serves a purpose: reeds filtering the channels, moss sealing a crack, a little floating greenery marking the direction of a current.

There are no fish, insects, birds, or hidden animals. Nothing stirs except water, plants, flame, and the mechanisms. The absence is not uncanny. The reservoir is simply too new, too controlled, too warm, and too frequently disturbed to support animal life. Its emptiness has the practical quality of a workshop floor after sweeping.

Tools lie on a raised platform: wooden mallets, stone chisels, clay jars, coils of fiber rope, wedges, scrapers, and a few simple metal hooks. The technology is early and labor-intensive. Every gate must be hauled open. Every blockage must be cleared by hand. The waterworks function because someone is always tending them. A person in a rough reed-woven harness moves along the far wall, chest-deep in the current, guiding a floating frame toward a blocked grate. Their presence makes the whole chamber feel more active, not less inhabited: this is not an abandoned underground pool, but a working piece of infrastructure.

The rules of the place are plain. Water descends through the western shafts, gathers in the central basin, and is forced upward through the eastern machinery. The gates cannot be opened together without flooding the lower stairs. The planted troughs must remain submerged to a particular depth. The lamps must be kept above the spray. The plastered marks on the pillars record the safe level, the dangerous level, and the level at which the entire chamber becomes a pressure vessel.

Nothing supernatural governs the reservoir. It is only geometry, gravity, heat, labor, and the persistent brightness of water.

Yet beneath the surface, with the warm current pressing against your clothing and the constructed floor rising in clean, deliberate steps beneath you, the place feels like a hidden machine large enough to enter but too small to escape into. Its world ends at its walls. Beyond them are soil, foundations, and the buried weight of the settlement above. Inside, there is only bright water, fresh masonry, scattered reeds, loud machinery, and a few people keeping the young reservoir alive.
