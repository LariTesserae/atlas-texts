The place is hidden inside a single root.

From outside, the root is no larger than a clenched hand: a dark, dried knot half-buried in a plain of gray mineral dust. It has no visible opening. Its surface is furrowed like old bark, but the furrows are not cracks. They are streets, gutters, and stairways worn into the root over an age so long that the original distinction between growth and construction has disappeared.

Inside, the root contains a city.

It is a low city, spread across a broad, nearly level hollow. There are no towers. The highest structures rise only to the height of a finger, and even these have been pressed flat by centuries of slow internal settling. Streets run in straight, deliberate channels between the ribs of the root. Houses stand as rows of pale, woody chambers, each one made from braided stems that have thickened into walls. Doorways are narrow black slits. Bridges cross empty spaces where the plant’s tissues have withdrawn, leaving deep wells and galleries beneath the streets.

Nothing here is wet. There is no sap, rain, condensation, or hidden pool. The plant has long ago abandoned water as a substance. Its living matter is dry, fibrous, and mineral-rich, taking nourishment from the faint chemical differences between the root’s inner layers and the surrounding stone. It grows by exchanging dust, heat, and pressure. When it needs to repair itself, it does not swell or soften; it sends a slow grain of new wood through old wood, like a sentence being written inside a fossil.

The city is all plant.

The roads are flattened root fibers. The walls are woody membranes. The paving stones are seed-cases, cracked open and fitted together by hand—or by something that once had hands. Towers that look at first like masonry are actually bundled stalks, their joints sealed with resin gone cold and black. Along the walls hang curtains of dead leaves, stiff as slate. They do not move in any breeze, because there is no breeze. They mark thresholds, districts, and perhaps the names of places that no longer exist.

The city is almost empty.

Its avenues are wide enough for processions, but only a few scattered forms occupy them: columns of pale grass standing far apart, solitary and perfectly still. Between them lies a tremendous amount of darkness. Courtyards contain one bench, one arch, one dry basin, and nothing else. Entire neighborhoods are separated by flat stretches of bare root where every structure has eroded away. The emptiness is not abandonment in the ordinary sense. It is the mature condition of the place. The city has been reduced to its necessary shapes, and even those necessities are now difficult to identify.

There are no animals. No insects tremble in the dry leaves. No worms inhabit the soft layers below the streets. No birds, no burrowing things, no scavengers. The plants have no need of them. Pollination is performed by pressure waves. Seeds travel through the root’s hollows as grains of dust. Reproduction is slow and architectural: a house may produce a new shoot after a thousand years, and the shoot will emerge already shaped like a wall.

The darkness is absolute.

There is no moonlight, firelight, glow, or bioluminescence. The structures cannot be seen so much as inferred by the hand, the foot, and the returning echo. A wall reveals itself when the air stops. A stair reveals itself by the change in pressure under the sole. A doorway is known by the way sound passes through it. Every surface is black, but blackness is not a color here. It is the absence of any distinction between near and far.

Yet the city is loud.

The noise comes from the plants.

Far below the streets, ancient fibers contract and release in slow, coordinated pulses. Their movement sends a deep vibration through the root, a continuous subterranean thunder that is felt in the teeth before it is heard. Hollow stalks act as pipes. Narrow alleys amplify the pulses into a booming, irregular chorus. Some chambers produce sharp knocks as hardened seed-casings strike one another. Others emit long, grinding tones, the sound of tissues shifting against tissues by less than the width of a hair.

The loudest district is an open square called, perhaps, the Bell Garden. It contains no bells and no plants that resemble them. It is simply a basin lined with thousands of hollow stems. When the root adjusts to the slow pressure of the surrounding earth, the stems resonate together. The result is immense: a dry metallic roar that fills the entire pocket, rises, fades, and returns several minutes later. The sound is not music, though it has intervals. It is not speech, though it has repetition. It is the city announcing that it is still alive.

The vibration changes only gradually. A pulse may last for an hour. A rhythm may persist for centuries. The oldest sounds are almost motionless, so low and prolonged that they seem like a property of the darkness itself. In some districts, the streets hum continuously beneath the feet. In others, one waits for a sound and grows old before it arrives.

The air is cool and dry, with the mineral smell of powdered bark and stone. It has very little circulation. Long-standing pockets of air remain trapped beneath ceilings of woven root, each pocket with its own faint pressure and chemistry. In the deepest chambers, the air is so still that a person could mistake it for solid space. The plant does not breathe in the familiar sense. It exchanges gases through microscopic seams and abandoned growth rings, opening and closing them across spans of years.

Everything has been eroded, but not by water.

Time has worn the city down through pressure, vibration, chemical leaching, and the persistent abrasion of its own growth. Edges have become rounded. Carvings have sunk into walls until they are only shallow bands beneath the fingertips. Roofs have sagged and fused with neighboring roofs. Stairs have become ramps. Whole buildings have flattened into low ridges, leaving behind the faint geometry of rooms.

The erosion is ancient enough to feel finished. Nothing is falling. Nothing is collapsing. The city has already passed through collapse and reached a final, stable form. Its ruins are not ruins in the human sense; they are the mature anatomy of an old plant, every break absorbed, every wound made into structure.

The primitive tools remain in a few sealed chambers. They are made from thorn, root, seed-shell, and flakes of hard mineral. There are wedges, scraping blades, pegs, and long hooked implements, all stored in orderly racks. Their makers used no metal. They shaped the city by cutting living fibers and persuading them to grow around the cuts. A wall was not built so much as trained. A street was not excavated so much as denied to the root until the surrounding tissues hardened.

The governing rule of the place is that nothing may change quickly.

A sudden flame would go out for lack of suitable air. A thrown stone would travel through a street for a long time, striking walls and returning in diminishing echoes. A cut plant surface would seal over before blood—or sap—could appear. Growth cannot be forced. Destruction cannot be completed in one act. Every action enters the city as a pressure, and the city answers only after the pressure has become part of its age.

The entire settlement may fit inside the original root, but within it distance behaves strangely. A corridor only a few body-lengths long can return a voice from several directions, making the near feel distant and the distant intimate. The central square seems enormous because its walls vanish into darkness. A room may contain only a single pillar, yet the pillar’s vibration fills volumes beyond the room. The city is small in size and immense in interior consequence.

At its center is the oldest living structure: a low, leafless trunk divided into hundreds of branches, each branch extending into a different district. It is neither monument nor ruler. It is the remaining core of the original plant, sunk into the floor and spread through the foundations. The trunk has no visible movement, but every sound in the city passes through it. When the root pulses, the trunk answers. When a wall shifts, the trunk carries the disturbance outward. It remembers the city not as images or events, but as tension: where something was built, where it failed, where it still stands.

Around it, the empty streets wait in darkness.

The plant-city does not sleep. It does not wake. It persists—cool, dry, crowded with structures and almost devoid of bodies—making its vast, patient noise inside a space small enough to conceal in a hand.
