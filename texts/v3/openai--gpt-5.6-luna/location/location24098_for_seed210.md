The place is a small high basin, no more than a few hundred paces across, held between black, newly broken ridges. It feels less like a valley than a room cut into the mountain: four abrupt walls, a floor of pale stone, and a ceiling of cloud resting so low that the upper cliffs disappear into it. The rocks are young-looking—knife-edged, glassy in places, still carrying the raw faces of something that has only recently risen from the earth. They have not yet learned the softness of weather. No ledge is rounded. No slope has settled.

And yet the basin is wet everywhere.

Water beads on the stone even when no rain is falling. It gathers in clear trembling skins over the black rock, runs uphill for a few inches, then vanishes into hairline cracks. Thin veils descend from the cliffs, not quite waterfalls and not quite mist. They seem to be exhalations of the mountain, condensing directly out of the dim air. The floor is divided by shallow channels filled with moss and silver mud. Nothing here is deep enough to drown in, but everything is saturated: roots, stones, feathers, the dark undersides of leaves.

The vegetation is lush without being crowded. Ferns grow in isolated green fans along the channels. Low shrubs with waxy leaves occupy the better pockets of soil, each one surrounded by several yards of bare mineral ground. Tufts of blue-green grass stand in the cracks like small flames. There are pale flowering things, almost translucent, whose blossoms remain closed in the daytime dimness and open whenever a shadow passes over them.

The emptiness between plants is as deliberate as the plants themselves. The basin has the sparse arrangement of a diagram. One tree stands near the center—short, broad, and heavily branched, its bark the color of wet iron. Another grows against the northern wall, though there is no soil beneath it and no visible root system. Its roots simply descend into the cliff and continue through the stone, pale tendrils visible in the rock like veins beneath skin.

The air is cold enough to sharpen the breath but not to numb the fingers. It smells of rain, crushed leaves, and hot metal. This last scent has no obvious source. Occasionally the whole basin fills with the odor of oil and ozone, followed by a deep mechanical vibration that seems to come from below the world.

There are no buildings, roads, fences, tools, or ruins. Nothing has been assembled here by human hands. Nevertheless, industrial power is present in the landscape as unmistakably as weather.

At irregular intervals—though “irregular” is not quite right, because the intervals never change—a seam opens in one of the black ridges. Behind it, far within the stone, immense mechanisms are visible. They are not housed in a factory. They are the factory. Wheels turn in darkness, pistons rise and fall, belts of luminous material pass over toothed drums. Their surfaces are wet with condensation. Some components resemble turbines; others resemble bones, jointed insects, or the folded parts of an enormous machine that has never been fully assembled.

The mechanisms produce a constant, punishing sound: a low engine-thunder layered with metallic clatter, the shriek of pressure escaping, and a rapid ticking like thousands of cooling pipes. The noise should fill the basin, yet it does not travel normally. Near the southern wall it becomes distant and muffled. At the center, it is so loud that the chest vibrates. Beside the tree, it can be heard inside the skull rather than through the ears. The source remains the same, but the sound changes according to where one stands.

Nothing in the basin moves very much.

The waterfalls maintain identical shapes. Drops of water hang beneath leaves without falling. Mist remains fixed in slanting ribbons. A bird may hold its wings halfway through a beat for an hour, while another bird crosses the same span of air again and again, always taking the same path, always arriving at the same branch. Ripples in the pools are permanent, each one preserved as a thin circular ridge.

The animals are numerous but widely spaced. Long-legged, dark-coated creatures graze among the stones, their bodies narrow and elegant, their heads turning in perfect synchrony whenever the buried machinery groans. Small white mammals nest beneath the moss, though they are often seen standing on the surface of the water. Their reflections do not always match them. A foxlike animal with transparent ears watches from the northern wall; its eyes contain slow-moving landscapes, different basins and different skies, none of them reachable.

There are insects everywhere, but they do not swarm. Large green moths rest singly on wet rocks, their wings marked with diagrams of gears. Beetles crawl along the water channels carrying sparks beneath their shells. A congregation of tiny birds occupies the branches of the central tree, each bird facing outward, each one silent despite the constant industrial roar. Their feathers glisten as though lacquered.

Predators and prey share the same spaces without conflict. A spotted cat lies beside a cluster of sleeping rodents. A snake passes through the legs of the grazing animals and is ignored. The animals seem to exist in stable arrangements rather than lives. They do not hunger, flee, mate, age, or decay. One can find a dead creature beneath a fern, still warm and breathing faintly, while its identical living counterpart stands several steps away.

The light is dim and directionless. There is no visible sun, only a pale illumination diffused through the cloud ceiling. Shadows collect beneath leaves and in cracks, but they do not point away from anything. Sometimes a shadow detaches from its object and remains behind on the stone. Sometimes the rocks cast shadows upward onto the mist.

At the western edge of the basin is a narrow pool, perfectly rectangular despite having no constructed sides. Its water is dark enough to seem solid. Looking into it does not reveal a reflection, but a sequence of possible arrangements of the basin: the same tree with no birds, the same ridge unbroken, the same animals lying motionless in different positions. In one version, the pool is full of bright machinery. In another, a human hand rises slowly from beneath the surface and remains there forever.

The boundaries of the place are firm. Walking toward the eastern cliff does not bring one closer to it. The ground extends beneath each step, but the cliff holds its distance, preserving the basin’s proportions. If one climbs the southern wall, one eventually arrives back at the floor, wet, uninjured, and carrying a small quantity of black grit in the mouth. If one shouts, the sound may return before it has been made.

Nothing truly changes here, but nothing is entirely still. The engines continue their work. Water continues to gather. Plants continue to open and close around shadows. Animals continue their suspended movements. The mountain continues to manufacture something deep inside itself—perhaps power, perhaps weather, perhaps the conditions necessary for this place to remain exactly as it is.

The basin is not a ruin, because it has not fallen apart.

It is not a factory, because nothing appears to be produced.

It is a stable error in the world: a cool, wet chamber high above the ordinary ground, lush with unaging life, sparsely arranged beneath a permanent dim sky, where natural rock and industrial machinery perform the same endless operation.
