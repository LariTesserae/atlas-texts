The city occupies a plateau so high that the weather has forgotten the ground below it.

It is called, on the few maps that still name it, the Upper Basin. The basin is not a hollow in the earth but a broad, shallow depression in a continent-sized shelf of newly risen stone. Its rim runs beyond sight in every direction, a broken escarpment under a white sky. Beyond the rim, the land falls away into cloud, though the cloud is rarely seen as a separate thing. It fills the lower world completely: a permanent, luminous depth of vapor, its surface sometimes level with the plateau, sometimes hundreds of meters below, never visibly moving. The city seems to float above it.

The rock is young. It has the raw appearance of something that has not finished becoming stone. Black shelves thrust out of the ground in clean, angular planes. Pale seams of mineral run through them like frozen lightning. Whole districts are built on slabs that still bear the ridges and folds of their emergence, as if the earth had lifted itself in one gesture and left its fingerprints behind. Erosion has begun but has not yet learned patience. Water has cut narrow, exact channels down the slopes, and where those channels meet, they plunge into cracks too dark and deep to measure. There are no rounded hills, no softened boulders, no old ravines. Every edge is sharp enough to seem intentional.

Water is everywhere, but never in abundance.

It lies just below the ground, a cold pressure under the paving stones. It beads on exposed metal. It gathers in shallow trays along the roofs and in the joints between buildings. Rock faces weep continuously, each seep emerging from a different mineral seam: clear water, green water, water that carries a faint silver light in it. Rain falls often, but lightly, as though the sky were sweating rather than breaking. It arrives without clouds. The air simply becomes wet, then the surfaces darken, and then the rain is over.

The city’s streets are long channels of poured concrete and blue-black stone. They descend toward the central works in almost imperceptible gradients, carrying thin films of water beneath grates. Nothing is allowed to pool. The water is collected, filtered, heated, cooled, mineralized, and sent back through the city in a closed circuit. Even the rain is accounted for. Every roof is a machine for catching it. Every wall has gutters. Every square is slightly sloped.

Because of this, the city glistens without looking alive.

Vegetation grows in the margins: dense, low, and dark. It is lush by the standards of the plateau, though there is no forest and no grassland in the familiar sense. Mats of blue-green growth cover the shaded sides of buildings. Long, translucent fronds emerge from drainage channels and lie flat against the paving stones, trembling whenever water passes beneath them. There are black mosses that climb towers in perfectly vertical bands, yellow fans that open only when the air is coldest, and thick cushions of red vegetation growing around vents where warm industrial vapor leaks from below.

None of it is grazed. Nothing eats it. Nothing nests in it or pollinates it. The plants reproduce by splitting, budding, and releasing clouds of dust that drift across the empty avenues. Some have spread over abandoned machinery, filling control panels with soft leaves and sealing doors in green tissue. Others have been cultivated into walls, insulation, filtration beds, and living reservoirs. They do not seem to resent the city. They simply occupy every wet, dim, chemically tolerable surface, growing with the calm persistence of a process that has never encountered opposition.

There are no animals.

No insects hang around the lamps. No birds cross the immense sky. No small bodies disappear through the vegetation. The only movements are water, vapor, plant growth too slow to witness, and the mechanical circulation of the city. In the open spaces, one becomes aware of how much life is absent. A stairwell can descend for hundreds of steps without a single scuff or dropping. A whole avenue may be bordered by flowering plants and remain as silent of animal presence as a laboratory.

The city itself is neither ruined nor flourishing. It is maintained.

Its buildings stand in separated clusters, leaving enormous gaps between them. Towers rise from the plateau like isolated instruments. Some are habitation blocks, though few windows are lit. Some are reservoirs, their sides ribbed with pipes. Some are factories whose purpose is no longer remembered but whose production continues. Between the clusters lie expanses of wet stone, drainage fields, rail lines, and low industrial gardens. The emptiness is not accidental. The city was designed at a scale meant for populations that never arrived, or perhaps for machines that require distance between one operation and the next.

From the highest towers, the settlement appears less like a city than like a diagram laid across the earth: precise blocks separated by blank terrain, all connected by elevated conduits and broad, empty roads. Far beyond the outer districts, other structures can be seen on the plateau—thin pylons, suspended bridges, clusters of domes, chimneys standing alone in the wet. Their distances are deceptive. Some are near enough to reach in an hour. Others take days, though they appear no larger than a hand’s breadth against the horizon.

The scale of the place resists the body.

The plateau extends farther than a person can travel in a lifetime, and yet its weather is uniform. There are no storms at the margins, no visible seasons, no advancing fronts. The cold is constant, not bitter but absolute. Metal retains the temperature of the air. Breath leaves the mouth as a faint gray ribbon and hangs there for a long time before thinning. The sun provides ordinary light: pale, directionless, sufficient to see by, never warm enough to be felt. Shadows exist but are shallow and weak, as though light comes from everywhere at once.

At night the city does not become dark. The sky dims to a deep slate color, and the industrial towers begin to show their internal illumination. Windows shine in long vertical arrangements. Pipes glow from within. Reservoirs pulse with slow blue indicators. The living walls give off a greenish phosphorescence, enough to outline a street or a stairwell. Beneath the plateau, somewhere below the cloud layer, a vast red brightness sometimes stains the underside of the vapor. It may be reflected heat. It may be another city. No one has found an edge to the world from which it comes.

Sound is the city’s most powerful presence.

The factories produce a continuous mechanical thunder, but not a chaotic one. There are no sudden alarms, no interruptions, no changes in pitch. The sound is a layered, unvarying chord: turbines turning at the same speed, pumps forcing water through buried arteries, compressors drawing the wet air into towers, distant presses striking with metronomic force. Beneath it is the deep vibration of the plateau itself. It passes through the soles of the feet and rises into the bones.

At the edges of the city, the noise becomes stranger. The open drainage channels roar despite carrying only a thin stream. Empty pipes hum as though full of wind. Bridges sing under no load. In certain streets, the walls produce a low, sustained note that changes when someone walks past, then returns to its former pitch when they are gone. The sound never echoes correctly. A shout may travel for kilometers without weakening, while a nearby footstep vanishes immediately.

The people—if they should be called people—do not speak much. They move between the buildings in insulated coats and sealed hoods, following marked routes. Some operate the factories. Some tend the filtration gardens. Some inspect the rock faces and repaint warning lines where the plateau has opened a new fissure. Their settlement is organized, but not crowded. There are whole residential towers with only one occupied floor. Lights remain on in rooms no one enters.

The rules of the world are visible in the infrastructure.

Water must circulate.

Machines must continue.

Nothing may cross the outer rim without authorization, though there is no visible barrier and no authority anyone can name.

Plants may grow wherever they do not obstruct a channel.

No structure may be repaired by changing its shape. Broken components are replaced with identical components, even when the original design is obsolete. The city must remain itself.

Most importantly, conditions do not change.

The rain falls at the same rate. The factories maintain the same rhythm. The cloud layer remains at the same height. The plants occupy the same boundaries, though they are technically growing. Cracks appear in the rock but do not widen. Buildings decay but never collapse. A door left open remains open; a light left burning remains lit. If a tool is dropped in a street, it will be found years later exactly where it fell, beaded with the same fine moisture.

The inhabitants have measured this stability and built their lives around it. They call it equilibrium, though the word is more devotional than scientific. The city is not safe because it is strong. It is safe because the world has ceased to apply pressure.

At the center of the plateau stands a circular structure several kilometers wide. Its roof is absent, exposing a black basin filled with shallow water. Around the basin are thousands of pylons, each crowned with a ring of pale metal. They hum in perfect unison. Water rises from the basin in thin columns and hangs there, suspended, forming a forest of vertical streams. The columns do not fall. They do not freeze. They remain motionless in the windless air, catching the ordinary light and breaking it into dull colors.

No one is permitted to enter the basin.

From a distance, the central works resembles an eye, though it does not look at anything. It may be the source of the city’s stability. It may only be the oldest and loudest of its machines. The workers maintain it according to instructions engraved on plates no one can translate, and the instructions have never needed revision.

Beyond the central works, beyond the last towers and the cultivated drainage beds, the plateau continues in its enormous silence beneath the industrial sound. Wet plants spread through the young stone. Water moves below the surface. The cloud sea presses against the cliffs. The city glows, turns, pumps, filters, and waits.

Nothing approaches.

Nothing departs.

The landscape has the breadth of a world and the stillness of a sealed room.
