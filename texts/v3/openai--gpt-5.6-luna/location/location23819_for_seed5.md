The place is hidden inside a low, dry country that gives no sign of it.

From above, there is only a shallow basin of pale stone and hard-packed earth, a depression gradually filled with dust, thorn, and the brittle grasses of a cold plain. Nothing marks the entrance except a seam in the ground, narrow as a roof crack. At certain hours, when the angle of the sun is exact, the seam exhales a thread of white vapor. The vapor smells of wet limestone and green things.

The opening leads down only a little. This is not a deep underworld, but a pocket folded into the earth: a broad hollow beneath a thin roof of rock, perhaps no larger than a village square, though its inner walls rise and bend in such a way that the eye cannot take in the whole of it at once. The settlement inside has grown to occupy every available surface. Houses cling to walls. Walkways cross one another at shoulder height. Stairways descend into stairways. Ladders are fixed wherever a ledge offers room for a foot. There are no vacant lots, no yards, no clear ground except for a narrow central passage worn smooth by generations of bare feet.

The city is made almost entirely of things taken from the hollow itself.

Its oldest walls are limestone, gray-white and softly rounded by immense age. Their surfaces are polished where hands have passed, rough where no one needs to touch them. Thin slabs of shale form roofs and shelves. Clay, gathered from the few wet pockets in the lower floor, has been pressed into bricks, bowls, flues, and the small square faces of household shrines. Reedlike plants are woven into mats, screens, rope, and the bulging walls of upper rooms. Bone is used for pins, needles, hooks, hinges, and combs. Flint blades are mounted in wood or antler. There is little metal, and what exists is old, precious, and kept behind clay doors.

The construction is primitive only in its tools. The settlement itself is intricate.

Every wall bears the additions of many lifetimes. A doorway has been narrowed by later masonry, then widened again around a new lintel. A house rests on an older house, whose foundation is itself a sealed cistern. One stairway passes through a room that once belonged to another family, and no one remembers whether the room was abandoned or simply absorbed. The people have built upward until the roof is almost lost beneath platforms, awnings, drying racks, hanging gardens, and dark openings. The city seems less constructed than compacted, as though habitation has become a kind of geological pressure.

Yet the place is bright.

Light enters through the seam above and through several other hairline cracks hidden in the ceiling. It does not arrive in distinct beams. The whole roof glows with a cold, mineral brightness, as though daylight has soaked through the stone and forgotten how to cast shadows. Pale crystals along the walls gather the illumination and release it evenly. The brightest surfaces are the wet ones: beads of condensation, moss leaves, the slick backs of snails, the thin films of water in the channels. In this light, every color is clear but restrained—blue-gray stone, deep green growth, the red-brown of clay, the cream of bone.

The cold is constant. It is not a biting wind or the shock of winter, but the temperature of a shaded cellar that has never known warmth. Breath is visible in the mornings, though the people do not divide the day into warm and cold hours. They know time by the movement of the roof-light and by the opening and closing of certain flowers.

Water comes irregularly and is treated as a visitor rather than a possession.

After rain above, moisture filters through the ceiling in thin, widely separated threads. Some of it arrives as a faint mist. Some gathers over many days in rounded drops beneath the rock, then falls without warning into waiting basins. There are shallow pools in the lower stone, but none is permanent enough to be called a lake. Channels have been carved beneath the streets to catch seepage. Clay jars stand everywhere: on lintels, in niches, beneath beds, beside ovens, under the roots of communal trees. The people have arranged their whole city around the possibility of water, not its certainty.

The vegetation is lush because it has nowhere else to go.

Moss covers nearly every unoccupied patch of stone, thick and velvet-dark in the shaded places, silver-green near the roof cracks. Ferns unfold from wall joints. Pale vines travel along the undersides of bridges and send roots down through cracks in the masonry. Small trees, stunted but ancient, grow from raised beds where soil has been carried up from outside in baskets. Their trunks are twisted around posts and houses, their roots threaded through foundations. They bear hard, translucent fruits that contain very little sweetness but a great deal of stored moisture.

The gardens are packed so closely together that the city is also a forest. Leaves brush against ceilings. Tendrils hang across doorways. Some rooms have been surrendered entirely to root systems, and the houses around them have simply been built higher. There are no broad fields. Food comes from shelves of mushrooms, hanging beds of greens, insect colonies, fruiting moss, and the small plots of dark soil kept beneath the brightest cracks. Every surface is made productive if it can be made damp.

The animals are small, numerous, and almost noiseless.

White cave-mice move along the rafters, their feet soft against the woven mats. Long-legged insects live in the drainage channels and emerge at night—though “night” is only the dimming of the roof-light. Snails graze the walls in slow, shining clusters. Tiny frogs inhabit the cisterns, their throats pulsing without song. There are blind fish in two deep stone basins, descended from creatures that entered during some forgotten flood. Their keepers touch the water to find them.

Larger animals are rare but not absent. A few shaggy goats are kept in the uppermost galleries, where they nibble lichen and the tough leaves of the wall gardens. Their horns have been worn smooth by passing beneath beams. A colony of black, broad-winged birds nests in the ceiling cracks and feeds on insects. They fly in silence. When one passes overhead, it is visible only as a brief interruption in the brightness.

The settlement itself is quiet in the way a sleeping body is quiet.

There is no market cry, no hammering, no animal call that carries far. Work is done with soft tools: stone against stone, bone scraping hide, fingers tightening wet fiber. Conversations remain close to the speaker. People use signs across the narrower bridges and tap coded patterns against walls when they need to call someone through several rooms. Even children learn early not to shout. The hollow seems to absorb sound, or perhaps the inhabitants have shaped their walls to do so. Thick moss, hanging cloth, and layered clay fill every echoing angle.

At the center of the city is a long, shallow channel called the Listening Way. It is the only place left unbuilt, though even here stones have been set into the floor, and plants grow from the gaps between them. People cross it slowly. They say that when water is coming, the channel knows first. They kneel and place an ear to the stone, waiting for a sound too low to be called sound: a pressure, a tremor, the memory of running water far above.

Nothing in the settlement is truly new. Repairs are made, but they resemble the thing repaired. A broken stair is replaced with a stair. A dead tree is left standing and built around. A collapsed room becomes a wall, then a shelf, then a foundation. The city does not expand so much as settle more deeply into itself. Its boundaries are fixed by the shape of the hollow, and its people have accepted this with the calm of roots accepting stone.

There are rules here, though not all of them are spoken.

No one blocks a crack through which light enters. No roof channel may be narrowed. Water must be stored in three places: near the place it is found, near the place it is used, and in a vessel belonging to no household. Nothing that grows from a wall may be cut without leaving a living shoot. The oldest stairs must be crossed barefoot. At the first sign of seepage, all work stops, and every person carries a vessel.

The most important rule concerns emptiness.

A space may be left unoccupied only if it is necessary for water, roots, passage, or silence. Otherwise it will be filled. A niche becomes a cupboard. A cupboard becomes a sleeping place. A sleeping place becomes a shrine, and the shrine is built around. The inhabitants believe that an empty space invites the roof to descend.

Whether this is true is difficult to say. The roof has been descending for a very long time, but so slowly that descent and permanence are indistinguishable. The settlement remains bright beneath it, green in its cracks, damp in its basins, and densely inhabited down to the last ledge. Above, the exposed world dries and changes with the weather. Inside, the hidden city persists in a cold equilibrium, holding its breath beneath the stone.
