The settlement occupies a shallow bowl of black stone where the ground has recently lifted out of the water.

It is not a town spread across a landscape so much as a town packed into one. The bowl is perhaps the size of a monastery courtyard, though nothing in it is arranged with such generosity. Houses, retaining walls, stairways, cisterns, sheds, shrines, drying racks, and narrow covered passages have been built against one another until almost no earth remains visible. Their walls are made from the place’s fresh stone: dark basalt, blue-gray shale, and pale mineral crusts that flake away under the fingers. The blocks are irregular and sharp-edged, quarried from the surrounding rise where the ground broke open. Many still bear the clean, angular faces of their birth.

Water is everywhere, but rarely in a broad or graceful form.

It gathers in the seams between paving stones. It runs down the walls in thin, persistent threads. It collects beneath the houses and breathes up through floor grates as cold vapor. The streets are shallow channels, deliberately sloped toward common drains, though the channels are often too full and spill over their rims. Every roof has a lip, every doorway a raised threshold. Ropes of moss-darkened fiber stretch above the alleys, carrying buckets and baskets over the heads of passersby. The inhabitants have learned to build upward without making anything tall: floors are stacked half a level above one another, bridges join neighboring roofs, and ladders disappear into covered openings.

Rain has recently passed, or is still passing somewhere beyond sight. The air remains saturated. It beads on skin and stone alike. Farther walls dissolve into a bluish veil, and the upper edges of the settlement seem less like architecture than a mass of wet slate. A cool wind moves through the bowl, but it cannot clear the damp. It only shifts it, pressing curtains of mist into one lane and drawing them out of another.

There are few plants. They survive in the places people cannot seal: a fan of wiry grass in a wall crack, pale stalks clustered around a drain, low black-green mats clinging to the shaded stone. Along the lowest edge of the bowl, where runoff has deposited a skin of silt, grow blunt reeds no higher than a person’s knee. Their leaves are waxy and nearly colorless, and they tremble constantly although the air there is still. Small gardens have been made in broken jars and roof boxes, but most contain only hardy roots, bitter herbs, and a few gray leaves. Vegetation has not yet claimed the exposed slopes. The rock is too new, too raw, and the water keeps cutting it back.

The bowl itself seems to be changing by the hour.

At its northern end, a fresh escarpment rises only a few dozen paces above the roofs. It has the appearance of a wound: a sheer face of wet stone, its ledges still unweathered, its fractures bright and clean. Water emerges from it in several places, not as springs with clear origins but as slow seepage through the whole wall. Each trickle carries a fine white sediment that gathers below in soft fans. Now and then a piece of the cliff lets go. It falls without drama, striking the lower shelves and splitting into angular fragments. The people pause when this happens, listening to the final small clicks as the stones settle among the houses.

No one builds directly against the cliff anymore.

The oldest structures stand there, however, darkened by constant wetness. They are partly buried, partly absorbed into the rock. Their lower rooms have become reservoirs, workshops, or places of worship. A narrow stair descends beneath the settlement to the water table, where the air is colder and the walls shine with condensation. The stair is always wet. At the bottom, water fills a long stone trough, perfectly still except when something moves below its surface.

The town’s technology is practical and close to the hand. There are no high towers, wheels, or polished metals. Wooden braces have been pegged into stone with bone, horn, and simple iron. Doors are thick planks swollen by damp. Roofs are layered with bark, woven rush, and slabs of shale held down by stones. Fires burn in clay-lined recesses, producing more smoke than heat. Their smoke drifts low beneath the eaves and mingles with the mist, giving the alleys a taste of peat and wet ash.

Yet the construction is dense and deliberate. The people know where every channel leads. They have carved measuring marks beside the drains to record rising water. They have placed small stone gates at the ends of streets, allowing a whole lane to be flooded or emptied. Wooden walkways can be lifted away. Ladders are hung on hooks above the highest expected waterline. At night, bundles of reeds are packed into cracks beneath doors, not to keep out cold, but to keep out the pale things that sometimes enter with the runoff.

Those things are rare enough to remain unnamed in ordinary speech.

Most of the animals here are small and easily overlooked: blind insects living in the drainwork, gray water-beetles, thin cave fish caught in the lower cisterns, and a few mud-colored birds that nest beneath the roofs. Their calls are infrequent. A bird may give one hollow note at dawn and then vanish for days. In the flooded foundations, something occasionally scrapes along the stone with a patient, dragging sound. No one claims to have seen it clearly. The settlement’s cats are absent; they do not tolerate the water, or perhaps they know something about it.

The larger animals have gone elsewhere, or have not yet found this new ground.

This is a quiet place despite its crowding. There is no open space in which sound can travel cleanly. Voices are muffled by walls and awnings, softened by rainwater and moss. Conversations pass through adjoining rooms, down stairwells, and across roofs in fragments. A mallet strikes stone somewhere below. A bucket knocks against a cistern. Water ticks from a gutter into a basin with the steady patience of a clock. When the wind enters the bowl, every loose rope and hanging reed answers, but the result is a low rustling rather than a roar.

At intervals, the whole settlement makes a subtle adjustment.

A sluice is opened. A roof is cleared. A wall is propped. Someone carries a basket of fresh stone from the latest collapse. Children kneel at a drain and push aside the pale worms that have gathered there. Women scrape mineral bloom from door hinges. Men and women together inspect the cliff, the roofs, the water marks, and the cracks underfoot. The place is not stable enough to be inhabited passively. It must be watched.

There are rules here, understood as physical facts rather than laws.

Water remembers every path it has taken and will return to it if blocked. Fresh stone must not be trusted until it has endured one full season of rain. No fire may be lit in a sealed room, because the mist carries flame strangely and may draw it toward the walls. A person who hears dripping beneath a dry floor must leave the building before dusk. White deposits around a drain mean that the water below is awake, and should not be disturbed.

The inhabitants do not say whether these rules are superstition. They follow them because the settlement was made from a landscape still in the act of becoming.

Here, the earth is young but not fertile. It has risen sharply, broken itself open, and received a town before it had time to soften. Stone is still deciding where to crack. Water is still finding its permanent levels. The people have built tightly because the habitable ground is so small, and because beyond the bowl the slopes fall into wet gray emptiness: a low basin of mud, exposed shelves, and shallow standing water under the cool sky.

At the center of the settlement is a square of uncovered stone no larger than a room. Rain falls there freely. The paving is new, black, and perfectly joined, except for a narrow fissure running from one side to the other. Water shines inside it.

Every morning the fissure is a little wider.

Every morning someone has laid a fresh strip of wood across it, and the town goes on building around the gap.
