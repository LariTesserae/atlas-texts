The place is a shallow basin no larger than a village green, though nothing human has ever made a village there. Its boundaries are clear: a ring of low, pale hills enclosing a floor of black water, reed beds, and old stone. The hills have been worn nearly smooth by ages of rain. They are not cliffs or mountains but the last, softened shapes of something once grand, their surfaces fluted with shallow channels where water still travels after the clouds have gone.

The stone is a chalky mineral veined with green and blue. In places it has become translucent, like thick ice left in the sun. Water moves inside it. Fine currents rise through the rock and seep out from the hillsides in hundreds of patient beads. The beads gather into threads, the threads into narrow runnels, and the runnels lose themselves among mats of moss and low, broad-leafed plants. Nothing here is dry for long. Even under the brightest sky, the ground gives slightly beneath the foot, and every stone wears a dark rim of moisture.

At the center lies a pond, perhaps fifty paces across. It is too shallow to be called deep water, yet its surface is dark enough to suggest distance. Pale stems rise through it in scattered stands. Their leaves are round, waxy, and silver underneath, turning whenever the air shifts. The water does not ripple as ordinary water does. A disturbance travels outward in a perfect circle, pauses, and then returns from the far shore, as if the pond has remembered the shape of what touched it.

The air is warm, heavy, and startlingly clear. Sunlight falls almost vertically, bright enough to whiten the stones and make every wet leaf shine. There are no broad clouds, only slow formations of vapor gathering over the pond and dissolving above the reeds. The basin seems to breathe. At intervals, a low mist pours from cracks in the surrounding hills—not rising, exactly, but unrolling along the ground in transparent folds. It carries the smell of rain on mineral dust, green sap, and something faintly metallic.

Vegetation is lush only where the water permits it. Between the wet channels are long expanses of exposed stone and thin, rusty soil. The growth is consequently scattered: cushions of moss under overhangs, waist-high reeds around the pond, small trees at the basin’s northern edge. These trees have pale trunks and sparse crowns, each leaf separated from the next by a surprising amount of sky. Their roots are visible, braided over the stone like old fingers. Some roots descend into the pond and continue growing beneath the surface, luminous at their tips.

The abundance is not in the plants.

It is in the animals.

They occupy the basin in such numbers that the place seems empty only because they have learned to leave space between themselves. Hundreds of narrow-bodied, translucent creatures rest along the wet stones, so still that they resemble shed skins. When the sun reaches them, they rise in a coordinated drift, wingless bodies floating a handspan above the ground. They pass silently through one another, changing color as they move—from amber to green to the blue of deep water—and settle again in new locations.

In the reeds, small black birds stand motionless on single stems. Their heads turn independently, each eye following a different motion. There may be fifty of them, or five hundred; distance behaves poorly here. When one bird lifts its wings, all the others become briefly visible, not as bodies but as gaps in the brightness, like pieces of sunlight cut out and carried across the marsh.

Long-legged animals graze in the open spaces. They are low to the ground, six-jointed, and covered in a fine coat that reflects the basin rather than the sky. Looking at one is like looking at a moving patch of wet stone. They feed on the thin mats of red growth between the channels. If startled, they do not run. They fold inward, becoming compact geometric shapes, and remain that way until the danger has passed.

There are predators too, but predation is nearly silent. A pale ribbon rises from the pond, loops around a reed, and lowers itself into the water with scarcely a sound. A cluster of floating motes gathers over one of the translucent insects, and the insect simply ceases to be. In the trees, something with too many elbows watches from the shade. Its body is hidden, though its attention is unmistakable. The entire ecosystem is active, crowded with feeding, mating, hatching, shedding, and pursuit, but it conducts itself with an almost ceremonial quiet.

The few sounds that remain are precise. Water ticking from stone. The soft knock of a seed against a hollow stem. A faint glassy vibration from beneath the pond. Sometimes the hills produce a long, descending tone, as though a great instrument is being played far below the earth. It lasts several minutes and can be felt more clearly in the ribs than heard with the ears. During these tones, every animal in the basin becomes still.

There are scattered remnants of construction, though their purpose is not immediately legible. Near the southern shore stands a narrow frame of dark material, no taller than a person. It has no walls and no visible joints. Droplets of water strike its upper edge and vanish before falling, converted into brief points of white light. The frame records the weather in some manner, or perhaps negotiates with it. Its inner space is always a little warmer than the surrounding air, and small animals sleep there during the midday brightness.

A second artifact lies half-buried beneath the northern trees: a curved plate of black glass, cracked but not broken. Its surface shows no reflection. Instead, it displays a slow map of the basin—not an image, but a living diagram of moisture, root systems, animal movement, heat, and the ancient pressure lines beneath the hills. The map is too large in time as well as space. It shows the basin as it was when the hills were higher, and as it may be after the pond has become a meadow. The present is only one glowing layer among thousands.

Nothing seems to maintain these devices. They do not rust, gather dirt, or fall into disrepair. Moss grows up to their edges and stops. Animals crawl across them without activating anything visible. They may be machines, or fossils of machines, or structures whose operation has become indistinguishable from the local geology.

The age of the landscape is most apparent in its lack of drama. The hills have already collapsed into their final low forms. The pond has found its level. The watercourses have worn their grooves so deeply into the mineral that they no longer need banks. Even the trees seem less like young growth than the latest expression of a process that began before the basin had a name.

Yet the place is not dead or finished. It is continuously, densely alive. New bodies emerge from the mud in translucent shells. Roots alter the water by fractions of a fraction. Predators move through the reeds. Warm vapor gathers and disappears. The old machines calculate, preserve, or dream. Every process is underway, but none is hurried.

At the basin’s edge, beyond the last wet stones, the hills rise only a little before flattening into a vast plain. From there the enclosed place looks modest: a bright patch of water, a few pale trees, some dark lines in the stone. But within its ring of worn hills, the world has concentrated itself. The smallness is not an absence of scale. It is compression—a whole ancient ecology held in a space one could cross in minutes, though no one crossing it would ever experience the same basin twice.
