The city stands on a high mineral plateau beneath a sky with no visible sun.

It is not night, exactly. Darkness has settled over the place as a permanent condition, thick and even from horizon to horizon, without stars, moon, cloud, or reflected glow. The air is warm and dry. It carries no scent of rain because rain has never fallen here—or fell so long ago that the idea has become irrelevant to the stone.

The plateau is broad enough to contain a city and a surrounding country of broken ridges. Beyond the outer walls, the land descends in shallow, ancient folds toward a basin filled with pale dust. The folds are not hills in the living sense. They are the worn-down remains of something once larger: mountain chains, perhaps, or the petrified surfaces of vast pressure waves. Their edges have been softened by ages of abrasion until the whole region seems to have been filed into its final shape.

Underfoot, the ground is dark red and gray, packed hard as fired clay. In places it shows through as plates of black glass, fractured into broad polygons. Between them lie fields of granular salt and metal-rich powder. The powder does not blow. There is wind, but only in the most technical sense: a slow, warm circulation that presses against the skin and drags a faint veil of dust along the ground. It never gathers into dunes. It never clears the air. Everything remains where it has settled.

The city is built in wide terraces cut into the plateau. Its streets are too broad for the sparse traffic that uses them, laid out with the severe geometry of a place designed all at once. Buildings stand far apart, each surrounded by hard-packed yards and service trenches. They are constructed from poured mineral composite, iron-black stone, and sheets of dull ceramic. Their surfaces have been scoured smooth by time, though no one can say by what weather. Corners are rounded. Towers taper upward like eroded teeth.

There are no windows in the ordinary sense. Some walls contain narrow horizontal slits, but they reveal nothing; behind them are only layers of glass, mesh, and darkness. Other buildings have entire facades of opaque panels that occasionally warm from within, glowing a deep, subdued amber. The light is so weak it seems less an illumination than a memory of illumination. It touches the nearby pavement and fades before reaching the next structure.

Between the buildings run pipes, rails, and elevated conduits. Most are larger than necessary. They cross one another in long, rigid spans, joining substations and processing halls whose machinery continues to operate without attendants. A line of pressure tanks stands beside a silent avenue. Their gauges are fixed at identical readings. Tall stacks release no smoke, but their mouths exhale a continuous heat that makes the darkness above them tremble.

The factories are not abandoned. They are simply unattended.

Behind sealed doors, belts move at a measured pace. Robotic arms repeat their old gestures, assembling components from cast metal and ceramic, stacking them in orderly rows, then beginning again. Some machines have been working so long that their housings are worn thin and polished by vibration. Others have ceased in the middle of a task and remain frozen there, their tools still touching the objects they were meant to alter.

There are no alarms. No shift changes. No voices giving instructions. Every system seems to know its own continuation, and nothing in the city appears capable of deciding that continuation should end.

The main avenues are empty except for occasional vehicles: low, six-wheeled carriers with enclosed cabins and no visible drivers. They move at a constant speed, never accelerating or braking. Their tires—or their equivalent—make almost no sound against the mineral road. When one passes, it emits a brief pulse of blue light that travels along its sides and disappears. The vehicle continues on, following routes that may have been established before the city was built.

Sound is scarce. The dominant note is a deep vibration beneath the ground, so low it is often felt more than heard. It comes from the industrial quarter, from turbines or pumps or the turning of enormous buried mechanisms. Over it, now and then, comes the dry click of a relay, the distant scrape of a suspended rail, the soft metallic knocking of something cooling inside a wall.

There are no birds. No insects gather around the weak lights. No weeds split the pavement. The vacant plots between buildings hold only gravel, slag, and the occasional growth of mineral spines—thin, translucent structures that rise from cracks in clustered fans. They are not plants. They have no leaves, sap, or recognizable life. Some are merely deposits forced upward by pressure below. Others seem to alter their angle when machinery starts, leaning toward the vibration. They never grow large, and no one has seen one appear.

Rare animals cross the outskirts at long intervals. They are pale, jointed things, low to the ground, with broad feet adapted to the sharp dust. Their bodies seem assembled from separate plates, as if armored against a world that has forgotten softness. They have no visible eyes. They follow the buried conduits and vanish into maintenance shafts. Once, one may enter a lit corridor and stand motionless before a closed door for several hours. The door does not open. The creature eventually turns away.

Nothing ages quickly here. Rust forms in thin, even skins but does not spread. Stone cracks without collapsing. Loose cables lie where they fell, half-buried in dust, while the systems they once served continue to draw power. The city is not preserved so much as held in place. Damage occurs, but decay has no momentum.

At the highest point of the plateau stands a central structure, larger than the factories and older than the streets. It is a circular tower sunk into the ground rather than raised from it. Only its upper rim is visible, a ring of black stone surrounding a perfectly flat interior filled with darkness deeper than the surrounding dark.

No road leads directly to it. The avenues bend away. Conduits approach and terminate at its foundations, but none enter visibly. Around the rim are markings worn nearly smooth: repeated geometric forms, each interrupted at the same point, as if a single instruction had been copied for centuries and never completed.

The tower gives off the low vibration beneath the city.

Sometimes, deep below the plateau, something shifts—not enough to change the terrain, not enough to disturb the machinery. The sound passes through the foundations and the empty streets. For a moment, every amber panel in the city brightens by the same imperceptible degree.

Then the place returns to its equilibrium.

The factories continue. The vehicles follow their routes. The mineral spines remain angled toward the earth. Warm air moves over the ancient plateau, carrying nothing with it.
