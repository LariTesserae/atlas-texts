The place is a shallow limestone hollow, no larger than a village square, sunk into otherwise level ground. Its edges rise only a little—low shelves and rounded banks of pale stone—but the hollow feels enclosed because every surface is close and occupied. There is no open distance inside it. Water lies in all the seams.

The ground is a mixture of old limestone, gray clay, and dark mineral gravel. Rainwater and springwater have worked through it for a very long time, smoothing the stone without carrying it away. The established forms are blunt and settled: low ledges, shallow basins, worn steps of rock, and narrow channels that seem to have been there for centuries. Some surfaces are yellow with sulfurous deposits; others are black and polished where warm water has passed over them. The hollow has no loose wilderness to it. Every depression is filled with silt, every crack is damp, every flat place occupied by something—water, stone, crust, or the remains of human use.

The water is not deep. It gathers in linked pools, usually no higher than the ankle, though a few dark pockets sink beyond sight beneath overhanging shelves. It does not run so much as collect and slowly exchange itself. A thin skin of condensation rests on the pools in the early hours, and drops form continually on the undersides of the stone. Water beads on the walls even when there has been no rain. The air is warm and wet, heavy enough to make clothing cling to the body. Breathing carries the faint mineral taste of the place: chalk, iron, and something bitter from the yellow deposits.

There are no plants. No grass takes hold in the clay, no moss softens the rocks, and no root has found a way into the saturated banks. The absence is not dramatic; it has the settled quality of a condition that has always been understood. The hollow is not dead so much as unsuitable. Its water is too warm in places, too alkaline in others, and its surface is repeatedly coated with fine mineral films. Along the margins, nearly invisible colonies of simple life darken the stone into dull streaks. They form no recognizable leaves or bodies. They are more like stains that happen to persist, eating what the water brings and leaving behind thin, glassy skins.

Small pale creatures live in the deeper pools, though they are seldom seen. On rare occasions a blind, many-legged thing folds itself beneath a shelf, or a translucent larva drifts against the edge of a basin and vanishes into the silt. There are no birds, insects, or larger animals. The place has no feeding calls, no wingbeats, no sudden movement. Its ecology is almost entirely hidden in the water and in the dark pores of the stone.

The light is dim even under a clear sky. The hollow lies beneath a broad overhang of limestone, or perhaps within the collapsed rim of an old cavern, so that daylight reaches it only as a pale reflected glow. The walls hold a warm gray brightness while the pools remain brown-black. At midday there is no sharp shadow, only layers of murk. Wet surfaces catch what little light exists and return it in small, tarnished gleams.

Scattered around the hollow are remnants of a primitive settlement. A low wall crosses one side, built from flat stones fitted without mortar. It has slumped but not fallen. Near it stands a roofless shelter, three shoulder-high walls enclosing a floor of packed clay. A line of rough pegs has been driven into the stone, some still holding strips of dark, water-stiffened leather. There are shallow bowls cut directly into the limestone, perhaps for gathering the springwater, and a few chipped tools left in niches along the wall: a scraper, a wedge, a short hammerstone worn smooth at one end.

Nothing suggests abandonment by catastrophe. The structures have simply become part of the hollow. Mineral crust has grown over the lower courses of the wall. The water passes beneath one threshold and darkens the floor. A broken bowl has settled into a pool, and the pool has taken the shape of it. Whoever used the place may have come here to collect water, soak hides, work stone, or wait out the frequent rains. Their marks are practical and sparse. There are no carvings, no monuments, no objects that insist on being remembered.

The sound is correspondingly small. Water taps from the ceiling at irregular but unhurried intervals. Somewhere beneath the stone, a slow seep makes a thick, nearly inaudible gurgle. When a drop falls into one of the deeper pools, the sound travels farther than it should, touching the walls and returning softened. The hollow amplifies no voices; it absorbs them. Footsteps make wet, muted presses. The only persistent noise is the quiet ticking of condensation.

The air barely moves. Warm vapor lies low over the water, not enough to obscure the far side but enough to soften every outline. At the boundary of the hollow, the outside world seems brighter and drier, though it may not be. Water has made the interior its own climate. Even after long periods without rain, the stone continues to weep. The pools neither drain nor visibly rise. Their level remains fixed, held by hidden channels beneath the floor.

There is a strict equilibrium to the place. Water enters through the rock, gathers in the basins, and disappears at the same patient rate. Mineral deposits form, but only slowly; old crusts are worn smooth before they become thick. The walls erode into familiar curves, and the scattered structures settle by fractions too small to witness. A stone placed on a ledge will remain there for years unless deliberately moved. A footprint fills with water and stays filled until the surrounding clay absorbs it.

The hollow does not feel dangerous. It feels complete. Its warmth, dampness, dimness, and emptiness have reached an arrangement that resists interruption. A visitor can stand among the shallow pools and hear the small sounds of water, see the old tools in their niches, and understand that the place will receive them without welcome or hostility. It will wet their feet, cloud their breath, and close around their presence with the same quiet permanence it gives to stone.
