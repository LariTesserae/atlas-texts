The landscape exists inside a pocket no larger than a room, though its interior insists on being a plateau.

There is a highness to it—not the highness of mountains, but of being held above ordinary ground. The whole place hangs within a fold of heated mineral, a shallow chamber in the side of something that has not yet finished becoming stone. Its floor rises in broad, unfinished shelves toward a narrow summit. Beyond the rim there is no distant view. There is only a pale, depthless brightness, as if the world ends a few meters away and light continues anyway.

The air is hot and dry, but not empty. It carries the faint pressure of a sealed industrial atmosphere: warm metal, powdered ceramic, ozone, and the bitter scent of minerals being separated somewhere out of sight. Nothing sweats. Nothing rusts. There is no rain, seepage, puddle, or trace of liquid water. Condensation is impossible here. Moisture, if it enters, is immediately translated into something else—a white crust, a burst of vaporless heat, a new vein of glass.

The ground is raw and only partly formed. It is not eroded so much as assembled. Plates of dark basalt lie beside pale slag, rose-colored crystal, black metallic sand, and broad membranes of stone that have the smoothness of poured material. Edges remain sharp. Slopes terminate abruptly, as though the terrain has been paused between instructions. A ridge climbs in one clean, improbable fold and then stops halfway through its own curve. In places the floor is soft-looking but rigid, made of gray dust packed into geometric ripples. These ripples do not follow wind. They follow the slow, invisible passage of whatever is manufacturing the chamber.

Vegetation is abundant, though scattered.

It grows in deliberate-looking islands across the mineral shelves: thickets of tall, jointed stalks, mats of copper-green fronds, low fans like blackened feathers, and pale tendrils that climb nothing and hang in the air. None of it resembles ordinary plant life for long. The stalks are hollow tubes lined with luminous fibers. Their leaves are thin plates of mica or translucent mineral, overlapping like scales, and their roots spread not into soil but into temperature gradients. Where one root touches a hot seam, it branches instantly into a fine metallic web.

The plants do not drink. They draw nourishment from heat, pressure, radiation, and the electrical difference between one kind of stone and another. Their sap—if it can be called sap—is a slow-moving glow beneath their surfaces. It gathers in knots, pauses, and then flows backward toward the roots. A cluster may remain motionless for years, then change its shape over the course of an afternoon, unfolding one new blade toward a source of warmth no one else can perceive.

They are lush without being crowded. There are broad empty reaches between the groves, expanses of bare mineral where the chamber’s geometry is plainly visible. The vegetation seems to observe spacing as a rule. No plant trespasses into another’s thermal territory. A single tall growth may stand alone on an entire shelf, its crown spreading like a green mechanical star against the light. Farther away, a dozen smaller colonies occupy a basin, each separated from the next by precise strips of untouched ground.

There are no animals. No insects move among the fronds. Nothing flies, burrows, grazes, or calls. The plants have no need of pollinators. At long intervals, when the chamber’s pressure changes, they release their reproductive material as tiny sparks or flakes of dark crystal. These drift upward, not on air currents but along invisible lines of force, and vanish before reaching the ceiling.

The industrial character of the place is heard more than seen.

Somewhere below the floor, immense mechanisms work at a speed too slow to register as machinery. There is a subsonic turning, almost below hearing, followed by a faint tremor through the soles. Every few minutes—though “minutes” are unreliable here—a distant relay clicks once. A low electrical hum rises, steadies, and fades. The sounds are sparse enough that the silence between them becomes the dominant feature.

When a new seam of stone is being made, there is a soft granular hiss. When an unseen cutter withdraws, the entire chamber gives a tiny, clean vibration. The plants respond to these noises by leaning, but their movement is so gradual it appears at first to be a trick of the light.

The light itself is ordinary in intensity and color: bright, warm, almost sunlike. Yet it has no obvious source. It comes from above and from every exposed surface at once. Shadows fall in the expected direction, then occasionally remain behind when their objects move. A frond can cast a shadow several seconds before it bends. A person standing near the summit might see their shadow continue climbing after they have stopped.

Perspective behaves poorly at the edges of the chamber. A stone lying a handspan away may seem to possess a distant horizon within it. The top of the highest shelf can be seen from below, but when approached it recedes by the same amount, preserving a fixed intimacy. The pocket is small, but its smallness is not consistent. One can cross it in ten steps, yet spend an hour looking across the same gap without coming closer to what lies on the other side.

Gravity is weak and slightly directional. It pulls toward the central mass of the unfinished terrain, but also downward along the chamber’s original seam. Loose mineral grains roll uphill, stop, and then decide they have been falling all along. The plants are anchored by no visible force, their root-webs extending into the air as readily as into rock.

Nothing here changes quickly. Heat accumulates over centuries. A crystal ridge advances by the width of a fingernail. The industrial processes beneath the landscape complete their cycles so slowly that geological formation and factory production become indistinguishable. A surface that looks freshly poured may have been cooling for ten thousand years. A mature grove may still be in the first stage of germination.

The place feels embryonic—not young, exactly, but unfinished in a way that does not imply eventual completion. Its raw geology is being built and its ecology is already taking advantage of the construction. Mineral shelves emerge beneath rooted colonies. New cavities open without collapsing. A dark, perfectly circular aperture appears in a wall, and from it comes a faint warm wind carrying no dust. By the time one looks again, the aperture has become a shallow basin full of black grass.

There are rules, although they are not posted anywhere.

Nothing may be fully distant. Nothing may become entirely solid. Heat cannot leave; it can only be converted into color, growth, pressure, or sound. The industrial systems do not produce objects so much as conditions. The plants cannot die in any familiar manner. When damaged, they lose their shape and become terrain. When cut, they root along the cut edge. When burned, they return as a patch of reflective stone.

And the chamber does not permit a complete view of itself.

Every attempt to understand its whole arrangement produces a small failure of reality: a slope that joins itself, a ceiling glimpsed beneath the floor, a grove seen growing upside down in the reflection of a crystal seam. The breakdown is quiet and almost courteous. Nothing screams. Nothing explodes. The world simply declines to remain singular.

At the highest shelf, one lone plant grows beside a seam of incandescent mineral. It is taller than the pocket should allow. Its crown presses against the bright boundary overhead and continues through it without opening a hole. Its leaves appear on both sides at once, green on the inner side and silver beyond.

Beneath it, the machinery turns.

The plateau waits in the heat, sparse and flourishing, while the unfinished ground slowly decides what it has always been.
