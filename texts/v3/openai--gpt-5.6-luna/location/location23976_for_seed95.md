The high country has been worn almost to its bones.

It is not mountainous in the usual sense. There are no sharp peaks, no young fractures, no slopes eager to collapse. The land rises in broad, exhausted shelves, each one the remainder of something far larger and more violent. From a distance the plateau appears nearly level, a vast tawny surface held above the lower world by long escarpments. Up close, its flatness resolves into shallow basins, folded terraces, stone fans, and channels so old that they seem less like cuts in the earth than the earth’s original pattern.

The rock is warm beneath the hand even in shade. Red-brown basalt shows through a skin of pale dust. In places the plateau has weathered into gray stone ribs, rounded and crowded together like the backs of buried animals. Elsewhere, the surface is covered in thin plates of mica or salt-dark shale, all lying in the same direction, as if a patient wind had arranged them. The erosion here is not ruinous. Nothing is actively being torn apart. Everything has already been reduced to its most durable shape.

The air is thin, dry, and surprisingly warm. Heat gathers in the broad stone hollows during the day and remains there after sunset, rising in faint distortions that make the farther terraces tremble. There is little wind. When it comes, it moves across the plateau in a single, low breath, carrying dust only a few body-lengths before falling still. The sky is immense and ordinary: blue washed nearly white overhead, deeper toward the horizon, with long clouds that form over distant ranges and dissolve before reaching this place.

Rain arrives rarely and without drama. A dark band may cross the sky, and hours later a few heavy drops strike the stone. They collect in the old channels, in hollows beneath overhanging ledges, and in cup-shaped depressions worn into the rock. Some water remains for days in shaded pockets, green at the edges, crowded with minute swimmers. Some sinks into the porous stone and reappears weeks later as a line of dampness along a cliff face. There are pools that seem permanent only because no one has watched them vanish. Around them, the ground is dark and fragrant, and the sparse vegetation gathers as if called.

The plants are low, widely spaced, and built for waiting. Thorned cushions cling to cracks. Pale grasses grow in rings around underground moisture, each blade stiff and silvered, their roots reaching far beyond what their small bodies suggest. There are squat shrubs with waxy leaves, and tall, leafless stalks that flower only after rain. Their blossoms open all at once, briefly, in colors too vivid for the subdued land: violet, rust-orange, the yellow of old bone. Afterward the flowers collapse, and the stalks remain for years.

There should not be so much life here.

Yet the plateau is full of animals.

They occupy every layer of the landscape, though few announce themselves. Small, many-legged things move beneath the stone plates. Pale lizards lie packed into the narrow bands of shade under ledges, their bodies overlapping, their eyes opening one at a time as a shadow passes. Beetles with blunt horned heads push through the dust in slow, purposeful lines. In the damp basins, translucent creatures cling to the undersides of stones, and clusters of black, membranous eggs tremble in the shallow water.

The larger animals are everywhere in the middle distance. Herds of long-necked grazers stand among the terraces, so still they might be weathered pillars until one bends to strip a tuft of grass. Their hides are the same colors as the ground, patterned with mineral streaks. Some carry shelves of hardened growth along their backs, small ecosystems of lichen and nesting insects. Others have no visible eyes. They orient themselves by the pressure of distant movement, raising their heads whenever a hundred smaller bodies shift at once.

Broad-backed animals travel the old watercourses in single files. They are not hurried. Their feet have spread into soft, many-jointed pads that leave only shallow impressions in the dust. When they pass close by, the ground seems to acquire another layer of silence. Smaller creatures follow them—not in pursuit, but in dependence. They feed on what the herd disturbs, shelter in the warmth of its tracks, and gather in the hollows left by its resting bodies.

Above, the air is crowded with wings.

Vultures, gliders, mothlike things, and enormous dark insects drift through the thermals. Their numbers are so great that they sometimes appear to form a second, moving geography between the plateau and the sky. Most make no sound. Their wings flex without a visible beat, and their shadows pass over the land in overlapping stains. At certain hours, when the heat is strongest, the entire airborne population turns together, banking in silence around some unseen center.

There are predators, but predation is not hurried here. A catlike animal with six narrow legs may lie beside a pool for half a day without moving, its body indistinguishable from the surrounding stone. A flock of small white hunters will descend on a carcass, but even their feeding is quiet: a soft rustling, the tiny click of beaks, the settling of wings. The plateau does not erupt into violence. It absorbs it.

Sound travels far, though there is very little of it.

A stone falling somewhere across the basin can be heard as a single dry note. The distant call of one herd animal may arrive minutes after the animal has vanished behind a rise. Most sounds are intimate: claws against shale, water ticking from a shaded seam, the papery movement of grass, the collective breathing of a mass of animals hidden just beyond sight.

Sometimes, without warning, the land makes a deeper sound. It may be the groan of heat inside a cliff, or the settling of strata too old to remember motion. The noise comes from beneath the entire plateau, a low vibration felt more in the ribs than heard. Animals freeze when it happens. Then, slowly, they resume whatever they were doing.

Scattered across the high shelves are remnants of construction. They are not ruins in the usual sense, because very little remains to suggest what they once were. A short wall follows the edge of a basin for perhaps thirty paces, made from fitted blocks the color of the surrounding rock. The joints are still tight. A solitary doorway stands without a building, opening onto open air. There are circular foundations half swallowed by dust, stairways descending into solid stone, and low towers whose upper portions have been worn smooth by centuries of weather.

No metal glints among them. No glass, wire, road, or machine interrupts the natural materials. If the builders had tools, those tools have left no clear trace. Some structures seem to have been shaped by hand; others look grown or persuaded into form. A wall may curve around a spring not to contain it, but to give the water a particular shadow. At noon, certain aligned stones cast shadows that meet in a narrow crack. When this occurs, animals gather there in dense, patient masses, as though following a rule older than memory.

The world obeys rules that are not quite laws and not quite habits.

Water never gathers in the lowest place. It gathers where the stone has been waiting for it. Animals do not cross some shallow channels, though no barrier is visible. A particular basin is always full of footprints but never contains a living creature. Herds move around certain ruins in broad arcs, maintaining a distance that does not change even as the structures crumble. At night, when the plateau cools, faint warmth remains in the old walls. Creatures press against them in layers—small bodies beneath large ones, wings tucked over backs, scales and fur and bare skin sharing the same ancient heat.

The density of life is greatest where the eye finds the least. A patch of apparently empty ground may hold burrowing animals beneath every handspan, seeds dormant under the dust, larvae in the roots of a dead shrub, and a whole colony of pale insects turning slowly through the underside of a stone. The surface seems sparse only because most life has learned to occupy the pauses between visible things.

By late afternoon, the long shadows of the escarpments reach across the terraces. The herds begin to move toward the damp places, though the damp places may be many miles away. Their progress is almost imperceptible. One animal steps. Another follows. The line continues, folding around gullies and old foundations, disappearing and reappearing until it resembles a migrating crack in the land.

The plateau changes at the same speed.

A channel deepens by the width of a finger over a century. A wall loses one block. A shrub dies, and its roots hold the soil in place long enough for another shrub to take its place. The water returns. The animals remain. The high country does not feel alive in the quick, green sense. It feels settled—full beyond measure, but moving according to processes so slow that a person becomes a brief disturbance within them.

At dusk, the sky turns copper above the worn shelves. The stone releases its heat. The first nocturnal creatures emerge in thousands, flowing from cracks and hollows without a sound. Across the basin, the old structures darken. Something large calls once from beyond the far ridge, and the sound crosses the open land, reaches the nearest pool, and is answered by the faintest trembling of the water.

Then everything is still again, crowded with life and waiting.
