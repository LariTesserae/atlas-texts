The plateau has no true ground.

It is a continent-sized growth of black botanical material, raised high above a lower world that cannot be seen. From a distance it resembles a mountain range worn smooth by ages of wind, but its ridges are made of bundled stems, root-beams, petrified tendrils, and layers of old plant tissue pressed together until they have the density of ceramic. The whole plateau is a single organism, or perhaps the fossilized body of one that never finished dying.

Its surface extends beyond sight in every direction. Broad terraces descend and rise again, though none of them are natural slopes. They are the flattened crowns of ancient growths, planed level by centuries of mechanical use. In places, the plateau folds into immense valleys where the walls are ribbed with woody buttresses. Elsewhere, it opens into industrial plains: dark, crowded expanses of pipes, stalks, vats, towers, and low spreading factories, all grown rather than assembled.

There is no soil.

There is no loose stone.

Every surface is occupied by plant matter or by something made from it. Roots have become foundations. Trunks have become gantries. Leaves have thickened into roofing plates, conveyor belts, shutters, insulation, and armor. Seeds are manufactured in thousands of sealed chambers, then fed into production trenches where they are coaxed into standardized components. The oldest buildings are indistinguishable from the plateau itself. They have been absorbing one another for so long that a factory wall may also be a root, a road, a reservoir for hot gas, and the outer surface of a living tree.

The trees are not trees in the familiar sense. They are columns, cables, vaults, and towers. Some rise hundreds of meters, though the high atmosphere gives them little light to seek. Their crowns spread laterally beneath the dark, forming interlocked canopies that fill every possible gap. The leaves are thick, matte, and nearly black, layered like roof tiles. They do not flutter. They clamp, unfold, and ratchet in response to pressure changes. Along their undersides, thousands of narrow pores open and close in timed sequences, drawing in the dry atmosphere and extracting what little chemical energy it contains.

Nothing here drinks water. The plants have no use for it.

Their sap is a dense, heated suspension of carbon, sulfur, metal salts, and volatile oils. It moves through the plateau in pulses, pushed by immense vascular pumps. In exposed channels it glows dull red, not with flame but with pressure and internal friction. The channels are covered by translucent bark, and beneath this bark the sap travels like a buried furnace. Smaller tubes branch from it everywhere, feeding boilers, cutters, growth chambers, and the pale root-lamps that mark the industrial paths.

The air is hot, thin, and dry enough to sting the lungs. It carries the smell of scorched resin, hot iron, bitter alkaloids, and the mineral dust produced when old plant tissue is ground down and recycled. The darkness is not complete. There are points of dim illumination everywhere: red vascular seams, blue-white electrical arcs trapped in glassy pods, and the occasional dull orange aperture of a furnace. The light never gathers into daylight. It remains local and insufficient, so that every object appears to emerge only a few meters before dissolving again into blackness.

Above the plateau, the sky is a sealed depth.

A ceiling cannot be seen, but the atmosphere behaves as though one exists. Heat accumulates in the upper layers and descends in slow, turbulent sheets. Vast pressure storms move across the region, bending the tallest stalks without producing wind in the ordinary sense. The canopy flexes as a whole. Entire districts lean, tighten, and reconfigure under the passing force. Long vertical folds open in the vegetation, revealing factories far below, then close again as the plant mass knits itself back together.

The landscape is ancient, but it is not still.

Its age is visible in the smoothness of the oldest surfaces. Ridges have been worn down to broad, stable forms. Root bridges have fused into solid shelves. Towers that once stood separately are now a single continuous wall. There are no fresh cliffs, no exposed strata, no raw wilderness. Even collapse has been incorporated. When a structure fails, its remains are swallowed by neighboring growth, digested, and extruded somewhere else.

Yet the interior is in constant convulsion.

The factories operate without workers. They are distributed organs of the plateau, each performing a narrow function: stripping mineral dust from the atmosphere, refining metals from ancient buried deposits, producing cutting blades from hardened cellulose, extruding cable-vines, distilling pressure gases, or cultivating new architecture. Their chimneys are swollen stalks. Their assembly lines are horizontal roots fitted with gripping leaves. Their turbines are flower-like organs whose petals rotate in the thermal currents.

They make replacements for themselves.

They also make replacements for the replacements.

Across the open plains, thousands of mechanical growths are always being formed, cut apart, grafted, redirected, or absorbed. A wall rises in one district while a tower is dismantled in another. Conveyor vines carry bundles of dark cylinders through the shadows. Great pruning machines move on root-like tracks, their hooked limbs trimming excess growth from the industrial thickets. Their work is almost silent at a distance. The density of the place swallows individual noises.

What remains is a low, continuous sound: a buried vibration underfoot, as if the plateau were purring in its sleep. Now and then a pressure valve opens with a soft, enormous sigh. Far away, something metallic clatters for several seconds and stops. The main sounds are not made by motion but by strain—the creak of trunks under thermal stress, the dry ticking of leaves adjusting their pores, the muffled boom of a chamber collapsing and being sealed.

There are no animals.

No insects move through the leaves. No birds cross the roof of the canopy. Nothing hunts, grazes, nests, or watches. The only life is botanical, and even that distinction has become uncertain. The plants think through chemical gradients, electrical pulses, and growth instructions. They do not possess eyes, but they can sense pressure. They do not possess hands, but they can manufacture tools. They do not possess mouths, but they consume failed structures and return them to circulation.

The central rule of the place is that separation does not last.

Anything left alone is gradually joined to something else. A cable becomes a root. A tower becomes a branch. A road becomes a vein. A machine becomes an organ. Even old air is drawn into the plant and transformed into tissue, heat, or pressure. The plateau is relentlessly consolidating itself, packing every interval until there is no true empty space—only temporary cavities, maintenance tunnels, sealed chambers, and the black distances between neighboring crowns.

Occasionally, those distances open wide enough to reveal the horizon.

Then the plateau appears not as a forest, nor as a city, but as one vast raised body extending over the high world: a dark, manufactured ecology, ancient beyond memory and feverishly alive beneath its own roof. Its surface is nearly flat from erosion, its interior violently active. It has outlived the conditions that first made it and now sustains itself through habit, pressure, heat, and endless fabrication.

At night—if the world has nights—the roots brighten slightly.

The factories continue.

The leaves close.

And somewhere far below the packed surface, an older layer of the plant begins to grow again.
