The plateau begins where the world has not quite finished hardening.

It rises in broad, unfinished shelves toward a high interior plain, each shelf ending in a clean break rather than a weathered cliff. The rock is dark and warm beneath the light: basalt-black, iron-red, and occasionally pale as kiln ash. Its surfaces have the smooth, swollen appearance of material poured and left to cool, though no river or glacier has ever passed over them. Cracks are rare and deliberate-looking. Edges remain sharp. Nothing has had time—or perhaps permission—to crumble.

There is no water anywhere.

The valleys are not dry riverbeds, because they have never carried rivers. They are long shallow depressions between uplifted plates, filled with gray mineral dust and glittering crusts of salt-like compounds that are not salt. In the lowest places, the crust gives off a faint metallic color: blue at one angle, green at another, as though the ground is remembering a liquid state. Here and there, pale vents breathe invisible gases from beneath the plateau. Their presence is marked only by the trembling of nearby growth.

The air is warm, thin, and remarkably clear. At this elevation the horizon is immense. Ridges recede in layers, not softened by haze but sharply separated against a deep, almost polished sky. The sun feels close without being harsh. Shadows are ordinary and black-edged. Nothing moves within them.

Vegetation is scattered across this emptiness in low, astonishingly green colonies. It does not resemble a forest, or even a grassland. The plants grow where the stone provides the right chemistry, sometimes singly, with kilometers between them, sometimes in circular arrangements around a mineral seam. Their forms are spare and unfamiliar: branching fans of translucent tissue, mats of thick emerald filaments, upright bundles like bundled copper wire dipped in leaf-colored glass. Some cling directly to vertical rock, drawing nourishment from the air and from the slow electrical gradients in the stone. Others spread as flat plates over the mineral dust, their undersides silvered and reflective.

They are lush in themselves but do not make the landscape crowded. A single plant may be broad enough to shade a small house, though there are no houses, and may stand alone in a plain large enough to contain a city. Between one living presence and the next is a great deal of bare ground. The green seems less like an abundance than a set of deliberate marks placed across an otherwise vacant surface.

No animals inhabit the plateau. There are no insects around the flowering structures, no birds overhead, no tracks, nests, burrows, or scavengers. Some of the plants produce elaborate colors and geometries that appear designed to attract attention, but attention is not given. Their reproductive organs open and close with the sun in a silent cycle, releasing clouds of luminous spores that drift only a short distance before settling. The spores do not ride wind; there is almost no wind. They rise, hover, and fall as if governed by weak local gravity.

The world’s technology is present without being made.

Far out across the plain, industrial processes occur inside the geology. A long ridge glows faintly beneath its crust, not with fire but with a dull orange radiance moving through seams in the rock. Along another horizon, black columns rise at regular intervals from the ground, too smooth to be natural and too integrated to be structures. They are pressure organs of the plateau, venting and condensing the planet’s buried gases. Every few hours—though “hours” are merely a convenient measure—a column pulses. The pulse travels through the terrain as a vibration too low to hear, activating distant growths and causing entire mineral shelves to settle by a fraction of a millimeter.

There are no pipes, cables, towers, roads, or machines. Yet the place performs the work of a factory.

Ore forms in exposed bands as if assembled by invisible conveyors. Thin metallic films creep across the faces of stones, pause at precise boundaries, and stop. Crystals emerge from the ground in standardized shapes: hexagonal rods, nested plates, hollow spindles. Some are colored the dark blue of oil, others carry a dull amber glow in their cores. Nothing mines them. Nothing carries them away. Production is continuous but nearly motionless, an industry reduced to chemistry, pressure, and time.

The plateau may be the interior of a machine that has mistaken itself for a landscape.

Its silence is not complete. Close to the ground, the air makes a faint dry ticking as it passes over mineral teeth. The green colonies give occasional minute clicks when their tissues contract, like cooling metal in a distant workshop. Beneath the surface, the pressure organs hum at the edge of hearing. If one stands alone in a broad basin, the sound seems to come from everywhere and nowhere: a subdued electrical resonance, steady as a held breath.

There is no weather in the familiar sense. No rain gathers. Clouds are rare, thin, and high, forming geometric bands before dissolving. Winds do not sweep across the plain; instead, the atmosphere adjusts itself in slow, even exchanges. Dust lies where it falls. Spores settle where released. A stone warmed by the sun remains warm long after sunset, and a shadowed stone cools only slightly. Gradients are weak. The whole region approaches equilibrium so perfectly that the difference between morning and evening is mostly a difference in illumination.

Even erosion seems incomplete as a concept. A cliff face may hold its shape for centuries. A shard of rock rests on a ledge with one point touching the surface, never sliding. Fine dust gathers in impossible-looking heaps that retain their steep sides. The plateau is not being worn down; it is still deciding what form to take.

In the far distance, immense terraces rise from the plain, each one broad enough to be mistaken for a separate country. Their edges are fresh, pale, and unscarred. Beyond them, the high ground disappears into the bright horizon, where the sky and stone meet without a mountain range to interrupt them.

The living things remain sparse and vivid. The industrial geology continues its patient work. The light passes over everything normally.

Nothing hunts. Nothing flees. Nothing decays.

The landscape is warm, elevated, green in widely separated places, and almost empty everywhere else—a quiet mineral world in the first stable moment of its existence, already manufacturing its future but not yet inhabited by it.
