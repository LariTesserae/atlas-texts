The plateau begins above the weather.

It is not a mountain range, exactly, though its margins rise in broken escarpments for thousands of kilometers. It is a continent of uplift: an immense, newly exposed table of black stone, thrust out of the planet’s interior so quickly that the land has not yet learned how to settle. Its surface is a succession of tilted shelves, pressure domes, fresh rifts, and long knife-backed ridges, all sharp enough to retain the pale seam of their birth. Valleys are not valleys but wounds—narrow, abrupt, and impossibly deep, their floors hidden beneath cloud, mineral vapor, or darkness.

The plateau is warm despite its height. Heat rises through it constantly. In some places the rock is hot to the touch; in others, warmth pulses beneath a thin skin of glassy basalt. The air is thin, but not clean. It carries a metallic tang, the smell of wet stone and ozone, and a faint sweetness produced by the vegetation that has colonized the cracks. The atmosphere gathers in hollows and along the lee sides of ridges, forming dim, amber-green fogbanks. They come and go without wind. A valley may fill in minutes, the fog climbing upward against gravity until it spills over a ridge and pours across the plain like a slow river.

There is little rain. Moisture arrives in other ways.

At night, the upper air condenses in long silver sheets on the exposed stone. Dew does not bead so much as crawl. It gathers around ridgelines, follows invisible gradients across the rock, and collects in shallow basins where it trembles under its own surface tension. Some pools are no more than handspan depressions, but they are everywhere: dark cups in the basalt, green-rimmed fissures, hanging films stretched between crystals. Every few weeks, a pressure front passes over the plateau and produces a violent, localized storm. Rain falls in a single corridor, perhaps a hundred kilometers long and no wider than a town. It may last twelve minutes. Afterward, the land steams.

Water runs briefly and with great purpose. It cuts new channels through ash, carves polished slots into the young rock, and vanishes into porous seams before reaching the lowlands. The streams are clear near their sources and cloudy with suspended minerals within a few kilometers. Some flow uphill for a time, climbing slanted shelves toward warmer ground, then abruptly remember gravity and plunge into the cracks.

Vegetation occupies every place where moisture can hesitate.

It is lush, but not continuous. There are no soft forests spread evenly across the plateau. Instead, life forms dense, extravagant thickets around the smallest advantages: a condensation trench, a warm vent, a seam of porous stone, the shadow of a ridge. The plants are low and broad in exposed country, their leaves layered like scales to resist the desiccating wind. In sheltered ravines they become enormous. Pale trunks rise from the chasms, jointed and hollow, branching into crowns of translucent fronds. Their upper leaves collect condensation and pass it downward through the plant in visible pulses. At intervals, a whole grove shudders as water moves through it.

Some growths are more mineral than botanical. Crusts of red and violet lichen spread over the rock in geometric fans. Threadlike vines run through hairline cracks and emerge hundreds of meters away, blooming simultaneously in distant places. Moss grows upside down beneath overhangs, its roots dangling toward the ground while its spore-bearing surfaces face the sky. In the dim light, these organisms are difficult to distinguish from rust, frost, or the staining left by old fires.

The plateau’s light is weak and oblique even at midday. A dense veil hangs high above it, not quite cloud and not quite atmosphere. Sunlight comes filtered through bronze, pearl, and bruised violet. Shadows are long and uncertain. A ridge may cast darkness in one direction while the vegetation beneath it remains lit from another, as if the land remembers a previous position of the sun.

At night, darkness does not deepen so much as rearrange itself. The stone gives off a low internal glow along its fractures. Pools shine from below. Patches of vegetation open pale apertures that resemble eyes, though they do not track movement. Far across the plateau, vertical lights rise from the earth in silent columns, illuminating nothing around them. They are the exhausts of underground processes—natural furnaces, pressure organs, or something that has never had a name.

The world here is industrial, but there are no factories.

The machinery is geological and alive.

Across the broad shelves stand fields of black chimneys, each one grown rather than built. They have ribbed sides, rotating crowns, and mouths that open and close with the rhythm of subterranean pressure. Some draw gases down into the earth; others exhale heat, mineral dust, or clouds of microscopic spores. Their surfaces are plated with conductive metals deposited from deep water. During electrical storms, the chimneys connect themselves in branching arcs, and power travels through them in blue-white pulses.

There are vast regions of needle grass whose blades are made partly of iron-rich crystal. When the wind passes over them, they generate currents strong enough to magnetize loose stones. The stones lift, hover briefly, and settle in new arrangements. Over years, whole slopes migrate grain by grain.

Other organisms operate like engines. Low, many-legged things move in synchronized herds across the flats, dragging combs of bone or shell through the soil. They separate minerals, concentrate them, and discharge the waste into clean, sharply bordered heaps. Their work resembles mining, though no creature appears to benefit from the material. Towering filter-feeders stand around warm pools, pumping fog through porous crowns. Their bodies are threaded with tubes, valves, and membranes. When they mature, they split open and release thousands of small, gliding machines that look like seeds until they begin to swarm.

There are no roads. There are routes.

They are made by repetition: the darkened paths of migrating animals, the polished strips where magnetized stones slide downhill, the channels worn by clouds of beetle-sized metal organisms. Travelers might follow a line of vegetation that blooms in sequence across otherwise barren shelves, or a chain of warm vents, or the regular appearance of enormous footprints that fill with condensation and remain wet for days.

The fauna is abundant, though rarely visible all at once. The plateau is full of movement concealed by scale.

Long-legged grazers pass through the high grass, their bodies narrow and flexible, their backs crowded with smaller organisms that harvest heat from them. They are nearly silent. Their feet divide before touching the ground, spreading their weight over fragile mineral crusts. When startled, they do not flee; they become transparent, revealing a second skeleton beneath the first, and then a third landscape beyond that.

In the ravines, wingless things climb the vertical walls. Some have dozens of jointed limbs and soft, lamp-like organs along their sides. Others are vast and rooted, their heads emerging only during the brief wet season. The pools contain quicksilver fish with no fixed shape. They gather into bodies, dissolve into ribbons, and reassemble elsewhere. In the fog are hunters that imitate absence. The eye notices a gap among the plants, and then the gap has moved.

A great many creatures live in the stone itself. They are heard as faint internal clicks beneath the feet, though the plateau is mostly quiet. Their tunnels intersect without collapsing. Occasionally a surface ruptures and releases a rush of warm air carrying pollen, dust, and a flock of small blind animals. The rupture closes again over the course of a few hours, as if the rock is healing.

The silence is not emptiness. It is compression.

Sound travels strangely in the thin, warm air. A shout may vanish a few meters away, absorbed by the vegetation or bent down into a ravine. Distant events arrive without their sources: the soft thunder of a collapsing shelf, the chitter of an animal several kilometers away, the sudden hum of an electrical discharge. Sometimes a person can hear their own footsteps from ahead, approaching before they have taken them.

The largest noises are brief. A ridge splits with a sound like a sheet of metal being torn. A storm strikes the plateau and every mineral blade sings at once. Entire ecosystems migrate overnight, their movement producing a low, continuous murmur that can be mistaken for wind. Then the quiet returns, thicker than before.

The land is young enough to be undecided.

Cliffs sometimes lean in directions that do not match their strata. Cracks close when observed directly. A slope may preserve footprints from creatures that have not yet crossed it. Rivers occasionally divide into branches that occupy different moments: one stream running clear, another carrying the same water several minutes earlier, and a third flowing with rain from a storm that has not occurred.

Distance is unreliable. A visible escarpment may require three days to reach, or stand behind you when you turn. Horizons buckle during pressure changes, and the far edge of the plateau can become a vertical wall, a second plateau, or a dark reflection of the first. Shadows sometimes detach from the objects that cast them. They continue moving for a while across the ground, searching for a new owner.

The weather has its own ecology. Storms gather around concentrations of living metal, feeding on electrical gradients. Fog breeds in warm cracks and migrates like an animal. Wind forms in enclosed basins, circles for several hours, then escapes upward in a spiraling column. There are regions where gravity fluctuates subtly during atmospheric disturbances. Grass lifts from the soil. Pools climb their banks. Large animals anchor themselves with hooked limbs while stones rise and drift overhead in slow herds.

Nothing constructed remains here because construction is unnecessary and unstable. Any arrangement of matter is eventually incorporated into the plateau’s processes. A wall becomes a mineral seam. A tool grows roots. A machine left unattended develops feeding organs, joins a local cycle, and begins doing work no one designed it to do. The distinction between organism, mechanism, and landscape has been erased by time—or perhaps was never present.

From the highest ridges, the full extent of the place can be seen: a continent of dark shelves under dim weather, green in the fissures, glowing at the seams, boiling softly in its deep valleys. Storms move across it like thoughts. Rivers flash and disappear. Forests open and close. Far-off industrial organs pulse beneath the crust, processing heat, water, metal, memory.

And everywhere, beneath the apparent stillness, the young world is making adjustments.

The plateau is not finished becoming land.
