The plateau is still being made.

It rises beyond the dim horizon in vast, unfinished steps: a country of black shelves, pale mineral folds, and steep walls that appear to have been pressed upward rather than eroded into shape. The highest ridges are so elevated that the sky has thinned to a bruised violet, though there are no clouds and no sun that can be clearly located. Light arrives from everywhere and nowhere, weak and cold, as if the world were being remembered by a dying star.

There is no water. Not a drop in a hollow, not frost in a crack, not a buried stream. The ravines are dry down to their deepest seams. Their floors are lined with salt-like glass and powdery blue deposits that give off a faint internal glow when stepped upon. What passes for moisture in this place is a slow exchange of vapors between stone and sky: invisible, metallic, and bitter. The plants drink it through their roots, or perhaps the roots are only the visible ends of a deeper mineral process.

Vegetation is surprisingly abundant.

It grows in scattered colonies across the high shelves, leaving broad expanses of naked stone between them. Some forms resemble low silver grass, each blade rigid and translucent, ringing against its neighbors whenever the ground shifts. Others are tall, branching growths like leafless trees cast in green-black glass. Their crowns unfold and refold over spans of hours, not toward the light but away from it, opening into the darker parts of the sky. There are mats of blue filament spread across warm rock, dense as moss and bright as old bruises. A few pale stalks rise hundreds of feet from cracks, carrying no leaves, only layered fans that tremble continuously despite the still air.

None of it flowers. Nothing bears fruit. There are no seeds that can be seen. The plants reproduce by subtraction: a branch becomes a separate stand, a patch of filament forgets its connection to the rest, a whole grove may be found one morning divided into identical fragments. Their growth is slow, but the landscape seems to have been growing for only a little while. The roots have not yet learned the shape of the ground. They spread in straight lines, turn abruptly, and sometimes emerge from the rock to continue along its surface before descending again.

At this elevation, the air itself is part of the terrain. It is thin enough to make distance uncertain. A ridge three miles away seems close enough to touch; a nearby cliff may remain unreachable after a day’s walking. The atmosphere has a dry electrical taste. Breathing is possible, though each breath comes with the sensation of drawing powdered metal through the lungs.

The silence should be immense.

Instead, the world is deafening.

A continuous low roar rises from beneath the plateau, not the roar of wind or machinery but the sound of pressure becoming matter. The newly lifted mountains grind against their own foundations. Entire slopes creak. Deep seams open and close with concussions that can be felt in the teeth. At intervals, a sound like a continent tearing passes across the highlands, arriving from one horizon and departing at the other long after the visible ground has stopped moving.

The plants add their own noise. The silver grasses hiss and clang. The tall glass growths strike one another in the thin air, producing sharp choral notes that gather into unbearable chords. Blue filament mats vibrate with a subsonic thrum. Their combined resonance makes the body feel hollow. Speech, if attempted, is useless. A person can shout directly into another’s face and see the words form, but the sound will be swallowed by the greater vibration—or emerge several minutes later from a cliff miles away.

The plateau changes very slowly, yet it is never still. A ridge may take years to rise the width of a hand, but its movement is constant. Stones do not roll downhill; they migrate toward the places where they will eventually belong. Cracks lengthen in clean geometries. Fresh faces of rock appear smooth and wet-looking, though they are bone-dry and warm only at their deepest centers. The beginnings of valleys are visible everywhere: shallow lines awaiting erosion, channels that have not yet decided whether they are channels, basins with no bottoms.

Erosion has barely begun. The land has not been weathered into character. It is raw, angular, and provisional. Cliffs end in unsupported corners. Boulders hover a finger’s breadth above slopes, held there by a local reluctance of gravity. Some stones are found halfway between two positions, their edges doubled, as if the world cannot choose which moment to preserve. A narrow bridge of basalt may lead over a chasm, but its far end sometimes connects to a different cliff depending on who is looking at it.

The shadows are wrong.

They fall in dim, diluted colors, usually pointing toward the nearest height rather than away from the light. A person standing beside a rock may cast a shadow that climbs the rock’s face and disappears over the top. The tall mineral plants cast several shadows at once, each belonging to a different angle of illumination. Now and then, a shadow remains after its object has been moved. These abandoned shadows lie across the stone like strips of dark fabric, and the vegetation grows poorly beneath them.

There are traces of construction, though it is difficult to tell whether they were made by inhabitants or by the place itself.

On one broad shelf stands a small ring of upright stones, each stone shaped with primitive tools into a blunt, wedge-like form. The ring is incomplete. Inside it, the ground has been scraped flat and covered with chips of white mineral. Nearby are three low shelters made from stacked slabs, roofed with woven stalks and sealed with resinous black growth. Their entrances face different directions, though all three lead into the same interior: a cramped chamber whose walls meet at angles that cannot exist from outside.

A path runs between the shelters and the nearest grove. It is not paved, only marked by repeated pressure: shallow hand-sized depressions in the dust. The marks continue for a long distance, then become vertical, climbing the side of a cliff without any visible footholds. Halfway up, they turn inward and vanish into the rock.

No tools lie abandoned. No bones, tracks, nests, or droppings suggest animal life. The place is empty of creatures, but not of intention. The plants lean toward certain stones. The stones gather around certain absences. On some evenings—if the gradual dimming can be called evening—the entire highland seems to pause around a particular crack in the earth, as though listening for an instruction.

The crack is narrow at the surface and impossibly deep. From it comes a pale, upward-flowing darkness. It does not block light so much as reverse it. Pebbles dropped into the fissure rise briefly, hang in the air, and then fall out somewhere far away. The roar beneath the plateau is loudest there, but the sound has no direction. It seems to come from inside the skull, from beneath the feet, from the unformed underside of the sky.

Beyond the crack, the land opens into a wide basin where the first true valley is beginning to exist. Its walls are low and unfinished, its floor covered in sparse growth and geometric plates of ochre stone. At the basin’s center stands a single green-black tree, much taller than the surrounding vegetation. Its trunk divides into six columns, each rising at a different angle. Every few moments, one column becomes transparent, revealing a second landscape inside it: another plateau, dimmer than this one, with other unfinished ridges and other motionless groves.

The image never lasts long enough to understand.

When the column becomes solid again, the basin is unchanged. The tree continues its slow work. The mountains continue lifting themselves. The plants continue ringing in the crushing air.

Nothing here has reached its final form, but nothing appears to be waiting for completion. The world is not young in the gentle sense. It is young like a blade being sharpened, like a thought forcing its way into reality. Every shelf, root, shelter, and impossible shadow is part of the same gradual construction.

And beneath it all, louder than thunder and slower than stone, something is still deciding what this place will be.
