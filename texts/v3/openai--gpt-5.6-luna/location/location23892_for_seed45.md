The high country begins above any recognizable horizon.

It is not a mountain range so much as a vast, unfinished uplift: slabs of dark mineral thrust upward from a plain that has not yet decided whether it is solid. The ridges stand at impossible elevations, their summits hidden not by clouds but by darkness. Even here, where the ground rises toward the edge of the atmosphere, there is no snow, no ice, no water-darkening in the cracks. The surfaces are dry to the point of abstraction—black basalt, dull ironstone, ash-gray plates, and occasional fields of pale salt-like crystals that are not salt and have never known an ocean.

The warmth is strongest in the stone itself. It comes through the soles of the feet as a steady, bodily heat, and in sheltered clefts the rock breathes out waves of it. There are no flames. Instead, the mineral layers glow faintly from within: a reddish pulse beneath thin crusts, amber lines in newly opened fractures, a low bruised radiance in the walls of the ravines. The light is too weak to illuminate the landscape. It only reveals that the darkness has structure.

At a distance, the country seems empty. A long incline rises toward a series of black tablelands, each one separated from the next by enormous, sharp-sided trenches. The trenches are not valleys carved by rivers or glaciers. They are seams where the world has failed to meet itself. Their walls are fresh, angular, and almost unworn, as if raised moments ago. No scree has gathered at their feet. No rounded stones lie in the gullies. The broken pieces remain close to the breaks that made them, or hover just above them, held in a slow and uncertain suspension.

This is a place with little erosion because nothing here has had time to become old.

The land is forming faster than it can decay. A ridge may rise a few handspans while one watches, not with a dramatic heave but through a gradual, undeniable rearrangement. A flat shelf tightens and buckles. A narrow pinnacle leans, then corrects itself. Hairline cracks travel over the ground in branching patterns, and from them emerge thin plates of mineral, pushing upward like the backs of buried things. They do not resemble plants, though their growth has a primitive insistence. They are geological events given the patience of roots.

The changes are turbulent but strangely quiet. There is rarely the crash one expects from moving mountains. Instead, the air carries a deep muttering, almost below hearing, and sometimes a dry clicking as whole fields of crystal adjust themselves. Far off, a cliff folds inward with a sound like a single sheet of metal being creased. The echo travels for a long time through the open spaces and then vanishes without returning.

There is no wind in the ordinary sense. The atmosphere moves in broad, uneven pressures. Invisible fronts pass over the uplands, bending dustless heat, flattening the faint internal glow of the rocks, and making distant forms jump several degrees sideways. At intervals, a turbulence sweeps down from the high ridges. It carries no sand, no leaves, no rain. It carries only a distortion of space and a dry mineral taste. The horizon shivers. The ground seems briefly farther away. Loose fragments rise, turn once, and settle in places they could not have reached by falling.

The sky is almost entirely black, even where the land is visible. There may be a star or two, but they are weak and remote, overwhelmed by the surrounding dark. Above the highest elevations, the darkness has a dense, velvety quality, as if the world ends just beyond the air. It is possible that the mountains rise into a night that is not night at all, but the unlit exterior of an unfinished atmosphere.

Occasional illumination comes from below. A whole escarpment may suddenly show its interior: faint veins running through it, geometric and branching, then dimming before they become bright enough to cast shadows. These brief revelations expose a landscape more intricate than the surface suggests. Beneath the crust are layered chambers, hollow galleries, and columns of material in different states of hardening. The highlands are not merely rock. They are a pressure-built anatomy, slowly assembling itself.

Nothing moves here that is alive.

There are no birds in the high air, no insects hiding in the cracks, no distant shapes crossing the plains. No plant has taken hold. The few upright forms scattered across the landscape are mineral spires, but they are too evenly spaced in places, too deliberate in their angles, to feel entirely natural. Some may be remnants of an earlier construction. Others may be the first rough gestures of one.

A handful of structures stand on the broadest shelves. They are small compared with the terrain: low walls, roofless chambers, isolated towers with openings cut through them. Their material matches the surrounding stone, though their surfaces are smoother and more regular. They seem less built than encouraged into shape. One structure consists of three upright slabs joined by a lintel that has begun to melt back into the ground. Another is a circular enclosure, open on one side, its interior filled with black dust and a dim warmth. A narrow stair descends from nowhere into a crack and stops after twelve steps at a wall of unworked rock.

No tools lie beside these works. No roads connect them. There are only faint lines across a few shelves, straight enough to suggest intention but too shallow to be called paths. They might be foundations. They might be measurements. They might be the marks left by something that was once present and is now indistinguishable from the geology.

The emptiness between these presences is enormous. A tower can stand alone for miles, visible only when a pulse of inner light catches its edge. The ruins do not make the land inhabited. They make its solitude more exact.

At the extreme elevations, the terrain opens into immense shelves of nearly level stone. From these heights, the lower country is visible as a sequence of rising and collapsing planes, a region-wide anatomy of ridges, basins, walls, and unfinished faults. There is no blue distance, no haze softened by moisture. Every feature is hard-edged until the darkness takes it. The scale is difficult to judge: a nearby ravine may be a narrow cut or a continent-sized chasm, and the slow movements of the ground offer no reliable sense of proportion.

Sometimes the entire horizon changes.

A low line of mountains will become a wall. A wall will split into separate columns. Between one glance and the next, a plateau will be pierced by a row of new vents, each releasing a column of heated darkness. The alterations are not destructive in the usual sense. Nothing is being ruined so much as revised. The landscape is an argument still in progress, and every ridge is provisional.

Yet for long stretches, nothing happens at all.

The quiet can last for hours. In it, the warmth of the stone becomes almost comforting. One hears only the faint internal creak of the highlands, the brittle ticking of cooling surfaces, and the soft pressure-sound of the atmosphere passing over the ridges. Then, somewhere beyond sight, the world begins again: a tremor without violence, a glow under the ground, a new black plane lifting slowly toward the dark.

The place has no weather, no seasons, and no visible life cycle. Its cycles are mineral. Expansion, hardening, splitting, settling, rising. It does not preserve what it makes. It does not wear it down, either. Each formation remains raw, close to its origin, until the next movement incorporates it or leaves it stranded on some higher shelf.

The governing rule seems to be that creation is the only form of motion here.

The land does not flow, but it continually becomes. It does not erode, but it continually separates. It does not shine, but it continually reveals a little more of the heat within. In the darkness above the extreme heights, the sparse towers and unfinished walls stand like tentative punctuation marks, waiting for the sentence of the world to find its final shape.
