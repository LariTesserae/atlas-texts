The city occupies a high basin where the mountain has forgotten how to be steep.

From a distance it looks less like a settlement than a dark residue left across the plateau: long lines of towers, roofless sheds, waterworks, gantries, and elevated roads spread over miles of black stone. Nothing is crowded. The structures stand far apart, each one isolated in its own hollow of wet ground, with enormous intervals of bare mineral between them. Streets cross the basin in straight, patient lines and then disappear into vapor. Whole districts may contain only one building.

The altitude is evident in the sky, though the air is hot. Heat rises from the ground in wavering sheets, making the far infrastructure bend and swim. The basin is high enough that clouds pass below its rim, but water is everywhere at the surface: gathered in shallow silver pans, dripping from overhangs, running in narrow channels beside the roads. Rain comes often, but not in storms. It condenses out of the low, warm air and falls as a fine, persistent wetness. The stone sweats. Walls glisten. Pipes bead with water even when nothing flows through them.

There are no plants.

No weeds split the paving. No moss darkens the concrete. No lichen colors the old machinery. The absence has a clean, almost deliberate quality, as if the landscape were being kept sterile by a rule older than the city. In the wet depressions, instead of reeds or grasses, thin mineral films spread across the water and vanish when touched by shadow. Some are dull gray; others flash with oil colors, though they have no oil in them. They may be chemical skins, or the outermost expression of something alive beneath the basin. No one knows, and there is no one here to ask.

The ground is made of basalt, vitrified ash, and pale seams of porous rock. In places, the surface has cooled into broad, folded plates, like a frozen black sea. Centuries of water have polished the exposed edges. Channels have been worn into the stone, but the erosion is old and settled: no raw cliffs, no fresh scars, only rounded cuts and shallow ravines that have become part of the city’s geometry. Water follows them beneath the roads, under the factories, and through the lower levels of buildings.

The settlement was designed around heat extraction.

Its towers are narrow and widely spaced, built from dark concrete and fluted metal. Tall vents rise from them like organ pipes. Some exhale warm vapor at regular intervals; others draw the mist down into their cores with a deep, slow inhalation. The largest structures are not buildings in the ordinary sense but machines scaled to architecture: pressure halls, condensate reservoirs, turbine houses, cooling yards. Their walls are plated in panels that have faded from industrial blue to the color of old bruises. Thick cables cross the open ground between them, suspended high enough to walk beneath. Most hang slack. A few pulse with a dim internal glow.

The city’s industrial logic remains visible even where its purpose has gone. Rails emerge from sealed factories and end in empty loading courts. Cranes stand frozen above immense rectangular pits. Conveyor belts pass through walls and continue into the hills, carrying nothing. There are substations with their doors open to the wet air, rows of ceramic insulators shining like teeth. Every component is oversized for the number of people who once used it. The infrastructure was made for abundance, yet the place now contains almost no presence at all.

The emptiness is not merely abandonment. Space seems to be part of the design.

Buildings are separated by fields of bare stone hundreds of meters wide. A single maintenance hut may sit at the center of a plain, reached by a road that takes half an hour to cross. Towers are placed at distances that make their lights—when they appear—seem like stars fallen to the ground. In the open areas, the eye has trouble judging scale. A drainage channel can look like a trench, a road like a seam, a warehouse like a low hill. The basin extends beyond immediate sight in every direction, its far districts reduced to vertical marks beneath the dim sky.

The light is weak and strangely constant. The sun may be above the clouds, but it never seems to arrive here whole. Illumination filters through vapor and hangs in the air as a gray amber sheen. At times the landscape brightens without casting shadows. At other times the wet pavement reflects a sky that is darker than the sky overhead. Night does not bring a clean change. It is simply a deepening of the same dimness, until warning lamps and furnace windows become the only clear colors.

There are sounds, but they are widely separated.

Water ticks from a high gutter into a basin below. Somewhere far off, a pump starts, runs for three minutes, and stops. Metal contracts inside a cooling tower with a sequence of soft knocks. Cables hum in the damp air. From the industrial quarter comes an intermittent vibration too low to be called music, traveling through the ground more than through the air. When the wind moves across the plateau, it makes no rustling—there is nothing to rustle—but it presses against the empty buildings and produces a long, hollow tone from their vents.

Most of the time, the city is quiet enough that individual sounds seem intentional.

A person crossing the open roads would hear their own footsteps return from distant walls several seconds late. The echoes do not always come from the correct direction. Sometimes they answer from beneath the ground. In the flooded service tunnels, sound travels unusually far, and a dropped tool may be heard moving through passages that have been sealed for decades.

Fauna exists only as an occasional interruption. Small, pale things live near the warm drains, though they are rarely seen clearly. They may be animals adapted to the mineral runoff, or fragments of an older biological system that has reduced itself to movement and appetite. They leave no footprints. On certain mornings, dozens of narrow wet trails appear across the stone, converging on the intake towers and stopping at their foundations.

Larger creatures are reported in the outer basin: long-backed forms glimpsed through steam, moving with a slow mechanical gait; dark shapes standing motionless on distant service roads; a flock—or perhaps a single many-jointed animal—seen passing between the pylons. Nothing remains after such sightings except disturbed water and a faint metallic smell. The city does not support a visible ecosystem. Whatever lives here is sparse, hidden, and patient.

The rules of the place are not entirely physical.

Water runs uphill along certain channels during the hour before dawn. Doors open more easily when approached by someone who has not decided where they are going. Machines that have been silent for years will activate if addressed by their original designation, even when the designation is unknown to the speaker. In the oldest district, shadows collect beneath elevated walkways and remain there after the objects that cast them have moved.

The city’s maps are unreliable. Roads shorten or lengthen according to weather. A factory may contain an interior courtyard larger than its exterior dimensions permit. From the upper floors of some towers, the surrounding basin can be seen in full—but never the tower itself. Water levels change in response not to rainfall but to distant sounds. If the deep vibration under the industrial quarter stops, every reservoir in the city drains at once, though no outlet can be found.

Still, nothing feels chaotic. The strangeness has settled into the landscape as thoroughly as the erosion. The city is not breaking apart. It is continuing in a reduced mode, executing old processes with immense patience. Pumps circulate unseen water. Vents breathe into the dimness. Signals pass between empty substations. Doors unlock and lock. Far below the basalt, heat moves through the rock in slow currents, and the whole high basin seems to be a machine built around that movement.

At the edge of the settlement, the roads end at a low ridge of black stone. Beyond it lies open plateau, wet and barren, dropping away into clouds. The city’s last towers stand behind you at great distances, their vapor columns rising without urgency. Ahead, there is only the glimmer of shallow water, the dark mineral ground, and occasional lights beneath the surface—small, steady lights traveling through channels where no cables have been laid.
