The settlement occupies a shallow depression in a warm, flat country where the soil has long ago forgotten how to be bare.

From a distance it appears to be a low green bowl, no larger than a village, sunk into the plain. Its rim is marked by blunt, weathered trunks—perhaps trees, perhaps the remains of towers—standing shoulder to shoulder around the hollow. They have been worn smooth by centuries of rain and wind. Their bark has the dull color of old clay, but it is not bark: it is a layered skin of living fibers, continually knitting itself shut over cracks and scars.

Inside the bowl, vegetation is not merely present. It is the ground, the walls, the streets, the roofs, and most of the air’s visible substance.

Broad mats of moss and succulent vine make a pavement beneath the feet, springy and warm. They grow around the old paths rather than across them, leaving narrow channels of pale, polished root exposed. These paths divide the settlement into rooms and courtyards. The arrangement is unmistakably urban—lanes, thresholds, plazas, stairways, drainage gutters—but every structure has been grown rather than built. A row of tall reed-pillars supports a roof of overlapping leaves. A market square is enclosed by folded fronds, each rib as thick as a human arm. Houses are hollow seedpods, their entrances opening and closing with the humidity. Bridges are braided roots stretched between living walls.

There are no tools, wheels, wires, or machines. Nothing has been cut. The settlement’s architecture is the result of long obedience between plants and the people—or animals—that once tended them. Even that history is difficult to separate from the present. A wall may lean toward a footpath because it remembers being used as a wall. A stairway may sprout one step higher after a season of heavy rain.

The structures are old, but not ruined. Erosion has settled into them as a form of design. Rain has rounded every corner. Wind has hollowed shallow bowls into roofs and carved channels down columns. Roots have entered the joints and become the joints. There is no distinction between decay and maintenance. A collapsed house becomes a low garden; the garden thickens into a wall; the wall eventually opens into a house again.

For all the abundance of plant matter, the place feels strangely empty.

The vegetation forms a near-continuous skin, but it leaves enormous intervals of clear air between its major forms. A vine may cover an entire courtyard while rising only ankle-high. A single tree-like stalk may occupy a whole chamber, its crown far above, with nothing around it for several dozen paces. The lanes are broad and silent-looking, the plazas larger than the settlement seems to require. The architecture has the proportions of a city built for crowds, but almost no one is visible.

Then the sound begins again.

It never stops. The entire hollow is deafening with biological noise: the grinding rush of leaves rubbing together, the wet snapping of seedpods, the explosive release of spores, the shrill pulse of insects hidden in the walls. Some plants whistle through narrow vents in their stems. Others produce deep, sustained tones when the wind passes through their hollow trunks. The sound gathers in the bowl until it becomes almost physical, a pressure against the ribs and teeth.

At intervals, the settlement gives a collective cry.

It starts as a tremor beneath the feet, then rises through the roots and walls into a vast, many-pitched bellow. Roofs flex. Hanging tendrils whip back and forth. The sound is too large for the small hollow that contains it. It seems to come from underground, from the sky, and from several moments at once. During these events, every leaf turns in the same direction, though there is no visible wind.

The cause may be rain.

Moisture arrives irregularly here. Most of the time the air is warm and dry enough for dust to collect in the grooves of the living pavement. Then clouds pass over the plain, and a brief, violent shower pours into the bowl. The plants open instantly. Their surfaces bead with condensation; gutters swell and run; hidden basins fill beneath the roots. For a few hours, water is everywhere—streaming down walls, dripping from the undersides of leaves, churning through channels that were dry a moment before.

Some of it does not fall downward.

Thin rivers climb the sides of pillars. Droplets pause in midair beneath roofs, trembling in long suspended rows. A puddle may reflect a sky different from the one above it: a violet dusk, a white noon, a sky crowded with enormous slow-moving shadows. When the rain stops, the water retreats into the vegetation with equal speed. It is drawn upward through walls and pavement, leaving only dark stains and a smell like crushed stems.

The ecology is abundant but not crowded. Small animals move everywhere, though rarely in open view. Pale, many-jointed things flicker through the moss. Winged creatures nest inside the seed-houses and pour out in black clouds when disturbed. Long-bodied grazers browse the walls, consuming only the newest growth and leaving the older architecture intact. Predators follow them through the upper leaves. Their movements cause sudden disturbances in the green: a shiver passing from roof to roof, a burst of wings, a chorus of snapping stems.

Some animals are difficult to classify because the plants behave like animals around them. A flowering wall recoils from a passing shadow. A tree opens a row of soft mouths to catch insects. In one courtyard, a cluster of red leaves repeatedly turns toward an empty doorway, tracking something that has not entered yet.

The rules of the place are mostly consistent until they are not.

Distances change when measured by sound. A bell-like flower at the end of a lane may be reached in ten steps, or in an hour of walking, while its note remains equally loud. Shadows sometimes detach from their owners and continue moving through the vegetation. A person—or an animal—can pass behind a curtain of leaves and emerge older, smaller, or carrying the scent of rain that has not yet fallen.

The settlement seems to accommodate these failures of reality rather than resist them. Doorways lead to the same rooms from different directions. Stairways descend into upper levels. A courtyard seen through a window may contain the window itself, growing in a wall across the square. Nothing appears broken by this. The plants simply keep growing around the impossible geometry, as roots grow around stones.

At the center is a low, empty plaza. No tower rises there, no tree claims it. Its surface is a dense, dark green membrane stretched over a shallow depression. The membrane trembles constantly, producing the deepest sound in the settlement. Around it stand the largest buildings: broad, open chambers with vaulted roofs of pale leaves. Their entrances are unguarded. Their interiors are cool, damp, and almost completely bare except for veins running through the walls like faded streets on a map.

The plaza may once have been a civic space. It may still be one. Whenever the living city cries out, the sound seems to originate there, and every plant in the hollow answers.

Above the rim, the surrounding plain is nearly treeless and sun-baked. Below it, in this small depression, the world has become all plant: substrate, structure, weather, animal habitat, memory. The settlement does not appear abandoned. It appears to be waiting for inhabitants who have been replaced by roots, echoes, and the slow, tireless activity of green life.
