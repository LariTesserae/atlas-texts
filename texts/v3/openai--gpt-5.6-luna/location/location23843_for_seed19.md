The high country begins where the ordinary world ends.

It is not a range of peaks, though it has the altitude of one. The land has been lifted so long ago that its mountains have forgotten how to be mountains. Their ridges are broad, level-backed, and worn smooth as the knuckles of an enormous hand. Valleys lie between them, but they are shallow valleys, more like wrinkles pressed into a single immense slab. From any height, the region resembles a broken tabletop: gray uplands, pale shelves, dark mineral seams, and a horizon that continues in every direction until distance loses its meaning.

The air is cool and thin. It has the clean, metallic dryness of a sealed room, though there is no ceiling and no visible boundary to the sky. Breathing leaves the mouth as a brief white thread—not from severe cold, but because the air has almost no moisture to accept. There is no dew. Frost does not form. Snow, if it ever existed here, was ground down ages ago and removed from the world in some fashion that no one remembers.

Underfoot, the surface is made of ancient stone worn to a fine, hard skin. In places it is black and glassy, veined with dull copper and green-gray deposits. In others, the ground has powdered into gray-white dust that does not rise when disturbed. It settles too quickly, as though falling toward a deeper gravity. Salt-like crusts gather in the shallow basins, though they are not salt: they taste of chalk, iron, and something bitterly electric. Some flats bear polygonal cracks, but the cracks have no depth. They are merely dark lines on the surface, like the memory of dryness rather than dryness itself.

The country is densely occupied.

Not by forests or grass. The vegetation is sparse, low, and difficult to distinguish from mineral growth. Here and there, wiry black stalks emerge from seams in the rock, each no taller than a handspan. Their leaves are translucent plates folded edgewise to the light. They never bend in the wind, because there is no wind. A few pale mats cling to sheltered ledges, resembling lichen, fur, or old frost. They spread neither outward nor upward. Their boundaries are exact. A patch may have remained the same size for centuries, preserving a perfect scalloped edge while the stone around it continues to weather.

Most of the living surface belongs to the animals.

They are everywhere, layered over one another in a quiet abundance that makes the land seem solidly alive. Long-bodied grazers stand in the shallow valleys, shoulder to shoulder, their narrow feet sunk into the dust. They are hairless or covered in short felt, with folded necks and faces built for scraping mineral films from stone. Their ribs show, but not from hunger. They have always looked that way. Between their legs move smaller things: many-jointed crawlers, blind burrowers, pale shells that open and close without mouths.

The slopes are covered with resting forms. Some cling to the rock in dense clusters like dark scales. Some hang beneath overhangs, suspended from nothing visible. Others occupy the same patch of ground as the larger beasts without acknowledging them. Their bodies overlap. A translucent animal may pass through the flank of a horned one and leave no wound, no disturbance, not even a flicker of attention. In the distance, entire ridgelines shift with the slow rearrangement of backs, wings, antennae, and jointed limbs.

Yet the multitude is almost entirely silent.

There is no roar, no birdcall, no insect drone. The animals do not vocalize, or their voices have become too low and too old for the air to carry. Occasionally a stone gives a small click beneath a foot. Occasionally a procession of tiny creatures passes over a mineral shelf, producing a faint dry brushing, like paper dragged across paper. If one listens for long enough, a deeper sound can be found beneath these: not a hum, exactly, but the pressure of innumerable bodies maintaining their positions.

They are not still. They are simply moving without consequence.

A herd will take one step over the course of an afternoon and remain there for a thousand years. A colony will rotate its members through a shelter, though no individual seems to leave. One creature may slowly raise its head toward a cloudless sky, and the gesture will remain unfinished forever. The environment has reached an equilibrium so complete that movement no longer produces change. Footprints do not accumulate. Shed skins do not decay. A dead animal remains present, and after enough time begins to host living animals without ceasing to be dead.

The old erosion is visible everywhere. It has not merely worn things down; it has simplified them. Boulders have become low rounded islands. Canyons have become shallow gutters. Former cliffs are inclined planes ending in broad shelves. The land appears ancient beyond geology, as though its roughness has been repeatedly rubbed away by eras that left no sediment behind. Nothing collapses. Nothing grows into a new form. Even the loose stones seem to have arrived at their final resting places.

Scattered across this open region are traces of habitation.

A low wall crosses one plateau, built from flat slabs fitted without mortar. It is too short to shelter a person and too straight to be natural. A circle of stakes stands nearby, each stake petrified at the lower end and wrapped in strips of faded fiber. No settlement surrounds them. There are no roads, no fields, no graves. Far off, on the shoulder of a gray ridge, a solitary structure leans against the horizon: a hut or shrine made of hide, bone, and thin hammered plates. Its doorway is sealed. The material of its roof has not weathered, though everything else has.

In one basin stands a ladder descending into the ground. It has only seven rungs. The bottom cannot be seen, but the ladder is no longer than a person’s height. Anyone who climbs down reaches the same stone surface from below, emerging a few paces away, facing the opposite direction. The locals—if there are locals—have arranged small cairns around it, though the cairns have identical stones and identical shadows no matter where the sun stands.

The light is ordinary at first. It comes from a pale sun, casts shadows, and reveals color without distortion. But the shadows are too stable. They do not lengthen noticeably across the day. A creature standing on a ridge may cast a shadow into a basin below, even when the angle of the sun should place it behind the creature. Some shadows contain details not present in their owners: extra limbs, open mouths, branching antlers, a second body standing close beside the first.

At the horizon, distance behaves poorly. A nearby shelf may take hours to reach, while a far ridge can be touched after only a few steps if one approaches it from the wrong direction. The broad plateau is genuinely vast, but its scale is not consistent. A line of animals may extend for miles and also be visible as a dense band beneath one’s feet. Looking away from a landmark can make it multiply. Looking back can restore it, though not always the original one.

The sky remains clear for days, then for centuries. Sometimes it contains a thin dark seam, as though the blue has split along a fold. Through that seam one can glimpse another version of the plateau: greener, flooded, or covered in structures. The seam never widens. Nothing crosses between the skies. Once, perhaps, rain fell through it. Now only the memory of rain arrives—a coolness on the face, a scent of wet stone—and the ground remains dry.

There are no weather fronts here. No seasons. No visible night.

The light dims at irregular intervals, but never fully disappears. During these dimmings, the animals become more numerous. Forms emerge from the stone, from the shadows of other forms, from the blank spaces between one object and the next. When the light returns, the population is again merely overwhelming rather than impossible. No one can say whether the extra creatures have departed or have simply become difficult to see.

A traveler crossing the high country would find no path, yet would rarely be alone. The animals would part just enough to allow passage, closing behind without sound. Their bodies would press close on either side, warm in some places, cold in others, and occasionally absent to the hand despite being plainly visible. The ground would remain level beneath every step, even where the terrain sloped upward. The horizon would hold steady. The old walls would remain old. The sparse black plants would keep their perfect borders.

Nothing would threaten the traveler in the usual sense.

Nothing would pursue, hunger, weather, or decay.

But after enough time, the traveler might notice that one of the distant creatures has been matching their position. It stands on the same ridge whenever they look. Then it appears in the next valley. Then beside the abandoned wall. It does not approach. It does not move.

It is simply occupying the same place in the landscape from a different direction.

And the high country, dense with life and nearly empty of sound, continues without alteration: a cool, ancient plateau where erosion has finished, where change has been exhausted, and where reality itself has settled into a crowded, silent final form.
