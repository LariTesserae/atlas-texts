The plain begins where the last ordinary ground gives up.

It is low country, nearly level, warm enough that the air never quite cools, though it is not wet in any continuous way. Moisture arrives as events. A rainstorm may pass in minutes, striking the leaves so hard that the whole landscape becomes a white, rattling blur; afterward, the water vanishes almost at once, drawn into stems, bark, mats of root, and hanging reservoirs. Between storms the soil is dry and powdery, but every surface shines with condensation. Dawn gathers in the hollows of leaves. Long ropes of moss exhale mist. Entire avenues drip from no visible source.

There is no naked earth for long. What looks like ground is usually a broad, living membrane: interlocked runners, flattened reeds, pale root-felt, and thick leaves layered over one another until they form a floor. It yields under pressure with the slow, muscular flex of an animal, though there are no animals here. Beneath it, the original land is young and unsettled—freshly risen shelves of dark mineral, raw cuts, steep little escarpments, and bright splinters of stone. The erosion is recent enough that the terrain still seems unfinished. Ridges end abruptly. Channels have no settled direction. A bank can stand vertical for years, then unfold into a stairway of roots overnight.

Vegetation has not occupied the landscape so much as replaced it.

The plants are the walls, roads, roofs, bridges, towers, drains, and weather. Some grow as immense columns, their trunks hollow and ribbed, opening into chambers large enough to contain a neighborhood. Others spread as sheets across the ground, their veins raised into curbs and embankments. Vines knot themselves into suspended walkways between the columns. Broad fronds overlap overhead in several green levels, making an urban canopy whose streets are not beneath it but inside it. A route may pass through a tunnel of folded leaves, climb the spiral grain of a living trunk, cross a bridge made from braided tendrils, and emerge onto a high, sunlit terrace of moss.

The settlement is everywhere and nowhere. It has the density of a city without the solidity of construction. Blocks swell, split, merge, and lean against one another. Green towers bud from plazas and then close their entrances. A district may be recognizable by its arrangement of tall stalks one morning and become a basin of low, flowering tissue by evening. There are courtyards filled with shallow water, though no channels lead to them. There are staircases that grow upward only while someone is looking at them. There are long, straight avenues whose edges are marked by ranks of identical trees—until the avenue bends, not through space, but through age, and the trees become seedlings, then seeds, then a memory of trees.

The built forms retain the logic of architecture but not its permanence. Doorways are dark slits in trunks. Windows are translucent membranes stretched between branches. Roofs are overlapping fans that close against rain and open again in sunlight. Walls thicken where they are touched. Foundations migrate toward moisture. No tool, machine, or artifact has ever been used here; every structure is made by growth, pressure, attraction, and the strange civic instinct of plants to arrange themselves into habitation.

There are no animals. No insects tremble in the flowers, no birds cross the canopy, no worms turn the soil. Flowers open for no pollinator. Fruit forms, ripens, and collapses into new roots without being eaten. Some plants reproduce by leaning toward one another until their tissues fuse. Others shed entire neighborhoods of spores that rise in dense green clouds, hang in the warm air, and descend somewhere else as walls, forests, or streets.

The absence of animals makes the vegetation seem less peaceful, not more. Every motion belongs to the plants, and there are too many motions to distinguish. Leaves clap and twist. Tendrils recoil from shadows. Root systems push up through the flooring in long, deliberate ridges. A stand of reeds folds flat all at once, then rises in a different arrangement. Vast carpets of groundcover ripple without wind, responding to pressures too slow or too vast to perceive.

The sound is deafening.

Rain striking the canopy is like a million hands beating on metal, though there is no metal anywhere. When the weather is clear, the noise does not cease. Stems creak under internal growth. Seedpods burst with reports like distant gunfire. Hollow trunks boom as air moves through them. Leaves rasp together in layered, continuous waves. Roots crack the mineral beneath the settlement with sounds like stone splitting in a quarry. Somewhere, always, a mass of vegetation is collapsing, blooming, dividing, or forcing itself through a narrow place.

The loudest sounds come from growth. In the central districts, great vascular columns pulse audibly. They draw water from hidden reservoirs with a deep, rhythmic suction, a subterranean heartbeat too large to belong to anything. Whole avenues rise by fractions of an inch with a wet, fibrous groan. When they settle, the impact travels through the living ground and returns as a shuddering chorus from every connected root.

The light is ordinary sunlight. That is one of the few ordinary things. It falls warm and direct through gaps in the canopy, illuminates wet leaves, and makes familiar shadows. Yet the shadows do not always agree with their objects. A tower may cast the shadow of a sapling. A leaf may cast the outline of a doorway. At noon, some shadows point toward the sun, while others remain attached to structures that have already moved. Nothing in the light itself is strange; it is the relationship between light and place that has come loose.

Distance is unreliable. The region extends broadly: districts, reservoirs, open green squares, collapsed groves, raw stone shelves, and belts of vegetation reaching toward a horizon that should be visible but rarely is. A person—or whatever might enter this place—can walk in a straight line and return to the same plaza without turning. A distant tower can remain distant for hours while its roots rise beside the traveler. From a high terrace, the settlement appears to spread over a wide, shallow basin. From within it, the basin seems to contain several different climates, though all are warm and all are breathing moisture.

Water is not a permanent feature but a recurring interruption. It gathers in leaf-bowls, root-cisterns, cracks in the young stone, and low districts where the living pavement has sagged. Some pools last only until the plants around them open their pores. Others remain for years, dark and perfectly still, reflecting a canopy that does not exist above them. During rain, streets become streams; during the dry intervals, the streams are lifted away through curtains of roots and released as mist from distant towers. The settlement circulates water like thought, never showing the whole process at once.

The world’s rules are biological, but biology here has become a kind of unstable geometry.

Growth is contagious to form. If one wall develops an arch, nearby walls begin to curve. If a street floods, all roads in the district may acquire channels. If a plant is cut—or merely imagined as cut—it may divide. Naming a structure can cause it to produce an entrance. Looking at a distant place makes it more likely to appear nearby. Neglect has physical consequences: unattended buildings unravel into runners, while frequently visited paths harden into woody ridges.

Nothing truly dies. A mature structure can collapse, but its collapse is another stage of construction. The fallen tower becomes a wetland; the wetland dries into a mat; the mat lifts itself into a new district. Decay is rapid, bright, and productive. Pale tissues bloom from black ones. Green dust rises from ruptured trunks. A whole neighborhood may dissolve in an afternoon, only to reappear as a dense stand of translucent grass several miles away.

The greatest instability is in the open spaces. There, with no canopy to constrain it, vegetation changes almost continuously. A plaza can fill with waist-high stalks, then erupt into a forest of branching columns, then flatten into a single enormous leaf whose central vein becomes a boulevard. The transitions are not smooth. They happen in bursts, accompanied by thunderous snaps and waves of pressure. The ground rolls. Buildings buckle into one another. New terrain arrives already weathered, already covered in mature growth, as if some other version of the landscape has been forced through the present one.

And still, beneath the noise and motion, the place possesses an order.

The plants lean toward water. They gather around warmth. They preserve routes, chambers, and patterns that have proven useful, though usefulness may last only a season. Certain groves always become bridges. Certain pools always evaporate upward. Certain towers always open at sunset, exposing interiors lined with soft green light. The landscape is not conscious in any recognizable sense, but it behaves as though it is continually solving a problem: how to become more landscape, more structure, more alive, with every surface occupied and every empty space converted into growth.

At the far edge of the settlement, where the living streets thin into open vegetation, the ground rises in sharp, unfinished shelves. Beyond them lies a broad expanse of green under normal daylight. No animals move there. No machines hum. The only signs of travel are paths that form under attention and vanish behind the traveler.

Then the rain begins again—not from clouds, but from the leaves themselves.

The canopy tightens. The plazas flood. The towers draw breath. Every plant in the region turns slightly toward the sound of water, and the entire city-forest starts to rearrange itself.
