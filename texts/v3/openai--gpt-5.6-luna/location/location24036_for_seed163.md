The plateau begins above the weather.

It is not a summit, exactly, but a broad unfinished shelf of pale stone suspended high along the mountain’s shoulder. Beyond it, the land drops away in long blue distances: folded ridges, cloud-filled valleys, occasional flashes of snow. The air is cold enough to keep every surface hard and clear, but not cold enough to kill the wetness. Water lies close beneath the ground. It beads on stone, shines in hollows, and gathers in thin, wandering sheets that have not yet decided where their channels should be.

The rock is newly exposed—gray, cream, and faintly green, with faces too clean to have been weathered into character. There are no deep gullies, no talus slopes, no ancient scars. The plateau seems less eroded than revealed, as if a great covering had only recently been lifted away. Its edges remain blunt and provisional. Cracks stop abruptly. Ridges do not taper so much as end. In places, the stone is still pushing itself into shape, rising in low ribs and domes under the living cover.

And the living cover is almost everywhere.

Plants have not colonized this place so much as become its second geology. Low mats of silver-green growth spread across the rock, thick as felt and springy underfoot. Long, jointed grasses rise in isolated stands, each blade narrow and glossy, their upper surfaces catching the white daylight. There are dark cushions of moss around every seep, and broad, translucent leaves that overlap one another like roof tiles. Some plants grow as columns, hollow and reedlike, twenty or thirty meters apart from their neighbors. Others form low bridges over wet fractures, their roots braided into load-bearing cords.

Yet the vegetation is sparse in the way a forest can be sparse when it is very large. There is space between each presence: a hundred paces of bare stone between one tower of grass and the next, a wide interval between moss islands, an empty slope shining with rain. The emptiness is not barren. It is occupied by roots below the surface, by dormant seeds, by pale threads of fungal tissue, by water moving in darkness. The place has the feeling of an ecosystem paused between breaths.

The plants are architecture without intending to be. A stand of reeds has thickened into a windbreak, its stems locked together by natural resin. A creeping vine has crossed a ravine and hardened into a narrow suspended walkway, though no one uses it. In one hollow, a ring of enormous leaves has cupped itself into a basin. Rainwater trembles there beneath a skin of pollen. Around it, tiny flowers open and close with the slow regularity of valves.

Nothing grows quickly. The landscape is intensely alive but nearly motionless.

Leaves turn by degrees. Pools fill over hours. The roots under the plateau advance a few centimeters each season. A fallen stalk remains upright for years, gradually hollowed from within, until one day it leans against another and forms part of a new wall. There is little decay visible. Dead vegetation is pulled beneath the mats and incorporated into the ground, leaving only smooth bulges and occasional pale ribs. The entire plateau appears to be constructing itself, one patient layer at a time.

Water is the only thing that moves with urgency.

It comes down from the high air as fine, cold rain, often under a sky so bright that the droplets look like falling threads of glass. It runs from the upper slopes in shining films. It leaks directly from the stone walls. It collects beneath the moss and reappears elsewhere, making springs that emerge without warning from apparently dry ground. In the morning, every plant is lacquered with condensation. By midday, the sun has turned the whole plateau into an expanse of reflected light.

The brightness is nearly physical. There are few shadows deep enough to hide in. The sky is a severe blue-white, and the clouds below the plateau throw up a diffuse glow from beneath, so that the cliffs seem lit from both directions. Wet stones glare. The leaves flash silver, green, and blue. At a distance, the scattered vegetation resembles a pattern of polished machinery laid across the mountain.

There is machinery here.

A line of narrow metal towers crosses one section of the shelf, each one standing alone in the open like a survey marker. Cables pass between them, sagging slightly in the wet air. Some carry power. Others carry nothing yet. Small insulated pipes emerge from the ground near the springs and lead toward a single low building at the plateau’s center.

The building is industrial but modest: a corrugated shell, pale with mineral staining, anchored into the rock on short legs. One wall has been swallowed by climbing plants. Their stems have entered through seams in the metal and spread across the interior, where they turn slowly around warm pipes. There are no roads, only a track of compacted stone running between the structure and the nearest drop. No vehicles are present, though the dark rectangular marks of their tires remain in the moss.

The installation seems to harvest the plateau’s water—or perhaps its pressure. Deep beneath the living mats, pumps thrum at a frequency too low to hear clearly, felt instead in the soles of the feet. A tall exhaust stack releases a constant white vapor that does not rise. It drifts horizontally, joining the clouds below. Occasionally the whole building shudders, and a distant valve opens with a prolonged metallic groan.

The sound of the place is overwhelming.

Wind passes through the tall plants in waves, and each kind of stem produces a different note: a hard rattling from the dry reeds, a deep hollow moan from the columnar growth, a high continuous hiss from the blade fields. When a gust crosses the plateau, these sounds arrive in sequence, as though something vast is running its fingers over a collection of instruments. The water adds its own noise—hundreds of streams slipping over stone, springs clicking into shallow basins, rain striking leaves with a sound like thrown gravel.

Then there is the machinery: the subsonic pulse of pumps, the rotation of distant turbines, the loose clatter of metal panels, the periodic release of pressure. All of it combines into a deafening, sustained roar. Conversation would be impossible even at arm’s length. Birds, if there were birds, would have to signal with light or movement.

There are very few animals.

Small pale insects occasionally appear around the flowers, but they seem less like pollinators than brief visitors blown up from the valleys. A dark shape may move beneath a mat of moss. Once, far across the plateau, something antlered and thin is seen standing among the reeds, perfectly still, until rain or distance erases it. No tracks remain for long. The water and the vegetation close over them.

The world here is otherwise ordinary in its strangeness. Gravity behaves. Fire burns, though poorly in the wet air. Metal rusts along every exposed edge. The plants follow light, water, and warmth. They do not move when watched. They do not bloom in patterns or speak through the machinery. Their apparent architecture is simply the result of growing in a place where wind, water, and open space have selected for strength.

But the plateau has one rule that becomes clear after time:

Anything left here is eventually made partially plant.

A dropped length of cable disappears beneath a mat of roots. A broken tool becomes furred with moss. The foundations of the industrial building are beginning to swell with green tissue. Even the survey towers have been fitted with living braces, not by workers but by stems that found their load-bearing points and thickened there. The stone itself is not being covered so much as joined.

This is a high, wet, bright place at the beginning of its history. It has not yet gathered the scars that make landscapes seem old. Its streams are tentative. Its vegetation is widespread but not crowded. Its machines are few and isolated, placed like temporary instruments in a region that has not decided whether it wants to be a factory, a garden, or a mountain.

For now it is all three.

Beyond the last tower, the plateau continues toward a broad white ridge. Between here and there lie sheets of wet stone, scattered plant-pillars, shallow green basins, and long stretches of dazzling emptiness. The wind rises. The reeds answer. Beneath the ground, pumps and roots work together in the dark, slowly drawing water through the unfinished mountain.
