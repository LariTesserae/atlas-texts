At the upper edge of the world, where the mountain has risen beyond weather, there is a basin called the **Long Hollow**.

It is not a valley in the ordinary sense. It is too high, too level, and too quiet in its shape. The basin lies on a broad shelf of black stone between several summits, each one blunt and immense, their upper faces hidden in a permanent lid of dim cloud. Nothing here appears to have been recently formed. The cliffs are rounded by a patient erosion that has worn them smooth without lowering them, as though the weather has been touching the same surfaces for a million years and has never once changed its mind.

The ground is mostly dark slate and pale mineral crust, fractured into plates the size of doors. Between the plates, moisture gathers in narrow seams. It does not flow downhill. It appears in the cracks, beads along ledges, fills shallow bowls in the rock, and remains there. Some pools are no wider than a hand; others occupy entire hollows, black and perfectly still. Their surfaces are often filmed with ice, though the ice does not necessarily form from the edges inward. Sometimes it is found in the center, a clear round disk surrounded by unfrozen water. Sometimes a pool is frozen beneath a layer of liquid, and the water moves slowly over the transparent floor as if the two states have forgotten which should be above the other.

Rain falls here from time to time, but it is difficult to say whether the rain belongs to the sky. A gray veil will descend over one portion of the basin, striking the stone with a loud, dry rattling, while a nearby ridge remains untouched. The droplets are cold enough to sting the skin, yet they leave no darkening on the rock. Hours later, moisture will be found clinging to the undersides of stones that the rain never reached.

The air is thin, and the cold has a mineral quality. Breathing tastes faintly of iron. Every exposed surface is pale with rime: the backs of stones, the stems of the few plants, the broken walls of old shelters. Frost does not soften the landscape. It outlines it. It gives every crack, grain, and raised edge a white emphasis, making the basin seem less like terrain than a diagram of terrain.

Vegetation survives only in scattered concessions. There are low black mosses in the damp seams, silver lichen spreading over the rocks in slow circular stains, and small grasslike tufts whose blades are translucent and rigid. They do not bend in the wind. When the air moves, they remain straight, but their shadows lean away. In a few sheltered depressions grow stalks no taller than a finger, each topped with a closed, colorless flower. The flowers never open, yet sometimes the basin smells of pollen.

There are no trees. There is no continuous green. From the highest ridge, the whole region looks almost empty: miles of dark stone, snowless despite the cold, interrupted by isolated stains of moss and a handful of water hollows. The emptiness is not hostile so much as complete. It leaves no sense that something is missing. The land has achieved its proper amount of life.

And yet the basin is loud.

The sound comes first from the ground. A deep, continuous vibration passes through the slate, too low to hear in the ordinary way but strong enough to tremble in the ribs. Over it lies a vast rushing, like surf heard through a wall. There is no ocean below, and no river large enough to make such a sound. The nearest visible water is motionless.

At intervals, the stones crack—not sharply, but with a drawn-out report, as if some enormous joint were being opened. The noise crosses the basin long after the stone has settled. A slab may split beneath one’s feet, and the sound will come from the far mountains several minutes later. On other occasions, the reverse occurs: a thunderous fracture rolls down from the cliffs, and only afterward does a hairline appear in the ground nearby.

Wind is present without being felt. It roars continuously through the high passes, though the air around a person may remain still. The roar has layers. Beneath it are voices, or things resembling voices: long vowel sounds, muffled calls, the scrape of breath in a throat too large for any animal. They seem to originate from the empty intervals between objects. If one shouts, the answer may return before the shout has been made.

The basin’s walls are marked by old structures. There are perhaps nine of them, scattered so widely that they seem accidental. Each is built from the same dark slate as the ground, though the stones have been fitted without visible mortar. Most are low, roofless enclosures, half sunk into the earth. One is a narrow tower leaning against a cliff, its upper half missing. Another is a shelter with a doorway that opens onto solid stone. A third consists only of four upright slabs around a shallow pool.

No tools, metal, tracks, or signs of machinery remain. The builders—if builders they were—left behind no useful objects. Their work has become indistinguishable from the landscape except where it contradicts the landscape. A wall may continue upward into the air for several feet before ending. A stairway descends into the ground but can be climbed from below. One ruin casts a shadow in the dim light, but the shadow belongs to a structure that is not there.

The most intact building stands alone near the basin’s center. It resembles a small, square house with no windows. Its walls are coated in white frost, and its door is permanently open. Inside is a room no larger than a closet, though the doorway leads into a darkness that extends farther than the basin should allow. Sound changes near it. The ground’s vibration diminishes. The rushing wind becomes a thin, distant thread. For a few steps, a person can hear the private noises of their own body: the heart, the blood, the small shift of bones.

Nothing lives inside the house. Nothing can be seen there. But footprints made in the frost outside sometimes continue through the doorway and emerge from the wall of a ruin half a mile away.

Fauna is rare and poorly defined. Once in a long while, a pale bird passes overhead, flying without moving its wings. It may remain in view for hours, always the same distance away, even as the observer walks. Small four-legged animals have been seen near the pools, but they are usually glimpsed only as disturbances in the frost. Their tracks begin and end abruptly. A few have been found frozen upright, perfectly preserved, their bodies hollow and filled with clear water.

There are also dark shapes on the distant slopes. They stand in groups of three or four, too tall to be mountain goats and too narrow to be people. They do not approach. They do not flee. Through the dimness they appear to be stones, until one notices that their shadows are facing the wrong direction.

The light never becomes bright. Even when the cloud cover thins, the sky offers only a diluted gray radiance. There is no visible sun. Shadows are weak but not absent, and they do not always agree with the position of the light. The basin’s central ruin casts a shadow toward the east in the morning, toward the west in the afternoon, and upward at night—though night is only marginally darker than day.

At the edges of the Long Hollow, distance loses reliability. A summit may seem close enough to touch, then remain equally close after half a day of walking. A pool glimpsed on the horizon can be reached in ten minutes, or found to contain the entire route taken to reach it, reflected in miniature beneath its surface. Looking across the basin, one may see oneself standing on the opposite ridge, watching the basin. That figure does not imitate movement. Sometimes it has already turned away.

Still, the place is not constantly changing. This is its most disturbing quality.

The same vapor hangs over the same notch in the mountains. The same fracture lies open in the same slab. Water gathers in the same hollows and does not evaporate. Frost remains on stones warmed by no sun and on stones touched by no cold. No avalanche falls, though the slopes are steep and loaded with old snow. No plant visibly grows. No ruin collapses. The few animals, if they are animals, do not age or migrate.

Time passes only in the mind of a visitor.

After an hour, the basin feels ancient. After a day, it feels eternal. After longer—if longer is possible—the landscape begins to suggest that the visitor is the changing thing, moving through a place that has already decided what every moment will contain.

The Long Hollow is not frozen in time. It is more exact than time. It holds a single condition and repeats it without repetition: damp stone, thin air, pale frost, sparse life, distant thunder, and the immense, unbroken noise of a world that has settled into an equilibrium too strange to be called natural.
