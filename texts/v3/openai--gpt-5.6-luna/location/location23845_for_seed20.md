The place is a small highland drowned beneath a warm, moving sea.

Not deep enough to be dark in the ordinary way, nor shallow enough to belong to the shore. The whole landscape occupies perhaps the size of a village square, though its edges refuse to remain where they are. A shelf of newly risen stone—black, pale, and violently angular—breaks through the water at intervals, vanishing, returning, and sometimes extending itself while watched. Its highest points stand several body-lengths above the surrounding current, like the roofline of a submerged chapel. They are not islands. They are the exposed upper teeth of something geological and unfinished.

Water pours over everything.

It comes not only from above, though above there is a constant warm rain, dense enough to resemble a translucent wall. It falls through the water already occupying the basin, making a second ocean inside the first. Vertical streams descend from no visible cloud and strike the stone with such force that the plateau seems to be growing in reverse. Every impact throws up boiling veils of bubbles and pale mineral dust. The water is warm against the skin—almost bathlike—but its turbulence has the violence of a mountain river in flood.

There is no stable horizon. The surrounding sea rises in ridges, then folds inward. Currents run sideways across the face of the stone, upward along its cliffs, and occasionally toward the sky. A wave may pass over the entire place without wetting it, leaving behind the sound and pressure of inundation but no change in the rock. Another may arrive as a solid, transparent wall, carrying suspended gravel, fragments of shell, and long silver threads of unknown origin. When it breaks, it does not collapse outward. It splinters into separate vertical sheets that hang in the air for several seconds before remembering gravity.

The stone itself is young. Its surfaces are fresh-cut, raw, and sharp, as though erosion has only just begun to teach them softness. Ribs of basalt or something resembling basalt thrust through layers of chalk-white material. The white stone bruises blue where the water strikes it. Black stone glows faintly red in its cracks, not with heat but with the suggestion of heat, like an ember recalled from a dream. Edges migrate. A ledge that was knife-thin at one moment swells into a blunt shelf at the next, then fractures into a stairway descending nowhere.

In several places, the rock has formed chambers: little hollows, archways, and steep-sided basins no larger than rooms. Their interiors are dim even when the rain above brightens. Light arrives diluted and greenish, filtered through layers of water, vapor, and something less physical. It pools rather than travels. A person standing in one hollow might cast a shadow toward the light, another shadow away from it, and a third shadow beneath the stone.

The dimness is not peaceful. It is full of motion.

Everywhere, water roars.

The sound is so great that it becomes material. It presses against the ribs. It shakes loose pebbles from the ledges. It fills the mouth with a metallic taste. There is the continuous thunder of the rain-wall, the grinding of fresh rock beneath currents, the explosive percussion of submerged collapses, and the vast muffled bellow of water moving through passages too large to see. At close range, individual sounds become impossible to separate. A falling slab, a distant crack of weather, and the cry of a living thing all arrive as one blunt concussion.

Sometimes the roar develops words—not words that can be understood, but patterns that briefly imitate language. A sequence of impacts may sound like a warning repeated by many throats. The stone may answer with a low, resonant note. If anyone speaks, the landscape takes the voice apart and returns it in pieces: one syllable from the rain, one from a whirlpool, one from the hollow interior of the plateau. Meaning is not preserved.

Vegetation is sparse and badly adapted to being in one world at a time. A few dark fronds cling to the highest ridges, each no taller than a hand. They are neither seaweed nor grass. Their leaves are flat, translucent, and jointed like fingers. They grow in patches, but the patches are widely separated, leaving long reaches of bare stone between them. When submerged, they wave in the current. When exposed to air, they stiffen and point downward, as though trying to root into the sky.

One plant grows only around the mouths of the smaller hollows. It resembles a low tuft of black moss, though its filaments are warm and faintly luminous. Water passing over it emerges perfectly still for a breath, creating small calm circles in the surrounding violence. Then the circles rupture and the plant is gone. Later it may be found several meters away, attached to a fresh surface that did not exist before.

Fauna is rare. A few pale things inhabit the deeper water: narrow, ribbonlike bodies with no visible eyes, appearing only as distortions in the falling rain. They do not swim so much as select a direction and cause the water around them to move. Once in a while one rises onto the stone, where it lies trembling in a thin film of water, its body showing glimpses of impossible interiors—an empty sky, a field of reeds, a human hand opening and closing. Then it slips backward into the current and leaves no trace except a sudden drop in temperature.

Small shelled organisms gather in the calmer cracks. Their shells are clear and angular, like pieces of broken glass. They open and close in unison when the rock shifts. No larger ecosystem seems to depend on them. They are present with the incompleteness of an early draft.

There are traces of construction, but they seem less built than stranded. On one high shelf stands a partial ring of pale uprights, each about waist-high. They might be the remnants of a wall, a marker, or the beginnings of a monument abandoned when the sea arrived. The stones are too evenly spaced to be natural, yet no tool marks remain. Water runs through them without wetting their inner faces. Their shadows point toward the center of the ring and deepen there, collecting into a dark, shallow pool that reflects not the place but a dry landscape beneath a white sun.

Elsewhere, a single narrow staircase descends into the water. It has seven steps. The eighth is always missing, though from a distance there appear to be nine. The staircase leads toward a low opening in the rock, but the opening alternates between being a cave, a doorway, and a patch of unbroken stone. From within comes a sound quieter than the surrounding roar: the delicate clatter of something arranging small objects.

There are no tools, no machines, no wires, no evidence of industry. Whatever made the scattered structures left no technological signature. The place is pre-instrumental, but not untouched. It has been marked by intention, then absorbed into a larger process that does not distinguish architecture from erosion.

The terrain changes constantly, but not smoothly. New ridges thrust up through the water with the suddenness of thoughts. Crevasses close like mouths. A basin fills with warm blue vapor and becomes, for a minute, a sky. Rain falls upward from its surface. A cliff face briefly displays layers of time: fresh black rock at the bottom, ancient sediment above, then the smooth interior of a human tooth. The layers seal themselves before they can be examined.

Distance is unreliable. The opposite edge of the plateau is always visible, but it can be reached only by routes that lengthen as they are crossed. Two points close enough to touch may belong to separate moments. A person stepping from one rock to another might arrive before they left, or leave behind an afterimage that continues the journey without them.

At the highest point, where the water is thinnest, the atmosphere gathers in a trembling lens. Looking through it reveals the surrounding sea as a vast vertical chamber, with the plateau suspended on one wall. Looking away reveals a dim ceiling of water, close enough to touch. Occasionally the lens opens completely, and beyond it there is an immense, sunlit world—but the opening lasts only until the first sound is made.

The place is not settling. It is forming.

Its sharp ridges are being born and worn away at once. Its sparse life appears and disappears according to pressures no creature can read. Its fragments of construction persist only because the turbulence has not yet decided what they are. Everything is caught between emergence and submergence: island becoming reef, rain becoming ocean, cave becoming sky.

And over all of it, deafeningly, the young landscape keeps changing its mind.
