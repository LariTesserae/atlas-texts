The land is a high country of stone and weather, broad enough that its far edges disappear into their own climate.

It rises in long, quiet shelves beneath a pale sky. The elevation is not expressed in sharp peaks so much as in exposure: there are no enclosing forests, no warm valleys, no thick cover anywhere. The horizon is a succession of dark escarpments and shallow ridges, each one softened by distance and rain. Clouds gather low over the plateau, moving in great gray bodies that sometimes conceal whole districts of the land. When the clouds lift, the light is ordinary and cold—clear enough to show every ledge and wall, but without warmth.

Water is close beneath the surface. It appears everywhere in restrained forms. It beads along the faces of stones. It gathers in narrow channels between paving slabs. It seeps from old retaining walls in thin, continuous threads, making their lower courses black and green. The hollows are filled with shallow tarns and marshes, not broad enough to be lakes, but too numerous to ignore. Rain falls often, though not always heavily: a long patter on roofs, a silver veil across the open ground, a sudden darkening of every surface. Afterward, the whole plateau seems to breathe vapor.

Across this high basin stands a settlement vast enough to be mistaken, at first, for a natural formation. Its streets and terraces run for miles. Buildings rise in stepped masses, built from the same blue-black stone as the surrounding ridges, so that distance blurs the boundary between city and mountain. Some structures are intact: long halls with sloping roofs, square towers without openings, low houses gathered tightly around courtyards. Others have settled into the land. Roofs have collapsed inward and become gardens of moss. Stairways descend into pools. Doorways open onto blank walls where the rooms behind them have filled with silt.

There is no metal gleam, no glass, no smoke, no machinery. The settlement was made by hand and remains in the language of handwork: fitted stone, fired clay, timber darkened by centuries of damp, woven screens sagging in their frames. Its infrastructure is extensive but quiet. Channels carry rainwater beneath the streets. Cisterns are cut into the foundations. Bridges cross ditches and marshy depressions. Broad roads, now nearly empty, climb in gradual switchbacks toward the upper districts. The roads are worn smooth in their centers, but grass has not conquered them; the climate is too cold and the soil too thin. Instead, pale mats of lichen occupy the cracks, and small black plants cling to sheltered seams.

The city is dense without being crowded. Buildings stand close together, often sharing walls, their courtyards stacked above one another on the slopes. In some places, three or four levels of construction are visible at once: a lower street half submerged in water, a middle terrace supported by arched masonry, and above it a row of sealed houses facing the weather. The density gives the landscape a strange sense of pressure. There are many surfaces, many corners, many dark interiors, but almost no movement.

Vegetation is sparse and disciplined by the climate. Low sedges grow around the marshes. Tough silver grasses form narrow bands where the soil has collected. There are dwarf shrubs in the lee of walls, their branches flattened by wind, and occasional trees—crooked, dark-barked things occupying protected courtyards or cracks in the lower cliffs. Their leaves are small and waxy. Nothing rises high without shelter. On the open slopes, the dominant color is stone: charcoal, iron-gray, muted violet, and the dull green of wet lichen.

The animals are few and widely spaced. Their presence is usually inferred rather than seen: a line of hoofprints filling with rain, a feather caught against a wall, the brief ripple of something moving through reed water. Birds pass over the city in small, silent formations, rarely landing. In the marshes, pale amphibians cling to the undersides of stones. A large grazing animal may appear once in a day on a distant ridge, standing motionless in the rain before vanishing into fog. Predators are almost never visible. The scarcity of fauna makes every living sound distinct.

The prevailing sound is water.

It ticks from eaves. It threads through gutters. It falls from one terrace to another and disappears beneath grates of carved stone. In the open country, rain moves across the plateau as a soft, advancing hiss. Wind passes through empty windows and produces low tones, not quite whistles, from the buildings’ many shafts and tunnels. Occasionally, somewhere far below, a section of saturated earth gives way with a muffled slide. The sound travels through the masonry and is felt more than heard.

There are no bells, voices, wheels, or fires. Even the settlement seems to have adopted the silence of the uplands. Yet it is not dead. Water is steadily changing its routes. Moss thickens on shaded walls. Mortar loosens grain by grain. A roof sags over many seasons, then collapses in a single wet morning. Marshes expand into squares that were once courtyards. The city persists through continuous small alterations, settling rather than decaying, as if it has entered an equilibrium with the plateau.

Weather has a scale of its own here. A rainstorm can occupy the entire basin, with its outer edge visible as a dark curtain moving along the horizon. Clearings of light cross the land in slow bands, illuminating one district while leaving the next under cloud. In the colder months, rain becomes sleet against the upper walls, and thin ice forms in the channels before breaking apart under the next thaw. Fog pools in the lower streets and pours over parapets like a slow flood. From the highest terraces, one can sometimes look down upon an entire neighborhood submerged in whiteness, its towers emerging as islands.

The rules of the place are old and physical. Water always seeks the lowest room. Stone remains where it has been laid unless water, frost, or gravity has worked upon it for a very long time. Sound does not travel far in the open air, but it lingers beneath arches and in enclosed courts. Warmth is temporary and precious; the landscape does not hold it. Any path left unused begins not with weeds, but with damp, slickness, and the darkening of stone. Anything abandoned is gradually incorporated into the slope, the channel, or the marsh.

Far beyond the settlement, the plateau continues: terraces, ravines, wet shelves, and distant mountain walls under the same moving sky. The city is large, but not the largest thing here. It is one dense interruption in a continent of high, weathered ground—an organized mass of masonry standing quietly inside a much older system of water, stone, cloud, and time.
