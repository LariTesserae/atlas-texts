The plateau begins above the weather.

There is no clean line where mountain becomes sky. The land rises in long, ancient folds toward a broad upland, and each fold has been worn nearly smooth by centuries of ice, rain, and a wind that seems to have been blowing since before the first trees. The highest ridges are not sharp. They are vast, rounded backs of dark stone, their surfaces polished to a dull sheen. Between them lie shallow basins filled with water, peat, and vegetation so dense that the ground has become difficult to distinguish from the living things covering it.

The cold is persistent but not severe. It settles into the rocks and remains there. Even under the gray daylight, every surface appears chilled: black stone, silver puddles, the blue-green leaves of the low growth. Mist gathers wherever water finds a depression. It drifts in thin sheets across the plateau, sometimes lying flat, sometimes rising in columns that bend against the wind without dispersing.

The vegetation is the true terrain.

There are no forests in the usual sense, no distinct boundary between soil and plant. A continuous skin of mosses, mats, stalks, root-fans, and pale spreading leaves covers much of the high country. It is not uniform. In some places it forms low, elastic turf that trembles underfoot. In others it rises in shelves and curtains, or descends into ravines as enormous green-black ropes. Long stems grow horizontally for hundreds of meters, bridging gaps in the rock. Their surfaces are ribbed like the sides of machinery. Water runs along them in narrow channels, disappearing into pores and reemerging farther down the slope.

The oldest growths have become geological. They wrap entire hills in thick layers, their outer tissues hardened into barklike plates and their interiors still circulating with sap. Some have split open, revealing dark, wet chambers where the plant’s fluids move through translucent tubes. Within those chambers, small animals nest, breed, and vanish. Others have collapsed into shallow valleys, where their decay has become the valley floor. New plants grow from them, and then grow over those, until the whole basin is a many-layered living deposit.

Space remains between the major presences. A lone stalk may stand on a bare expanse of rock, six meters high and bent permanently toward some invisible source of light. A circular patch of red lichen may occupy an entire basin by itself, with nothing growing within it. Long stretches of the plateau are open water and stone, broken only by scattered colonies of dark reeds. The emptiness makes the vegetation appear larger than it is. A single spreading plant can seem to own the horizon.

Water is everywhere, but rarely in rivers. It lies under the ground, just below the skin of the plateau, and rises through the plants in sudden wetness. Footsteps release clear beads from the turf. Rocks weep from their seams. A shallow depression may be dry at dawn and filled by noon with cold, trembling water. The mist condenses directly on leaves and falls upward again in the wind.

In the low basins, the water gathers into tarns the color of smoked glass. Their margins are occupied by thin, jointed plants that make a constant mechanical clicking as they flex. Beneath the surface, root systems turn slowly, though there is no current strong enough to explain their motion. Every so often a whole section of the lake rotates like a wheel, carrying floating moss, insects, and dead leaves in a broad spiral. The water never spills. It simply rearranges itself.

The plateau is loud.

Wind rushes over the rounded stone and through the plant architecture, producing a deep, continuous roar. The tall stalks whistle at different pitches; the root chambers thump and pulse; wet leaves slap against one another in the gusts. In the distance there is a sound like a factory running behind a wall: rhythmic impacts, rotating machinery, pressure valves opening and closing. No building produces it. The sound comes from the vegetation.

Some groves contain thick vertical trunks with no branches, each marked by bands of pale tissue. At regular intervals they shudder, and the whole grove answers with a heavy metallic vibration. Their roots are buried deep in the rock, where they grind stone into slurry and draw it upward. Other plants have long, articulated limbs that move back and forth across the ground, crushing dead growth and feeding it into central vents. The process is industrial in scale and precision, but organic in every detail. There are no tools, no roads, no towers, no evidence of manufacture. The land performs its own excavation, refining, pumping, assembly, and disposal.

Occasionally, a plant produces something that resembles a machine.

A black, many-sided object may rise from a bed of moss, turning slowly as it hardens. It may have vents, belts, pistons, or a glassy lens. It may hum, gather water, and travel a short distance on jointed legs. Then it will root itself, flower, and become part of the surrounding growth. Nothing here is made to last in a separate category from life. The industrial forms are phases of metabolism.

Animals are abundant, though not always easy to recognize as animals. Small white things travel in columns beneath the mats, their backs briefly visible through translucent leaves. Broad-winged creatures cross the mist in silence, their wings so thin that the sky can be seen through them. They land on wet stone and fold themselves into what appears to be a cluster of dead fronds.

There are grazing herds in the open areas: low, long-bodied animals with many narrow legs and soft, flexible mouths. They feed on the turf without seeming to diminish it. Wherever they pass, the ground closes behind them. Their bodies are covered in blue-gray hair that holds droplets like frost. When startled, they do not run. They separate into several versions of themselves, each taking a different direction, and gradually recombine when the danger has passed.

Larger animals inhabit the plant-chasms. Their calls echo from the root chambers, although the animals themselves may be miles away. Sometimes a head emerges from a wall of vegetation: blind, antlered, and covered in flowering tissue. Sometimes the sound belongs to nothing that has a visible body. At night—or what passes for night under the permanently dim sky—whole stretches of growth heave as if something enormous is moving beneath them.

Light arrives without a reliable source. The sky is usually a low, colorless ceiling, brighter toward the horizon and dark overhead. Shadows are weak and do not always agree with the objects that cast them. A stalk may throw a shadow toward the wind, or a pool may reflect a sky full of stars while the actual sky remains clouded. Near the largest plant colonies, faint green and amber radiance leaks from the tissues, illuminating the mist from within.

The land is ancient, but it is not still.

Erosion has almost completed its work. The mountains have been rounded, the cliffs lowered, the sharpness taken from every edge. Yet the final state never arrives. Slopes collapse in slow ripples. Basins migrate. A ridge may flatten over the course of an afternoon, its stone carried away as powder through the roots of the plants. The water table rises and falls in sudden steps. Entire ecosystems shift several meters sideways without breaking apart.

At the far end of the plateau, the horizon occasionally bends downward, revealing another landscape beneath it: a second high country, inverted and trembling, with its own mist and vegetation. It lasts only a few minutes. During that time, animals may walk toward the false horizon and disappear before reaching it. Rain may fall upward into it. The sound of the unseen factories becomes deafening.

Then the horizon settles back into place.

Nothing has been built here, but everything is operating. The plateau is a worn, wet, living system at the edge of completion—sparse enough to reveal its emptiness, crowded enough to make that emptiness feel inhabited. Its plants are ground, weather, engine, shelter, and animal. Its oldest stones are still being processed. Its water is still choosing where to be. And beneath the roar of wind and biological machinery, the whole landscape seems to be trying, with immense and turbulent patience, to become something it has already been for ages.
