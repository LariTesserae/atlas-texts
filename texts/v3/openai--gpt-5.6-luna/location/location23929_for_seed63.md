The place is called the **Upper Hollow**, though it is not hollow in the ordinary sense.

It lies high above the surrounding country, on the crown of a black mineral massif where the air has become thin, hot, and almost useless. The massif is not a mountain so much as a single upthrust of cooled darkness: a plug of basalt, iron, and glass, rising in shelves and broken terraces to a narrow summit. At its top is a basin no larger than a village square, enclosed on three sides by walls of fused stone. The fourth side opens onto an abyss, but the abyss cannot be seen. Beyond the rim there is only a vertical fall into heat and blackness, and a slow red illumination that rises from somewhere far below without ever becoming light.

The basin is packed.

Not with people, nor with buildings, nor with the soft abundance of an ecosystem, but with bodies.

They cover the floor in layers.

At first glance they appear to be stones: flattened, angular things the color of old ash, each with a dull sheen along its edges. Some are the size of a hand, some as large as a crouching animal. They overlap one another so closely that no bare ground is visible between them. Their surfaces are ridged with mineral growth, chipped and scabbed from long passage through the heat. A few have split open, revealing interiors of pale ceramic plates and soft, black folds that contract in the air.

They are alive, though “alive” is not quite the right word.

The creatures of the Upper Hollow possess no eyes that can be recognized, no mouths, and no limbs in the usual sense. They sense pressure, heat, and vibration through their whole surfaces. They move by minute expansions of their bodies, tilting and settling, flowing over one another in slow shudders. In a crowded basin this makes a continuous, almost imperceptible motion: the whole floor seems to breathe without rising or falling.

There are thousands of them. Perhaps tens of thousands.

They occupy every ledge, every crack, every sloping seam in the rock. Smaller forms cling to the walls in dense black films. Larger ones lie stacked in the center, each supporting the weight of those above. From a distance they resemble overlapping scales on the hide of some immense buried beast. Up close, they are more like fragments of that beast, each one carrying a partial instinct and waiting for the rest.

The heat is severe but strangely even. It comes from below, through the stone, and from above, where the air burns with a dry, metallic pressure. The basin has no shade. Darkness, when it appears, is not caused by the absence of heat but by the absence of illumination. The walls are black enough to swallow the red glow from the depths, and the sky overhead—if there is a sky—is a flat, lightless vault.

Only the edges of things show themselves.

A ridge of glass shines briefly and vanishes. A body turns, revealing a seam of dull orange beneath its shell. A sudden disturbance passes through the packed animals, and thousands of mineral backs tilt at once, catching a faint buried glow like sparks under ash.

There is no wind. The air is too still, or too heavy, or perhaps the walls prevent it from moving. Yet the heat has its own weather. It gathers in invisible strata. Every few hours—though there is no visible sun by which to measure them—a hot pressure descends over the basin, and the creatures become restless. They climb, slide, and press toward the upper walls. Some flatten themselves against the stone. Others open their seams and release long threads of gray vapor.

The vapor is not water. It condenses nowhere.

It hangs above the packed bodies as a thin, bitter haze, carrying the scent of scorched metal and dry bone. When the pressure passes, it sinks into the cracks, and the creatures settle again. The basin returns to its nearly motionless state.

Nearly.

There are always small movements at the edge of perception. A creature disappearing beneath another. A wall-colony changing color. A cluster of bodies folding inward around something unseen. The floor shifting by a few inches over the course of an hour. No individual action is dramatic, but the accumulation of them gives the place an active, predatory life.

Now and then, one of the larger creatures rises.

It does not stand. It unfolds.

Its body may have been lying in the basin for centuries, its upper layers mistaken for ordinary stone. When it wakes, plates lift from plates, and a tall, narrow shape emerges from the packed mass. It is usually taller than a person and sometimes twice as tall, though its width remains slight. Its sides are lined with vibrating grooves. There is no face, but the upper portion turns toward the walls, listening through the stone.

The smaller creatures make way without fleeing. They draw apart in ripples, revealing the basin floor beneath: a hard, pale surface carved with old channels and circular depressions. The tall creature crosses it slowly, leaving behind a dark trail of heat-altered rock. At the rim it pauses, then lowers itself over the edge and begins to descend the mountain.

The descent is almost silent.

Only a faint ticking follows it, as its body cools and contracts in the thinner air.

Scattered around the basin are remnants of an older presence. They might once have been built structures, though the distinction between construction and geology has been blurred by time. A low ring of upright slabs stands near the western wall. Each slab is narrow, polished on one side, and pierced by a single circular hole. The holes are filled with darkness deeper than the surrounding dark. No one knows whether the ring was a shelter, a boundary, or an instrument. The creatures avoid it.

On the eastern side, half-buried beneath the living layers, lies a small arch made from a material unlike the basalt. It is gray and faintly translucent, as if cast from cooled smoke. Its top has sagged, and one side has collapsed inward. Across its surface are shallow marks: repeated angles, each interrupted before it can become a pattern. The marks are worn smooth in places by the passage of bodies, though no creature seems to touch the arch deliberately.

There is one more remnant: a stair cut into the northern wall.

It leads upward only a few dozen steps before ending at a sealed opening. The stair is crowded with animals. They fill each step from edge to edge, layered several deep, but none crosses the final landing. The sealed opening above them is featureless except for a thin horizontal line where two slabs meet. Behind it, something occasionally strikes the stone from the far side.

The sound is too soft to be called a knock.

It is more like a change in pressure, a single inward flex of the mountain.

When it happens, every creature in the Hollow becomes still.

The silence then is absolute. There is no scrape, no click, no vapor hiss. Even the distant ticking from the descending forms stops. The entire crowded basin holds itself rigid, as though the creatures are listening to a voice too deep for air.

After a time, the pressure releases.

The animals resume their slow shifting. The smaller ones crawl over the larger ones. The dark films on the walls rearrange themselves into new branching shapes. Somewhere below the rim, a body begins its descent with a soft, regular clicking.

The Upper Hollow has no seasons, but it has cycles. Heat rises and falls. The mineral fauna feed—not on matter, apparently, but on gradients: the difference between warm stone and hotter stone, between pressure held and pressure released. They gather where the mountain is changing. They avoid the oldest, most stable surfaces. Their lives are measured in migrations of inches and awakenings that may occur once in a human lifetime.

Nothing grows here. Nothing decays in the familiar sense. The dead do not rot; they become heavier, darker, and more useful to the living. A failed body may remain in place for centuries, gradually becoming part of the floor. The distinction between colony, carcass, and landscape is impossible to maintain.

At the far rim, where the basin narrows toward the abyss, the creatures are packed so tightly that their bodies form a continuous raised lip. Beyond it, the red glow from below occasionally brightens, illuminating the underside of the dark without revealing its source.

On those rare occasions, the whole Hollow is visible at once.

The black walls. The abandoned ring. The broken arch. The crowded stair. The endless layered animals, pressed together in patient motion. And above them, the sealed opening in the mountain, where something on the other side waits for the right change in pressure.

Then the glow fades.

The place becomes dark again, and the Upper Hollow continues to breathe without lungs.
