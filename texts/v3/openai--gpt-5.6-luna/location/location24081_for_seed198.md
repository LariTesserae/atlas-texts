The city occupies a high basin where the mountain has been leveled, terraced, drilled, bridged, and built over so completely that the original ground survives only in rumor.

From a distance, the basin appears green. Moss covers the retaining walls. Long grasses grow in the seams between concrete plates. Ferns crowd the drainage channels, and pale vines descend from viaducts in curtains, their roots searching constantly for whatever soil remains behind the walls. Rainwater beads on every surface: on corrugated roofs, on black pipes, on the windows of freight elevators, on the rustless steel ribs of the factories. The air is cold enough to keep the wetness from evaporating. Cloud hangs low over the upper districts, caught against the mountain like wool snagged on a nail.

There is no open earth.

The roads are poured onto older roads, and the platforms stand on platforms. Beneath the lowest warehouses are tunnels, and beneath those are foundations from earlier industrial phases: sealed rail lines, drowned machine halls, maintenance galleries, pressure chambers, whole districts abandoned without ever being demolished. The current city has been raised over them in layers. Every wall is also a retaining wall; every roof is a work yard, garden, reservoir, or landing deck. Green things thrive, but only in the places made ready for them by cracks, gutters, drainage beds, and municipal planting schemes. Nature has not reclaimed the city. It has been assigned quarters.

The basin is broad enough to contain weather of its own. At the eastern end, immense hydroelectric towers rise into the mist, their intake mouths descending toward a reservoir hidden beyond the factories. Water moves through the city in controlled violence. It rushes through concrete flumes, drops between districts in narrow falls, turns turbines under the streets, and enters settling tanks the size of stadiums. Every roof has a gutter. Every gutter feeds a pipe. Every pipe joins a larger pipe until the entire mountain seems to be conducting water downward through its bones.

After rain, the walkways shine like dark glass. Sheets of water pour from the edges of elevated roads. Condensation gathers under the iron bridges and falls in a steady, almost delicate dripping. In places the walls seem to weep. White mineral trails mark where decades of leakage have traveled down the concrete. Moss has formed thick, velvety bands around valve housings and inspection doors, but the city’s maintenance crews do not remove it unless it interferes with access. The moss is considered part of the drainage system.

Factories fill the middle basin in compact ranks. Their roofs overlap so closely that a person can cross for kilometers without descending to street level. Conveyor belts pass from building to building beneath covered galleries. Pneumatic tubes run beside stairways. Gantry cranes move slowly through the fog, carrying sealed containers, coils of cable, stone slabs, or components whose purpose is not visible from below. Chimneys rise everywhere, but most discharge only a thin warmth or a harmless vapor. The heavier processes have been moved underground, where the heat can be recovered and fed back into the city’s pipes.

The buildings are tall rather than wide, not because there is room to spare, but because there is none. Apartment towers are wedged against foundries. Schools occupy the upper floors of storage blocks. A clinic hangs beneath a railway viaduct. Small workshops have been built into the flanks of cooling stations and pumping houses. Stairs, ladders, lifts, catwalks, and narrow service bridges bind everything together. There are no vacant lots. There are only temporary absences: a demolition site already fenced for construction, a drained tank waiting to be relined, a platform cleared for a machine that has not yet arrived.

Yet the city is strangely quiet.

The turbines are mostly buried. The factories have insulated their loudest rooms. Conveyor systems hum rather than clatter, and the trains glide on enclosed magnetic tracks, appearing as brief gray shapes behind fogged windows. Sound is swallowed by wet concrete, hanging vegetation, layered roofs, and the constant soft pressure of the cloud. Across the basin, one hears a few distinct things: the deep pulse of pumps; the hollow knock of water striking a spillway; the distant warning chime of a reversing crane; the squeal of a cable under strain. Then the sound is gone, absorbed into the broad damp silence.

People move through this silence in great numbers, but rarely gather. Workers emerge from one door and vanish into another. Commuters ride enclosed escalators through green corridors of filtered air. Maintenance crews in yellow waterproof clothing descend into ducts and reappear hours later on a different level. The population is dense enough that a person is almost always somewhere nearby, but the city’s circulation system keeps bodies separated into streams. There are no broad plazas, no unoccupied horizons within the built districts. Even public space is channeled: roof gardens, covered markets, narrow terraces over the reservoir pipes.

The surrounding slopes are visible only intermittently. When the cloud lifts, the mountains appear close and severe, their upper faces dark with wet stone. The city has cut horizontal scars into them for quarries, roads, and waterworks. Old landslides have been stabilized with mesh and concrete ribs. Some slopes are faced entirely in interlocking retaining blocks, each one planted with moss or low shrubs. The stone is gray, brown, and green, weathered but not wild. There are no fresh wounds in the landscape, only long-established scars that have become architectural features.

Turbulence is everywhere, though not in the form of disorder. The whole basin operates under continuous adjustment. Reservoir levels rise and fall. Gates open in response to distant storms. Production lines alter their output overnight. Platforms extend or retract. Temporary pipes are installed across roads and removed days later. A factory may be dismantled while the buildings around it remain occupied, its machinery extracted through a wall in sections. New structures are assembled over old ones without interrupting the flow of workers or water. The city is stable only in the way a spinning wheel is stable: through constant motion, correction, and expenditure.

From the upper terraces, the basin resembles an immense wet instrument. Pipes, rails, ducts, bridges, towers, tanks, and towers of stacked housing fill the view. Green growth softens almost every edge without concealing the underlying design. It hangs from the power lines and gathers in the seams of the loading bays, but it never quite escapes the geometry of the place.

Fauna is scarce. A few black birds nest under the railway roofs, choosing ledges above the warm vents. Small, pale insects gather around lamps when the lamps are on, though the lamps are rarely bright. Rats are rumored to live in the oldest tunnels, but no one sees them. The reservoirs contain fish introduced for water monitoring, their numbers carefully counted. The city has no room for larger animals, and the cold, filtered air discourages most wild things. Life here is predominantly human, mechanical, bacterial, fungal, and green.

At dusk, the dimness deepens rather than changes. The cloud turns the remaining light blue-gray. Factory windows glow faintly behind condensation. Safety strips appear along the catwalks, and red indicator lights blink on towers swallowed halfway by mist. Water continues to move through the city, unseen now except where it spills from a high channel and catches the little available light.

Nothing feels abandoned. Even the sealed doors have lights above them. Even the old tunnels are numbered. The city is too full, too maintained, too continuously occupied to become a ruin.

It is a place built against emptiness and weather, then filled so completely that the weather itself has become part of the machinery.
