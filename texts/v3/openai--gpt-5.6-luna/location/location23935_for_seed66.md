The city stands high above the world on a tableland of dark stone, though “above” has become difficult to measure. The plateau is broad enough to possess weather: long banks of warm vapor move across it for days, gathering against the escarpments, thinning, returning. Beyond the rim, the land falls in terraces and broken shelves, each one softened by age but not by vegetation. There are no grasses in the cracks, no shrubs on the slopes, no green stain anywhere. Water occupies every absence instead.

It lies just below the surface of the stone. It beads along walls. It gathers in shallow basins worn smooth by centuries of seepage. It descends the escarpments in thin, innumerable threads, not waterfalls so much as the plateau slowly giving itself away. After rain, the whole highland shines with a skin of water, and the streets appear to have been poured from black metal.

The rock is mostly basalt, iron-dark and dull except where freshly broken. In places it has weathered into pale gray shelves, honeycombed with old cavities. Other formations rise from the plain like enormous fused teeth: mineral towers, rounded by the same patient forces that have worn the plateau into steps. Their surfaces are filmed with condensation. They weep steadily, even under a clear sky.

The settlement occupies a basin between these formations and has spread outward in dense, concentric construction. From a distance it resembles a machine left running too long. Low industrial halls cover the floor of the basin, their roofs connected by pipes, gantries, covered ramps, and walkways. Cylindrical tanks stand shoulder to shoulder in rows that vanish into the vapor. Towers vent warm white plumes. Rail lines enter the city through cuts in the stone and leave it again along raised embankments, carrying sealed cars toward the outer terraces.

Nothing here is ornamental, but everything has acquired the weight of age. The oldest buildings are made from enormous fitted blocks, their seams black with mineral runoff. Later structures have been bolted onto them in corrugated sheets and ribbed concrete. Newer additions are mostly conduits: thick hoses, pressure mains, cable bundles, sluice controls, and elevated tunnels through which water and freight pass above the streets. Buildings lean against one another. Streets have become channels. Channels have become foundations. A square may contain a factory wall, a reservoir, a tram line, and three generations of repair, all occupying the same few meters.

The industrial work is slow and continuous. Great pumps draw water from beneath the plateau and lift it into settling towers. The towers release it again through filter beds of crushed stone and metallic ceramic. Other works reduce the water to salts, metals, and dark organic residues gathered from the outer basins. There are furnaces, though they burn without flame: immense chambers glowing red through narrow inspection slits, their interiors fed by measured streams of fuel and air. Their vibration travels through the ground and into the bones.

The city never quite falls silent. It does not roar constantly, but produces a deep, layered noise that seems to have settled into the atmosphere. Pumps thud at regular intervals. Pressure valves knock. Rail wheels shriek briefly and then fade beneath the continuous hiss of vents. Somewhere, a foundry releases a long descending groan. The sound is loud in the manner of surf heard from inside a sealed room: broad, persistent, and difficult to locate. Individual noises emerge only when they are very near.

The warmth is heavy but not oppressive. Vapor hangs low across the streets, especially in the morning, though morning is determined by the slow pale cycle of the sun rather than by any change in activity. Moisture condenses on every exposed surface. Metal railings are perpetually slick. The undersides of bridges drip. A person walking beneath a pipe may pass from dry warmth into a sudden cold shower, then back into warmth before they have looked up.

Visibility changes by degrees. On clear days, the whole basin can be seen: a dense spread of roofs and towers, the black roads, the glint of reservoirs, the outer walls descending toward distant plateaus. On wet days, the far side dissolves. Buildings appear and disappear in the vapor, first as silhouettes, then as lit windows, then as dark walls looming at arm’s length. The sky remains ordinary—clouded, blue, or sunlit—but the land below seems to have its own enclosed climate.

The animals are everywhere.

They gather beneath the raised streets, in the drainage channels, around the warm foundations of the factories, and along the waterworks where mineral-rich runoff collects. Their numbers are so great that the city’s open spaces seem less like empty places than places temporarily occupied by no animals. They move through the settlement in slow, congested streams.

Most are broad-bodied, low to the ground, and built for traction on wet stone. Some resemble armored amphibians, though their skins are dry and plated, their many blunt limbs moving in careful sequence. Others have translucent bellies filled with dim, shifting organs. There are long segmented creatures that climb the retaining walls in sheets, each body no larger than an arm, and larger forms that pass beneath the rail bridges with their backs hidden in vapor. Their anatomy is difficult to read. Limbs become feelers; mouths open along the sides of the body; eyes occur in clusters beneath protective folds. None look fantastical at first glance. They look adapted—efficiently, anciently adapted—to a world in which stone is wet and warmth comes from industry.

They are not kept as livestock, though the city uses them. Some species are drawn to the settling ponds and consume the residue that would otherwise foul the filters. Some carry mineral deposits in their shells, which workers harvest from their molting grounds. Others are tolerated because they feed on the smaller forms, or because removing them would cause a change in the waterworks no one fully understands. The largest animals are simply built around. Their routes are marked on city maps. A particular underpass may be closed for six hours while a migration passes through. At such times, the gates are lowered, the pumps are slowed, and everyone waits.

The migrations are not hurried. A dark mass may take half a day to cross one district. Thousands of bodies press together in the streets, wet flanks rubbing against concrete, their movements producing a soft collective rasp. Workers stand on platforms above them. The animals pass beneath, filling the basin from wall to wall, and then are gone, leaving behind flattened sediment, shell fragments, and a smell like warm stone after rain.

There are smaller presences in greater numbers: pale things clinging to tank walls, needle-legged scavengers in the runoff, soft-bodied swarms that gather around lights. In the reservoirs, whole surfaces tremble with the movement of minute life. The water is never still, even when the pumps stop. It quivers from below.

Yet the ecology is not frantic. Nothing multiplies explosively. Nothing seems to bloom or collapse within a season. The animals follow routes older than the city, and the city has learned to bend around them. Their abundance is not a sudden infestation but a settled condition, as permanent as the weathering of the rock. Over long periods, the channels deepen, the migration paths shift by a few meters, and buildings are raised or abandoned accordingly. Change arrives as pressure, not event.

At the highest point of the basin stands an observation district built around a circular reservoir. Its walls are polished by water and generations of touch. From there, the city spreads in layers below: the old stone core, the industrial middle, the newer outer works, and finally the animal roads leading toward the plateau’s rim. Beyond them lie immense shelves of mineral land, wet and empty of plants but not of life. Herds move there as dark lines. Pools gleam in the hollows. Vapor collects in the valleys and pours slowly over the edges like a tide.

The horizon is never entirely still. Even on a calm day, distant weather advances across the highland. Cloud shadows pass over the rock. Far-off curtains of rain descend and remain visible for hours. The escarpments darken as water finds old channels. The city’s vents rise into the moving air and are carried toward the same blank distance.

At night, the industrial lights become the dominant sky of the basin. Lamps burn along every bridge and rail. Warning beacons pulse on the towers. Windows appear in long horizontal bands. Under the structures, the animals reflect the light in wet plates and lidless eyes. The reservoirs take on the color of tarnished copper.

Nothing in the landscape is new enough to feel temporary. Even the machinery seems to have entered the geology. Pipes are furred with mineral growth. Tracks have sunk into their beds. Factory walls carry the dark tide marks of decades of flooding. A pump station may be dismantled, but its foundations remain warm for years, and animals continue to gather there. Roads are rerouted around old nests, old leaks, old pressure fractures.

The place is not hostile so much as indifferent to individual scale. It has room for cities, migrations, weather systems, and processes that take centuries to become visible. Water moves through it constantly. Animals fill it. Machines regulate it. Stone receives everything and slowly changes shape. The high plateau endures beneath the sound of pumps and the passage of living bodies, wet to its core, warm under its weather, and vast enough that the city is only one dense, industrious interruption in a much older world.
