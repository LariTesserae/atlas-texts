The landscape is contained inside a hollow no larger than a clenched hand.

It lies at the summit of a mountain that cannot be seen.

The hollow is a fresh fracture in black volcanic stone, a narrow pocket opened by heat and pressure. Its walls rise sharply on every side, not enough to be called cliffs, but enough that the whole world feels vertical: a basin with no horizon, an alpine cirque compressed into a dark cup. The rock is young, still angular, its surfaces unsoftened by weather. Ridges meet in thin blades. Chips of glassy basalt cling to one another like frozen drips. There is no dust. Every loose grain has been washed away or fused into the walls.

Water fills the cracks.

It does not run downward in any ordinary sense. It gathers in trembling beads, joins itself into clear cords, and hangs from the stone in long, bright threads. Some drops climb. Some fall upward and disappear into the black ceiling of the fissure. The walls sweat constantly, though the hollow is hotter than a kiln. Steam breathes from the pores of the rock, condensing almost at once into a shining film. The air is thick and wet, with the mineral taste of hot rain trapped underground.

At the bottom of the pocket is a shallow pool, perhaps the size of a thumbprint. Its surface is never still. It dimples beneath the feet of the creatures gathered around it, dimples again where nothing visible touches it, then draws itself into tiny ridges that point toward whichever wall is currently highest. The pool reflects no single place. In it, one may see the white underside of a cloud, a red desert, a moon at the wrong phase, or the same pool from above—though there is no room above it for a person, a cloud, or a moon.

The heat comes from below and from everywhere else.

The basalt glows faintly in its deepest seams, not red but the color of a coal remembered through closed eyelids. Near these seams, the water boils in narrow lines. Elsewhere it remains cool enough to touch, though anything touching it is changed slightly: a claw grows transparent, a joint forgets which way it bends, an eye acquires a reflection of an unfamiliar creature. The transformations are small and usually temporary. The hollow seems to be trying possibilities on.

There is no vegetation. No moss, no lichen, no roots hidden in the stone. Nothing rises from the water except mineral needles and the pale bodies of animals.

The animals are everywhere.

They crowd the basin in layers, pressed belly to back, wing to shell, mouth to mouth. Most are small enough to fit beneath a fingernail, but there are so many of them that their movement becomes a landscape of its own. The floor appears to ripple. A black slope shifts and reveals thousands of jointed legs. A wet silver patch opens a dozen mouths. Pale, blind things thread through the mass, using the larger animals as tunnels.

Some resemble shrimp made from smoked glass. Some are soft and many-segmented, with little hooks along their sides. Some are all eye: dark spheres rolling on delicate stalks, each one carrying a tiny, bright image of a different landscape. There are flat creatures that cling to the walls and drink the condensation. There are long, hot-bodied things that coil around the glowing seams, their skins inscribed with moving cracks. A few have the general shape of birds but no feathers, only wet membranes and narrow hands. They cannot fly in the pocket. They climb upward, vanish at the ceiling, and return from the floor.

The fauna are not merely numerous. They are layered through reality.

A creature may pass behind another and emerge before it. A cluster of pale mites may occupy the same patch of stone as a larger animal, visible only when the larger animal moves. Several predators pursue the same prey along paths that diverge without distance between them. One animal bites another, and the bite appears first as a wound in the wall. The wounded creature continues crawling, while a dark red line opens in the basalt and begins to drip.

Despite the crowd, the hollow is almost silent.

There is only the softest sound: water gathering, water releasing. A faint ticking from the hot stone. The papery brush of thousands of bodies against one another, so quiet it seems to be occurring inside the listener’s teeth. When a predator catches something, there is no cry. The prey simply becomes absent, and all the animals nearby turn briefly toward the space it occupied.

Their silence is not peaceful. It is the silence of an immense event taking place too slowly, or too privately, for sound to escape.

At the highest ledge, where the walls nearly meet, several of the larger creatures have gathered. They have four limbs, long torsos, and faces concealed beneath overlapping plates. They use crude tools: chipped fragments of basalt, splinters of glass, sharpened mineral flakes held in gripping mouths or tucked beneath folded limbs. No shelter, mark, vessel, or structure has been made. Their tools are temporary things, worn down in minutes and abandoned to the living floor.

They use them to harvest the water-beads.

A creature cuts a hanging thread of condensation, and the severed water remains suspended between them. Another scrapes a glowing seam until a flake of heat comes loose. They gather these pieces with careful, practiced movements, feeding some to the smaller animals and pressing others into their own bodies. The work is primitive but purposeful. It is the only sign that anything here distinguishes between tool and stone, food and prey, self and surroundings.

Then one of the tool-users looks directly outward.

There is no outward.

The creature’s head turns beyond the limits of the pocket, beyond the close black walls, and for an instant the hollow opens onto an immense elevation: a whole mountain range rising above a boiling cloud sea, every summit crowned in white fire. The vision lasts less than a blink. It is followed by another, in which the same creature is looking back from far below, tiny and trapped inside the pocket. Then the walls close again.

The temperature does not change, but the meaning of heat does. Steam begins to flow downward. The pool darkens. The animals all pause at once.

A rain starts from the ceiling.

The drops fall slowly, large and perfectly round, striking the stone without splashing. Each impact produces another small pocket in the rock, another hidden world nested inside the first. In one, there is a strand of green vegetation. In another, a cold blue ocean. In another, a single enormous eye opening beneath the water.

The creatures rush toward the new hollows.

The basin becomes denser still. Bodies pour into bodies, predators into prey, memories into surfaces. The sharp young rock holds them all without breaking. The hot water keeps rising. The light remains ordinary—neither dim nor bright, simply the colorless illumination of a place that has no visible source.

At last, the entire pocket shudders.

For one quiet moment, the floor becomes a wall, the wall becomes a ceiling, and the ceiling becomes a pool. The animals continue moving as if nothing has happened. Their paths remain certain. Their hunger remains certain. Their small stone tools remain warm in their grasp.

Only the water hesitates.

Then it chooses a direction, and the hidden landscape begins again.
