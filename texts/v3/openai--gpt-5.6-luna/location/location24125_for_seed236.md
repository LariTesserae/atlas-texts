The landscape occupies a high, enclosed basin no larger than a town square, lifted above a plain of pale mineral haze.

It is not a mountain summit exactly, though it has the feeling of one. The basin seems newly made: a slab of dark stone thrust upward from the surrounding country, its edges still sharp, its walls broken into fresh planes that have not yet been softened by weather. The rock is black-green and faintly translucent in places, with seams of amber mineral showing beneath the surface. Along the rim, angular shelves project over emptiness. Nothing has had time to round them. There are no gullies, no old riverbeds, no sediment fans. The plateau appears to have emerged all at once and then simply remained.

There is no water anywhere.

No stream threads the settlement. No dew gathers on the leaves. No puddle darkens the stone after the warm, steady light. The air is dry without being harsh, and the plants have evolved around an entirely different chemistry: their tissues carry a thick, clear resin drawn from the mineral beneath the plateau, and their leaves open and close like valves around it. When damaged, they bleed silver-white fluid that hardens almost immediately into glassy seams.

Despite the absence of water, the place is lush.

Vegetation fills every available gap. It grows from cracks, over walls, between paving slabs, and in dense shelves along the cliff faces. Broad, overlapping leaves rise in shades of copper-green, blue-black, and pale yellow. Some plants are low and fleshy, like carpets of folded metal; others stand in clustered columns, their trunks jointed and polished, each segment holding a faint inner glow. Their crowns are not broad enough to cast deep shade, but they layer one over another until the ground is almost hidden.

The warmth is constant. The sunlight is ordinary—clear, pale, and direct—but the stone retains heat and gives it back slowly. The basin feels like a room warmed from beneath. At midday the air trembles over the black terraces. At the edges of the settlement, the plants lean outward over the cliffs, their leaves turning in unison toward the surrounding brightness. By evening they fold themselves into narrow, vertical forms, and the whole plateau seems to become taller.

The town is built among them, though “built” suggests tools, plans, and separate materials that do not quite apply here. Its structures have been grown, trained, and persuaded into shape.

Low dwellings occupy the central terraces, their walls made from pale mineral ribs braided together with living vines. Doorways are narrow and rounded, often no more than openings between two thickened trunks. Roofs are layered with leaf plates, each one overlapping the next like scales. Some buildings resemble clustered seedpods. Others have the severe, vertical form of newly split stone, with rooms hollowed inside them by generations of slow biological growth.

Paths run everywhere, but they are not paved. They are lanes of compressed black root and hardened resin, smooth from passage. They wind between close-set structures, beneath archways of living wood, and up short staircases cut directly into the plateau. The settlement is dense enough that one can pass from one building to the next without crossing open ground. Every surface is occupied: shelves hold nests, walls hold climbing plants, lintels support sleeping animals, and narrow balconies have become gardens of narrow-leaved growth.

There are no machines, no wires, no lamps, no wheels. The town’s infrastructure is alive.

A long avenue of columnar plants acts as a windbreak and a boundary. Their trunks are connected underground by pale, fibrous cords that slowly redistribute warmth and nutrients. In the center is a broad communal hollow, shaded by a canopy of overlapping leaves. Around it stand upright mineral pillars, each perforated with small openings. They serve as seats, granaries, meeting places, and nesting towers. No one seems to have made them in a single effort. They have thickened over centuries wherever bodies rested, goods were stored, or voices were once carried.

The town’s inhabitants—if there are inhabitants—are difficult to distinguish from its ecology.

Small, many-legged animals move through the streets in quiet streams. They have soft, dark bodies and translucent shells, and they cling to walls with dozens of delicate feet. Larger creatures lie half-buried beneath warm platforms, their broad backs covered in gardens of pale moss-like growth. Long-necked browsers reach into the upper foliage, moving with the careful slowness of cranes. They do not break branches so much as invite the plants to open. In the gaps above the roofs, winged things drift on nearly motionless membranes, steering by subtle changes in heat.

Fauna is abundant, but the abundance is muted. Nothing screams. Nothing drums or chatters. The animals communicate through touch, color, posture, and low vibrations passed through the resinous ground. A colony may gather around a wounded plant without making any sound at all. A flock may lift from a roof in perfect silence, each body turning at the same instant, as if the air itself had issued an instruction.

The quiet is not emptiness. It is saturation without disturbance.

There is a constant faint sound beneath perception: the dry clicking of leaves adjusting themselves, the minute crackle of mineral cooling, the soft contraction of roots in the walls. Occasionally a structure gives a single low note as heat moves through it. The sound travels through the town and vanishes. Nothing echoes for long. The dense vegetation absorbs even the noises that should carry across the basin.

At the outer rim, the plateau ends abruptly.

Beyond it lies a vast lower world, seen only in pieces through curtains of plants. Pale dust fields stretch beneath a sky blurred by mineral vapor. The horizon is too far down to feel horizontal; it seems instead that the whole surrounding country has fallen away. There are no clouds and no visible watercourses. Far below, other fresh landforms rise from the haze—ridges, shelves, and isolated towers with the same sharp, unweathered geometry. The plateau may be part of a young upheaval, a landscape still forming in slow, imperceptible increments.

Everywhere, the world appears caught just after emergence.

Cracks have not widened into valleys. Cliffs have not become slopes. Houses have not become ruins. Plants have not yet buried the stone beneath old growth, though they are trying. The settlement is dense because nothing has decayed enough to make space. New stems push through old walls. New animals occupy the nests of older ones. Resin seals breaks before they can become erosion. Even the paths seem temporary, held in place by the repeated passage of living bodies rather than by any durable construction.

Time moves here, but with great patience.

A building might lean by the width of a finger over a season. A cliff might shed one angular shard in a year. A plant might open a new chamber in its trunk over several lifetimes. The town’s changes are visible only when compared with memory, and memory itself may be stored in the architecture: bands of growth, sealed doorways, old nests embedded in walls, layers of leaf roofing turned amber and hard.

At the warmest hour, the entire basin settles into a kind of stillness. Animals press themselves against the stone. Leaves narrow. The inhabitants, if they are watching, can see the settlement breathing in slow unison: roofs flexing, pillars thickening, roots drawing mineral from below, pale fluids moving through translucent stems.

The place is a high, young, waterless garden-city—compact enough to cross in minutes, crowded enough to feel almost enclosed, and alive in every direction. It has the geometry of a newly broken world and the intimacy of a room shared by too many quiet creatures.
