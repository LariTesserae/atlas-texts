The city stands on a high shelf of pale stone, above the weather.

Not above all weather—clouds sometimes arrive below the cliffs, filling the valleys with a white, soundless sea—but above its movement. The cloud layer rests there as if it has been poured into the lower world and left to set. Its upper surface is smooth for miles, occasionally broken by the dark tips of distant ridges. Nothing travels across it. No storms build. No fronts pass. The sky above remains a clear, mild gray-blue, bright enough for ordinary sight but never harsh enough to cast strong shadows.

The plateau itself is broad and open, a long, uneven table of recently lifted rock. Its edges are sharp. Runnels have begun to cut into the slopes, but they have not yet had time to become ravines; the stone is still displaying the clean wounds of its emergence. Pale shelves, black seams, and slanting faces show where the earth cracked and rose. Here and there, whole slabs stand on edge like pages in a half-closed book. There are no rounded hills. Everything is angular, fresh, and unfinished.

Moisture does not fall so much as appear.

At dawn, condensation beads on every exposed surface: walls, grasses, roof tiles, the backs of sleeping animals. In the shadowed streets, the droplets gather into thin shining threads and run toward channels cut into the paving. These channels lead to public cisterns, stone basins, and narrow gardens sunk below street level. Rain comes only in brief, isolated events—a darkening of the air, a few minutes of silver needles, then nothing. A person may stand in one square under a dry sky while rain falls steadily in the next.

The water is cold and nearly flavorless. It gathers in hollows in the rock, in the bowls of broad leaves, and in hanging clay vessels placed under every eave. The people of the city have built their lives around finding it. Their roofs are not steep enough to shed rain quickly; they are shaped to hold it. Every building has a cistern. Every courtyard has a damp wall. Every road is slightly sloped toward some reservoir, though the slope is so gradual that a dropped bead of water may take an entire day to reach the drain.

Vegetation is abundant, but it does not form a continuous green. It appears in deliberate-looking islands, though no one remembers planting all of them.

Low silver grasses fill the spaces between the buildings. Their blades are narrow, soft, and always wet. Clumps of dark moss cover the lower faces of walls where the sun rarely reaches. Blue-green shrubs grow in cracks between foundation stones, their branches stiff and jointed, each tipped with a small translucent leaf. In the courtyards, people cultivate tall reedlike plants with hollow stems, using them for mats, tubes, baskets, and the quiet flutes hung above doorways.

There are trees, but they are widely spaced. Their trunks are pale and smooth, their crowns sparse and umbrella-shaped. Each tree holds a shallow dish of leaves around its upper branches, where mist collects. At midday, droplets fall from them one by one—not enough to make rain, but enough to mark the hours. Beneath the trees the ground is always green, and small animals gather there to drink.

The city is built around these pockets of living moisture.

From a distance it resembles a scattering of stone districts across the plateau, with long empty reaches between them. The streets do not form a dense web. They run in straight lines between reservoirs, workshops, gardens, and observation towers, then end abruptly at open ground. Some roads are raised over shallow basins. Others pass between tall retaining walls, their surfaces polished smooth by generations of feet. The broadest avenues are empty most of the time, flanked by low houses of fitted stone and pale plaster.

Construction is organized but modest. There are administrative halls, market shelters, water courts, communal granaries, and residences grouped into neighborhoods around wells. The oldest buildings are made from dark blocks with no mortar, each block cut to fit its neighbors. Newer walls use clay, woven reeds, and thin plates of the local pale stone. Roofs are often flat and weighted with stones, though many have small raised frames covered in stretched hide or translucent mineral film to trap condensation.

The city has the appearance of having been planned by people who expected it to remain exactly as it is.

No building is especially tall. Towers rise only as high as necessary to watch the cloud sea and measure the cisterns. Bridges cross narrow cuts in the plateau, but none are ornamental. Stairways descend toward the edge and then stop, because the cloud layer conceals the lower slopes. There are no roads down to the valleys. The city has no known neighbor, though distant lights can sometimes be seen on another high shelf beyond the white expanse.

Those lights never move.

The people use simple tools: stone hammers, bone needles, copper blades, wooden levers, ceramic weights. Metal is rare and carefully repaired. Most work is performed by hand. Waterwrights maintain the channels with hooked tools. Gardeners scrape moss from one wall and press it into another. Masons replace stones that crack along their natural seams. The city’s greatest technical achievement is not a machine but a system of measurement: marked staffs, calibrated bowls, and lines of colored tile that record the levels of every reservoir.

The records have not changed in living memory.

A cistern fills to the same notch every season. A particular spring emerges from a particular fissure and vanishes at the same rate. The cloud sea remains at the same height, neither rising to drown the plateau nor sinking to reveal the valleys. Even the cracks in the rock appear fixed. A stone may be loosened by human hands, but left alone, it will not fall. A wall may be dismantled, but it will not collapse by itself. Nothing decays quickly. Fruit remains edible for weeks. Wood keeps its strength for generations. Bones lying in the open become clean and pale, but never seem to crumble further.

There is no wind.

This is the first thing visitors notice, though they often notice it only after hearing their own breathing. Grass stands in whatever posture it was placed by a hand. Smoke rises straight upward and remains in a narrow column until it thins into the light. The leaves of the trees do not stir, even when their surfaces are covered with fresh droplets. Cloth hangs from lines without fluttering. Voices travel farther than they should.

The city is quiet, but not empty.

Animals are everywhere in the open spaces: small, long-legged grazers with dark eyes; white burrowing creatures that emerge from beneath paving stones; broad-winged birds that perch on roofs and watch the streets. There are flocks of slate-colored birds in the grass, though their calls are infrequent. They feed without scattering, moving around one another in slow, unhurried patterns. At night, pale moths gather on the damp sides of cisterns, and tiny amphibious things hide in the water channels, making faint clicking sounds when touched.

Larger animals inhabit the abandoned edges of the city. Moss-backed deer pass between the outer gardens. Thick-bodied cats sleep on warm masonry, their coats marked with geometric bands. Long-necked herbivores browse from the lower branches of the rain trees, standing perfectly still for hours at a time. They are not hunted often. The city has enough stored grain and cultivated root plants to survive, and the animals seem to understand the boundaries of the inhabited districts.

Predators do not chase prey here.

A cat may watch a bird for an entire afternoon. A hawk may descend, touch the ground, and remain there with its wings folded. The smaller animals do not flee unless directly approached. Death happens, but it is quiet and oddly final: an animal simply lies down and does not rise, while the others continue feeding around it. Scavengers arrive soon afterward, not in a rush, but as if responding to a scheduled appointment.

Sound is sparse and sharply defined. A bucket touching stone. The click of a tool against a cistern rim. The soft scrape of sandals. Water entering a basin one drop at a time. Far below, beneath the permanent cloud, something large occasionally calls—but the sound is so muffled and distant that it resembles a vibration in the teeth rather than a voice.

At the city’s center is a square paved in black rock. A shallow pool occupies its middle, though the pool is never seen to fill. Its surface remains perfectly level and perfectly still. Around it stand twelve stone pillars, each carved with a different animal, plant, or celestial mark. The people meet there to exchange goods, settle disputes, and announce births. They also come to listen.

Every few days, at no predictable hour, the pool produces a single sound like a drop falling into deep water. Nothing visibly changes. The surface does not ripple. Yet every animal in the square turns toward it at once, and every person becomes silent.

The city’s unusualness is not dramatic. There are no impossible colors, no violent distortions of space. Instead, the world has been deprived of small uncertainties. A flame burns without flickering. A shadow remains the same length for hours. A person who walks away from a wall and returns finds the same bead of condensation still clinging to the same point. The sky seems vast, but the clouds beneath it never drift. The plateau is young, sharp, and full of life—yet it has entered an equilibrium so complete that even change appears to require permission.

Beyond the final houses, the grasslands extend toward the plateau’s broken rim. Here the built world thins into isolated watch posts, gardens, and half-finished foundations. Between them are long reaches of pale ground where moss grows in lines around ancient footprints. The footprints are too large to belong to any animal known in the city, and too regular to be natural. They lead toward the edge, stop there, and never return.

From that edge, one can look down through the motionless cloud sea and see nothing of the land below. The whiteness is not featureless: faint terraces, towers, and dark forests seem to exist inside it, suspended at different depths. Sometimes a shape is visible for an hour. Sometimes for a year. It never approaches.

The people have built no boats for the clouds, and no stairway has reached through them. Their city remains on its high shelf, green in scattered places, damp but not wet, populous but spacious, its stone still carrying the clean angles of birth.

Morning comes.

The droplets on the roofs brighten. The grasses shine. A line of birds crosses the open square and settles together on a wall. No one hears wings. No shadow moves across the ground. In the reservoirs, the water reaches its marked level and stops.
