The place occupies a shallow basin no larger than a few city blocks, though its boundaries are difficult to locate. Everything beyond it is hidden behind vegetation: walls of reed, curtains of broad leaves, trellises furred with moss, and the dark green verticals of trees growing so close together that they form a second horizon. The basin floor is nearly level, a long-settled mudflat held in place by roots. Water lies just beneath the surface. Wherever the ground has been cut or compressed, it appears as a black, shining paste between slabs of brick and concrete.

Rain is not falling, but the landscape is wet everywhere. Leaves carry beads of water in their folds. Stone sweats. The undersides of bridges drip steadily into channels that have been worn smooth over many years. Warm air gathers beneath the plant canopy and hangs there, saturated and faintly sweet with the smell of sap, wet bark, machine oil, and rich anaerobic soil. Mist forms low along the drains in the morning, though by midday it has become only a sheen on every surface.

The settlement is built into the vegetation rather than replacing it. Its streets are narrow raised walks, some paved with dark concrete, others made from bolted plates supported on living trunks. Houses and workshops occupy the spaces between enormous plants. Walls of woven cane have been plastered, painted, and repaired so many times that they have become thick, load-bearing growths. Roofs are layered from corrugated metal and enormous waxy leaves. Gutters run into the stems of plants bred to carry water downward; their pale roots emerge through grates and disappear into cisterns below.

The plants are not ornamental. They are the ground, the walls, the shade, the insulation, and much of the infrastructure. A low avenue of columnar trunks supports a conveyor line. Tough climbing vines sheath electrical cables and keep them cool. Thick mats of red-green moss cover the roofs of pumping stations, drinking from condensation and sending surplus water into the gutters. In the center of the basin stands a grove of broad, jointed trees whose hollow trunks have been fitted with windows, doors, ladders, and service pipes. The oldest trunks contain whole rooms. Their interiors smell of warm wood and mineral water.

Between the buildings, vegetation fills every available volume. Ferns push through cracks in loading yards. Small flowering plants occupy seams in the paving, their roots lifting the edges of old tiles by a few millimeters each year. Long ribbons of grass hang from balconies and brush against passersby. The leaves do not thrash or surge; they lean, sag, and slowly recover under the weight of water. The entire green mass seems to be resting under its own enormous patience.

Industry runs through this living density in organized bands. At the basin’s lowest point is a processing yard, surrounded by retaining walls and broad-leafed windbreaks. Pumps pull groundwater from below the mud and send it through settling tanks. Crushers and presses process fallen timber, fibrous stems, seed husks, and harvested leaves into board, paper, fuel bricks, insulation, and thick translucent sheets used in construction. The machinery is practical and old-fashioned: belt drives, piston pumps, toothed wheels, electric motors housed in rusting cages. Their surfaces are lacquered with moisture and green dust. A boiler exhales from the far end of the yard, its pipe wrapped in insulating vines that have been trained around it for decades.

Nothing here is entirely clean, but nothing is in ruin. The settlement has reached an accommodation with wear. Rust has been painted over, then allowed to return in narrow orange seams. Concrete has cracked, and the cracks have been filled with root fiber and resin rather than sealed. Metal walkways sag slightly between supports, but the plants beneath them have thickened to bear the load. Every repair has become part of the structure. Every structure appears to have been repaired repeatedly, patiently, by people who expected it to remain in use.

The loudness comes from machines rather than activity. A compressor knocks at a steady interval. A turbine turns somewhere behind the leaf walls with a deep, continuous vibration. The water pumps make the ground hum. Chains rattle over sprockets. Presses close with heavy, muffled blows. The sound is softened by foliage but never stopped; it passes through stems and broad leaves as a low, layered roar. Conversations occur in gestures or close to the ear. Birds would be difficult to hear here, if there were many birds to hear.

There are few animals. A handful of dark insects live in the drainage vegetation, and their small movements can be seen in the wet shine of the leaves. Fish sometimes occupy the deepest settling ponds, but they are harvested before they grow large. Rats, stray dogs, and other familiar creatures are absent or uncommon. The ecosystem is overwhelmingly botanical: roots feeding on mineral mud, plants exchanging water through joined beds, fungi breaking down fallen matter in the dark beneath the walkways. Spores drift through the warm air, though not in great clouds. The living world is crowded, but it is mostly still.

The basin’s ecology has a slow, municipal rhythm. Water rises after rain and drains over several days. Vines are cut back on a schedule, though the cut surfaces close with new growth before the discarded lengths have been cleared away. Certain trees are harvested only once in a generation. Moss spreads across a wall over the course of years, and no one considers this neglect. The oldest machinery and the oldest plants have been maintained together for so long that their operating cycles overlap: a pump’s vibration keeps a root channel open; a tree’s shade prevents a motor from overheating; a factory roof collects the water that feeds the nursery behind it.

Daylight is ordinary, filtered but not transformed. The canopy dims the streets without turning them into darkness. Sunlight arrives in broken squares on the paving, bright enough to show the floating dust and moisture in the air. At noon, the metal roofs are hot to the touch, while the ground remains cool and soft. In the evening the plants darken first, becoming masses of overlapping leaves, and the industrial lights come on beneath them—yellow lamps over the yards, blue-white strips along the walkways, red indicators blinking through hanging roots.

There are boundaries, though they are not walls. A ring of drainage canals marks the outer edge of the settlement. Beyond the canals, the ground becomes too saturated for construction and the vegetation grows unmanaged, forming a low, impenetrable belt. Bridges cross the water at three points. Their foundations are anchored in old concrete, and their railings are almost entirely covered in vine. No one expands outward. The basin is considered complete. Buildings are rebuilt inward, platforms raised, roots redirected, machines replaced where they stand.

The place does not feel abandoned or alive in a wild sense. It feels occupied by a long arrangement between cultivation, engineering, and wet ground. Its forms have settled. Its surfaces are softened by years of water and growth, but its purposes remain clear. The pumps continue to work. The plants continue to thicken. Beneath the noise, water moves slowly through the dark soil, and every wall, path, and machine seems to be waiting for the next season’s small adjustment.
