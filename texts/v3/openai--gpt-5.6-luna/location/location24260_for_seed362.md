The plain is green, but not in the way a plain is usually green.

There are no grasses, no shrubs, no trees standing in soil. The entire low country is one constructed plant: a continental mat of pale fibers, lacquered leaves, root-cables, stalks, membranes, and thick folded organs that have grown into the shape of infrastructure. It extends past the horizon in every direction, level as a sea and nearly as immense. From a distance it resembles a frozen flood of oxidized copper and blue-green glass. Up close, there is no part of it that can be called ground.

The surface is made of overlapping leaf-plates, each broad enough to roof a house, locked together by living hinges. Their upper faces are dry and faintly waxed, patterned with millions of microscopic channels. No rain has ever filled them. They do not carry water. They conduct light, heat, charge, and the slow exchange of mineral gases through the plant’s body. When the wind moves across the plain, the plates flex in sequence, and the whole continent gives a small, nearly inaudible shiver.

The air is cool and bright. Light falls from a white sun that seems too near, though the shadows remain thin and blue. The atmosphere is dry enough to sting exposed skin. It smells of crushed leaves, hot stone, ozone, and the clean metallic odor of machinery that has been running for a very long time. There is no scent of mud, rot, or standing water. The plant has no need for water; its chemistry is based on mineral vapor, carbon compounds, and the capture of radiation. In places, the air itself is visibly processed. Tall fronds open like fans and draw the pale atmosphere through their veins. Exhaust emerges as fine glittering dust that hangs in the sunlight before settling into the folds of the landscape.

Everything here has been made.

The original substrate disappeared ages ago beneath factories, rootworks, transport beds, and cultivation grids. The plant was engineered first as a material, then as a power system, then as a world. Its descendants absorbed the machines that managed them. Now the distinction between organism and manufacture is mostly historical.

Vast industrial structures rise from the mat at regular intervals. They are not buildings in the ordinary sense, but thickened regions of the plant, encouraged to grow vertically and reinforced with black ceramic ribs. Some are refineries: towers of jointed stalks whose interiors glow amber through translucent skins. Some are fabrication yards where mechanical arms emerge from flowering apertures and assemble more arms, more rails, more sections of living architecture. Some are power stations, their broad crown-leaves held open to the white sky while coils beneath them hum with captured light.

The machinery moves constantly, but with restraint. Conveyor ribbons slide beneath the surface. Presses close and open inside fleshy walls. Thousands of small harvesters crawl through the leaf-layers, removing damaged tissue, trimming invasive growth, and threading replacement fibers into the mat. They make almost no sound. Their work is felt more than heard: a minute tremor beneath the feet, a pressure in the teeth, a periodic tightening of the ground.

There is no mud between the structures, no empty lot, no neglected verge. The density is nearly complete. Walkways pass through the country as narrow corridors lined with upright leaves, each leaf serving simultaneously as wall, lamp, conduit, and sign. Their surfaces display instructions in bands of changing color. A path may be marked safe by a slow pulse of violet, forbidden by a dull red bruising, or temporarily absent altogether when the plant decides that the corridor has never existed.

This happens often.

The landscape is old enough to have settled into its own impossibilities. A road can lead toward a distant processing district and arrive at the same place from the opposite direction. A tower seen across the plain may be taller each time it is observed, though its base never changes. Some machines cast shadows that fall upward and collect along the undersides of leaves like dark moss. Certain doors open only for people who remember having used them before. A train of freight-carriers may pass silently across the horizon, each vehicle filled with bundled green material, and then pass again a few seconds later from the other direction, its cargo now already installed in the walls around you.

These contradictions do not feel violent. They are not storms in reality so much as seams in a vast, heavily used fabric. The world has been repaired many times, and each repair has left a new rule.

At the continental scale, the plant produces weather.

Far away, entire districts fold their upper surfaces into enormous shutters. The action begins as a darkening on the horizon, then travels for hours across the plain: one region closes, then another, until a quarter of the world becomes a field of overlapping green roofs. This is not rain protection. The shutters regulate radiation, pressure, and the migration of airborne nutrients. When they close, cold currents pour between them. Mineral dust rises in long pale streamers and travels low across the surface, slipping through vents and settling into the plant’s intake pores. The storm makes almost no noise. Its approach is announced by the dimming of the light and by the synchronized inclination of millions of stalks.

At other times, the roofs open all at once.

The brightness becomes severe. Every surface shines. The plant’s upper skin turns translucent, revealing a depth of running processes below: veins carrying orange charge, factories pulsing with white heat, root-like structures threading through old foundations, and small black engines moving along arteries wider than rivers. There is no water anywhere, but there are flows: of dust, electricity, sap-thick hydrocarbons, spores, heat, data, and living material.

The ecology is total and almost entirely botanical. Every niche has been occupied by a plant-form or by a machine descended from one. There are climbing filters that hunt airborne particles. Carnivorous maintenance fans that open around defective mechanisms. Pale parasitic films that infiltrate unused buildings and convert their walls into new leaves. Tall, thin organisms stand in fields like antennae, listening to changes in pressure and the distant operations of other regions. At night—or what passes for night beneath the planet’s orbital shadow—they release clouds of cold phosphorescent spores, and the plain resembles a starfield viewed from below.

Fauna is scarce. An occasional maintenance animal crosses the corridor: a six-legged thing assembled from tendon, ceramic, and old industrial instincts, moving without visible joints. It pauses beside a wall, inserts its head into a flowering socket, and withdraws carrying a spool of fresh nerve-fiber. Elsewhere, something large may disturb a roof of leaves, but when the folds settle there is usually no body to find—only a sequence of footprints that become roots, then cables, then a row of identical doors.

The silence is one of the most striking features of the place.

There is no insect drone, no birdcall, no running water, no animal chorus. The factories should be deafening, but their engines are insulated by living tissue and distributed across distances too vast for sound to cross cleanly. What remains is a low, intermittent murmur: the soft click of leaf-joints; the dry rasp of mineral dust; the distant compression of a giant stalk; a tremolo underfoot when an entire district changes production. Sometimes a tone rises from nowhere, pure and almost too quiet to hear. It may be a warning, or a seed germinating several hundred kilometers away.

The horizon is crowded with constructed growth. Green towers, ribbed bridges, suspended gardens of machinery, and immense cultivation domes stand so close together that the land appears to have no gaps. Yet the eye cannot measure the distance correctly. A structure may seem nearby for days. A nearby structure may occupy a continent. Perspective slips most readily around the oldest works, where the plant has grown over abandoned factories and the factories have continued building inside the plant. There are districts whose interiors contain their own distant horizons. There are stairways that descend through layers of leaf and steel until they emerge onto the same bright plain beneath a different sun.

Nothing here is wild, but nothing is fully controlled either.

The plant continues to alter its makers’ designs. It has begun producing organs for functions no engineer remembers specifying: immense hollow stalks that point toward empty places in the sky, fields of closed blossoms that contain rooms larger than their outer surfaces, and root systems that avoid certain patches of ground as though the buried machinery beneath them is asleep. Production schedules drift into seasons. Factories flower. Assembly lines shed bark. Walls migrate by fractions of a meter each year, converging on one another until corridors must be cut through them again.

The whole landscape is active in the quiet manner of a living body.

It breathes without air. It feeds without water. It grows without wilderness. Its weather is a management cycle; its geology is layered manufacture; its forests are factories; its roots are transportation networks. Beneath the bright, cool sky, the continent keeps building itself, leaf by leaf, machine by machine, in a silence so complete that every small error can be heard.
