The place is a low basin, nearly level, where the land has been worn down to its oldest shape. There are no ridges left, no exposed slopes, no stones standing high enough to cast a meaningful shadow. The ground extends only as far as the eye can follow the vegetation—perhaps a few hundred meters before the basin closes in with another wall of stalks—but within that small area there is a surprising amount of air.

The plants grow in enormous, widely separated forms.

Some are reeds, though calling them reeds gives the wrong impression. They are pale green columns, thick as towers and hollow for most of their height, with smooth skins marked by old horizontal joints. Others lie flat across the basin like mats of tough, jointed leaves, their surfaces serving as the actual ground. There are broad, low plants with ribbed leaves stretched from one distant stem to another, making suspended floors and shallow troughs. Between these structures are open spaces: cool chambers of gray air, ten or twenty paces wide, with nothing in them but drifting vapor and the occasional insect.

The vegetation is total, but it is not dense. It does not form a wall. It forms a sparse architecture.

Every visible surface is living or recently living. The soil, where it can be seen, is black and fibrous, a compressed accumulation of roots, fallen leaves, and the soft remnants of older growth. Underfoot, the mat gives slightly and then holds. It has the resilience of felt. Patches of brown water lie in shallow depressions, collected from the last rain or from condensation gathered overnight on the broad leaves. They are not ponds so much as temporary mirrors. Some are no deeper than a hand; others sink into dark root holes and continue underground.

Water is never far away, but it is seldom present in abundance. Moisture beads along the stalks in the cool hours. It gathers in seams and drips at regular intervals. After rain, the basin fills with thin rivulets that run over leaf surfaces rather than through soil, descending from one plant to the next in a slow, distributed drainage system. Within a day or two, almost all of it has vanished into the roots or risen again into the air.

The air is cool enough to keep everything subdued. Breath is faintly visible in the shadowed spaces. The plants are not lush in the warm, soft sense; they are massive, durable, and economical. Their greens are dark, blue-green, or gray, with waxy surfaces and pale undersides. Here and there, an old stalk has dried to the color of bone, but even these are not truly dead. They remain upright for centuries, carrying water, spores, small animals, and the constant vibration of the basin.

That vibration is the first thing noticed.

The place is loud.

Not with birdsong or surf, but with a continuous industrial roar that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere. The hollow stalks carry wind through their interiors. Their joints contract and release as water pressure shifts, producing deep pulses like distant engines starting and stopping. Broad leaves tremble against one another in long sheets. Narrow seed cases chatter in their thousands. When a gust passes through the basin, the sound becomes the rush of a factory floor: belts turning, ducts shuddering, turbines running behind walls.

There are no walls, no pipes, no constructed machines. The machinery is the vegetation itself.

The oldest plants operate through simple, relentless systems. Their roots draw moisture from below. Their stems raise it. Their leaves condense it, shed it, and draw it back through capillary channels. Air moves through the hollows. Spores and dust travel on timed releases. Pressure builds in sealed chambers and escapes in heavy, resonant breaths. Nothing is quick, and nothing is improvised. The entire basin has settled into a permanent process, a natural production line that manufactures leaf tissue, seed, heat, moisture, and shelter.

The cycles are so stable that they appear motionless. A leaf may remain half-open for years. A stalk may incline by a fraction of an inch and never go farther. Rain comes intermittently, but its arrival does not transform the place. The system has no visible season, only slight variations in dampness and the changing angle of ordinary daylight. Growth and decay occur together at such a low rate that the landscape seems frozen at the exact balance between them.

Animal life is abundant, though scattered through the emptiness.

Small pale insects move along the stems in slow, purposeful lines. Some live beneath the leaf skins, visible as dark beads traveling through translucent tissue. Long-legged flies gather around the condensation pools. Beetles with matte shells climb the dry stalks and disappear into openings no wider than a finger. There are moths, but they keep low, using the undersides of leaves as shelter from the persistent air currents.

In the higher hollows, small birds nest in the joints of the plants. Their calls are sharp and brief, nearly lost in the larger sound. They launch themselves between the stalks and vanish into open air, appearing again far away without crossing any visible cover. A few larger animals move through the root mats—compact, furred things with broad feet, adapted to the soft ground. They leave shallow impressions that fill with water and are quickly occupied by larvae.

Nothing here is conspicuous for long. The open spaces make every animal seem isolated, but there are signs everywhere: clipped stems, faint tracks in the root felt, shed skins caught on waxy leaves, and the occasional tremor traveling through an entire stand when something heavy passes beneath it.

The basin feels ancient not because it is ruined, but because it has gone beyond ruin. Erosion has removed nearly every sharp distinction. Water has polished the low ground. Roots have softened the remnants of stone. The plants have incorporated everything that could be incorporated, covering old mineral surfaces with layers of living tissue. There are no cliffs, no exposed foundations, no artifacts, and no evidence that anything has ever been built here. Even the oldest fallen stalks do not make a pile; they are drawn down into the floor and returned to circulation.

At ordinary midday, the light is pale and even. The open sky above the basin is visible in broad, unbroken sections between the upper leaves. Sunlight enters as a diffuse silver illumination rather than as bright shafts. Shadows are shallow, soft-edged, and greenish. In the deepest hollows, the light has the color of water, though there is nothing supernatural in it: only layers of leaf and the cool reflection of damp surfaces.

The place is small enough to cross in minutes, but it does not feel traversable. Each open chamber leads to another by a narrow passage between living columns, and each passage is filled with sound. A person could walk from one end to the other while remaining within sight of the sky, yet the repeated stalks and suspended leaves would erase any sense of direction. The basin has no landmark except the rhythm of its growth.

Occasionally, a large central plant releases a pressure wave. Its outer leaves fold inward with a series of heavy snaps, and the hollow trunk gives a low, resonant boom. Every other plant answers—not deliberately, perhaps, but according to the same old mechanics. The sound rolls outward through the basin, through roots and stalks and empty air, until the whole landscape seems to be running.

Then it settles again.

The leaves resume their tremor. Condensation gathers. Insects continue along their routes. Water drops through the green machinery at measured intervals. The flat ancient ground remains exactly where it has always been, and the living factory continues its work without invention, interruption, or end.
