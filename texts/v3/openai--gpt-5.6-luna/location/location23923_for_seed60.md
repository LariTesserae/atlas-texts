The landscape occupies no more than a few hundred paces, but it is difficult to see its boundaries. It is a low place, almost level, where a shallow basin has been filled—not with soil, exactly, but with the accumulated bodies and exudations of plants. The ground gives slightly underfoot. It is not muddy; it is fibrous, springy, and warm, a vast mat of roots, runners, collapsed stems, and pale underground tissues knitted into something more durable than earth.

Everything grows from everything else.

The principal plants are broad-leaved and low, with leaves layered like roofing tiles. Their surfaces are slick and dark green, though here and there a coppery bloom or a blue-black vein passes through them like oxidized metal. Vines descend from the air and disappear into the floor. Mosses climb the trunks of larger growths, while smaller flowering things root in the moss, and smaller still root in the flowers. No clear patch of bare ground remains. Even the stones are furred with living membranes that pulse faintly when touched.

The air is warm and wet, but not oppressively tropical. Moisture hangs close to the surface. It collects in the hollows of leaves and along the undersides of bridges of root, then drops in slow, irregular patters. Water lies just below the vegetation mat, and in places it rises through narrow cracks as clear seepage. The old stone walls weep steadily. Their dampness has turned the mortar into a soft gray paste, and tiny plants have taken possession of every seam.

The basin may once have been an industrial yard, though the remains are nearly hidden. A low brick building stands at one edge, its roof swallowed by a colony of enormous ferns. One wall has been pushed outward by a trunk-like growth, but the wall has not collapsed; it has settled into a new agreement with the plant, brick and wood fused in a slow embrace. Rusted pipes emerge from the greenery and vanish again after a few feet. A narrow metal stair leads nowhere, its lower half buried under leaves, its upper half serving as a trellis for pale tendrils.

Something still operates beneath the basin.

From below comes a deep, continuous thudding—not fast enough to be a conventional engine, and not regular enough to be a pump. It resembles the beat of an enormous heart heard through walls. Every few minutes the thudding is joined by a grinding shudder. The whole green floor trembles. Water rises in the seams. Leaves turn their faces toward the sound.

There are other noises, layered thickly over it: the metallic knock of a loose pipe, the hiss of pressure escaping from somewhere unseen, the clatter of seed capsules striking one another, the wet creak of stalks bending under their own weight. The vegetation is not quiet. It rustles even when there is no wind. Large leaves fold and unfold with soft, leathery reports. Long hollow stems whistle through their pores. In the enclosed parts of the basin, the sound becomes almost industrial—a factory muffled in compost, machinery continuing its work after the factory has been forgotten.

The plants have taken on some of the functions of machines.

A stand of tall reeds near the center draws water upward in slow pulses. Their jointed stalks are fitted with natural valves: dark rings that close and open at intervals, sending vibrations through the grove. Nearby, a cluster of fleshy towers exhales warm vapor. Each tower bears a grid of tiny pores, and when the buried mechanism beats, the pores open together, releasing a cloud that smells of wet iron, crushed mint, and electrical insulation.

There are cables too, but they are difficult to distinguish from roots. Some have been wrapped in bark. Some carry a faint green current beneath transparent skins. A few terminate in small glass bulbs embedded in tree trunks, where they glow dimly even in ordinary daylight. The bulbs do not illuminate much. They merely indicate that something is flowing.

Light reaches the place without drama. The sky overhead is pale and clear enough, but the vegetation divides it into a green-brown twilight. Sunlight falls in broken plates across the upper leaves and is caught there, converted into a subdued internal radiance. The lower levels remain softly shadowed. Every surface shines. The air has the visual thickness of a room after rain.

The boundaries are marked by three things: a rise in old concrete, a band of black water, and a sudden thinning of plants. Beyond the basin, the surrounding land is ordinary—low scrub, damp grass, perhaps the remains of a service road. Within the boundary, however, the growth is packed so tightly that moving through it is less like walking through a garden than entering a body. There are chambers and passages, but they are formed by pressure and habit rather than design. Some are roofed by interwoven stems. Others open briefly onto shallow pools where pale roots drift like submerged cables.

Few animals live here. Insects are present, but even they are scarce: a handful of long-legged flies, small moths with transparent wings, and black beetles that shelter under the machinery stones. There are no birds nesting in the upper growth. Nothing appears to hunt. The plants have occupied nearly every ecological role, including scavenger, shelter, filter, and predator.

Now and then, a soft-bodied thing is found beneath the leaves: a translucent grub, a blind amphibian, a knot of tiny white legs. These creatures do not flee quickly. They seem to have grown accustomed to the slow movements of the place. They may be fed by it, or merely tolerated.

The strangest rule is that nothing here changes suddenly.

A fallen branch may remain suspended in a cradle of vines for years before being lowered to the ground. A pool fills one millimeter at a time. A wall leans for decades without falling. Leaves turn toward light so gradually that their motion can only be confirmed by comparing them to old marks. The machinery beneath the ground makes its great effort in patience rather than speed.

But the place is not still. It is always becoming more fully itself.

If a metal object is left among the plants, roots begin to circle it within days. After months, the metal darkens and softens. After years, it is incorporated into the living floor, retaining only a suggestion of its original shape. The plants do not consume objects so much as translate them. A pipe becomes a vein. A cable becomes a root. A wall becomes a ridge of bark-hard tissue. The buried industrial system, in turn, appears to be translating the plants back into machinery.

No one agrees whether the engines maintain the ecosystem or whether the ecosystem has grown engines to maintain itself.

At the center is a small clearing, though “clearing” is generous. It is a round room formed by the inward arching of enormous leaves, each ribbed with brown metal and green flesh. A low concrete pedestal stands there, surrounded by shallow water. On the pedestal is a control panel with three remaining switches. They are furred with moss. Above them, a row of dead indicator lights has been replaced by tiny blossoms.

Two of the switches are permanently fused in place.

The third moves, but only slightly. When pressed, it does not turn anything on. It alters the rhythm of the underground thudding. The change is almost imperceptible at first. The plants respond over the course of minutes: the reeds lengthen their intervals, the vapor towers close, and the whole basin seems to draw one long breath.

Then, somewhere beneath the floor, a machine begins to turn in the opposite direction.

Water trembles in every leaf cup. The walls give a slow creak. The dense green mass shifts—not enough to open a path, not enough to reveal what lies below, but enough to suggest that the landscape is capable of movement and has simply chosen, for now, to move slowly.
