The city is hidden inside a hollow.

From outside, there is almost nothing to find: a low ridge of pale mineral rising from an endless plain, weather-rounded and cracked like an old bone. It has no entrance large enough to suggest habitation, only a narrow black seam at its base. Warm air pours from this seam with such force that dust streams uphill toward it. The sound is already loud there—a deep, continuous bellow, as if the ridge were breathing through clenched teeth.

Inside, the hollow opens suddenly.

It is not large. A person could cross it in less than an hour, and yet it contains streets, courtyards, towers, work yards, shrines, storehouses, and entire neighborhoods pressed into the concave walls. The city has been built downward and inward rather than outward. Its foundations follow the natural ribs of the cavity. Houses occupy ledges. Bridges span narrow shafts. Stairways wind around pillars of uncut stone. Roofs overlap like scales, leaving strips of brilliant light between them.

There is no water anywhere.

The inhabitants do not speak of this as a deprivation. Water is an old word, almost ceremonial, used for something from stories about the outer world. No cisterns stand in the streets. No wells descend beneath the floors. No channels divide the courtyards. The city’s basins are filled instead with white mineral powder, and the fountains are dry columns hung with bells.

The vegetation is nevertheless lush—thick, exuberant, and almost aggressively alive.

It grows from cracks in the stone, from roof gutters, from abandoned rooms, from the seams between paving slabs. Long green blades emerge in clustered fans, their surfaces waxy and translucent. Their roots do not seek moisture but warmth and dissolved salts. They draw nourishment from the mineral walls and from the faint chemical vapors rising through the hollow. Some plants are jointed like reeds, though they contain no liquid sap. Break one and it releases a hot, sharp-smelling gas. Others form broad, pleated leaves that collect light and grind airborne dust into food.

Vines climb everywhere, but they are not soft. They are corded and fibrous, with pale tendrils that tighten around railings and masonry as they grow. Their flowers are hard, many-petaled things like carved glass. At midday they open with a succession of loud clicks, filling the streets with the sound of a thousand tiny locks being turned.

There are trees, though calling them trees is an accommodation. They are columns of layered bark rising from the floor and bending toward the ceiling, their crowns spread flat beneath the vents where the hot air enters. Their leaves hang vertically in silver-green curtains. When the wind passes through them, the whole city roars.

This is the deafening part of the place.

Air moves through the hollow in permanent currents. It enters through fissures high above, accelerates down the walls, and escapes through the black seam below. The architecture has grown around this movement—or perhaps the city was founded because of it. Every arch, alley, and tower is shaped to catch or redirect the wind. Narrow streets become flutes. Doorways boom. Bridges hum underfoot. The dry fountains ring continuously, their suspended plates striking one another in the gusts.

Conversation is conducted by touch, gesture, and colored flags. Children learn to read the movement of leaves before they learn speech. In the markets, a buyer presses a palm to the seller’s wrist to signal a price. At night—if night can be said to exist beneath the luminous roof—people sleep in rooms lined with felt and packed plant fiber, though the wind still shudders through the walls.

The brightness comes from above.

The hollow’s ceiling is pierced by a long, irregular opening, far too narrow to be seen from the plain. Sunlight pours through it in hard white shafts. Dust hangs in those shafts like a second architecture. The light strikes the mineral walls and scatters, turning every surface bright: chalk, bone, old plaster, salt, bleached wood. There are no shadows in the central avenues, only thin blue stains beneath the bridges and under the leaves.

The city has settled into this shape over a very long time. Nothing looks newly made. The stones are worn smooth at the hand-height of generations. Steps have become shallow bowls. Doorways have been rubbed wider by shoulders. Roof tiles have fused with dust and plant resin. Whole buildings lean against one another, not from collapse but from age, as if the city has gradually grown tired and begun to rest.

Construction is simple and exact. Walls are fitted from slabs split with wedges, heated, and struck along their natural lines. Timber is rare, replaced by braided stalks, mineral blocks, hornlike plant fiber, and thin sheets of hammered soft metal. The people use no engines. Their machines are wind-driven and mostly stationary: bellows, mills, lifts, fans, and great rattling frames that grind stone into the powders needed by the plants. Every mechanism is operated by ropes, counterweights, and hands.

The most important structures are the wind houses.

They stand along the upper terraces, tall and narrow, pierced with hundreds of openings. Their walls are covered in membranes stretched over wooden ribs. When the air passes through them, the houses produce different tones—some low enough to vibrate in the chest, others sharp enough to hurt the teeth. Each district maintains its own wind house. The tones are not music in any gentle sense, but they carry information: warnings of dustfall, changes in pressure, the opening of distant vents, the beginning of work, mourning, celebration.

When several wind houses sound together, the city becomes unbearable. The bridges tremble. Loose stones creep across rooftops. The plants bow in unison. People tie strips of cloth around their heads and move quickly through the bright streets while the entire hollow cries out.

Fauna is scarce. A few pale, many-legged creatures live among the foundations, feeding on shed fibers and mineral films. They are seldom seen except as quick movements beneath mats or inside wall cracks. Small winged things inhabit the upper vents, but they are more like drifting scraps of living paper than birds. Once each warm season, a larger animal enters through the lower seam—a blind, jointed grazer from the plain. It remains for several days, stripping the outer vegetation, then follows the wind back into the open world. The city closes its gates around it and waits.

Most of the space between buildings is empty.

This emptiness is one of the city’s defining features. Its avenues are broad, its courtyards oversized, its towers separated by long expanses of pale stone. There are whole plazas containing only a single tree, a dry fountain, and a shrine no larger than a cupboard. The population is small enough that a person can stand in the center of a district and see no one. Yet the infrastructure suggests many more lives: hundreds of doorways, abandoned workshops, roof gardens gone wild, stairways leading to sealed rooms.

The city is not deserted. It is simply sparse, as if built for a multitude that never arrived—or as if its inhabitants have learned to spread themselves thin against the immense pressure of the hollow’s sound.

There are rules here that are not laws and cannot be broken safely.

Nothing may be sealed completely, because still air is considered dangerous. Every room must have two openings, however small. No plant may be cut at its root. No one may carry a vessel designed to hold liquid. The dead are not buried; they are placed in high niches where the wind strips them down to mineral and fiber. At the beginning of each season, every household must remove one stone from its own walls and set it in the public streets. This keeps the city from becoming too rigid, the elders say. A city that cannot change its shape will eventually crack.

The oldest residents claim that the hollow is not a cavity but a seed.

They say the city has grown inside it because the seed has not yet decided whether to open. The vegetation is its first thought. The towers are its teeth. The wind is its sleeping breath. If the city ever becomes quiet, even for a moment, the shell will split and something enormous will emerge into the plain.

No one knows whether this is true.

No one wants to test it.

So the plants continue clicking open in the sun. The mineral streets shine. Dust climbs toward the hidden entrance. The wind enters, passes through every arch and membrane and silver leaf, and leaves again as a deafening voice.

Within the small hollow, the old city remains bright, warm, green, and almost empty—moving constantly, but never going anywhere.
