The place is a drowned district beneath a frozen inland sea.

It is not large. A person could cross it in ten minutes if there were a road, though there are no roads now—only streets descending through black water, their paving stones furred with pale mineral growth. The district occupies a shallow basin between low ridges of slate, all of it enclosed beneath the lake’s dark pressure. Above, somewhere beyond several dozen meters of water, the surface is sealed by winter ice. No daylight reaches the streets. The water has its own permanent night.

The buildings stand close together, as if they had been designed for a population far larger than the one that remains. Narrow tenements lean against workshops. A municipal hall, square and windowless, presses its roof into the underside of the frozen water. Pipes and gantries bridge the gaps between structures. Metal walkways run from door to door, from roof to roof, and down into the flooded interiors, forming a second city suspended in the first. Everything is near-touching: walls, ladders, cables, tanks, chimneys, the hanging loops of old power lines. There is hardly an unoccupied cubic meter.

The water is cold enough to make all color withdraw from the world. Brick is charcoal. Copper has become a green-black skin. Painted signs have faded into smears, their letters visible only where corrosion has spared them. The stone streets are pale and slick, their joints filled with a fine gray silt that moves in slow streamers whenever the water shifts. Here and there, patches of white frost cling to submerged surfaces—not ice, exactly, but a salt-like deposit that grows in feathery fans around leaking valves and cracked foundations.

The district was built around a factory that no longer knows it is abandoned.

Its central plant occupies a low, many-sided building at the basin’s center. A smokestack rises from it and ends against the ice overhead. The stack is sealed, but every so often a hidden mechanism inside the plant exhales. A column of bubbles travels up through the darkness, stirring the water and making the surrounding cables tremble. The factory’s pumps still cycle according to a schedule established long ago. They draw water from one part of the district and release it into another, keeping some chambers empty, some half-filled, and some under a continuous inward flow.

The machines do not run loudly. Their motion has been reduced by water, distance, and age to a series of small physical facts: a valve turning somewhere behind a wall; a belt shivering on a submerged wheel; the soft knock of a piston; a line of bubbles slipping from a seam. The silence between these events is immense. It is possible to stand beside an engine large enough to fill a cathedral and hear only the tick of cooling metal.

In the factory’s lower levels, production has not ceased so much as changed purpose. Conveyors carry black nodules from one bin to another. Robotic arms, jointed and encrusted, sort lengths of cable according to no visible rule. A stamping press descends once every few hours onto an empty die. Its impact is almost soundless, but the force passes through the buildings and makes all the suspended lamps swing together.

There are lamps, though few still work. They are protected in thick glass cages and burn with a weak blue-green radiance generated by the plant’s residual current. Their light does not spread far. It catches on nearby surfaces, illuminating a railing, the underside of a stair, a row of dead windows, and then loses itself in the water. Beyond each small circle of illumination is not ordinary darkness but a layered darkness, full of depth and architecture: the next building, the next pipe, the next street, all waiting just beyond recognition.

Vegetation is almost absent. A few growths survive in the warmest places, beneath exhaust vents and around the plant’s electrical housings. They resemble thin black reeds or drowned grasses, though they are made of translucent tissue and mineral threads. They grow in dense little mats, each filament bending toward a faint current rather than toward light. When disturbed, they close like fingers. Their roots enter cracks in concrete and draw heat from the machinery below.

The more persistent life is smaller and stranger. Pale films spread over walls in the dark, feeding on rust, oil, and the weak electric fields around cables. Tiny white organisms—too narrow to be called worms, too jointed to be roots—move through these films in coordinated lines. They avoid open water. They avoid one another. When a machine starts, they all turn toward it, forming temporary roads over the masonry.

Fauna is rare enough that each animal seems like an event. A blind, seal-sized thing sometimes glides through the upper streets, its body almost transparent, its ribs visible like the hoops of a barrel. It has no eyes and makes no sound. Pressure-sensitive whiskers trail from its face and brush against walls, doors, and submerged signs. It appears to know the layout better than the machines do. Once, perhaps, it lived in the lake beyond the district. Now it returns only to feed on the mineral mats and the small white things that gather around them.

There are no fish in the usual sense. The water is too cold, too chemically altered, and too full of old industrial residue. Yet something moves in the tanks beneath the factory, where the liquid is warmer. Shapes gather there in layers, packed tightly together, each one no larger than a hand. They are not always visible as bodies. Sometimes they are seen only when the tank’s blue light passes through them and breaks into hundreds of brief, synchronized sparks.

The water itself has an order.

It does not behave entirely according to gravity. In the open streets it settles, but in the factory it rises through vertical shafts, hangs in narrow columns, and flows sideways along ceilings. A submerged doorway may open onto a room where the water is held back as though by an invisible wall. A staircase may lead downward through liquid and emerge into an air-filled chamber beneath the lake. Air pockets persist in impossible places: under a bridge, inside a clock tower, behind a sealed apartment door. They are not bubbles. They have boundaries, pressure, and weather of their own.

Rain falls inside some of the buildings.

It descends in thin vertical curtains from ceilings where no opening exists, gathering in basements and disappearing into drains that lead nowhere. The rain is colder than the surrounding water. It leaves tiny beads on submerged metal, each bead containing a warped reflection of the factory lights. In certain rooms, the rainfall moves upward. In others, it travels sideways across the floor and climbs the walls.

The old inhabitants prepared for flooding, but not for this. Their signs remain: arrows toward shelters, evacuation routes, colored bands around doorways marking safe pressure levels. The shelters are still sealed. Behind one circular hatch, a row of chairs can be seen through a porthole, all facing a dark screen. Behind another, hundreds of coats hang in orderly ranks, their fabric scarcely moved by the water.

Human traces are everywhere and human presence is nowhere. Canteens are stacked in wall lockers. A tram sits halfway through a tunnel, its doors open, its interior filled with silt. In a small square, a clock has stopped at different times on each face. The square is crowded with statues of workers, but the statues have been turned away from the central plant, as if they are watching something beyond the walls.

The district has weathered into a stable condition. Its surfaces are worn smooth by years of current and sediment. Rust has reached the point beyond collapse and hardened into dark, thick shells. Foundations have settled. The leaning buildings no longer lean further. Cables have sagged into permanent curves. Every break has become a habitat, every leak a boundary, every failed machine part of the district’s new circulation.

Nothing rushes here, but much is happening.

Currents pass through the streets in slow tides. Pumps alter the pressure in neighboring rooms. Mineral growths open and close. Lamps dim, recover, and dim again. The black reeds flex toward the machinery. The blind animal makes a circuit through the lower blocks. Deep beneath the factory, something turns in its tank, and the entire district adjusts around that movement.

The quiet is never empty. It is made of distance, insulation, and restraint. A gloved hand against a railing produces a dull vibration that travels through half the city. A door opening releases a soft plume of silt. Somewhere, a cable snaps and hangs motionless. Somewhere else, the factory stamps its blank shape into nothing.

Above all of it is the ice: a low, unseen ceiling, faintly luminous only when the machinery sends pulses upward through the water. On rare occasions, a crack forms in the frozen lake. The sound does not arrive as a crack but as a vast, slow pressure, a muted groan passing down through the submerged streets. Dust lifts from the rooftops. The lamps flicker. Every living thing folds itself close to stone.

Then the ice settles again.

The pumps resume.

And the little industrial city continues to maintain its darkness.
