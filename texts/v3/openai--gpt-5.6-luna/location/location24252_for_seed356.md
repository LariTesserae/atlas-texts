The landscape is called a forest only because there is no better word for a thing that occupies the whole world.

It lies low beneath a ceiling of darkness, though “beneath” is uncertain. There is no visible sky, no horizon, and no reliable distinction between ground and roof. The terrain extends outward in broad, shallow undulations: basins of black water, raised shelves of root-mat, long wet escarpments where the living surface has split and curled upward. Everything is close to sea level, or to whatever serves as sea level here. The world has not yet decided on its continents. Its landforms are fresh, abrupt, and unweathered, as if lifted from a soft mold only recently.

Water is everywhere, but rarely in motion. It gathers in clear, warm films between leaves. It hangs in beads on vertical curtains of moss. It rises through translucent stems and appears as slow, bright pressure beneath their skins. The hollows are filled with dark water that reflects nothing—not even the faint internal glows of the vegetation above it. Along the root-shelves, water seeps sideways rather than downward, traveling in narrow channels through the plant matter. It emerges from walls in steady threads, crosses the floor without a sound, and disappears into mats of pale growth.

The vegetation has no edge. It is not a collection of plants growing in a landscape; it is the landscape’s substance.

The ground is braided root, swollen fiber, fallen frond, fungal-looking film, and green-black tissue fused into one continuous substrate. Some stretches are firm enough to walk across, though there is no walker and no path. Other regions flex imperceptibly beneath their own weight. They resemble enormous carpets, but their weave is vascular: vessels as wide as canals run through them, carrying warm fluids from unseen interiors. The surface is crowded with stems, fans, reeds, shelves, tendrils, and flat mineral-green membranes. They grow so densely that empty air is almost absent. Every gap is occupied by another layer of life.

There are groves in which the trunks are hollow tubes, clustered by the thousands. Their walls are dark and glossy, marked with tiny pores that open and close together. There are plains of low leaves, each leaf broad enough to be a roof, overlapping in disciplined ranks. There are towers—if they can be called towers—that rise far beyond sight, their upper reaches lost in the absolute black. They do not sway. Nothing here sways. Their immense bodies are held in a still equilibrium, supported by pressure and root tension, each one touching its neighbors so closely that the forest behaves like a single object.

No animal has ever crossed this place. There are no tracks, nests, gnawed stems, wings, eyes, shells, or bones. The only life is botanical, though even that distinction becomes unstable. Some growths behave like organs. Some behave like machines.

In the darkness, the plant-world performs its industry.

Vast columns draw water upward with a slow, tireless pulse. Sheets of tissue filter the damp air and strip it of warmth, trace gases, and suspended minerals. Buried reservoirs contract in sequence, pushing fluids through the root plains. Far below the surface, unseen chambers ferment dense chemical slurries. The processes are scaled beyond any human factory: whole regions function as pumps, refineries, cooling towers, storage tanks, and power stations, yet there are no buildings and no artifacts. The machinery is alive, and its production is not for use. It is simply what the world does to remain itself.

From time to time, a distant pressure moves through the ground. It is not an earthquake. The root-mat tightens, the wet shelves rise by a fraction, and a low system of vascular throbs travels across the plain for hundreds of kilometers. The motion is so slow and complete that it seems less like an event than a property of the terrain. When it passes, the landscape is exactly as it was before.

The atmosphere is warm, saturated, and nearly still. Moisture condenses continuously on every exposed surface. The air has the heavy closeness of a greenhouse sealed for centuries, but without the smell of decay. There is no rot because nothing dies in the ordinary sense. Old tissues are absorbed, reprocessed, and returned to the larger body. Leaves darken and become walls. Walls soften and become soil. Soil lifts itself into stems.

The temperature does not vary enough to create weather. There are no storms, no seasons, no fronts. The world’s warmth is held at a steady level by the immense transpiring mass. Condensation forms and remains. Fog occupies the lowest basins in motionless layers, as though poured there. In some places it fills an entire valley and touches the underside of the darkness above, concealing the upper vegetation. In others it lies in narrow ribbons between root ridges, perfectly clear at its edges.

Light is almost absent. The darkness is not merely night; it is a material condition of the place. It presses into the vegetation, surrounds the trunks, and erases distance. Yet scattered within the plant mass are faint sources of green, amber, and blue-white illumination. They are not flowers or lanterns. They are biochemical windows: membranes opening to display internal reactions, channels carrying a cool glow through the tissue, enormous slow pulses in the depths of leaves. The light is too weak to reveal the landscape. It only reveals that the darkness has layers.

At times the glow aligns across the forest. A line of pale radiance may travel along a root shelf, then divide into branching channels and vanish. A whole stand of hollow trunks may illuminate internally, one after another, though the sequence takes several hours to complete. No signal announces itself. There is no observer to receive it. The processes continue with the impersonal regularity of a factory running after the workers have disappeared.

Sound is rare and soft. Water makes the clearest noises: a periodic drip into a submerged basin, the faint release of a bubble beneath a leaf, the whisper of seepage inside a wall. The vegetation emits almost no audible movement. Even when a stem opens, it does so without a snap. Even when a root mass settles, the sound is swallowed by the yielding ground.

Beneath these quiet sounds is a nearly inaudible industrial hum—not mechanical, but close to it. It is the combined resonance of billions of fluids under pressure. One hears it only when standing against the living walls, and then it seems to come from everywhere: a deep, steady note without beginning or end. It does not grow louder or softer. It is the sound of the world maintaining its internal circulation.

The landscape is strange because it has no outside.

There is no untouched patch, no clearing, no barren rock, no exposed mineral floor. Even the newest fractures are immediately lined with translucent runners. Even the water is alive with suspended threads. The apparent emptiness of the darkness conceals further vegetation, extending upward and outward beyond the range of any sense. The scale is too large for the eye to comprehend. Weather may belong to distant regions of the organism. A single stem may be a continent. A low ridge may be the edge of a root system whose other boundary has never been reached.

And yet the place feels young.

Its surfaces are sharp rather than rounded. Ridges end in clean breaks. Fresh green tissue covers raw seams. The channels have not widened into valleys; the basins have not become lakes; the towers have not leaned or fallen. Nothing bears the softened marks of long exposure. The world appears recently formed, recently rooted, still holding the shape of its first expansion.

There is no construction, but there are arrangements that resemble architecture: vaulted leaf caverns, columned groves, suspended vascular bridges, flat industrial plains covered in repeating pores. They have not been designed. They have emerged from the plant’s need to transport, filter, store, and endure. Their order is too consistent to seem natural, and their organic origin is too complete to seem manufactured.

The most unsettling feature is the absence of change. Not silence alone, but stability.

The water does not gradually drain. The forests do not visibly grow. The fog does not lift. No leaf falls. No trunk decays. No new horizon appears. The immense living system has reached a condition in which every process balances another process so exactly that time has become difficult to perceive. It may have been in this state for a day, or a million years. The same warm condensation gathers on the same leaf. The same pressure passes through the same buried conduits. The same dim internal lights appear in the same places.

Far across the black, a region of vegetation exhales.

The sound takes nearly an hour to arrive. It is a soft, continent-wide release: air moving through pores, water slipping through fibers, a million membranes opening in perfect agreement. The fog lifts by the height of a hand. The submerged roots brighten faintly. Then everything settles again.

The landscape remains vast, wet, warm, and alive.

Nothing enters it.

Nothing leaves.
