The high country is not a mountain, exactly, but a vast work of masonry raised into the cold.

It begins far below the horizon and climbs in broad, shallow stages: platforms, ramps, retaining walls, sunken courts, spillways, and long flights of steps. Every surface has been made by hand. There is no soil between the stones, no wild ledge, no pocket of earth hidden beneath a wall. The builders covered the original ground so completely that the plateau has become indistinguishable from its construction. Even the cliffs are faced with fitted blocks, their edges still sharp from recent making.

The whole region resembles a city after its streets have been stretched into a landscape. Its districts extend outward beyond immediate sight, not as houses or towers but as systems: a stairway vanishing into mist, a line of low reservoirs following the ridge, a causeway crossing a basin packed with square stone pillars. Buildings are present, but they have been pressed flat and spread wide. Their roofs form terraces. Their walls form ravines. Their courtyards are now lakes.

Water is everywhere, though rarely in a free, natural course. It gathers in designed places.

Thin sheets glide down polished inclines. Cisterns brim beneath stone lips. Channels cross one another at right angles, passing through slots cut into walls and emerging from mouths shaped like simple geometric masks. Rainwater stands in shallow rectangular pans, bright as metal under the high light. In some districts, the water seems to come directly through the masonry: beads swell along a wall, tremble, and fall into grooves prepared to receive them. The stone is cold and faintly damp to the touch even where no cloud has passed for days.

The atmosphere condenses against everything. A pale vapor rests in the lower courts and moves slowly through the stairways, never thick enough to conceal the architecture, only softening its far edges. The distant terraces appear and disappear by degrees. At dawn, if dawn can be distinguished here, the surfaces are silvered with a skin of moisture. Later, under the bright sky, the water shines white and blue, and the entire constructed plateau seems to have been carved from wet bone.

There is no vegetation. No moss stains the walls. No grass has forced itself into a joint. The absence is not merely barren; it is maintained. The seams between blocks are too narrow and too clean, filled with a dark, glassy compound that has hardened like pitch. Where a crack has appeared, it has been squared off and sealed. Nothing roots, spreads, blooms, burrows, or grazes. The only life-like activity belongs to water and mineral: slow deposits forming around outlets, translucent crusts thickening in the channels, pale films appearing on submerged steps and vanishing when the flow changes.

These films are not recognized as organisms. They have no scent, no pulse, and no visible structure beyond their tendency to gather. Left undisturbed, they align themselves in delicate parallel ridges. A handprint pressed into one remains for months.

The materials are simple but used with obsessive completeness. Gray stone, blue-black fired clay, white mortar, reddish brick, hammered copper, and plates of a dull mineral that catches the light without reflecting faces. The copper is greenless and dry-looking despite constant immersion. Metal exists, but sparingly, in hinges, clamps, hooks, and old tools left embedded in walls. Most of the great works were made with abrasion, wedges, rope, poured slurry, and patient labor. Many blocks bear the shallow marks of repeated hand tools. Their surfaces are not elegant. They are exact.

The plateau is densely occupied by construction. There are no empty fields, only empty functions: a basin without water, a stair leading to a sealed door, a row of pillars supporting nothing visible, a square yard whose drains remain perfectly clear. Presences press against one another. Channels run beside channels. Walls stand within walls. A cistern has been built atop a roof, and another beneath it. Roads divide into narrower roads, then into footways, then into grooves barely wide enough for a finger. From a high overlook, the city-landscape looks less like a settlement than an enormous instrument, its parts packed together to catch, conduct, store, and release water.

Yet almost nothing moves.

Water descends at a patient pace. Mist shifts when the light changes. Thin mineral curtains lengthen along the mouths of spillways. Occasionally a counterweight drops inside a tower, opening a sluice with a muted clack, and the resulting flow may continue for an entire season. Some reservoirs fill only when the air is wet enough. Some drains empty according to the shadow cast by a particular wall. The system is governed by gradients so weak that their effects must be watched for hours.

The people who made the place are absent, or have become indistinguishable from its maintenance. There are no footprints except those pressed into old clay and fired there permanently. No tools lie scattered. Instead, tools have been placed in niches beside every important mechanism: stone mallets, wooden levers, ceramic scoops, lengths of braided fiber gone stiff with mineral. They are crude, but each fits its designated task. The handles have been worn smooth by use.

Nothing here breaks suddenly. A wall does not collapse; it begins to lean over decades. A basin does not drain; its outlet narrows grain by grain. A bridge does not fail; its underside develops a line of pale crystals and waits. The landscape is young in its surfaces—fresh cuts, raw faces, unweathered corners—but old in its processes. It was built recently enough that erosion has barely begun to soften it, yet it was built for timescales longer than its makers’ lives.

The light is unusually bright, almost without warmth. The sky above the high terraces is a hard, washed blue, and the wet stone returns the brightness upward. Shadows are sharp at their edges but thin inside, as if the air itself were luminous. In the mist-filled lower courts, light enters in long white bars and catches on suspended droplets. There is nowhere to hide from it. Even the deepest passage eventually ends in a square of brilliance.

Sound is sparse and magnified by the density of walls. The dominant note is dripping: separate drops striking separate basins across the expanse, each with its own pitch. A narrow runnel whispers over a sloped tile. Water descending through a hidden shaft produces a low, continuous throat-sound beneath the paving. Far away, an unlocked gate may knock once in the wind and then remain still for years.

There is wind, but it does not cross the plateau freely. The architecture divides it into narrow currents. It sighs through slots, presses against parapets, and hums briefly in the mouths of empty conduits. In the open spaces, the air is nearly silent. Voices, if there were voices, would travel a great distance along the channels and return from the walls altered but understandable.

The strangest rule of the place is that water seems to recognize completion.

A newly repaired channel remains dry until its final stone is set. A basin with one missing tile gathers only dust; once the tile is fitted, condensation begins along its rim. A blocked drain will not overflow. Instead, water waits in the uppermost chamber, perfectly level, sometimes for years, as if the unfinished route has no meaning to it. When the intended path is restored, the entire system resumes with no surge or violence. Water simply appears farther along than it should be, already moving through the next completed section.

There are also places where the structures have begun to imitate natural forms without becoming natural. A ridge of buttresses rises like a frozen wave. A drainage district spreads in branching patterns resembling a leaf, though no leaf has ever grown there. A tower has been hollowed into a chimney that exhales cold vapor at regular intervals. These resemblances feel accidental only at first. The longer they are observed, the more they seem like the landscape’s own vocabulary: not life, but an architecture learning how to behave like weather.

At the outer edge of the constructed highland, the terraces end abruptly. Beyond them lies a great white distance, perhaps cloud, perhaps lower country entirely hidden beneath condensation. The final retaining wall is unfinished, its blocks stacked in a clean ascending line. Water trickles from the last completed drain and falls into the brightness below. It does not splash. The sound is lost before it reaches anything.

Behind you, the packed reservoirs and corridors continue across the plateau, bright, wet, quiet, and exact. Ahead, the world has not yet been built.
