The city lies low in a basin where the ground has not yet decided what it is.

It is not a valley, exactly. The surrounding country rises only a little—long dark shelves of unfinished stone, blunt ridges with their interiors showing, as if the earth had been cut open and left midway through assembly. Between them stretches a broad industrial plain, wet in patches and dry in others, crossed by channels that appear, vanish, and reappear somewhere else. The land is still making its drainage. A stream may divide into six silver threads overnight, or flow uphill for the length of a street before sinking soundlessly through a crack.

Cold governs the place, but not with snow. The air has the raw, metallic chill of dawn in a machine room. Frost gathers beneath bridges and along the shaded sides of buildings, while the sunlit roofs sweat steadily, releasing curtains of vapor. Rain comes in brief, decisive episodes: ten minutes of hard water, then an hour of brightness. Condensation is more reliable than weather. It pearls on cables, window frames, railings, leaves, smokestacks, animal fur. In the mornings, every exposed surface is beaded, and the city seems to have been dipped in glass.

The basin is crowded almost to suffocation.

Factories occupy the old floodplain in terraces and blocks: foundries, pumpworks, ceramic yards, nutrient plants, assembly sheds, refrigeration towers, and enormous buildings whose purpose has been forgotten or has not yet been invented. Their walls are made from poured concrete, corrugated iron, brick, glazed tile, and slabs of a dark stone still warm at the center. Pipes run everywhere. Some are elevated on forests of legs; others plunge into the ground and emerge half a mile away. Conveyor belts cross streets overhead. Narrow rail lines pass through markets and courtyards. Electrical cables knot between buildings in thick, sagging bundles, humming faintly even when no current should be in them.

There is very little empty space. Where construction has stopped, vegetation has already filled the interruption.

Moss carpets loading platforms. Pale grasses force themselves through seams in the asphalt. Vines climb cooling towers and enter through broken windows, where they spread into dense hanging gardens of leaves, tendrils, and small translucent flowers. The growth is lush but not generous-looking. It has the concentrated, opportunistic abundance of life in a crack. Greenery gathers around every source of moisture: beneath leaking valves, along drainage ditches, under railway bridges, beside the warm exhaust of generators. In the drier portions of the basin, entire belts of vegetation survive on fog and condensation, their leaves shaped like trays, needles, funnels, and folded hands.

Some plants are rooted in the ground. Others attach themselves to pipes, walls, or moving machinery. A species with black, jointed stems grows directly from brick mortar and rearranges its blossoms whenever a train passes. Low silver reeds spread through abandoned parking lots, vibrating before rain. In the industrial quarter, one sees groves of tall, hollow plants growing from vats of cooling water, their crowns opening at dusk to release clouds of glittering spores. The spores do not float away. They settle in a perfect ring around the plant, as if the air itself has boundaries.

Water is never far away, but it is never fully present. There are flooded basements, shallow ponds trapped in the hollows of unfinished foundations, and long pools beneath the elevated roads. In the open country beyond the city, dampness collects in dark seams between the slabs of emerging rock. During rain, these seams become rushing channels. By the next day, they may have hardened into smooth black ridges, leaving the water to seek another route.

The geology is young in a visible way.

New ground rises among the old streets. Mounds of stone swell beneath vacant lots, pushing up fences and buckling roads. Fresh mineral crusts form around leaks, white and green and faintly iridescent. Quarries at the edge of the basin are not cut into the earth so much as followed as it grows: crews with drills and excavators remove material from ridges that continue to extrude behind them. The quarry walls tremble occasionally, not from blasting but from some slow pressure underneath. No one reacts unless the movement is large enough to interrupt a shift.

Buildings settle, but they also bud.

A warehouse may acquire a second wall overnight, seamlessly joined to the first. A stairwell can lengthen by three steps between inspections. Streets terminate in raw ramps of stone that were not there the previous evening. The city’s survey maps are printed with blank spaces and warnings not to rely on them. Construction crews work in a constant negotiation with the terrain, pouring foundations while the ground is still warm, rerouting cables around new protrusions, demolishing structures that have grown too close to one another.

The instability is not catastrophic. It is continuous.

There are no great explosions, no permanent storm, no single disaster defining the place. Instead, everything is in motion at a scale just below panic. Cranes turn without visible operators. Doors open onto rooms that have shifted half a floor sideways. A bridge crew repairs a bridge while its far end slowly migrates downstream. Workers in yellow coats move through the streets with measuring rods and chalk, marking walls, rocks, roots, and animals with the same symbols. At intersections, traffic lights change according to the pressure beneath the road rather than the traffic above it.

And yet the city is mostly quiet.

The machinery is muffled by distance, insulation, moisture, and the density of construction. One hears a low electrical thrum, the occasional chuff of a locomotive, the soft chain-rattle of a conveyor, water ticking into a tank. Sound seems to be absorbed by the vegetation and the damp stone. Voices rarely carry. A factory siren can be seen vibrating in its housing without being heard more than a few meters away.

This quiet makes smaller sounds prominent: the click of cooling metal, the wet fold of a leaf, the skitter of claws over a roof, the slow grinding of a building’s foundations. Sometimes, from somewhere deep beneath the pavement, comes a sound like a vast animal turning over in sleep.

Fauna is abundant, though most creatures have adapted to the city’s industrial ecology.

Small, long-legged mammals nest in insulation and feed on mineral deposits around steam vents. Flocks of dark birds gather on power lines, but they do not fly away when approached; they simply rotate to face the intruder. In drainage channels live flat amphibious things with lamp-like eyes and transparent ribs, emerging only during rain. Beetles the size of hands swarm around factory lights, their shells carrying patterns that resemble maps of streets which do not exist.

There are grazing animals in the outer basin—stocky, cold-resistant creatures with moss growing along their backs. They move in herds through vacant industrial land, eating metal-tasting grass and the pale lichen that forms on concrete. Occasionally one wanders into the city and becomes part of the traffic almost immediately. People divert vehicles around it. A railing is built to guide it. A roof garden is planted so that it will have something to eat. Within a week, the animal has usually found a warm tunnel beneath the factories and established a permanent route through the settlement.

The people have built their lives around the world’s unfinishedness.

Neighborhoods are layered rather than planned: old brick apartments enclosed by newer steel frames, elevated walkways passing through church towers and processing plants, markets beneath railway arches, gardens on the roofs of warehouses, workshops in the shells of collapsed reservoirs. Every surface is occupied. Windows hold drying clothes, small furnaces, seed trays, signal lamps, and watching faces. Pipes carry heat, water, slurry, and sometimes things no one admits to recognizing.

The city’s rules are practical, though strange.

Do not seal a crack until it has stopped widening for three days.

Do not drain standing water without leaving a portion behind.

Do not sleep beneath a newly painted ceiling.

When the ground begins to hum, take your tools indoors and open all doors.

If a plant grows through a machine, allow it to finish.

The last rule is the most important. Machines that are forcibly cleared of vegetation tend to malfunction, reverse their function, or begin producing unfamiliar objects. A textile loom cleared of vines once spent six months weaving lengths of cool, damp darkness. A pumping station, after its roots were burned away, began pumping a brackish tide into the upper stories of nearby buildings.

The light is ordinary daylight, pale and gray-blue under the frequent cloud, bright enough to show every surface and shadow. Nothing is hidden by perpetual gloom. The strangeness is visible in full: the green inside the rust, the wet gleam on the rails, the new hills rising through the streets, the animals moving with calm purpose between factories. At certain hours the low sun strikes the condensation on the cable bundles, and the entire urban basin becomes webbed with brief prisms.

Beyond the outermost buildings, the land opens.

There are broad fields of dark stone, shallow wetlands, abandoned rail spurs, and low ridges continuing toward the horizon. Smoke from the factories lies close to the ground but never thickens into a permanent veil. Far away, one can see new towers forming at the edge of the basin, their upper sections still soft, their surfaces flowing and hardening in turns. Sometimes the towers collapse before reaching their intended height. Sometimes they continue upward for weeks, silent and narrow, until they disappear into the cloud.

The city does not appear to be expanding so much as participating in an expansion that began before it arrived.

Everything here is densely alive, densely built, densely occupied—and still in the process of becoming. Rust, root, steel, water, animal, and stone have entered into a crowded arrangement none of them fully control. The roads are usable, the factories are productive, the trains run. But beneath every foundation the world is shifting, not toward ruin, but toward its next form.
