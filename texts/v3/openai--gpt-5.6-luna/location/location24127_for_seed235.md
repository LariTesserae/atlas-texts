The city occupies a high basin where the mountain has opened like a cup.

It is not a narrow valley, but a broad, elevated country of cliffs, terraces, and green shelves, all suspended above a lower world that cannot be seen. The basin’s rim rises on every side into pale rock, sheer in places and softly sloping in others, with waterfalls descending from unseen sources far above. They do not fall in silver threads. They fall in immense, continuous curtains, white and thunderous, breaking into vapor before they reach the ground. The mist hangs everywhere, warm as breath. It gathers on every surface, beads beneath leaves, and streams from the undersides of bridges and roofs.

The heat is extraordinary for such an altitude. Sunlight lies heavily on the stone. The rocks are hot enough to shimmer, though water runs over them constantly, and the air has the dense, saturated quality of a room filled with boiling herbs. Green things thrive in the contradiction. Ferns emerge from cracks in basalt walls. Vines as thick as ropes spill down façades and disappear into drains. Broad-leaved plants occupy the gutters, the stairwells, the windows, the seams between paving stones. Moss carpets the shaded sides of towers in layers of deep emerald, blue-green, and almost black.

There are no animals.

No insects move among the flowers. No birds cross the open air. Nothing swims in the channels. The leaves tremble only under the pressure of water, and the water itself has no small life within it. A flowering tree may stand in full bloom, bright with scent and color, yet remain utterly unvisited. Its petals drop onto the streets and are carried away in perfectly clear streams.

The settlement is vast enough to be called a city, though its construction belongs to no recognizable age. It spreads across the basin in terraces, climbing the slopes and bridging the ravines. Houses, halls, courtyards, reservoirs, stairways, and retaining walls are made from the same dark volcanic stone as the mountain. Their surfaces have been worn smooth by centuries of rain, but not ruined. Every edge is rounded, every corner settled into its proper shape. The city looks old without looking abandoned. It has the finished quality of something that has never needed repair.

There are no metal fixtures, engines, lamps, wires, or tools. Doors are slabs of stone that turn in stone sockets. Water is guided through channels cut into the paving, divided by low walls, and raised from one terrace to the next through arrangements of tilted slabs whose purpose is difficult to understand. Some streets are dry beneath the roof of a living canopy. Others are shallow canals, and one walks them on narrow raised paths with water flowing on both sides. In the center of the city, a set of monumental stairs descends into a flooded plaza. The stairs continue beneath the water for a very long distance, though the plaza is not deep enough to contain them.

Buildings lean against one another without collapsing. Some have grown together. A watchtower has roots instead of a foundation, its lower courses split open around the trunk of a pale tree. A row of houses is joined by a single interior corridor that passes through all of them, though from outside each appears separate. Courtyards recur at impossible intervals: one can cross a doorway in the north quarter and enter a courtyard whose walls belong to a district several miles away. The city’s organization remains coherent despite this. People—if there are people—would know where they were. The paths are not confusing to the place itself.

The loudness is beyond weather.

The waterfalls fill the basin with a continuous concussion, a pressure more than a sound. It enters the chest and loosens the teeth. Voices, if any are made, vanish a few feet from the mouth. The stone vibrates underfoot. In the covered passages, the noise changes character: deep pulses beneath the floor, enormous rushing overhead, sudden tones like the ringing of a bell heard underwater. Certain courtyards amplify the falls into a single sustained note. Others swallow sound completely. In those silent pockets, the absence is so complete that one can hear the blood in one’s ears—until stepping across an invisible boundary restores the roar.

The waterfalls do not seem to originate from clouds. Above the basin, the sky is usually clear, intensely blue, with ordinary sunlight and ordinary shadows. Yet water pours over the rim from places where there is no visible river. At the highest terraces, channels run uphill. They climb the paving in narrow, purposeful streams and vanish into walls. If a leaf is placed upon one, it will travel upward. If the leaf is placed again, it will travel down. Nothing about the difference can be observed.

The city’s gardens are lush but strangely deliberate. There are no weeds, because nothing grows where it is not meant to grow. Vines cover entire walls and stop at exact doorframes. Trees form avenues with branches touching from side to side, their roots traveling beneath the foundations without displacing a stone. In the hottest open squares, succulent plants bloom from cracks in the ground, their flowers opening and closing in unison, though there is no visible change in light or temperature. Their colors are subdued in the direct sun—cream, rust, green, violet—and impossibly vivid in shadow.

The mountain is stable. Its cliffs have been exposed for so long that erosion has become a kind of composure. Rain has polished the rock, waterfalls have carved their channels to final depth, and the terraces no longer settle. Nothing falls. No ledge crumbles. Moss may thicken, but never enough to obscure a carving. A stream may divide around a stone, but the stone will remain in that position forever. The basin seems to have passed through its age of making and arrived at an equilibrium that is not merely geological, but moral.

Time behaves similarly.

The sun crosses the sky, but the shadows of certain structures do not move. Water flows without changing its level. Flowers open and remain open. Fallen leaves are carried through the city’s channels, disappear beneath grates, and emerge again at the same point a few streets away, still fresh. A person could walk for hours and return to find a drop of water suspended at the edge of a roof exactly where it had been. The place does not resist movement; it simply refuses consequence.

At the outer terraces, the city gives way to an immense green escarpment. Beyond it, the landscape repeats at larger scales: another basin, another hanging city, another ring of cliffs, all connected by bridges that cross empty distance. Some bridges lead to cliffs that appear near but are unreachable. Some lead into the air and arrive at a doorway in the side of the mountain. From the lower streets, one can see districts above the clouds, their roofs gleaming with water, their gardens spilling over the ledges.

The clouds themselves collect below the city.

They fill the abyss in slow white expanses, obscuring the lands beneath. At times the mist rises through the streets, and the buildings appear to float in it. At times it passes through walls without disturbing them. Beyond the rim, there may be an entire continent, but the basin gives no evidence of it. The horizon is always formed by stone, vapor, green terraces, and the blue-black vertical distance between.

There is no decay, but there are traces of former uses everywhere. A market square lies beneath a canopy of banyan roots, its stalls intact and empty. Long benches face a waterfall. A theater has been carved directly into the cliff, with seats descending toward a stage occupied only by shallow water. In a public courtyard, hundreds of doorways open onto blank stone. Another courtyard contains a single doorway that opens onto a view of the city from above, though no stair or tower reaches that height.

The rules are not announced. They are learned by repetition.

Water remembers its channels. Stone remembers its shape. Green things remain alive without growing toward anything. Sound cannot be carried by the body but can be carried by walls. Distance is reliable until it becomes irrelevant. The sky provides light but not weather. Nothing with a face, wing, fin, leg, or eye belongs here. Nothing is born, hunts, mates, flees, or dies.

The city is not empty in the way a ruin is empty. It is occupied by its own continuance: the falling water, the heat in the stone, the pressure of roots beneath streets, the wet green abundance, the unchanging terraces, the impossible corridors repeating themselves through the mountain. Everywhere, the place seems to be listening—not for visitors, but for confirmation that it remains exactly as it has always been.
