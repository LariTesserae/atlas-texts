The city lies beneath a warm, shallow sea, sealed inside a hollow in the old limestone.

It is not a drowned city. Nothing here was built for air and then abandoned to the water. The streets, factories, residences, and gardens were designed to exist below the surface, and have been doing so for longer than anyone remembers. Water occupies every passageway. It presses against doors, fills the stairwells, laps through the upper floors, and hangs in the open chambers like a second atmosphere. Some sections are completely flooded; others contain pockets of trapped, breathable air beneath low stone vaults. The boundaries between street and canal, building and reef, machine and plant have softened until they are difficult to name.

The settlement is small, no larger than a neighborhood enclosed within a single cavern. Its limits are visible everywhere: a ring of dark rock, a ceiling lost in green-black water, and walls crowded with construction. There is no open ground. Pipes cross one another in dense bundles overhead and underfoot. Walkways cling to the walls in narrow terraces. Buildings have been stacked inward and upward, their foundations resting on older buildings, their windows looking into other buildings only a few feet away. Bridges pass through chambers and emerge directly into rooms. Every available surface is occupied.

The stone is pale, honey-colored limestone, worn smooth by centuries of warm water. Iron has been used heavily and repeatedly, and most of it is now dark with mineral bloom. Copper pipes shine dully beneath films of algae. Thick cables lie in the silt like roots. Riveted pressure doors stand open onto submerged corridors. Old warning signs remain bolted to walls, their letters blurred but their colors still faintly red and yellow. The machinery has the squat, durable shape of things built to survive corrosion: turbine housings, filtration drums, elevators with caged platforms, pumps as large as houses despite the settlement’s modest scale.

Nothing runs quickly.

The pumps turn at a patient speed, each revolution taking several seconds. Their vanes stir the water without urgency, drawing the surrounding sea through grates and layers of charcoal, shell, sand, and engineered moss. Conveyor belts move baskets of mineral growth from one factory to another. Small lamps glow inside glass casings, powered by generators whose low vibration is felt more often than heard. The whole city seems to operate through pressure rather than motion. Valves open gradually. Gates descend over the course of an hour. Water levels change by the width of a finger.

The quiet is immense.

There is the soft click of relays. The distant, repeating knock of a piston. The muted churning of water through a pipe. Occasionally, somewhere in the dark, metal settles with a long, delicate groan. Voices do not travel far. They become bubbles and vanish. Footsteps are reduced to faint tremors through the walkways. When the machinery pauses, the silence is not empty but occupied by the slow movement of water through all things.

Vegetation grows everywhere the machines cannot prevent it.

Warm green mats cover the walls, thick as felt, with fronds that wave in currents too slight to feel. Pale vines wind through handrails and disappear into ventilation ducts. Small gardens have been built in recessed chambers, each one a packed arrangement of edible kelp, bulbous aquatic leaves, translucent fungi, and trees trained sideways beneath the ceiling. Their roots are held in wire baskets, ceramic troughs, and old engine housings. Some plants have adapted to the dimness so thoroughly that they produce no green at all. They are ivory, blue-gray, or nearly black, and their leaves resemble folded skin.

The city’s cultivated growth is carefully managed, but it has long since become more than cultivation. Moss seals cracks in the masonry. Algae polish the water. Root systems reinforce old walls. Flowering colonies bloom around electrical conduits, feeding on the gentle heat and trace metals. A generator hall has become a humid forest of submerged trunks, each tree rooted in a disused fuel tank. Its branches fill the chamber in a dense canopy, and from them hang hundreds of clear seed-pods. They rise and fall with the current like a suspended collection of lungs.

There are few animals.

The occasional blind shrimp drifts through a flooded doorway. Small silver fish shelter in the shadows beneath the platforms, though no one is certain whether they are wild or maintained. A long, soft-bodied creature sometimes appears in the lower filtration tunnels, moving so slowly that it may be mistaken for a length of cable. It has no visible eyes and does not flee from light. The residents leave it alone. It cleans the intake screens.

Most life here is vegetal, microbial, or mechanical. The machines are the loudest inhabitants, though even they have become subdued with age. Their surfaces are furred with mineral deposits. Their joints are padded by moss. Their warning lights pulse at such long intervals that a person can stand beside one for several minutes before realizing it is signaling.

Light comes from a few weak sources: green maintenance lamps, amber bulbs behind thick glass, the blue radiance of electrical growths in the plant rooms. Beyond the inhabited chambers, the water is nearly black. Shafts leading upward reveal no sky, only layers of drifting sediment and the distant, wavering brightness of the sea above. Sometimes a broad shadow passes over those shafts. It may be a whale, a storm cloud seen through kilometers of water, or simply a shift in the ocean’s suspended darkness. The settlement never knows.

The warm water has made the air heavy where air remains. Every surface sweats. Condensation gathers beneath the ceilings and falls in steady drops, though the rainfall is so quiet it resembles thought. In the dry pockets, the smell is of wet stone, warm metal, green growth, and faint salt. In the flooded sections, everything smells mineral and old.

People, if there are still people here, have left very little empty space for themselves. Narrow sleeping niches are fitted between pipes. Meals are grown in vertical racks along the transit corridors. Workstations have been built into the walls, their chairs shaped to hold a body in place against the gentle pressure of the current. Personal possessions are attached by hooks and cords. A book, a tool, a cup—nothing is allowed to drift away.

The rules of the place are practical and nearly invisible. Water must always be moving. No chamber may be completely sealed. Plants must be pruned before they touch a pressure line. Certain doors are never opened at the same time, because the surrounding tunnels would fill or empty too quickly. Heat must be distributed slowly. Light must remain dim in the lower districts, where sudden brightness causes the pale growths to close and release clouds of spores.

There are older rules as well.

Do not disturb the sleeping engines.

Do not drain the cistern beneath the central square.

If you hear knocking from the stone, wait until it stops.

The city is not decaying. It is settled into its condition. Rust, sediment, roots, mineral crust, and human construction have reached an equilibrium that resembles permanence. The place does not feel abandoned, though it may have been. Its systems continue because they have become interdependent: the plants feed the filters, the filters preserve the machines, the machines circulate the water, and the water carries warmth through the whole enclosed world.

Nothing here seems to be waiting for rescue or collapse.

The settlement simply persists in the dimness, packed tight beneath the sea, slowly turning its pumps, slowly growing its gardens, slowly receiving the patient weight of the water above.
