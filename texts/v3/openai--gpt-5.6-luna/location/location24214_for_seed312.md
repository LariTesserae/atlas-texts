The land rises beyond the last tree line into a plateau so broad that its far edges are hidden by the curvature of the weather. It is not a mountain range in the familiar sense, but the upper surface of something recently lifted: a continent-sized shelf of dark stone, thrust upward from the earth in long, broken planes. Its margins are still raw. Cliffs descend in fresh faces, their ledges angular and unsoftened, as though the world has only lately finished breaking.

The rock is mostly black and blue-gray basalt, with seams of pale mineral running through it like frozen water. In places the uplift has split the plateau into immense steps. Whole districts of the inhabited land occupy these levels, connected by ramps, stairways, switchback roads, and narrow bridges laid across chasms whose bottoms are rarely visible through the haze. The stone underfoot is sharp enough to abrade leather. Every exposed edge retains the geometry of fracture: wedges, blades, shelves, splinters, and clean planes that catch the daylight.

Cold occupies the place more thoroughly than wind does. The air is thin, dry for long periods, and bright with an almost ordinary light, but moisture arrives in sudden, localized events. A bank of cloud may settle against one quarter of the plateau and remain there for days, turning walls black with condensation while the neighboring streets stay pale and dusty. Rain comes as short, hard falls. Water runs immediately along the rock, gathering in channels cut by hand and by recent erosion, then vanishing into cracks or collecting in deep stone basins.

The city has grown around these temporary waters.

It is not a city of towers. Its height comes from the land itself and from the accumulation of walls. Dwellings, storehouses, animal pens, shrines, workshops, and burial chambers are packed together in tiers, their foundations fitted directly into the dark shelves of the plateau. The oldest structures are low and broad, made from stacked slabs held by clay and woven root. Later walls rise over them. A house may have another house buried beneath its floor, and a road may pass over the roof of a former street.

Every usable surface has been claimed. Steps lead to steps, ledges become courtyards, and courtyards are crossed by ladders or plank walks. The buildings are primitive in their tools but not in their density. Stone hammers, bone wedges, wooden levers, and simple copper or soft-iron blades have been used for generations to enlarge the settlement. The work is visible everywhere: chisel marks on retaining walls, grooves worn by hauling ropes, sockets where poles once stood, ramps polished by the feet of countless animals.

There are no great machines. Movement is done by hand, by sled, by pulley, and by animal. Long lines of pack-beasts travel the upper roads carrying blocks, salt, fuel, dried meat, and water jars. At the steepest rises, people work in teams, passing loads from one narrow shelf to the next. The city advances slowly, not by sudden expansion but by the patient addition of another wall, another stair, another covered cistern. Against the scale of the plateau, its construction seems almost negligible. Yet the settlement is so concentrated that the stone appears inhabited wherever one looks.

Beyond the walls, vegetation is sparse and low. There are mats of dark moss in the shaded cracks, pale grass flattened by frost, and small tough shrubs whose roots descend into seams in the rock. These plants grow thickly only around water traps and sheltered hollows. A few twisted trees survive in the deepest ravines, their crowns bent permanently away from the prevailing winds. Their wood is precious and carefully guarded. Most fuel comes from dried dung, scrub bundles, and the slow harvest of dead branches carried up from lower country.

The plateau’s abundance is animal rather than botanical.

Small, many-legged grazers move through the open stone in dense herds, feeding on lichen, moss, and the thin grass that appears after rain. They have broad, padded feet for crossing broken ground and heavy coats layered against the cold. Around the city they are penned in descending terraces, their warmth and manure as important as their meat and milk. Their movements make the streets seem alive even when the people are indoors: the dull shifting of bodies, the scrape of horns against gates, the soft impact of hooves on stairways.

There are cliff-nesting birds everywhere. They occupy the cracks in unfinished walls, the undersides of bridges, and the high ledges above the city. Most are quiet, but their presence is constant: pale shapes gliding through the air, quick shadows passing over courtyards, feathers caught in drainage grates. Larger birds circle above the escarpments, rising on thermals so slow that their wings scarcely move. At dusk, they descend in numbers to the roofs and parapets, where they stand shoulder to shoulder in the cold.

Under the stones live colonies of burrowing animals that emerge after rain. Their tunnels honeycomb the softer layers of the plateau, and some old neighborhoods have collapsed where the ground was hollowed from beneath. The people have learned to listen for the faint shifting of earth through floors and foundation walls. Certain animals are allowed to nest inside the settlement because they eat insects and preserve warmth in their packed burrows. Others are driven away with smoke and noise. The boundary between domesticated, tolerated, and wild is narrow and practical.

The city is mostly quiet.

Distance swallows ordinary sound. A person can hear a hammer striking stone several streets away, but the blow arrives as a small, isolated note, followed by a long interval. There are no forests to carry birdsong and no running rivers to fill the air. Wind moves across the plateau in broad, unbroken currents, but often it is felt before it is heard. It presses against doors, lifts dust from the roads, and passes through gaps in the walls with a low breath.

The loudest sounds come during weather: rain striking thousands of roof slabs, water rushing briefly through channels, the sudden clatter of shutters being closed, and the collective lowing of animals brought under cover. Afterward, the city becomes even more still. Droplets hang from ropes and eaves. The black stone shines. People emerge to inspect cisterns, clear silt from drains, and set bowls beneath the last slow drips.

Fog is common in the lower streets when the plateau’s moisture arrives. It gathers in the terraces and stairwells, leaving the upper roofs in clear daylight while the city’s foundations disappear below a white surface. From above, the settlement looks like an archipelago of masonry rising from cloud. Bells are not used often, but on fog days a few are sounded at fixed intervals so that people and animals can find their way between districts. The notes are deep and widely spaced. They seem less like music than like markers placed in the air.

Time moves slowly here, though nothing is still.

The cliffs shed fragments each winter. Thin cracks widen. Roads settle. Retaining walls bulge and are rebuilt. New gullies appear after each major rain, and the inhabitants redirect them with stone channels before they can become ravines. Frost pries apart unfinished masonry. Moss spreads across the north faces by increments too small to see from one season to the next. The plateau is young, but its youth is expressed in long processes: uplift continuing somewhere beneath the crust, slopes learning their shapes, water finding paths that will eventually become permanent.

The people understand the land as unfinished. Their laws are built around this fact. No wall may block a natural drainage line without a spillway. No cistern may be emptied entirely, because the next condensation may not come for many days. Foundation stones must be marked with the date and name of their maker, so that later builders know which surfaces are trustworthy. Certain cracks are considered public places, not because of any supernatural rule, but because everyone knows that water, animals, and travelers will use them.

There is no sense of a clear frontier around the city. Roads leave the settlement and continue across the plateau, fading into expanses of fractured stone, animal tracks, isolated watch shelters, and old walls half-buried by scree. Far away, other settlements appear as pale bands along the cliffs or as clusters of dark marks around hidden springs. They are connected by slow caravans and by sightlines between signal fires. Between them lies an immense cold geography that is not empty, only thinly occupied: grazing herds, nesting cliffs, water pockets, abandoned terraces, and the gradual work of erosion.

At midday the light is ordinary and almost gentle. It reveals everything without warmth. The city’s walls cast hard-edged shadows over stairs and courtyards. Smoke rises straight from roof vents, then bends and disappears. Beyond the highest parapets, the plateau stretches outward in blue-gray planes until distance and cloud make it indistinguishable from the sky.

Nothing here is soft except fog, wool, and the breath of animals.

Even those are temporary.
