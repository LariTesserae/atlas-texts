The city lies beneath a broad, unbroken plain, though there is no city above it yet—only a low, cold basin of stone and scrub where rain collects briefly before vanishing into cracks.

Below ground, the settlement occupies a cavern still being made.

Its roof is not a clean vault. It hangs overhead in slabs and blunt shelves, the undersides of strata newly split from one another. Pale rock pushes through dark rock everywhere, sharp-edged and unfinished. The walls have the look of something not yet weathered: faces of stone still carrying the pressure-marks of their formation, seams pinched shut, mineral threads exposed in thin white and green lines. Nothing has been rounded by ages of water. Corners remain corners. Shards remain where they fell. The cavern is not ancient in the manner of a limestone cave; it is young, raw, and structurally uncertain, a hollow opened by the slow lifting and separating of the earth.

It extends far beyond sight.

The inhabited region follows a long subterranean trough, broad enough in places to hold neighborhoods, work yards, cisterns, market lanes, and fields of pale fungus. In other places the ceiling lowers until the city is forced into narrow terraces. Bridges cross black clefts. Stairways descend into older-looking hollows, although “older” here means only less recently broken. The main road runs along the floor of the trough for several miles, straight where the stone permits it, then abruptly bent around a pressure ridge or a fallen section of roof.

The settlement is dense almost to the point of suffocation.

Every level of usable stone has been claimed. Houses lean against one another in close-packed rows, their walls made from fitted fragments of the cavern itself. There are no grand buildings and few open squares. Instead there are passages: covered lanes between shoulder-high walls, staircases threaded through houses, ladders fixed to rock faces, narrow galleries hung above the main streets. Roofs serve as floors. Floors become bridges. A family’s storage chamber may lie beneath another family’s sleeping room, while above them a public walkway passes under a ceiling of timber poles and stretched hide.

The construction is practical, repetitive, and unfinished. Stone blocks are stacked without mortar where the rock is stable; elsewhere they are held with clay, wedgewood, and cords made from tough subterranean grasses. Doors are slabs of timber brought down from the surface, or woven mats sealed with animal fat against drafts. The oldest beams are blackened by smoke and polished by hands. New beams are pale, raw, and still shedding splinters.

Everything is close to everything else. The city has no wilderness within it, but it has no true emptiness either. A wall contains a family, a tool shop, a drying rack, a shrine of ordinary stones, a crack patched with moss, a sleeping animal, or a drain. The density is not ornamental. It is defensive and thermal. Bodies, fires, walls, and roofs conserve warmth in the cool underground air.

Moisture arrives irregularly.

Some mornings—if morning can be called the dimming and brightening of lamps—the ceiling sweats. Condensation gathers along the rock in beads, then runs down the walls in narrow, shining threads. During periods of rain on the surface, water finds the new fractures and falls in intermittent curtains from above. It strikes roofs, stair rails, and stone floors with a soft, persistent tapping. In low places, it collects in shallow pools darkened by dust and ash. Children and water-carriers wait beside these hollows with clay jars, scraping away the floating grit before filling them.

There are no broad underground rivers. The water has not had time to make them. It appears in pockets, seams, dripping ledges, and sudden temporary channels that vanish as quickly as they form. The people build around these places with care. A wet wall is marked in chalk. A dripping crack may be fenced, diverted through wooden gutters, or declared unsafe. When a new spring breaks through, the nearest houses are dismantled before the water can undermine them.

The air is cool, close, and faintly mineral. It smells of wet stone, lamp smoke, damp wool, clay, old wood, and the sour-green odor of cultivated growth. In the lowest districts there is a permanent breath of cold moving along the ground. It slips under doors and through ankle gaps in walls. Higher galleries are warmer, crowded with hearths and bodies, and thick with smoke that gathers beneath the roof before escaping through shafts.

Light is ordinary, but scarce enough to be managed.

At the entrances and larger shafts, gray daylight falls in long slanted columns, illuminating suspended dust and the pale vapor rising from wet stone. Farther in, people use oil lamps, tallow cups, resin torches, and bowls of glowing charcoal. The lamps do not transform the cavern into a magical underworld; they make it visible by portions. A wall emerges, a face disappears, a stair continues beyond the edge of illumination. Every neighborhood has its own low constellation of lights, and from a distant ridge the city looks less like a single settlement than a spread of embers caught in the folds of the earth.

Vegetation is sparse, deliberately maintained, and concentrated wherever water and light can be secured.

Patches of gray-green moss cling to damp walls. Thin-stemmed cave grasses grow in trays beneath shafts, their leaves narrow and nearly colorless. Small gardens occupy ledges warmed by lamps: bitter roots, threadlike onions, low medicinal herbs, and a few spreading fungi cultivated on shelves of rotting wood. Most of the city’s food is not grown underground. Grain, fruit, and fuel come down from the surface in seasonal loads, carried through the entrances by hand, sled, or pack animal. What grows below is supplemental and precious. A dark patch of edible fungus is guarded as carefully as a well.

The fauna is correspondingly limited. Pale insects live around the dampest walls, though they are not wholly blind and retreat from lamp heat. Small cave mice nest in insulation and storage rooms. There are occasional long-bodied scavengers in the drainage clefts, hunted when they can be caught. Bats enter through the upper shafts during certain seasons, hanging in clustered shadows and leaving behind a dry, pungent dust. Most animal sounds are rare: a scrape in the dark, the quick patter of something crossing a roof, the sudden wingbeat from a high opening.

The city itself provides the dominant ecology. People, smoke, waste, mold, stored grain, working animals, and water channels have created a web of life pressed into every available crevice. Nothing is left unused for long. Ash goes into plaster. Bone becomes needle, hook, or knife handle. Water from washing is carried to moss beds. Broken pottery becomes drainage tile. The dead are placed in sealed niches above the inhabited levels, because there is no room to spare and no desire to contaminate the cisterns.

The technology is simple but highly practiced. Stone hammers, bone awls, wooden wedges, copper blades, woven baskets, cordage, pulleys, clay vessels, and hand-built carts are common. Metal is scarce enough that it is repaired repeatedly and passed through generations. Most excavation is done with fire-setting: a face of rock is heated, then cooled with water until it cracks. The resulting rubble is carried away in baskets. The work is slow, and every new chamber bears the marks of it—blackened walls, soot in the seams, heaps of angular stone waiting to be sorted.

The settlement is always under construction, but not in the confident manner of a growing city. It is making room for itself inside a place that has not decided what shape it will hold.

Surveyors tap walls with stone hammers and listen for hollowness. Builders measure new fissures with stretched cords. Watchers are stationed beneath unstable shelves. When a tremor passes through the cavern, the whole city pauses: hands stop, lamps are shielded, and everyone listens for the settling of stone. Sometimes nothing happens. Sometimes a crack opens across a street, clean and narrow, with water shining at its bottom. Sometimes a roof section drops in a distant district, producing a deep concussion that travels through the inhabited tunnels long after the falling has ended.

For all this activity, the place is quiet.

There is no constant roar of machinery, no crowd-noise rising into open air. Sound is absorbed by packed walls, hanging cloth, moss, and the multitude of small chambers. A hammer strikes stone somewhere far off: one clear knock, then a pause. Water taps from a ceiling. Footsteps pass overhead, muffled by a floor of planks and packed earth. A cart wheel creaks. Someone calls from one passage to another, and the words become indistinct almost immediately.

The silence is not empty. It is inhabited by small continuities: the rubbing of a broom, the hiss of a lamp, the click of a loom shuttle, the breath of people sleeping behind thin walls. Every so often the cavern gives a low settling groan, not loud but broad enough to be felt in the ribs. Then the city resumes its work.

There are rules here imposed by the landscape rather than by law. Never build directly beneath a fresh seam. Never seal a dripping wall until the water has been observed through a full cycle of rain. Leave a clear path to every shaft. Keep fire below the smoke marks. Do not widen a passage without first bracing the stone above it. Do not mistake a dry floor for a stable one.

The deepest districts are the newest. They smell of broken rock and wet clay, and their streets are still lines marked through rubble. Beyond them, the cavern narrows into raw galleries where lamps reveal only tool scars and the pale glitter of unopened mineral. The city advances there slowly, one chamber at a time, while behind it older neighborhoods are repaired, crowded, subdivided, and built over.

Nothing in the landscape is finished—not the stone, not the watercourses, not the settlement, not even the boundaries between one and the other. Yet it is not chaotic. Its order is the order of compression: lives fitted around cracks, buildings fitted around lives, paths fitted around buildings, and all of it pressed into a young hollow that continues, quietly and actively, to become itself.
