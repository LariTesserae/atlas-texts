The city occupies a high basin where the mountains have been worn down almost to their oldest shape.

They are still extreme mountains, but no longer sharp. Their ridgelines rise in long, solemn shoulders, each one broad-backed and dark with age, as if the whole range had once been molten and had cooled into immense waves. The highest slopes are not cliffs so much as exposed planes of stone, smoothed by glacial passage and centuries of freezing, thawing, and wind. Here and there, a tooth of harder rock remains standing above the general level: a tower, a blunt pinnacle, a black island in the pale grass. Beyond them, the horizon extends in layers—plateau, ravine, further plateau, sky—until distance and cloud become indistinguishable.

The air is cold enough that every surface seems to keep a trace of night. Water does not run constantly here. It appears in intervals and in unlikely places: a silver thread under a bridge, a shallow basin gathered in a depression of stone, droplets shining along the undersides of leaves. Rain comes in brief, slanting curtains and then vanishes. Mist climbs from the lower ravines at dawn, spilling over the basin floor in slow sheets. Condensation beads on railings, windows, cables, and the rusted flanks of machinery. By noon, most of it has disappeared into the soil, the moss, or the deep cracks between the city’s foundations.

The vegetation is unexpectedly abundant. It does not form a soft wilderness but a dense, layered occupation of every stable surface. Low blue-green grasses cover the old quarry terraces. Thick mats of moss fill the joints between paving stones, their surfaces starred with minute cups that collect the night’s moisture. Dark shrubs crowd the drainage channels, their branches close and interwoven, bearing small translucent leaves that tremble even when the air is still. In sheltered cuts, waist-high stands of pale reed rise from wet gravel, their dry stalks clicking together whenever the weather shifts.

Trees grow only in certain places, where their roots can reach buried water. They are short, wind-shaped things, with trunks bent permanently away from the prevailing storms. Their crowns are dense and black-green, pressed low against the earth. A few have forced their roots into the foundations of abandoned buildings, lifting walls by fractions of an inch over decades. In the city’s older quarters, vegetation has become part of the architecture: vines descending through elevator shafts, ferns opening in stairwells, roots threading behind ceramic tiles and emerging through electrical cabinets.

The settlement is spread across the basin in several levels. At the center stands an old industrial district, built from dark stone and weather-stained concrete, its structures connected by gantries, covered walkways, and elevated service lines. The buildings are not tall by metropolitan standards, but the terrain makes them seem vertical. One factory is sunk into the side of a ridge, with only its upper floors visible from the valley. Another straddles a ravine on enormous pillars. Residential blocks cling to the slopes in terraces, their windows narrow and deeply recessed against the cold. Between them run stairways, switchback roads, drainage trenches, and old funicular tracks.

Nothing is quite abandoned, though much of it is no longer used for its original purpose.

A power station still operates in the lower quarter, drawing energy from pressure differences between the mist-filled ravines and the high reservoirs. Its turbines turn slowly beneath the ground. Every few minutes, the machinery sends a low vibration through the stone, too deep to be heard as a sound so much as felt in the soles of the feet. Along the rooftops, small wind machines rotate in the thin air. Their blades move with deliberate, uneven patience, and their housings knock softly as they turn into the weather.

The factories have become quieter than factories should be. Conveyor belts lie still beneath translucent roofing, but water pumps continue to work. Ventilation fans start and stop without warning. Automated cranes shift their loads inside sealed halls, making a brief procession of metallic impacts that echoes across the basin and then fades. On certain mornings, a whole row of chimneys exhales white vapor, though no fire is visible within them. The vapor drifts only a short distance before flattening into the dim air.

There are lights, but few of them are bright. Most windows give off a subdued amber or greenish glow, protected behind fogged glass. Streetlamps burn inside metal cages and illuminate only small islands of wet pavement. Their light catches on suspended moisture, making the spaces between buildings appear filled with fine dust. Some signs still function, their letters flickering in incomplete sequences. A word may glow for a second on a warehouse wall, disappear, and return with one character changed.

At the highest edge of the city, the construction gives way to open ground. Roads end in gravel fans. Pipes emerge from the earth and bend downward into the ravines. Old retaining walls extend for kilometers along the slopes, their stones fitted so tightly that moss has had to grow in a thin green film over their faces rather than between them. Beyond the walls, the land looks almost untouched, though it is not gentle. It is a vast, weathered system of shelves, hollows, wind channels, and shallow valleys, all shaped by processes so slow that they seem finished.

The ancientness of the place is visible in its lack of sharp transitions. Nothing begins or ends cleanly. The mountains soften into terraces; terraces become roads; roads become drainage beds; drainage beds vanish into grass. The exposed rock has been scoured to a dull satin. In some areas, layers of stone are visible like pages in a weather-beaten book, but the pages have been rubbed nearly blank. Fossils appear in the cut faces of walls, not as distinct forms but as faint whorls and repeated shadows. The city’s newest foundations rest directly on material that was already old when the first structures were built.

Despite this stillness, the landscape is active everywhere.

Water gathers, evaporates, condenses, and gathers again. Fog advances through the streets at night and retreats uphill after sunrise. Frost pries apart small cracks in the rock. Moss thickens over one season and is torn away in the next by a maintenance vehicle or a sudden wash of rain. The grasses release pale seeds that lodge in roof gutters and machinery vents. Somewhere beneath the soil, roots are slowly turning old asphalt into dark, porous earth.

The wind is strongest along the ridges. It does not howl continuously; it arrives in long pulses, passing over the basin like a hand over the strings of a massive instrument. During the quiet intervals, the place seems nearly silent. There is the dripping of water from ledges, the muted ticking of cooling metal, the soft electrical hum of lines overhead. Far below, something mechanical cycles on and off. A cable trembles against a pole. Reeds whisper in a drainage ditch.

Animal life is present but difficult to find. Small, dark shapes move through the vegetation and vanish before they can be identified. There are pale insects clustered around the warm vents of the factories, and thin-winged things that appear only during the brief periods of sunlight. Tracks cross the wet silt beside reservoirs, narrow and deliberate, but no animal is ever seen making them. Birds are rare at this elevation. Occasionally one passes over the basin, its call absorbed by the fog, its body reduced to a momentary silhouette above the roofs.

The absence of fauna gives the city a peculiar density. The world is crowded with plants, pipes, walls, cables, mist, mineral surfaces, and slow machines, but almost no visible creature moves through it. Every open space is already occupied by another kind of presence. A vacant lot is packed with reeds. A stairwell is webbed with roots and condensation. A warehouse floor is covered in shallow water and reflected ceiling lights. The silence is not emptiness; it is the sound of many processes operating below the threshold of attention.

At evening, the dimness arrives early. The upper ridges remain faintly illuminated while the streets below turn blue, then gray. Moisture gathers on the buildings. The grasses darken and bend. In the distance, the industrial quarter begins its night cycle: pumps engage, warning lamps blink along the rail lines, and vents release thin white plumes into the cold.

From a high terrace, the entire settlement can be seen spread across the basin—small enough to understand, broad enough to lose oneself in. Its structures follow the old contours rather than conquering them. Towers rise from ravines, roads disappear beneath growth, and power lines cross the open ground in long, taut lines. Beyond the city, the eroded mountains continue without drama toward the horizon, their immense age making every human construction appear temporary.

Then the mist reaches the upper streets.

It comes in low and thick, filling the spaces between buildings until the lamps become floating stains and the farther terraces disappear. The city remains active inside it. Engines turn. Water moves through buried channels. Roots widen their grip. Somewhere, a door opens and closes. Somewhere above, on the dark shoulder of the mountain, a wind machine continues its slow rotation against the sky.
