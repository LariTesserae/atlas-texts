### The Pocket Mountain

It is hidden inside a place too small to contain it.

A thumb-sized aperture in a slab of black stone opens onto a mountain landscape, though the mountain has no foothills and no surrounding country. Its entire world is a single vertical rise, packed into a chamber no wider than a clenched hand. The walls are close enough to touch at every level, yet the summit appears impossibly far above. Looking upward does not reveal distance so much as an accumulation of heights: ledges stacked over ledges, stairways ascending into folds of dim green vapor, towers emerging from towers.

The mountain is being made.

Nothing here has weathered into softness. Every surface is raw, sharp, and deliberate. The ground is not ground but a dense construction of fitted slate, fired clay, pale mortar, braided root, hammered copper, and black volcanic glass. The stones have been placed before they cooled. Some are still warm enough to steam. Their edges meet too cleanly, without cracks or moss-softened joints, but water already occupies every seam. It beads along the mortar, wells from beneath the paving, and moves in thin shining threads down the vertical faces.

There is no natural soil. Vegetation grows in baskets, gutters, wall cavities, and shallow trays built into the architecture. Ferns spill from ceramic mouths. Lush ropes of vine bind staircases together. Broad leaves emerge from cracks that were made for them, their roots packed into fist-sized plugs of black compost. Moss clings in thick green pads to the undersides of bridges, where no rain should reach. Some plants have been trained into railings; others have grown through their intended shapes and now hold them in place.

The mountain is steep beyond reason. A stairway can climb ten times its own length in a few steps, because the steps are not always arranged according to the same gravity. A person ascending may feel their weight shift sideways, then upward, then briefly toward the wall. Water obeys similar local customs. It pours down one channel, pauses on a vertical surface, and then runs toward the sky in a silver filament. A drop falling from an eave may strike the floor before it has left the eave. In the deepest gullies, rain falls upward from pools into clouds.

The highest summit is visible from the lowest courtyard, but never in the same position twice.

Sometimes it is a little shrine of unfinished brick. Sometimes it is a tooth of white stone wrapped in wet roots. Sometimes it is directly overhead, close enough that the hand could reach it, though the climb toward it takes hours and leads through levels that were not there before. The mountain’s height is extreme only because height here is a habit of space. The chamber remains small. One can see its opposite wall beyond the terraces, touch it with a fingertip, and still be standing beneath an abyss.

The air is hot and wet. Heat rises from the stones and gathers under the low invisible ceiling. Condensation forms in the hollows of carved faces and in the eyes of small clay figures set along the walls. The atmosphere tastes of mineral water, green sap, ash, and old iron. Mist moves through the built passages in slow, muscular folds. It does not merely drift; it chooses routes, pooling in stairwells and squeezing through doors too narrow to admit a body.

The light is dim, though there is no clear source of darkness or illumination. A submerged amber glow comes through the masonry, as if the mountain were lit from inside by banked coals. Green leaves catch the light and hold it. Water shines more brightly than stone. In places, the shadows are not cast behind objects but laid across them like dark fabric. A wall can be lit on one side and shadowed on the same side at once.

Everything is crowded. There are no empty ledges, no unused walls, no unoccupied air currents. Every opening contains a stair, a vine, a basin, a shrine, a tool, a nest, a drain, or another opening. Terraces overlap one another so closely that their inhabitants must turn sideways to pass. Roofs support gardens; gardens conceal roofs. Channels cross bridges, bridges pass through towers, towers lean against retaining walls that are themselves built over older retaining walls. The place feels less inhabited than completely claimed.

Yet there are very few animals.

Now and then a pale water-skink appears beneath a slab, its body translucent enough to show a beating green organ. It watches the movement of the water and vanishes when the water looks back. Small black insects cluster around lamps made from nutshells, but they do not fly; they climb directly into the light and become part of it. There are no birds, though high cries echo from the upper levels. No one has ever found their source.

The dominant life is vegetal and architectural. Roots travel through walls with the purposeful speed of fingers. Vines tighten when footsteps approach. Moss grows in patterns resembling written instructions. A flowering plant with fleshy red bells opens only when a section of masonry is removed, then closes when the stone is replaced. Gardens shift slowly toward sources of heat, sometimes dragging their containers behind them. A patch of fern may be found in the morning growing on the opposite face of a column, with no sign of having moved.

The place is noisy.

Water hammers through narrow channels and strikes copper plates mounted beneath the paving. The plates answer in dull, irregular notes. Runnels hiss. Reservoirs gulp. Clay pipes knock from within as though something is trying to get out. The mountain gives off the constant sound of construction: the click of stones settling, the grind of raw surfaces meeting, the brittle crack of fresh mortar. Occasionally there is the distinct ringing of a hand tool against rock—three blows, always three—but no craftsperson is ever present when the sound is found.

Other sounds have no visible cause. A staircase may creak under the weight of an approaching crowd when no one is there. A wall may whisper with the voices of people standing on its far side, despite having no far side. In the densest chambers, every sound arrives slightly before its source. One hears water strike the basin, then sees the water fall. One hears a door close, then watches a hand reach for it.

The builders, if there are builders, work with primitive means. Wooden levers lie beside blocks too large for any human to lift. Stone mallets, bone chisels, woven measuring cords, and shallow baskets are stacked beneath awnings. Copper is used sparingly, beaten thin and hammered into clamps, hooks, mirrors, and rain tongues. There are no engines. The mechanisms are simple: counterweights, sluices, wedges, ladders, pulleys made of carved wood. But their effects are not simple. A small lever can redirect a waterfall upward. A cord stretched across a doorway can determine which room exists beyond it. A clay seal pressed into wet mortar may cause a whole terrace to remember that it was always there.

The unfinished quality is not decay but emergence. Raw stone protrudes from completed walls like bones not yet covered. Stairways end in vertical cuts where the next step has not been invented. Towers are capped by smooth, warm surfaces that tremble faintly, waiting for another course of brick. In one courtyard, half a mountain hangs suspended from wooden braces. Beneath it, workers’ footprints lead into the wall and stop.

The world’s rules are still being decided.

A crack does not necessarily weaken a structure. Some cracks are instructions. A newly laid stone may be heavier in the morning than at night, or heavier when observed. Water remembers the shape of the vessel that held it and continues to flow in that shape after being poured out. Plants rooted in different terraces may share one shadow. If a person speaks their destination aloud, the route there becomes shorter but the destination becomes less certain.

Nothing can be taken out of the mountain unchanged. A pebble carried toward the entrance becomes a seed, a tooth, a folded scrap of skin, or a second entrance. Water brought from outside turns warm and tastes of distance. A flame becomes a green bead of light and sinks into the nearest plant. Objects that remain too long acquire architectural functions: a knife becomes a hinge, a coat becomes a curtain, a name becomes a corridor.

At the pocket’s lowest visible level is a basin surrounded by newly plastered walls. The basin is always full, though its overflow rises in a narrow column and disappears into the ceiling. Around it stand dozens of small unfinished figures, each made from a different material: salt, clay, charcoal, copper, packed leaves. Their faces are blank. Their bodies are crowded shoulder to shoulder, leaving no space among them.

When the mountain shifts, they all turn toward the same direction.

Far above—or perhaps immediately behind the wall—the summit gives a deep internal shudder. Fresh mortar brightens. Roots tighten. A new stair begins to form where there had been only wet stone.

Then the rain starts again, falling upward through the dark, and the entire tiny mountain continues to climb.
