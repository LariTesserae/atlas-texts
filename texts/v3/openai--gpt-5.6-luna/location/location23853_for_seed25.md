The basin is low and broad, though there is no horizon in the ordinary sense. Its far edges dissolve into darkness before they can become distance. A shallow country lies beneath a roof of black stone, stretching for miles in a long, sunless hollow. The ground is old basalt, smoothed and broken by water, its surface arranged in overlapping shelves and shallow channels. It has the settled look of something that has been wearing itself down for an age: no fresh violence, no raw cliffs, but every edge rounded, every hollow polished, every crack widened by patient passage.

Moisture comes irregularly.

Sometimes the ceiling sweats. A cold mist gathers in the upper dark and falls as fine condensation, silvering the stone. Sometimes a whole section of the cavern exhales a rain that lasts only minutes, gathering in the old channels before vanishing into porous black ground. Water stands in pools no deeper than a handspan, though their bottoms cannot always be seen. Other places remain dry for long periods, dusted with pale mineral residue and the brittle shells of things that have died there.

The air is cool enough to keep every surface clammy. It smells of iron, wet stone, salt, and the faint sourness of countless bodies. There is no green anywhere. No stem, leaf, moss, or root interrupts the rock. Even the usual softness of decay is absent. The world is made of mineral, water, chitin, flesh, and darkness.

And life is everywhere.

It fills the basin in layers.

Small, blind creatures crowd the wet channels: translucent, many-legged things that move in continuous sheets, their bodies touching so closely that the water seems to ripple with a living skin. They are no larger than fingers, though some have grown into pale knots the size of fists. Their shells are thin and colorless, showing dark organs pulsing within. They feed on whatever the water loosens from the stone, and on one another when the channels shrink.

Between them move the skittering hunters, black-backed and jointed, with bodies like folded knives. Their limbs strike the rock in rapid, precise bursts. They do not seem to see. They feel the tremor of movement through their feet and turn toward it immediately. When one finds prey, ten more arrive. The feeding is abrupt, soundless for only a second, and then the whole colony breaks apart in a frantic rush.

Larger animals inhabit the open shelves. Some are low and broad, armored like living slabs, dragging themselves through the damp on dozens of hooked limbs. Others rear upright from the darkness, pale and soft-bodied, their narrow torsos twisting above a forest of grasping feet. Their heads are little more than sensory fans: frilled, trembling structures that taste the air and read the echoes. They are not grotesque in the manner of malformed things. They are complete. They belong to this place with the certainty of a law.

The greatest of them are long, low predators that cross the basin in slow, undulating waves. They resemble eels only from a distance. Up close, each body is segmented into armored rings, each ring bearing a pair of small limbs. Along their flanks are rows of sealed openings that pulse when they breathe. They travel by sound and pressure. When they pass, the smaller animals flatten themselves against the stone, though there is nowhere truly to hide.

Their movement never stops.

A channel dries. A colony abandons it and streams toward another. A ceiling vent opens and releases a cold fall of droplets. The droplets strike the stone, and every creature in the vicinity reacts at once. The basin shivers with fleeing bodies. Hunters converge. Larger animals turn their heads toward the disturbance. The change propagates outward in waves until a whole region of the cavern is in motion.

The sound is immense.

At first it seems to be one continuous roar, like surf heard from inside a cliff. Beneath it are the innumerable small impacts of feet and claws: ticking, scraping, pattering, skittering. The shallow water chatters through its channels. Shells rasp against one another. The broad animals drag their armor over the rock with a noise like distant machinery, though there are no machines and no evidence that such things have ever existed here.

The predators produce the deepest sounds. Their calls are mostly below hearing, felt in the ribs before they are understood by the ear. When several call together, the shelves tremble. Dust loosens from the ceiling. Pools dimple outward in concentric rings. The sound passes through the bodies of the smaller animals, and for a moment they all freeze, as if the entire basin has become a single listening organ.

There are echoes, but they do not return cleanly. The cavern is too crowded. Sound strikes bodies, shells, wet walls, and the low ceiling, breaking into countless delayed fragments. A footfall may answer from three directions. A splash may seem to travel across the dark before it happens. Some calls return altered, stretched into tones the animals did not make. The place has a strange acoustical life of its own, a second ecology made of reflections.

Nothing is lit, yet nothing is entirely invisible.

The creatures carry a dimness with them. Not light exactly, but a pallor: wet skin catching what little brightness exists, translucent limbs showing cloudy internal movement, mineral dust shining faintly where it clings to their backs. In the deepest areas, certain animals produce brief cold flashes when touched or frightened. A body is disturbed, and a blue-white line runs along its side. Another answers. Soon the darkness contains a scattered pulse of ghostly signals, like distant lightning trapped beneath water.

These flashes do not illuminate the landscape. They only reveal fragments: a hundred legs, a curved shell, the slick wall of a pool, a mouth opening and closing in the dark. Then they are gone, and the basin becomes sound again.

The stone itself has a geography.

There are long flats where the animal traffic has polished the basalt to a dull sheen. There are raised ridges, not high enough to be hills, that divide the watercourses and force the migrating colonies into narrow passages. There are shallow depressions filled with old shells, layered over centuries into pale drifts. Some drifts have become cemented by mineral deposits, forming low terraces with the texture of bone. The largest pools occupy collapsed hollows and are connected by cracks beneath the surface. Their water is cold, dark, and crowded with suspended life.

Across the basin stand occasional pillars of stone, remnants of a former ceiling or the slow growth of mineral deposits. They are not monuments and do not form any pattern that suggests intention. Their surfaces are worn smooth up to the height of passing bodies, while their upper sections remain rough and dry. Creatures cling to them in dense vertical bands. From a distance, the pillars appear furred. When the darkness shifts, the fur moves.

There is no sign of construction, no tool-mark, no displaced stone. The place has not been made for anything. It has merely become what it is through the repeated pressure of water, animal passage, hunger, and time.

The animals have rules, though none are written and none are broken without consequence. The small creatures follow moisture. The hunters follow vibration. The great predators follow the concentration of both. When rain falls, every hierarchy changes. The water draws prey into the channels, prey draws hunters, and hunters draw the long armored bodies from the regions of dry stone. For a brief interval, the basin becomes violently dense, its life packed into the narrow wet places. Then the moisture sinks away, and the survivors spread again across the black shelves.

Nothing grows here in the botanical sense. There is no rooted life to soften the rock or gather the light. Instead, the ecosystem feeds on contact: bodies rubbing against stone, shells dissolving in water, mineral salts drawn through membranes, one animal opening another and taking what can be used. The world is not barren. It is past the need for plants, or perhaps it began without them.

Far across the basin, beyond the reach of any brief phosphorescent flicker, something enormous moves through the dark. Its passage displaces the air. The sound arrives first: a slow, subterranean booming, repeated at long intervals. Smaller animals pour away from it in every direction. Water shivers in the pools. The great predators turn and retreat into side channels.

The movement continues toward the unseen edge of the hollow.

For a while, the entire landscape seems to lean away from it.

Then the sound fades into the general thunder, the water settles, and the crowded darkness resumes its endless activity.
