The city stands on the summit of a drowned mountain.

Not on the sea floor—far above it, where the mountain’s upper slopes rise into a black, pressureless-looking ocean. The summit is small, no more than a few minutes’ walk across, but its edges fall away so abruptly that the surrounding water seems to begin there. Beyond the rim is only depth: a dark blue-black volume descending without visible end, though sometimes a faint pallor appears far below, as if the buried world still remembers daylight.

The mountain is young in the geological sense. Its rock has not had time to soften. It forms clean planes and splintered ridges, pale basalt and iron-dark glass thrust upward in sharp, fresh geometries. There are no rounded stones, no sediment, no mud. Every exposed surface looks newly broken. Narrow seams in the rock emit a weak, colorless glow—not heat, not radiation, but a mathematical indication of stress. The mountain is still deciding what shape it will have, though it has remained in exactly this shape for as long as anyone has measured.

The city was built over the summit without leveling it.

Its streets are bridges, ramps, and narrow platforms suspended between the stone points. Buildings cling to the mountain like barnacles designed by mathematicians: tall, hollow prisms; ribbed cylinders; pressure shells with no windows. Their surfaces are made from a dark ceramic-metal that drinks the little light available. In places, the material has been grown rather than assembled, each wall emerging from the next in seamless folds. There are no signs of welding, no bolts, no construction waste. The city appears less built than persuaded into existence.

Its highest structures rise only a few dozen meters above the rock, yet they feel like towers. In the surrounding water, there is no horizon and no sky to give them scale. Their upper ends vanish into darkness. Their lower foundations disappear into the summit’s fractures, where cables and structural tendrils descend into the mountain and perhaps much farther.

The city is not abandoned, exactly. It is maintained by things too small to see.

A skin of microscopic machines moves over the surfaces in slow, coordinated sheets. They do not swarm or glitter. They simply alter the texture of a wall from rough to smooth, close a hairline crack, replace a translucent membrane, and become indistinguishable from the building again. Their work is so gradual that the city seems motionless, yet nothing ever decays. A broken handrail is found intact. A door left open is eventually closed. Dust does not collect, because the water carries no dust.

There is no vegetation. No kelp, no algae, no moss in the seams. Nothing takes root in the mineral or reaches toward light. The city’s only colors are the gray-blue of the water, the black of its construction, the white traces of embedded circuitry, and occasional red points indicating a pressure difference that no longer exists.

The water is cold but not hostile. It presses equally from every direction, a tremendous and perfectly balanced weight. Inside the buildings, the inhabitants—if inhabitants remain—have made no attempt to recreate ordinary air. Chambers contain water of different densities, held apart by invisible membranes. One can pass through a wall and feel the pressure shift by a fraction, though the body experiences it as a change in thought rather than sensation. Some rooms are filled with warmer water, some with colder; some contain suspended spheres of gas that do not rise or collapse. They hang in place like miniature moons.

The rules of buoyancy have been edited here.

Water flows around the city but never through it. A cup released from a table remains beside the table. A person stepping from a platform does not sink or float; they come to rest at the same height, held by a field that recognizes the city’s boundaries. Loose objects stop moving after a short distance. Bubbles form perfect cubes and remain where they appear. Sound travels only along designated paths, arriving at a listener without crossing the intervening water.

This is why the city is so quiet.

There is a low, nearly inaudible tone beneath everything, produced by the mountain or by the machines below it. It is not a hum so much as the sound of pressure being continuously refused. Occasionally a deep note passes through the structures. It has no direction. It makes the ribs of the buildings tremble and causes the black water outside to brighten in thin vertical bands. The note lasts several minutes, then ceases. Nothing follows it.

No current disturbs the water. No rain falls from above, because above is simply more ocean. No particulate matter drifts downward from the unseen surface. The darkness is not complete, but it is thick and local: light exists only where the city chooses to spend it.

Illumination appears as narrow planes laid across floors, or as dim halos around doorways. It does not spill. A lit corridor ends at a precise boundary, beyond which the architecture is immediately swallowed. There are no shadows in the usual sense; darkness occupies the places where light has not been authorized.

Rare animals visit the summit.

They are small, pale, and nearly transparent, with bodies adapted to pressure rather than movement. They resemble fragments of glass or folded film. One may drift out of the dark and hover beside a tower for weeks, its internal organs visible as faint blue threads. It does not feed. It does not reproduce. It seems to use the city as a reference point, orienting itself against the impossible stillness.

Once, something larger passed beneath the summit. The water darkened below the bridges, not because a body blocked the light but because the depth itself appeared to withdraw. No one saw its shape. For three hours, every animal in the city aligned toward the same empty section of water. Then they dispersed, and the water returned to its previous condition.

There is no conventional weather here. The atmosphere is the water: its pressure, its clarity, its suspended fields. At the city’s perimeter, invisible boundaries divide one condition from another. The water outside is cold, dense, and endless. Inside the settlement, it is arranged into habitable layers. A visitor crossing between districts might pass through a membrane that tastes faintly of metal, enter a region where gravity points sideways, and emerge in a chamber whose ceiling is an open black sea.

The city has streets, but not destinations. It has doors, but no obvious rooms behind them. It has plazas where nothing gathers. A central structure rises from the mountain’s highest point: a narrow, faceted shaft with a circular opening at its top. Through the opening, a vertical column of water extends upward into darkness. Sometimes a line of pale light travels down that column from an immeasurable height. It takes exactly seven minutes to reach the shaft, pauses there, and disappears.

The event repeats at perfectly regular intervals.

Nothing else changes.

The rock does not erode. The metal does not tarnish. The water does not warm or cool. The rare creatures do not multiply. The lights do not fail. Even the machines seem less like active workers than a permanent property of the city, like hardness or weight.

At the edge of the summit, a low parapet follows the mountain’s broken rim. Beyond it, the deep water waits without movement. Looking outward gives the peculiar impression that the city is high above the world, perched on a narrow alpine ridge beneath an inverted sky. Looking down gives the opposite impression: that the mountain is a tower descending into a depth so vast it has become another kind of atmosphere.

The city occupies the small interval between those two immensities.

It is sparse enough that one can stand alone on a bridge and see no other presence, no other light, no sign of life. Yet the empty spaces are organized. They have been measured, named, and incorporated into the design. Silence here is not abandonment. It is a maintained condition.

Somewhere in the dark, behind the walls or beneath the mountain, the city continues to calculate itself.

And the water remains perfectly still.
