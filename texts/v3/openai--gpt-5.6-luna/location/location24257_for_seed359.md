The place is called a **room**, though it is larger than a house and smaller than a village. Its builders made no distinction between architecture and landscape. There is no earth beneath the feet, no original stone, no soil waiting under the paving. Everything is constructed: the floor, the ridges, the hollow trunks, the pale cliffs pressing close around the edges. Even the dust is manufactured, ground from old walls and baked into new ones.

It occupies a low basin enclosed by four short, inward-sloping walls. They are not quite walls anymore. Their surfaces have softened into terraces, shelves, and blunt escarpments, as if a small city had been left unfinished and then taught to imitate a valley. The highest point rises only as far as a person can throw a stone, but perspective behaves badly here. A ledge may recede for days before arriving at the far side of the room. A narrow gully may contain three horizons. From the center, the enclosing walls seem close enough to touch; from their upper steps, the same place opens into a broad, sunlit country with no visible exit.

The material of the landscape is a warm, porous ceramic the color of old bone, ochre, and bruised peach. It was pressed into blocks by hand, though many blocks now appear to have grown together. Seams run through everything: floors, pillars, plants, animals, and occasionally the air. At certain angles they are visible as dark, hair-thin lines. At others they open into impossible depths, showing a second version of the basin beneath the first, where all the slopes are reversed.

The ground is sharply new. Its edges have not yet learned to be round. Steps end in square corners. Ridges rise like the folded rims of fresh clay. Cracks are clean and narrow, with no long history of weathering. A broken column lies where it fell, still wearing the fingerprints of the people who shaped it. Nothing has had time to become ruin. The place is instead in the first quiet stage of becoming something else.

It is warm, but not oppressively so. Heat gathers in the walls and gives itself back slowly. The air smells of baked mineral, leaf resin, dry starch, and the faint metallic scent of tools. There is no open water anywhere. No pools, streams, rain, or damp earth. The plants survive by drawing a clear, tasteless vapor from the seams in the architecture, condensing it inside their bodies into amber gels and milky fluids. Their leaves are thick and translucent, their roots are not roots but bundles of ceramic filaments inserted into cracks.

The vegetation is lush in a manner particular to a waterless world. It does not form a green carpet; it forms a dense occupation. Vines cover walls in overlapping mats of blue-green plates. Fan-shaped fronds unfold from sockets in the paving. Tall, jointed stalks crowd the terraces, each one capped with a crown of soft, folded leaves that open and close by fractions over the course of a day. There are flowering things, though their flowers are dry and powdery, releasing clouds of colored dust rather than scent. Some grow from the eyes of statues. Some emerge from roof tiles. One species sends pale tendrils into the footprints of passersby and remains there, feeding on the shape.

Because the plants are made as much as grown, their arrangement is unnaturally dense. Nothing spreads where it pleases. Every grove seems carefully packed, with stems near enough to brush one another and leaves layered like scales. Yet no one remembers planting them. The inhabitants say that the first gardeners built the beds, and the plants have been making their own decisions ever since.

There are many animals. They are small, quiet, and close to the ground: six-legged grazers with hinged backs, mothlike creatures whose wings are thin ceramic flakes, long-bodied things that move through wall seams without disturbing the surface. A herd of pale, rabbit-sized animals feeds on the dry flowers. When startled, they do not flee. They become flat, lift slightly from the ground, and slide beneath the nearest threshold.

In the denser groves, birds perch on horizontal surfaces that do not exist. They sing rarely. Their songs are usually no louder than the tap of a fingernail on a cup, and sometimes the sound comes from behind the listener, even when the bird is plainly in front of them. Small predators wait among the plants, their bodies assembled from mismatched parts: a catlike forequarters joined to a column of jointed legs, or a long beak attached to something with the padded feet of an insect. The animals seem unconcerned by these arrangements. They hunt, sleep, breed, and age with the patient seriousness of ordinary life.

The inhabitants are few but constantly present. Their dwellings are built into the basin’s walls, each one little more than a recess closed by a woven screen. They use flint, bone, fired clay, and simple copper tools green with age. Their hands are stained with pigment from the flowers and with the gray dust of the walls. They carve channels, repair steps, split stalks, and make small vessels for the plants’ stored fluids. Their technology is humble, but the environment itself is so thoroughly made that even a primitive knife participates in a larger machine no one fully understands.

There are rules here, spoken as practical advice.

Do not count the doors.

Do not repair a crack until it has finished opening.

Do not ask a plant what it remembers.

Do not sleep beneath a ceiling that has begun to resemble the sky.

The inhabitants accept these rules in the same tone used for warnings about thorns. They know that the world is unreliable, but they do not consider unreliability unusual. A stair may gain or lose a step. A wall may become warm to the touch because, somewhere far away, someone has leaned against its opposite side. Shadows sometimes detach themselves and remain behind after their owners have moved on. If two people walk around the same pillar in opposite directions, they may meet in different seasons.

Reality does not collapse violently here. It loosens.

The sun is visible above the basin, but it does not always occupy the same sky. At midday it may appear as a white disk, a square of polished metal, or a bright opening through which another room can be seen. Light is otherwise ordinary and warm, laying clear shadows across the terraces. Those shadows, however, are slightly delayed. A hand lifts; the shadow follows a moment later. A bird flies; its shadow continues along the wall after the bird has landed.

The air is mostly quiet. Sound is absorbed by the thick construction and by the crowded vegetation. There is the faint clicking of stalks adjusting themselves, the scrape of tools, the dry rustle of leaves rubbing together. Somewhere within the basin, something large is slowly dragging itself through a tunnel, but the noise comes only once every few minutes: a soft mineral groan, followed by silence.

Now and then, all sound stops.

During these pauses, the landscape does not become empty. It becomes attentive. The plants turn their leaves toward one another. The animals hold still. The people lower their tools and wait. Across the basin, a new seam appears in a wall. It opens by the width of a hair, then closes again. Nothing emerges, but afterward one of the terraces is subtly longer, and a familiar grove contains one additional tree.

Change proceeds at this pace: almost imperceptibly, with long intervals of stillness and occasional irreversible adjustments. A dwelling settles into a different shape over several years. A path gradually forgets where it led. A plant spends a season growing toward a person who has not yet arrived. No storm comes to redraw the basin. No river cuts through it. There is only construction, accumulation, breeding, rooting, and the slow rearrangement of what was built.

At the center stands the oldest structure: a low circular platform crowded with vegetation. Its surface is made of dozens of fitted pieces, none quite matching the others. In the middle is a shallow depression that should hold water, but never has. Instead, it contains a layer of pale dust and one small green shoot.

The shoot has no visible leaves. It grows upward through the dry air, stops at the height of a knee, and bends toward whoever is watching.

If watched long enough, it begins to resemble a doorway.
