The place is a high basin no larger than a village green, though the surrounding mountains make it feel like a fragment of a much larger world. It rests on a shelf of unfinished stone, a shallow bowl lifted above the cloud line. The rim is not eroded into cliffs or gullies. It simply ends, as if the land had been pressed upward yesterday and has not yet decided what shape to take.

The ground is dark, warm, and wet.

Here and there, plates of black mineral protrude through a skin of pale clay. They have the blunt, swollen forms of stone that has begun to become rock but has not completed the process. Some are soft enough to dent under a boot, then slowly return to their original shape. Others ring like glass when struck. Fine seams of blue-white radiance move beneath them, not like lava, but like thoughts passing through a sleeping nervous system.

Water gathers everywhere without quite flowing. It beads in the hollows of stones. It hangs from the undersides of ledges. It emerges from the ground in clear, trembling domes, rises a few centimeters, and collapses again. The basin is fed by a constant condensation from the dim air: droplets form on empty space, briefly outlining invisible currents before falling.

There is no true stream. Instead, there are innumerable small decisions made by water. A wet patch darkens, brightens, shifts sideways. A film crawls across a slab against the slope. Pools rearrange their edges when no one is looking directly at them. In the center of the basin, a shallow depression holds a sheet of water no deeper than a hand. Its surface is perfectly still despite the wind, except when something beneath it turns over.

The warmth is gentle but inescapable. The air feels like the inside of a machine left running. Mist clings low to the ground, while the upper air remains clear enough to show the pale, bruised sky. Light arrives reluctantly. Even at what should be midday, the landscape is lit in diluted gray-gold, as though the sun were shining through several layers of water. Shadows are soft, but they do not always point away from the light source. A rock may cast a shadow uphill. A bird may cast none at all.

Vegetation is sparse, distributed in deliberate-looking clusters. There are low fans of rust-colored fronds, each no taller than a hand, growing only where the mineral crust has cracked. Their leaves are translucent and veined with moving points of green. When the air pressure changes, they fold themselves flat against the ground. When rain begins, they open and emit a brief, sweet tone.

Elsewhere grow stalks like black reeds, solitary and widely spaced. They do not seem rooted. Their lower ends taper into the wet earth, while their upper ends carry small cups that collect condensation. Every few minutes, one of the cups fills and releases its water in a heavy drop. The impact makes a sound like a distant door slamming.

Most of the basin is empty. The emptiness is not barren so much as carefully preserved. Wide intervals separate the growths, the stones, the pools, and the scattered remains of construction. Nothing crowds anything else. Even the animals seem to respect invisible boundaries.

The fauna does not.

Small creatures move through the gaps in sudden abundance. They emerge from under stones, from the water, from the mist, and occasionally from the air itself. Many resemble long-legged insects with smooth, wet bodies, but their anatomy changes as they travel. A six-legged thing may become eight-legged when it climbs. A crawling animal may flatten into a translucent sheet to cross a pool, then gather itself back into a body on the opposite side.

They are not quiet. The basin is full of clicks, shrill pulses, liquid chattering, and the rapid patter of many feet. Some creatures produce sound by striking their own ribs. Others sing through vents in the ground. The loudest calls come from pale, squat animals that resemble hares made from wax. They sit beside the pools and emit bursts of compressed noise, each one strong enough to ripple the water. After calling, they listen with their entire bodies.

Above them, narrow-winged shapes circle in the dimness. They have no visible heads. Their wings are lattices of metallic membrane, and each wingbeat briefly reveals a different pattern on the underside: an eye, a map, a human hand, a field of stars. They feed on heat rather than flesh, descending toward warm stones and hovering there until the rock grows cold.

Predation is frequent but oddly clean. A ribbon-bodied hunter slips through a cluster of fronds and causes three small animals to vanish without touching them. Later, their shadows appear beneath the water. A flock of glassy birds tears apart a reed, and from the broken stalk pours a cloud of tiny luminous organisms. The birds consume only the light. The reed remains standing, though changed: its black surface now carries a faint pattern resembling writing.

At scattered points around the basin stand the remnants of something built.

There are six structures in all, each small enough to cross in a few steps. They may have been shelters, instruments, or markers. Their walls are composed of thin, pale panels set at impossible angles. No joints can be seen. One building has collapsed into a low crescent, but the crescent continues to hold its shape several centimeters above the ground. Another is a tower only as high as a person’s chest, containing a vertical slit through which the weather can be seen before it arrives.

The structures are not ruined in the ordinary sense. They are incomplete. Their materials are still deciding whether to become solid, liquid, or information. A hand placed against one panel feels warmth, then a faint vibration, then the sensation of being remembered by something that has never met anyone.

Near the western edge is the most intact artifact: a narrow arch made of dark metal and milky crystal. It has no door and frames only a patch of empty air. Through the arch, the basin appears almost the same, except that the water lies in different places and the animals move backward. Once every few minutes, the arch produces a low tone. The tone is loud enough to shake droplets from the reeds, but it seems to come from below the world rather than from the structure itself.

The weather changes rapidly and without the usual transitions. A clear interval becomes a downpour in the space of a breath. Rain falls in vertical cords, warm as bathwater, then stops while the air continues to drip. During one kind of storm, the droplets rise from the ground instead, collecting in clouds beneath the stones. During another, the basin fills with a fine metallic dust that makes every living thing glow at the edges.

The landscape is still being made.

New ridges swell from the basin floor. Cracks close behind passing animals. A stone will be smooth in the morning and covered with angular facets by evening. The ground occasionally gives a long, slow shudder, not an earthquake but a correction. Afterward, every pool has moved a few meters, and one new object stands somewhere it did not stand before.

Nothing seems fully permanent except the process itself.

The rules here are physical, but not familiar. Heat attracts living things and also causes certain stones to grow. Sound can push water, bend mist, and wake dormant machinery. Attention has weight: when watched, some structures become more solid; when ignored, they begin to dissolve into the air. Distance is reliable across open ground but unreliable through the arch, around the pools, and inside the shadows of the black reeds.

The basin has boundaries. Beyond the rim, the clouds are close enough to touch, and the mountains descend into a white, sunless expanse. No path leads out. The few tracks that approach the edge return from another direction, sometimes bearing the footprints of animals that have not yet entered the basin.

At intervals, all the creatures fall silent.

The silence lasts only a few seconds, but in it the place becomes enormous. The small basin opens into a depth without visible bottom. The wet stones, the unfinished structures, the sparse plants, and the crowded hidden life all seem to be parts of one immense mechanism, operating slowly beneath the surface.

Then something moves in the central pool.

The water rises into a standing shape. The animals begin calling again. The reeds release their stored drops. Deep below, the new stone shifts into its next form, and the whole little world resumes its loud, warm, unfinished becoming.
