The landscape begins above the weather.

Not on a mountain, exactly. There are no mountains yet—only an immense elevation of dark, unfinished matter, a continent still lifting itself out of the planet’s crust. It rises in a broad, almost imperceptible swell, high enough that the lower clouds break and pass beneath it, high enough that the air is thin and cold in the open places. The horizon is hidden not by distance but by growth. Every direction is occupied.

The ground is black-blue stone, smooth in some places, raw and angular in others, as though it has only recently cooled. It has not had time to become soil. There are no valleys carved by age, no rounded boulders, no cliffs softened by weather. Fissures run through it in straight, branching patterns. Their edges are sharp and glassy. From every crack, water wells upward—not in springs, but in slow luminous pressures, pushed from below by a buried ocean or by the mineral body of the world itself.

The water does not collect in ordinary pools. It spreads beneath everything as a thin, moving skin.

It travels through the stone in translucent sheets. It beads along stems. It gathers in the cupped surfaces of leaves, then trembles there, held by forces stronger than gravity. In the dimness, the whole plateau seems covered by a film of rain that is falling upward and sideways at once. Mist condenses continuously in the air, though there is no clear source for it. Moisture forms on every surface, runs a few centimeters, and disappears into the living ground.

The vegetation is not growing upon the landscape. It is the landscape.

There are no forests in the familiar sense, because there is no meaningful distinction between tree, moss, grass, root, and terrain. A green-black tissue fills the spaces between the stones and then fills the spaces between itself. It rises in shelves, walls, columns, folded plains, and immense suspended membranes. Some growths resemble ferns scaled to the size of countries; others are low and granular, a close-packed carpet of tiny translucent fronds that pulse in sequence. There are towers of braided stalks, their interiors hollow and wet, standing kilometers high. Their crowns vanish into the cloud layer, where they continue to spread as horizontal canopies.

Nothing is sparse. There is no bare patch large enough to stand in comfortably. A step onto what appears to be open rock presses down a mat of minute leaves, each no wider than a fingernail, all packed together so tightly that they behave like a flexible mineral surface. Between the leaves, pale roots move constantly, not searching for soil but adjusting the pressure of the whole living mass. They knit the raw stone into temporary shapes. They brace a ridge, close a fissure, redirect a stream, then withdraw and begin again elsewhere.

The growth is rapid but not lush. It has no softness. The plants are cold, dense, and structural. Their tissues are fibrous as cable, glossy as wet slate, and in places faintly metallic. Some are transparent, revealing channels filled with silver fluid. Others contain lattices of dark cells that flicker with blue-white signals. A stand of tall reeds may communicate across hundreds of kilometers in less than a second, sending changes in pressure and chemistry through their connected roots. The signals are not thought, exactly. They are weather patterns inside the organism.

Above the plateau, the sky is permanently dim.

Light arrives filtered through layers of cloud, vapor, and airborne spores. The sun is present somewhere beyond them as a pale, cold pressure rather than a visible disk. At times the clouds glow from within, revealing the colossal silhouettes of leaves overhead. At other times the air darkens to a bruised violet, and the only illumination comes from the vegetation itself: slow currents of green under the mats, amber veins in the trunks, sudden white pulses traveling across whole fields as the plants respond to a distant change in temperature.

There is no night, but there are deep phases of darkness. During them, the roots become more active.

The plateau is loud enough to make silence unimaginable.

The tallest plants flex against winds that do not belong to any local storm. Their trunks groan in vast harmonics, each one resonating through the ground. The sound is less like a forest and more like an industrial machine operating at the scale of a continent: pressure, vibration, wet impacts, the continuous tearing and reforming of fibers. Water hammers through the root systems. Leaves strike one another overhead in dense, relentless sheets. From beneath the stone comes a deep periodic thunder as the rising crust shifts by fractions of a meter.

There are higher sounds too—thin, almost inaudible tones that become painful when they pass through the body. The plants release them to coordinate growth. A whole region may suddenly emit a piercing chord, and within minutes thousands of stalks will bend in the same direction, not toward sunlight, but toward a new source of minerals detected far below.

No animals inhabit this place. There are no eyes in the undergrowth, no wings, no burrows, no hunting calls. The ecology is entirely vegetal, yet it is not peaceful. Competition occurs through occupation. A root system will seal another’s channels, starve a rival patch of water, or grow a dense wall of tissue through it. Some plants dissolve their neighbors chemically. Others absorb them, incorporating their structures into new growth. Vast regions advance and retreat like slow armies, though no individual organism can be separated from the mass for long.

The living surface is also the weather.

When pressure builds beneath the plateau, the plants open thousands of narrow vents. Warm mineral vapor rises through them, carrying droplets that freeze in the upper air and fall as fine needles. When the atmosphere becomes too dry, entire plains of leaves fold inward, exposing reservoirs of water beneath them. When cold descends from the upper sky, the vegetation releases heat through synchronized cellular reactions, and the land glows faintly under the mist.

Storms form here without clouds.

A dark disturbance may begin as a shiver in the root network, then move across the continent at walking speed. The plants flatten before it. Water surges through the channels. Stalks bend, split, and regrow behind the passing front. The event may last for weeks. From a distance it would look like a black-green wave crossing an ocean, except that the wave is made of forests, stone, and coordinated pressure.

The world’s advanced intelligence is present everywhere and nowhere.

There are no towers, roads, instruments, or artifacts. Nothing has been built because construction is unnecessary. The vegetation itself performs functions that would require impossible machines elsewhere. It measures atmospheric composition through its leaves, computes changes in the crust through root tension, stores patterns in layers of hardened tissue, and sends information across the highlands as electrical and photonic pulses. The system is more sophisticated than any modern technology, but it does not appear designed. Its engineering is emergent, grown directly from the planet’s chemistry.

Occasionally, something happens that suggests a purpose beyond survival.

A field of low plants will open in a perfect circle, exposing wet black stone beneath. The circle will remain for several hours while blue light descends from the clouds into the exposed rock. Then the plants will close again, leaving no trace.

A line of towers may lean toward one another until their crowns touch, forming a bridge kilometers above the ground. Their tissues will fuse, exchange signals, and separate. No one can say whether the structure was communication, reproduction, or simply a consequence of the plants solving a problem too large for any single organism.

In some places, the land is still being made. New shelves of stone rise from the fissures, pushed upward by heat and pressure. They emerge already coated in translucent growth. The growth anchors them, but also reshapes them, spreading across the fresh surfaces before they have cooled. Continents may be assembling here—slowly by geological measure, rapidly by the measure of a living thing. The terrain is embryonic, not because it is empty, but because it is crowded with beginnings.

There are no ancient ruins. No worn paths. No dead wood. Even decay has not separated itself into a distinct process. When a plant collapses, the surrounding vegetation opens around it, draws out its water and minerals, and grows through the remaining fibers. Death is a phase of redistribution lasting only as long as the system requires. The landscape retains almost nothing unchanged.

From the highest exposed ridge, if such a place can be reached, the view is immense but frustratingly incomplete. Beneath the cloud layer, the green mass extends to every horizon. Above it, the atmosphere curves faintly. Far below, perhaps, are the lower worlds: oceans, deserts, cities, familiar weather. Here they are hidden beneath vapor and distance.

The ridge itself begins to move while you stand on it.

Not collapse—growth. The stone presses upward. Wet black leaves unfold around your feet. A seam of water opens, hissing, and a new column of vegetation rises from it, blind and certain, carrying the dim light of its internal channels toward the dark sky.

The whole continent shudders.

The sound is deafening.

Then, across the vast packed surface, every leaf turns at once toward something beneath the world.
