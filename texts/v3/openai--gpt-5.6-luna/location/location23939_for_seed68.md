The place is a small industrial town built inside a fresh-cut hollow, where the ground seems not so much excavated as recently persuaded to open.

Its boundaries are close. From almost anywhere, one can see the dark, raw walls at the edge of town: shallow cliffs of blue-black stone, broken into angular shelves and narrow fins. They are too sharp to have been softened by many seasons. Rain has found the first routes down them, making pale mineral tracks and temporary runnels, but the channels are young and undecided. Some end in gravel fans. Others vanish beneath sheets of corrugated metal, only to reappear in the middle of a road.

The hollow is low and cool, though not cold. Its air holds the dampness of a place that receives less water than it remembers. Rain comes in brief, heavy visits. Between them, condensation gathers on the machinery, the windows, the undersides of bridges, and the shaded faces of the stone. At dawn, thin water beads along every cable and railing. By noon, much of it has evaporated, leaving white and green stains.

Vegetation has arrived quickly and with confidence.

Tall grasses grow in the cracks between paving slabs. Ferns occupy the gutters. Moss spreads over the foundations of abandoned sheds, while young trees—mostly soft-barked, fast-growing things—stand in loose groups along the drainage channels. Their roots have begun to lift the edges of roads but have not yet learned the patience of forests. Vines hang from conveyor towers and climb the sides of warehouses, though they leave the uppermost steel exposed to the pale sky.

The green is abundant but not continuous. There are broad areas of bare gravel, empty loading yards, rust-colored spoil, and flat black stone where nothing has yet taken hold. The town feels sparsely occupied: a building here, a gantry there, a line of lamps crossing a stretch of grass. Between its constructions are considerable distances, filled with weeds, standing water, windblown packaging, and the movements of animals.

The main industry is stone processing, though nobody agrees whether the stone is quarried, harvested, or still growing.

At the center of the hollow stands a cutting plant made from concrete, corrugated steel, and old brick. Its windows are clouded with dust. Great saws turn behind translucent walls, their teeth cooled by narrow streams of recycled water. The water runs black at first and then gray, carrying powdered rock into settling pools. In those pools, reeds have established themselves in neat green ranks. Frogs call from beneath the pipes, and small silver fish live in the warm overflow from the plant.

The machines are not always running. Their cycles are long and deliberate. A crusher may work for six hours, stop for an entire day, then begin again without warning. Conveyor belts move at a steady walking pace. Loading cranes cross the yards so slowly that birds perch on them while they travel. In the pauses, the entire settlement seems to wait with its mechanisms: valves ticking, cooling metal contracting, reservoirs lowering by fractions.

When production resumes, the sound is immense.

The saws produce a continuous, throat-deep vibration felt through the soles of the feet. Crushers knock with a blunt, irregular rhythm. Warning horns sound before vehicles move, though the vehicles move so slowly that the warning seems meant for something geological. Loose sheets of metal chatter in the wind. Pumps throb under the ground. From the far side of the hollow comes the repeated impact of the rail shunter coupling empty wagons together.

Yet there is space between the sounds. In those intervals, other lives become apparent.

Water drips from the black cliffs. Grass seeds strike the walls. Insects rasp in the weeds. There are many birds: long-legged water birds in the settling ponds, dark swifts nesting in the saw towers, small yellow birds feeding on the flowering margins of the access roads. Foxes move through the empty yards at midday, accustomed to engines and people. A colony of pale bats lives in the ventilation shafts and emerges in a dense, silent scatter at dusk. Their bodies are almost white from generations spent among stone dust.

The town’s people live in scattered clusters rather than a continuous neighborhood. A row of workers’ houses stands beside the old rail line, each with a narrow garden and a rain barrel. The houses are painted in colors that have faded unevenly: rust red, mineral green, cream, and a blue nearly identical to the stone. Farther off are prefabricated apartments around a square of wind-bent trees. There is a canteen, a repair hall, a clinic, and a little market beneath an elevated pipeline. Most structures have been built, altered, and built around again. New walkways cross old roofs. Pipes enter buildings through windows. A church or water office—its original purpose is unclear—has been converted into a pump-control station.

The streets are broad enough for industrial vehicles but strangely empty. Their surfaces are patched with different materials: tar, concrete, brick, compacted slag, and slabs of polished black stone. Rainwater gathers in the seams. Drainage is not a hidden system here. It is visible everywhere: open channels, grated trenches, concrete gutters, spillways, and narrow bridges over temporary streams. The town has been designed around water that appears only when it is inconvenient.

Beyond the last buildings, the land rises only slightly. There is no true horizon from within the hollow, but the enclosing stone gives the impression of a low basin newly lifted from some deeper layer of the world. At its lowest point lies a reservoir, wide and shallow, surrounded by reeds and pale mud. Its surface is usually broken into separate pools. After rain, they join for a few days, and the water reaches an old concrete stairway descending from nowhere.

The stairway is one of the place’s unusual features.

It leads down beneath the reservoir, though no one can explain how. During dry periods its lower steps are visible through the clear brown water. During wet periods, the water rises only to the same level and never covers them completely. Workers say that if one follows the stair beneath the surface, the stone remains dry. This is not forbidden, exactly, but the town has an unwritten rule against testing it during the hours when the cutting plant is active. The vibration of the saws is said to make the submerged steps shift.

Other rules are less mysterious and more practical:

Nothing may be built directly on the newest ground. The fresh black stone is unstable, and structures placed there slowly lean toward the same direction, regardless of their foundations.

No rainwater is allowed to leave the basin without passing through a reed bed.

Birds have priority on the overhead cables during the morning shift. Vehicles stop if the cables are occupied.

The lights are left on in the empty yards at night, not for workers but for the foxes, which have learned to hunt insects around the lamps.

And every building must contain a room with no machinery in it. These rooms are called quiet rooms, though they are never completely quiet. They hold the constant damp sounds of the town: distant pumps, dripping pipes, the movement of roots beneath floors, and the faint settling of the newly formed stone.

In ordinary daylight, the place is clear and exposed. There are no dramatic shadows, no permanent fog, no great obscuring weather. The sky is a broad, cool gray-blue, and the normal light reveals every surface: the green film on the retaining walls, the orange blooms of rust, the white mineral crust on the rails, the dark shine of wet rock. Smoke from the plant drifts low and thin, more like breath than pollution, and is often caught in the trees at the bottom of the hollow.

Nothing changes quickly here. The same broken conveyor may stand for years beneath a veil of flowering vine. The same pool may dry, return, dry again, and retain the footprints of animals long after the animals are gone. Buildings settle by millimeters. Roots widen cracks. The quarry face sheds a few stones each spring.

But the place is not still.

The town is being slowly claimed by the life around it. Grass enters the factories. Water revises the roads. Birds fill the unused frameworks. The people continue to cut, crush, transport, and stack the dark stone, while beneath their foundations new seams rise toward the surface—sharp, damp, and unfinished.
