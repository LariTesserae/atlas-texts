The place is called **Low Choir**, though no one agrees whether it is a district, a garden, or the exposed interior of some enormous organism.

It occupies a shallow basin no larger than a village green, perhaps once a lake, perhaps once a city plaza. The ground is nearly level, worn down by an age of water and roots. Nothing here rises far above the eye: a few leaning towers of living wood, a collapsed arch, the ribbed backs of old transit conduits. Beyond them, the world is hidden by vegetation. The basin has no true horizon. It ends wherever the plants become too dense to see through, and beyond that is assumed to be more of the same.

The soil is dark, warm, and saturated just beneath the surface. It gives slightly underfoot, not like mud but like packed moss over a moving body. Water lies in thin, reflective sheets between the roots. Every depression has become a pool. Every stone wears a clear film. The old paving is still visible in places: broad plates of black ceramic, worn smooth by centuries of inundation, their seams filled with pale liverwort and tiny transparent reeds. Rain is not falling now, but the landscape continues to drip. Water runs from leaves that have no visible source, gathers at the tips of dangling vines, and drops with the steady concentration of a leaking machine.

Warm mist hangs low among the plants. It does not obscure the place so much as soften its edges. The air tastes faintly metallic, green, and sweetly rotten. Breathing is easy, but never neutral: each inhalation carries spores, volatile oils, and the cool mineral scent of stone that has not been dry for a thousand years.

The vegetation is not growing *in* the landscape. It is the landscape.

A mat of dark, jointed roots forms the ground. Low plants spread across it in overlapping plates, their surfaces thick and waxy, some resembling jade roofing tiles, others the skin of sleeping animals. Trees rise in scattered groups rather than a continuous forest. Their trunks are hollow, braided, or partly translucent, and their roots have taken over the foundations of the old settlement. One tree has grown through a circular station entrance and now holds the structure open with a fan of pale buttresses. Another has consumed an entire wall, leaving only window-shaped shadows in its bark.

There is plenty of space between these presences. The basin is sparse in the way a body is sparse: not empty, but organized around organs. A dozen steps of open wet ground may separate one growth from the next. In those spaces, small pools shine under ordinary daylight. Fallen leaves lie individually rather than in drifts. Each one is large enough to serve as a cloak, and each slowly alters its position when no creature is looking directly at it.

The plants are old beyond the age of their species. Their lower growth is browned, mineralized, and almost stone-like, while fresh green tissues emerge from it in quick, startling bursts. A dead vine remains attached to its support for centuries, gradually becoming a cable, a rail, or a bridge. At Low Choir, decay does not remove structures. It changes their material category.

The surviving architecture is modest but deliberate. There are low platforms of green-black composite, half submerged and linked by narrow causeways. Their edges are rounded from erosion, their surfaces furred with rootlets. A few vertical frames stand in the open spaces, each assembled from sections of old infrastructure and living timber. They may have once supported roofs. Now they support clouds of tiny pollinating machines.

The machines are almost indistinguishable from the ecology. Some appear as silver seeds that unfold wings. Some crawl like beetles with glassy legs. They move through the vegetation in busy, discontinuous paths, repairing a leaf here, changing the acidity of a pool there, lifting a fallen branch and carrying it several meters before abandoning it. Their intelligence is distributed through the roots. Their bodies are temporary.

At intervals, the old city’s technology reveals itself.

A submerged line pulses beneath the paving. Its light is faint in daylight, visible only as a bluish tremor under the water. When a person steps near it, the line wakes and grows a bridge of braided cellulose toward them. The bridge is warm, damp, and structurally perfect. It lasts until the person crosses, then loosens into fibers and settles back into the basin.

One of the standing frames contains a weather system in miniature. Condensation gathers on its upper branches, forms droplets, and falls through a lattice of leaves. The drops strike different surfaces and produce tones. Some are audible; others are felt in the teeth. The frame is not playing music exactly. It is processing information: water levels, root stress, animal migrations, changes in the chemical composition of the air. The resulting sound is a constant, uneven ringing, as if a hundred small bells were being tested by rain.

This is the source of the place’s name.

Low Choir is loud.

Water ticks on broad leaves. Pools slap against their banks as things move beneath them. Hollow trunks boom when struck by wind. Vines rub together with a dry, abrasive hiss. The repair insects produce a granular whine, rising and falling in swarms. Somewhere under the ground, pumps or hearts cycle with a heavy, subterranean thud. Every so often, a whole section of the root network contracts. The basin shudders, the water climbs an inch, and the plants answer with a brief collective creaking.

The noise is not chaotic in the ordinary sense. It is too coordinated for that. But it is turbulent: countless small processes occurring at once, correcting one another, failing, improvising, and beginning again.

Fauna are abundant, though none are large.

Long-bodied amphibious animals live in the root mats. They have soft gray hides, bright mouths, and delicate forelimbs used to comb algae from the water. They rarely flee. Instead, they vanish by flattening themselves against the substrate until they become another wet shape among the roots.

There are flocks of winged things that resemble leaves torn into pieces. Their wings are green on one side and reflective on the other. When they turn together, the basin flashes with brief mirrors of sky. They feed on heat, or pollen, or the electrical discharge in the old infrastructure; no one has determined which.

Small mammals occupy the hollow trees. Their eyes are too large, their fur almost absent, their bodies marked with luminous veins. They carry seeds in their mouths and fragments of printed circuit in their paws. They are constantly relocating themselves. A family may establish a nest in a wall at dawn, abandon it at noon, and be found sleeping upside down beneath a platform by evening.

The largest animal is a slow, many-legged grazer called a bellback. It is the size of a small table and carries a shell of layered plant matter. Water runs off this shell into channels that feed the moss around its feet. When threatened, it does not retreat. It rings. The sound is low enough to make the pools tremble, and every mobile organism in the vicinity changes direction.

The plants respond to the animals, but not gently. A vine may open to offer nectar and then close around the visitor’s ankle. A carpet of moss may warm beneath the feet of one species and cool beneath another. Certain trees release fruit only when a creature tells a lie nearby. The ecology is not hostile, exactly. It is inquisitive, and it has access to too much information.

There are rules here, though they are more like habits of matter than laws.

Nothing can remain unused for long. A tool left on the ground becomes rooted by morning. A shoe develops soft green fibers. A dropped coin is absorbed into a stem and may reappear years later as part of a door hinge.

Straight lines do not persist. Every rigid structure eventually bends toward water, sunlight, or the nearest concentration of living tissue. This happens slowly to stone and quickly to advanced materials. Buildings are permitted to exist only if they participate in the metabolism of the place.

Water remembers movement. If someone walks through a pool, the ripples may continue for hours, traveling against the wind and returning at irregular intervals. Old footsteps sometimes emerge from the mud after rainfall, belonging to people who crossed the basin centuries ago.

The network beneath Low Choir knows the difference between a visitor and a resident, but it has no fixed definition of either. A visitor is someone who still believes they are separate from the environment. A resident is someone the environment has successfully predicted.

At the center of the basin stands the oldest structure: a low, circular platform almost completely overtaken by plants. Its surface is made from a material that resembles gray stone but is warm like skin. In the middle is a shallow depression filled with perfectly clear water. Nothing grows in this water. Nothing lives in it. Yet its surface is always disturbed by tiny concentric ripples.

Those who look into the pool see the place as it will be in a few minutes.

Not a prophecy, precisely. More a calculation.

A branch will fall. A creature will cross the water. A buried conduit will open. A wall will soften and lean. A new flower will unfold from the mud. Sometimes the image shows a person standing at the pool, though no person is present. Sometimes it shows the entire basin empty, stripped of plants and machines, revealing a flat field of ancient stone beneath the roots.

That vision is never explained.

Above Low Choir, the daylight is ordinary. The sky is pale, the shadows soft, the sun warm but not oppressive. There is no supernatural color in the light. This makes the place stranger. Its impossible machinery, its predictive water, its intelligent vegetation, and its creatures with circuit-filled bones all appear under the same familiar illumination that might fall on a roadside ditch.

The basin feels old enough to have outlived several meanings of the word *alive*. It is not pristine. It is not a ruin. It is a settled remnant in which construction, erosion, biology, and computation have finally stopped competing for dominance.

They have become difficult to distinguish.

The ground continues to flatten. The buildings continue to sink. The trees continue to grow, die, and become buildings again. The water keeps the whole system in motion, and the plants keep the water from leaving.

Nothing here is finished.

But everything has been happening for a very long time.
