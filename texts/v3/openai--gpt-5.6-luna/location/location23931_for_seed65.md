The high country begins where the lower world seems to have been cut away.

There is no gradual climb into it. The land rises in long, pale shelves and black escarpments, each one broader than a valley but too narrow to be called a plain. Far below, the lower country is reduced to a blue-green impression between the ledges: a broken rim, a few distant folds, and a haze that never quite resolves into forest or settlement. Up here, the horizon is enormous. It goes on in all directions, though the ground itself is divided into terraces, gullies, and immense stone bowls.

The rock is old and orderly. Dark volcanic bands show beneath gray limestone, while the exposed faces are furred with pale mineral crusts. Weather has worked on them for ages but has not dismantled them. Ridges have been rounded, not shattered; gullies are smooth-sided and settled into their courses. The whole plateau has the appearance of something that has already finished becoming what it is.

Water does not run freely here. It appears in strict, widely separated places: a black pool in a hollow, a thin seep along a fault, a necklace of beads beneath an overhang. The stone sweats during the coldest hours, though there is no visible shift in temperature to explain it. Moisture gathers in the same cracks and on the same sheltered faces, as if the mountain has a memory of rain. Small streams descend from the higher shelves, but they move with a peculiar permanence—never swelling, never thinning, never freezing entirely. Their surfaces are filmed with ice, their centers dark and slow.

The air is cold without being cruel. It has the thin, clean dryness of great altitude, but every exposed surface carries a skin of dampness: silver condensation on stone, dark moss in crevices, translucent films over the backs of lichen. Breath does not plume so much as unravel immediately into the wind.

And there is wind everywhere.

It comes across the open shelves in a continuous, immense roar. The sound is not gusting or fluctuating; it is a single unbroken pressure, as constant as surf. Narrow ridges whistle, but the broader basins boom. The hollow undersides of the terraces turn the wind into a deep vibration that can be felt through the soles of the feet. In certain places, the rock sings—not with melody, but with a sustained metallic tone produced by air passing over long seams of quartz. A person standing in one of these places cannot hear their own voice. Even the movement of animals is reduced to glimpses: claws on stone, wings opening, mouths wide in silent alarm.

The brightness is nearly absolute. The sky is a hard, washed blue, and the light strikes from every direction, reflected from salt, ice, and pale rock. Shadows are short and sharply bounded. In the largest basins, the brightness seems to come from below as well as above. White mineral floors throw the sky back upward, filling the hollows with a flat, glare-filled radiance. There are no forests to soften it, no clouds to give scale. Distant cliffs appear close; nearby stones can seem impossibly far away.

Vegetation survives in a low, disciplined form. There are mats of black-green moss around the permanent seeps, thin yellow lichen on the windward faces, and small cushion plants pressed nearly flush to the ground. They grow in isolated colonies with great distances between them. A patch may occupy an entire shelf, yet there will be no second patch visible for miles. Their roots penetrate deep into cracks, and their leaves are thick and dull, designed less to gather light than to endure it.

The apparent emptiness is deceptive.

Life is abundant, but it is concentrated in stone. Every damp fissure holds pale insects with jointed, translucent bodies. Flea-sized animals leap among the lichen in such numbers that the rock seems to flicker. Long-legged burrowers emerge from gravel pockets and freeze in the open, relying on their mottled hides. There are colonies of small, broad-winged birds nesting in the undersides of cliffs, though their calls are lost completely in the roar. They launch in great spirals, dozens at a time, and ride the permanent currents without flapping. Their undersides are white, so that when they pass beneath the sun they vanish into the glare.

Larger animals travel the shelves in scattered herds. They are lean, wool-coated things with wide feet and dark, reflective eyes. Their movements are slow and deliberate, not because they are weak but because nothing here requires haste. They graze on lichen and the tough edges of cushion plants. Some have long whiskers that stream horizontally in the wind. They stand facing into it for hours, perhaps sleeping, perhaps listening through their bones.

Predators are present in equal abundance, though rarely seen together. Narrow-bodied cats or catlike hunters lie along the shadow lines beneath the terraces. They are almost the same color as the limestone and move only when the wind’s pressure changes—which is never. Instead, they have learned to remain perfectly still until prey passes within reach. Scavenger insects gather around old bones, but the bones do not seem to accumulate. Nothing remains on the plateau for long. Flesh disappears into cracks, and the cracks close over it with a soft mineral bloom.

There are no paths, no walls, no cairns, no worked edges. No artifact has a place here. Even a stone moved by hand seems to settle back into the landscape’s visual order, as if disturbance cannot persist. The few tools a traveler might carry would be simple—stone blades, bone hooks, woven hide—but there is little to make them from and nowhere to shelter. The high country does not forbid construction; it simply offers no reason for it. Its surfaces are already shaped into ledges, hollows, windbreaks, and water-traps. The mountain provides, but only in exact quantities.

The world here has an unusual stability. Nothing appears to begin or end. Water remains in its basins. Ice remains in its seams. Plants remain at the same height, neither advancing nor dying back. Herds occupy the same shelves, and their trails never deepen. Even erosion seems complete: stones have been worn smooth, slopes have reached their final angles, and loose scree lies in perfectly balanced fans beneath the cliffs.

The wind never changes direction.

Clouds, if they exist, remain below the plateau. Rain is remembered only in the dampness of rock. Day does not seem to progress toward evening; the light holds its hard, white intensity. An animal may lie down beside a pool and rise again with the pool, the brightness, and the position of every distant ridge exactly as before. There is no obvious decay, but neither is there growth in the ordinary sense. The plateau is not frozen in time so much as settled outside of it.

At the far edge, the shelves descend toward a vertical wall of blue stone. Beyond that wall is only glare: no lower land, no clouds, no visible sky boundary. The wind pours over the rim and into that blankness, carrying dust, loose feathers, and the faint scent of wet minerals. Standing there, one feels not exposed but suspended—as if the entire high country were a broad, ancient instrument, held motionless beneath the sun, while every fissure and hollow sounds one endless note.
