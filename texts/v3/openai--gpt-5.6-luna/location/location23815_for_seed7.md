The chamber is small enough to understand at once, but not small enough to feel safe.

It lies beneath a thin crust of black ground, in a pocket where the earth has split and cooled around a trapped breath of heat. The ceiling is low in places, high in others: a jagged roof of fresh basalt, still sharp along its fractures, with pale seams where minerals have begun to bloom. Nothing here has been softened by age. The stone has the dark, glassy look of something recently made, and the floor is broken into shelves, tilted plates, and shallow channels that appear to have frozen in the act of flowing.

Water does not enter as a stream. It arrives reluctantly.

At intervals, the ceiling gathers a film of condensation. Droplets swell along the stone, tremble, and fall. Some strike the floor and vanish in a faint hiss. Others collect in narrow hollows, making black pools no larger than washbasins. Around each pool, a small world has taken hold.

The vegetation is lush but scattered, as though each plant has been allotted a precise patch of survivable ground. Ferns spread in dark green fans from cracks in the basalt. Their fronds are thick and waxy, almost succulent, and their undersides carry beads of moisture that do not evaporate. Low mats of moss cover the edges of the channels, though the moss is not soft; it has grown into crisp, branching plates, like miniature coral made of plant tissue. Long, pale vines descend from the ceiling and stop just above the floor, never touching it. Their roots remain embedded in the rock overhead, drinking from the condensation before it falls.

There are no flowers, no insects among them, no hidden movement in the leaves. Nothing browses. Nothing pollinates. The plants reproduce by slow division and the release of fine green dust, which drifts in the warm air and settles nearby. Each generation remains close to its parent. The vegetation is abundant where the stone permits it, then ends abruptly, leaving several yards of bare rock between one green island and the next.

The heat is constant, not fierce. It comes from below and from the walls, a deep mineral warmth that fills the chamber like the breath of a sleeping machine. The air is damp only in pockets. Near the pools, it is thick and cool against the skin; elsewhere it is dry enough to tighten the throat. A faint metallic taste lingers in it. The darkness is nearly complete, but the plants give off a weak, unsteady green radiance along their veins. It is insufficient to illuminate the chamber. It merely reveals the nearest surfaces: a wet ledge, the edge of a root, the glint of standing water.

The only stronger light comes from the machinery.

Near one wall stands a compact pumping station, or what remains of one. It is built from riveted steel, ceramic housings, and thick cables sunk into the rock. The structure is too small to be a factory and too deliberate to be abandoned mining equipment. A vertical cylinder rises from the floor, its paint burned away in places. A narrow glass gauge is clouded from within. Every few minutes, a motor engages.

The sound is almost too quiet to call mechanical: a low interior vibration, followed by three measured clicks. A valve opens. Somewhere beneath the floor, water shifts through a pipe. Then the motor stops, and the chamber returns to its broad, patient silence.

The pump does not drain the place. It redistributes the moisture. It draws water from the deepest cracks and sends it through thin pipes along the walls, where the pipes release it as vapor. Condensation forms overhead, falls into the hollows, and is gathered again. The whole chamber is an artificial cycle that has become, over time, an ecology.

Not a thriving one. A contained one.

There are occasional signs of human presence beyond the pump: a short length of rail disappearing into a sealed fissure; yellow hazard stripes half-buried beneath a crust of mineral; a maintenance platform with its ladder ending against solid rock. One wall bears a rectangular doorway, but the door was never installed. Beyond it is only a clean, dark cut into the stone, as if the workers had begun excavating and then stopped when the earth changed its mind.

The newest features are the sharpest. Fresh cracks run across the floor, their edges unweathered, their interiors faintly warm. In one place, a slab has lifted by the width of a hand, exposing a narrow orange glow beneath. No lava moves there. The light comes from a hot seam, steady as an ember sealed under glass. Around it, the plants have grown in a careful arc, stopping just outside the intolerable heat.

Nothing happens quickly. The pump cycles. Water gathers. Stone cools by fractions too small to witness. Roots extend into mineral seams. The green dust settles. A fissure widens perhaps the thickness of a hair over a year.

The chamber is not dead, but it has no animal life and no need of it. Its processes are closed, sparse, and self-maintaining. The plants do not seem to seek the machinery, yet they have arranged themselves around its leaks and vents as if they understand the shape of its usefulness. The machinery, in turn, continues operating without a visible operator, following some old program or geological necessity.

At the farthest end, where the ceiling dips low, the basalt has formed a smooth black basin. A single pool occupies it. The water is perfectly still except when a droplet falls from above. Each impact sends a ring across the surface, and for a few seconds the faint green reflections of the surrounding plants tremble there.

Then the ring disappears.

The pump clicks once in the darkness, and the chamber waits.
