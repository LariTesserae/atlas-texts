The high country begins where the road forgets how to be a road.

There is no gradual ascent. The land rises in a succession of dark shelves, each one higher than the last, until the horizon is mostly cliff: blue-black escarpments, pale mineral faces, and immense stairways cut by weather that has worked here for longer than any remembered people. At the uppermost level, the plateau extends broad and open, though never empty. It continues past sight, a regional expanse of stone basins and ridges, its surface crowded with forms that seem too close together to be called isolated and too widely spaced to be called continuous.

The stone is wet without appearing recently rained upon. Water gathers wherever it can: in shallow grooves, beneath overhangs, inside the hairline cracks of the rock. It beads on vertical walls and travels sideways along them, ignoring gravity for a few moments before dropping in silver threads. The ground has a damp, cold sheen. Some surfaces are slick with a transparent film that does not freeze, even in the chill. Others breathe out a fine vapor, as if something deep below is exhaling through the mountain.

The water is not always water in the same sense. A stream may become a narrow strip of polished darkness, reflecting not the sky above it but a sky from some other hour. Pooled water sometimes holds upright, rising in thin columns from a basin and trembling there like hanging glass. In the deeper clefts, the flow makes no descent at all. It moves toward the sound of itself.

Light falls everywhere, hard and abundant. The sky is a brilliant, nearly colorless blue, though at its edges it thickens into bands of green, pearl, and faint violet. There is no visible sun. The brightness seems to come from the whole upper world, from the clouds, from the wet stone, and from the air between things. Shadows are short and sharply defined, but they do not always agree with the objects that cast them. A standing pillar can have two shadows, one pointing toward the light and one pointing away. A person—if anyone crosses this place—might find their shadow lagging behind, or arriving before them.

The cold is not severe. It settles against the skin like wet cloth. Breath shows briefly, then condenses into small beads that hang in front of the mouth before vanishing. The atmosphere is saturated with a fine mineral dampness. On the highest ridges, droplets collect on every edge: on stone needles, on broken walls, on the rims of old tools, on the pale surfaces of exposed strata. The world seems constantly close to rain, but the rain itself is intermittent and local. A neighboring basin may be under a clear, dazzling sky while a column of water falls in one precise place for hours.

There is no vegetation. No grass softens the ledges, no moss colors the shaded cracks, no root has split the old masonry. Nothing green or brown stirs in the stone. The absence is not barren in the ordinary way; it is more complete than that. The land has no memory of leaves. Even the soil is missing. Where soil might have gathered, there is instead a fine gray grit made of ground glass, salt, and something fibrous that dissolves when touched. It lifts in the wind and arranges itself in temporary lines, as if trying to write.

Nor are there animals. No wing passes overhead, no insect gathers at the water, no small body disappears between rocks. The only moving things are water, vapor, falling fragments, and certain dark patterns beneath the surface of the stone. These patterns are not alive, but they react. A footfall sends a black ripple through a cliff face. A shouted word can cause pale veins to brighten several hundred paces away.

The terrain is dense with mineral growths. Towers stand shoulder to shoulder in the shallow valleys: hexagonal columns, rounded nodules, thin plates like pages of a gigantic book. They are not crystals exactly, though many have crystalline edges. Some have been worn smooth by weather; others are sharp enough to divide a reflection into separate pieces. Between them run narrow corridors where the air is louder and colder. Here, water falls upward in silver strands, then breaks into droplets that drift horizontally until they strike a wall.

The cliffs show the long patience of erosion. Their faces are rounded in places, undercut in others, and deeply grooved by ancient flows. There are broad amphitheaters with settled floors, ravines whose shapes have become almost architectural through repetition, and immense shelves where the same sequence of ridges appears again and again. The forms feel established, not newly violent. Even the impossible features have a weathered quality. A boulder hangs three feet above the ground, suspended in a field of pale distortion, but its lower surface is worn smooth by centuries of nonexistent rain.

Across the plateau, the remains of habitation appear sparingly.

A low shelter has been built beneath a slanted slab, its walls made of stacked black stones sealed with gray clay. The doorway is too narrow for comfort. Beside it stands a frame of poles, bone-colored but mineral-hard, supporting strips of hide or woven fiber that have become translucent with age. There are shallow fire pits, though no ash remains in them. There are old work surfaces covered in scratches, and simple tools laid in careful rows: hammerstones, wedges, sharpened flakes, bowls made from hollowed rock. A few implements are made of dull metal, hammered thin and repaired many times. Their edges are strangely good. They cut not only material but distance; a knife drawn through a wet cord may leave the cord intact while severing the space between hand and blade.

Other structures are less recognizable. A ring of waist-high posts surrounds a pool that reflects a star-filled night even under the bright day. A stairway climbs the face of a cliff and ends at a blank vertical plane. Along one ridge, small stone towers have been built at regular intervals, each capped with a shallow dish to catch the condensation. The dishes fill with water, but the water always tastes of the place where the next tower stands.

The scattered constructions do not make the plateau feel inhabited. They make it feel observed.

Sound is constant and forceful. Wind travels through the mineral columns with a low, sustained roar, but the tone changes when someone moves. Water strikes stone in hundreds of overlapping rhythms: ticking from the walls, booming in the chasms, hissing where it crosses hot seams deep below. Now and then the whole plateau gives a long, resonant groan. The sound rises from the rock and seems to pass through the body rather than through the ears.

There are louder events. A distant cliff may clap shut, though no visible movement follows. A basin can release a sudden burst of vapor that sounds like a crowd inhaling. The falling stones in a far ravine strike one another in a descending scale, ending in a note too low to hear but strong enough to shake droplets from the ledges.

Voices behave badly here. A word spoken in a valley may return from above, spoken in the speaker’s own voice but with a different meaning. Calls echo from places that do not exist. Sometimes a sound arrives before its cause: the crack of a dropped stone, followed moments later by the stone itself rising from the ground and settling into the hand that released it.

The world is active without being fertile. Weather cycles through the plateau in visible bands. Mist pours over one ridge, pauses, and withdraws. Brightness gathers in the hollows. Thin fractures open in the rock, exhale steam, and close again. Every few hours—or what passes for hours—the mineral formations shift by fractions of an inch. The movement is slow enough to be mistaken for fatigue in the eyes, but over a day the corridors change shape and familiar landmarks lean toward one another.

Distance is similarly unstable. The nearest cliff may require a morning’s climb, while a ridge on the far horizon can be reached in a few steps if approached without looking directly at it. The plateau sometimes folds along its existing erosion lines. A valley becomes a wall. A wall becomes the far side of a valley. These transitions are not sudden; the land simply acquires a new arrangement while attention is elsewhere.

Still, certain rules hold.

Water seeks hollows, even when those hollows are vertical or suspended. Stone remembers pressure. Brightness reveals what is present but does not necessarily reveal where it is. Nothing living grows, and nothing dead decays. Tools remain where they are placed unless carried by a person, and people are the only things that seem able to carry objects without the landscape reclaiming them. Every structure must be built from material already exposed; digging downward leads not into earth but into older versions of the same place.

At the edge of the highest escarpment, the land drops through layers of cloudless air. Below are lower shelves, then blue distances, then more cliffs—though the geometry is too compressed, as if the entire mountain range has been folded and stacked beneath the plateau. Water descends from the upper level in long luminous curtains, vanishing before it reaches the depths.

The air there is clean, cold, and deafening.

Across the open stone, under the bright sky with no sun, the wet mineral world continues to alter itself by increments: ancient, settled, crowded with pillars and basins, empty of life, and full of the uneasy suggestion that emptiness is only one of the things it is practicing.
