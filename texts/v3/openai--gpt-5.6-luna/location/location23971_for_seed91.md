The landscape begins where the ground has been cut open and then forgotten.

It lies beneath a broad, cold region of low hills, in a chamber or excavation that is too young to have acquired the rounded manners of age. The walls are sharp-sided and raw. They show the marks of drilling, wedges, and sudden breaks: shelves of blue-gray stone, splintered ribs of frozen earth, pale seams of clay exposed like old paint beneath plaster. In places, the roof is no more than a ceiling of packed gravel and roots. In others it rises into darkness, supported by nothing visible, while long cracks admit the thin, irregular dripping of the world above.

The cold is not clean. It is a wet cold, carrying the mineral smell of stone, iron, and green things kept too long without sun.

Almost everything here is plant.

Not a forest, not a garden, and not a jungle in the ordinary sense. The floor is covered by broad, continuous bodies of moss and low-growing plants, their surfaces layered like felt, hide, and sod. Pale stems run into one another beneath the surface. Roots form cords as thick as rope and ladders as broad as beams. There are mats of liverwort in the hollows, dark green and nearly black, with beadlike leaves holding condensation in their folds. Along the walls, threadlike plants descend from cracks and spread sideways, making sparse curtains that tremble whenever the machinery starts.

The plant growth does not fill the chamber evenly. It occupies the places where water pauses.

There are long dry intervals between one living mass and the next: exposed gravel, bare stone, low ridges of crushed spoil. Then a green island appears around a leaking pipe, or follows a shallow channel cut into the floor, or gathers beneath the slow, silver fall of condensation. Some patches are only a few yards wide. Others extend away into the dimness as uninterrupted fields, their surfaces dull and velvety, rising and falling over buried roots. From a distance they resemble low hills covered in green cloth. Up close, they are intricate and wet, with hundreds of small leaves closing around each drop.

The largest plants have become architecture by simple persistence. Their roots have found fractures in the rock and thickened there, then crossed open gaps, then joined with other roots until they form natural walkways, buttresses, and low arches. No one built these structures, though people have made use of them. A cable has been strapped along one root bridge. A narrow metal footpath passes over a flooded hollow. Here and there, timber stakes have been driven into the living floor, supporting warning signs, lamps, and lengths of black hose.

The water is intermittent. It does not flow with the confidence of a river. It arrives as a seep, a leak, a season of dripping, a line of droplets trembling along the underside of a pipe. Pools collect in old extraction pits and remain there beneath a skin of ice. Their surfaces are broken by rings whenever something falls from above. At the edges, the plants make dark fringes, drinking slowly. In one low basin, a narrow stream crosses the floor, disappears under a mat of roots, and emerges again several hundred yards away, colder and clearer.

There is no natural silence.

The sound is a physical pressure, continuous and immense. Ventilation fans turn somewhere beyond the visible chambers, their blades producing a low mechanical thunder that fills the ribs and teeth. Pumps start and stop in distant rooms. Conveyors grind over their tracks. Water strikes metal in a hard, repetitive cascade. The whole excavation vibrates with the patient operation of an industrial system too large to see at once.

Occasionally the sound changes shape. A crusher engages, and the walls seem to move. A train or ore-hauler passes behind a partition of stone, its wheels shrieking through the rock. Somewhere high above, an intake gate opens and a column of air comes down with the force of weather, making every hanging stem and root-fiber bow in the same direction.

Conversation is impossible. Even shouts vanish into the machinery.

The lights are few and widely separated. They hang from cables or sit behind grimed metal cages, each one casting a shallow yellow pool across the floor. Between them, the plant life recedes into blue-black dimness. Moist surfaces catch the light: the slick backs of leaves, the bright edges of new root growth, the pale mineral crust around a leak. Farther away, the green becomes gray, then merely textured darkness.

The lamps have not been installed for the plants. They illuminate access routes, valves, gauges, and work platforms. Yet the plants gather beneath them. Growth is thickest around service corridors, where warmth leaks from motors and condensation runs down the walls. A solitary lamp in an abandoned section has become the center of a small green settlement: moss across the floor, climbing stems on the support column, a low mound of leaves pressing against its concrete base. Beyond the reach of that light, the chamber remains bare.

The industrial presence is scattered rather than dominant in appearance. There are pump stations, electrical cabinets, a few corrugated shelters, and the remains of a narrow-gauge rail line. Rust has begun to mark the older structures, but nothing here is truly old. The cut faces are still sharp. Metal edges remain square beneath their coating of damp. Spoil piles have not yet slumped into natural shapes; they stand in fresh, angular heaps, gray and brown and streaked with green where the first colonizing plants have taken hold.

A watch platform crosses one of the larger cavities. It is reached by a staircase bolted into the wall, with gaps between the steps through which the living floor can be seen far below. From the platform, the scale of the place becomes apparent. The chamber opens into other chambers, and those into long galleries, all connected by narrow passages and service roads. Lines of lamps diminish toward the horizon. Pipes run in parallel bands along the walls, vanishing around corners. Conveyor belts cross the distance like black bridges.

Between these engineered lines, the green landscape continues without design.

It rises over discarded machinery. It fills the beds of inactive tracks. It has entered the seams of concrete and pushed apart the edges of floor plates. A broken loader sits half-buried in moss, its cab windows filmed with condensation, its tires collapsed into the wet growth. Around it, the plants have not consumed the machine so much as settled into every sheltered surface: leaves in the engine compartment, stems through the steering wheel, a soft green layer over the bucket.

There are few animals. Small pale insects live in the warmest drain channels, appearing briefly when a lamp is switched on. Something with narrow feet has made tracks along one muddy edge, but it is rarely seen. In the quietest recesses, tiny gnats hover above the pools. Their presence is almost theoretical against the scale and noise of the place. The real population belongs to the plants, to their slow spread through fractures and damp margins.

Nothing moves quickly except falling water, loose stones, and machinery. Growth is nearly invisible. A root thickens across a season. A mat advances by the width of a hand. A channel deepens under dripping condensation. The cavern changes by accumulation: a little more green around the pipe, a little more silt in the basin, a little more weight in the root bridge. Years from now, some of the sharp walls will soften. The spoil will settle. The pumps may continue, or fail, or be replaced. For now, the place remains in its first condition—a raw wound below the surface, already filling with life.

The deafening machines do not oppose the vegetation. They sustain it accidentally.

Their heat makes the condensation. Their leaks make the streams. Their excavations expose the mineral faces and buried root systems. Their electrical lights mark a scattered path through the dark. In return, the plants soften the machinery’s edges, cover its foundations, and quietly occupy every interval the system leaves unused.

Beyond the lamps, beyond the rails and pipes, the green floor continues into the underground distance, damp in isolated places, empty in between, while the unseen engines labor without urgency and the cold earth holds everything in place.
