The settlement clings to a high basin where the mountain has folded inward and kept its own weather.

It is not a large place. One could cross it in half an hour if the paths held still. They do not always hold still.

The basin is a shallow bowl of dark stone, bounded on three sides by pale cliffs and on the fourth by a drop into cloud. Water lies close beneath everything. It gathers in cracks, under flagstones, behind walls, in the hollows of old stairs. It comes down from the cliffs in thin, innumerable threads—not waterfalls so much as the mountain sweating through its skin. The rock is black, blue-black, and green with mineral sheen. Every surface is polished by long weather: doorsteps, bridge rails, grave markers, the rounded corners of houses. Nothing looks newly made, though much of it is continually being rebuilt.

The light is astonishing.

Even beneath cloud, the whole basin is flooded with a clear white brilliance, as if dawn has been trapped overhead and cannot move on. Snowfields above the cliffs throw back a hard radiance. Water catches it in every seam. At midday, the settlement seems cut from glass and wet slate. Shadows are sharp but strangely shallow, lying close beneath objects. At night—if night comes at all—the clouds glow from within, and the basin remains dimly visible by reflected ice-light.

There are few plants. No forest descends this far into the bowl. The slopes carry only low, dark cushions of moss and wiry grass, each tuft pressed flat by cold wind. Pale lichens spread over the stones in branching maps. In protected corners, small red leaves grow from cracks in the masonry, but they are waxy and hard, more like little hands than foliage. The people cultivate no broad fields. Their food comes from the water, the cliffs, and the animals that pass through.

And there are animals everywhere.

They occupy every ledge, drain, roof, and pool, though their presence is often known before it is seen. Small white bodies move beneath the surface of the channels. Long-legged birds stand motionless on parapets, their feathers the color of wet ash. They have no visible eyes, only smooth dark patches that turn toward movement. Clusters of furred creatures nest in the warmest walls, emerging at dusk to drink from the mortar. Their paws leave no prints. On the roofs, shaggy mountain animals lie in dense huddles, steam rising from their backs, while narrow, translucent things thread between them like fish moving through reeds.

The animals are not tame, exactly. They have simply accepted the town as part of the mountain.

The town is built in tiers around a central reservoir. Houses are low and close-set, roofed with overlapping slabs of slate and sealed with pitch. Their walls are packed stone, bone, clay, and the pale fibrous material stripped from certain cliff-dwelling plants. Doors are small. Windows are covered with stretched membrane that becomes transparent when wet and opaque when dry. Almost every building has a gutter, and every gutter leads somewhere: into a cistern, a channel, a washing court, a bathhouse, or the reservoir.

Nothing here is allowed to waste water.

The streets are narrow trenches between buildings, paved with rounded stones that fit together like teeth. They slope in deliberate spirals. In ordinary weather, water creeps along their edges in quiet threads. After one of the basin’s sudden disturbances, the streets become streams, and the inhabitants move through them on raised plankways, carrying baskets above their heads.

The construction is old-fashioned but intricate. There is little metal: a few iron blades, hooks, hinges, and bells, all dark with age. Most tools are stone, horn, hardwood, or fired clay. The people are skilled at joining materials without nails. Walls are laced together with roots and hide cords. Bridges are made of stacked slabs balanced on stone pins. Buildings lean into one another, sharing buttresses and roof beams. Some appear to have grown rather than been erected.

The settlement’s organization is more urban than rustic. There are districts, public cisterns, covered markets, work courts, shrines, animal pens, and a system of signs carved into every major wall. Yet no street has a true name. Each is identified by what it is doing: the street where water sleeps, the stair that turns three times, the passage that sings in frost, the lane beneath the birds.

The people speak quietly. Sound does not carry well in the wet stone, and they have learned not to force it. Conversation takes place in murmurs, gestures, and taps against walls. A knock on a door may travel through an entire block, altered by the masonry into a particular sequence of notes. The reservoir makes a barely audible sound, a soft internal shifting like someone turning over in sleep. The gutters tick. Water beads and falls. Animals breathe.

There are no loud markets, no shouting children, no hammering workshops. Even the town’s greatest activity is nearly silent.

The basin is unstable.

At irregular intervals, the mountain changes its mind. A stair may lengthen by six steps overnight. A roof may flatten itself. A wall may open a narrow blue seam and begin to pour out warm water. The central reservoir can rise without rain, spilling over its rim in perfectly even sheets. Once, according to the oldest records, the entire western quarter slid three body-lengths downhill, with its inhabitants still asleep inside. They woke to find their houses intact and their streets rearranged.

These events are not disasters in the usual sense. They are part of the settlement’s weather, anticipated and incorporated. People keep ropes beside their beds. Every household has a movable threshold and a basket of marked stones for measuring the inclination of floors. Children learn which cracks are harmless, which are thirsty, and which are beginning to think.

The most important rule is never to obstruct a change.

If a wall begins to bulge, the people remove what they can and wait. If a channel reverses its flow, they redirect the gutters but do not dam it. If an animal appears where no animal could have climbed, they leave it a path to depart. The town survives by yielding in small, organized ways.

Beyond the last houses, the basin narrows toward a bright cleft in the mountain. The air there is colder, and the water hangs in it as suspended beads. Travelers say the cleft leads to another valley, but no one has returned with a consistent account. Some say there is a second settlement below, built upside down beneath the clouds. Others say the mountain opens into a sea of light.

At the cleft’s mouth stands a low wall covered in handprints. The prints are dense, layered, and of many kinds: human hands, webbed hands, paws, three-fingered impressions, and long marks made by something with no fingers at all. They are renewed whenever the basin changes.

In the white, brilliant quiet, the town waits—not peacefully, but attentively. Water moves under its foundations. Animals crowd its roofs. Stone remembers every season. Somewhere beneath the paving, a new passage is forming, and the people have already begun listening for where it will come through.
