The landscape is hidden inside a mountain.

Not in a cavern, exactly. It occupies a shallow fold in the high rock, a pocket no larger than a village square, sealed on three sides by pale, unfinished stone. The place feels less excavated than still being made. Its walls rise close and steep, warm to the touch, their surfaces smooth in some places and swollen into blunt mineral folds in others. Nothing has had time to carve them. Water has not yet learned its routes.

It is always dark here.

Above, a narrow split admits no visible sky—only a dim pressure of warmth and the occasional soft arrival of condensed water. Droplets gather along the lip of the cleft, swell, and fall without urgency. They strike the stone at irregular intervals, but the intervals are so long and the chamber so quiet that each drop seems to happen alone. Most of the water does not run. It beads on the rock, clings in transparent films, or gathers in shallow black hollows that have no channels leading away.

The floor is a basin of raw material: gray-white stone, ochre clay, and granular black fragments that glitter faintly when touched. The ground has the unfinished look of something recently exposed, though there are no fresh scars, no crumbling edges, no sign of movement. The forms simply exist in their first stable arrangement. Ridges stop abruptly. Slopes flatten before they become slopes. A low shelf extends from one wall like a half-formed platform, its edge rounded but not worn. Here and there, the mineral has lifted into knobs, ribs, and small clustered pillars, as though the mountain were growing an interior organ.

Water lies everywhere in small quantities. It darkens the floor between stones. It hangs in beads beneath overhangs. It fills the seams between plates of rock with a black, perfectly still shine. The air is warm and saturated, with the enclosed heat of a body or a greenhouse left without glass. Breathing feels easy but intimate; every breath seems to belong to the chamber for a moment before disappearing into it.

Vegetation is sparse, almost embarrassed by the stone. A few pale, rootless growths cling to the wettest surfaces: thin fans of gray-green tissue, translucent as old skin, and small black tufts that resemble moss seen before it has decided to become moss. They do not spread in carpets. Each occurs alone, or in tight little knots around a seep. Their leaves—or leaves’ primitive equivalents—hold no color that darkness can use. They are soft, swollen, and nearly motionless.

The density of the place comes not from life but from proximity. Everything is close to everything else. The walls, shelves, pools, mineral folds, and hanging drops occupy the same small volume in layered abundance. There are no open distances. A person standing in the basin could touch the wall, the floor, and a wet overhang without taking a step. The chamber feels crowded despite being empty.

Someone has used it.

Against the driest wall stands a low structure of lashed wood and stone, more shelter than building. Its roof is made from overlapping slabs, each held in place by wooden pegs and strips of hide gone stiff with mineral deposits. The wood is dark, swollen, and strangely preserved. A shallow sleeping platform sits beneath it. Beside the platform are three rough vessels: one a clay bowl, one a hollowed stone, and one made from a gourdlike material that could not have grown anywhere in this chamber. Their mouths are crusted with pale deposits. A chipped hand-axe rests nearby, its edge blunt, its handle worn smooth by many grips.

There are no ashes. No bones. No discarded food. The shelter is not abandoned so much as concluded. It has been left in the same condition for so long that abandonment has become another mineral layer. The objects do not decay, but neither do they gather dust. Fine moisture settles over them and withdraws, leaving them clean.

The only clear mark of passage is a narrow line of flattened clay leading from the shelter to a pool at the back of the pocket. It ends at the water. Beyond it, the floor is untouched.

Something lives in the pool, though it is rarely seen. Occasionally, beneath the dark surface, a pale thread folds itself into a new position. The movement is slow enough to be mistaken for a change in reflection. Once, perhaps, there were several of these creatures. Now there may be one. It has no visible eyes, no obvious mouth, and no interest in the dry world. When the falling droplets disturb the pool, it rises just far enough to press a soft, colorless ridge against the surface, then sinks again.

There are no insects. No birds reach this height. No roots descend from above. The few signs of fauna are indirect: a faint groove in the clay, a translucent skin caught beneath a stone, three tiny circular impressions around the pool. They might belong to the same creature, or to different creatures that have never encountered one another.

Sound is reduced to its smallest forms. Water touching stone. A faint internal ticking from somewhere in the walls, as if the mountain is cooling, though the air never grows cooler. The subtle flex of wood under its own weight. At long intervals, a deep vibration passes through the chamber—not a rumble, not an earthquake, but a pressure so low it is felt in the teeth. Nothing visibly shifts when it occurs.

The darkness is complete but not uniform. Near the water, blackness seems dense and wet. Around the shelter, it is softer, occupied by the lingering impression of human proportions. The pale rock does not shine, but it retains a suggestion of brightness, as if remembering daylight from a time before the pocket closed. If a flame is brought in, it burns with a low, steady yellow and casts almost no shadow. The surfaces absorb the light rather than returning it. In the flame’s glow, the mineral folds appear less like stone than like the inside of a half-closed hand.

The world here follows a few quiet rules.

Water arrives but does not travel far. Warmth remains constant. Nothing erodes. Nothing grows quickly. Shapes do not decay so much as settle into permanence. A dropped object will remain where it lands unless someone moves it. A footprint will stay legible for years, perhaps centuries, but will not multiply into a path unless repeatedly made. Sound does not echo; it is taken into the walls. Fire does not spread, but it also does not consume the old wood. Living things persist in narrow, patient forms, as though the chamber permits survival but discourages change.

The pocket is not a refuge from the mountain. It is one of the mountain’s smallest thoughts: warm, wet, crowded, and unfinished, held in perfect stillness high above the ordinary world.
