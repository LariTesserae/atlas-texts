The plateau is underwater, but not deep enough to forget the sky.

It rises in long, dark shelves above the surrounding ocean, a high country drowned to its shoulders. The highest ridges and isolated tors break the surface like the backs of sleeping animals, though no animals live here. Water pours continuously over their edges in pale curtains, and the exposed stone is slick with a vegetation so abundant it seems less like growth than a second terrain. Moss carpets the ledges. Long black-green ribbons hang from cliffs and drift in the wind. Pools on the summit are crowded with translucent blades, low red fans, and soft mats of something resembling grass, all rooted in cracks where there should be only salt and gravel.

The water is cold enough to hurt the hands. It has the clear, mineral color of glacial melt near the surface, but below the first few fathoms it darkens abruptly to blue-black. Looking down from a ridge gives no sense of depth—only a vertical country of drowned valleys, suspended forests, and pale shelves falling away into absence. The ocean is not calm. It moves in broad, deliberate surges through the plateau’s cuts, rising and lowering with a rhythm that seems too slow for tides and too organized for weather. Sometimes an entire basin empties, revealing a floor of shining stone and rooted plants; sometimes the water returns as a wall, climbing without breaking and swallowing the basin, the ridges, and any person foolish enough to remain there.

There are no fish, birds, insects, worms, or other animals. Nothing swims. Nothing crawls. The water contains only drifting fragments of plant matter and fine white motes that gather around the roots. Some of the motes are mineral. Some are not. They arrange themselves in rings around stones, then scatter whenever noticed.

The vegetation is lush but silent in its own way. It does not rustle with small lives. It folds, flexes, and opens according to pressure, light, and the changing height of the water. Whole meadows of broad, waxy leaves turn toward the sound of approaching currents. The submerged forests are made of tall stalks anchored in cracks, their crowns spreading beneath the surface like inverted umbrellas. They bear no flowers and no visible seeds. Instead, when the water becomes still, they release faint green clouds that drift upward and settle on other plants. New growth appears there by morning, already rooted and mature.

In the exposed places, the plants are darker. Mosses the color of wet ash cling to the stone. Low shrubs have glassy stems and leaves like thin black coins. Their roots reach into seams in the rock and exude a white crust, preserving the cracks from further erosion. The land has been weathering for a very long time, but it does not seem to be wearing away. Stone breaks, yes, and ledges collapse, but the fallen pieces are gradually drawn back into the plateau. They fit themselves into hollows. Gravel climbs slopes in narrow streams. Boulders settle into old positions after storms. The whole landscape is established, not static but self-maintaining, as if erosion here is a form of memory.

The plateau is broad enough to contain weather of its own. From one ridge, the far side may be hidden behind rain. From another, one can see miles of broken summits and flooded valleys, each carrying a different color of darkness. There are zones where the sea lies flat and heavy, and others where it rushes constantly between stone walls. Rain falls upward in some basins: droplets lift from the surface in trembling veils and vanish into low clouds. Elsewhere, clouds descend into the water and remain there, forming pale underwater weather that drifts through the drowned forests.

Most of the light comes from nowhere visible. The sky is nearly black, even at what should be midday, but the water and plants retain a dim internal glow. It is not enough to illuminate distance. It only outlines nearby things: the edge of a rock, a strand of vegetation, the steps of an old stairway disappearing beneath the surface. At night—or what passes for night—the underwater plants open luminous seams along their leaves. Their glow is cold and green, like light trapped under ice. In the deepest water, there are occasional slow flashes far below, too large to be sparks and too irregular to be signals.

The loudness of the place comes from its water. Every channel has a voice. Narrow cuts boom as waves force themselves through. Submerged caverns throb like enormous drums. Rain strikes the exposed pools with a hard, metallic clatter. When the tide—or whatever governs the water—turns, the whole plateau groans. The sound travels through stone as much as through air, so that a person standing on dry ground feels vibrations in the soles of the feet before seeing the water move.

There are periods of sudden quiet, but they are not peaceful. When the currents stop, the silence becomes an immense pressure. The plants cease their slow bending. The floating motes hang motionless. Far below, something knocks once against the floor of the ocean, and the sound arrives minutes later.

Human construction appears only in fragments. On the highest shelves stand small, roofless structures made of stacked slate and dark timber, their walls rounded by weather but not collapsed. Some are shelters; others may have been markers, though their openings face the wrong directions for doors. Primitive tools lie embedded in the moss: stone wedges, bone-colored chisels, hooks of hammered copper gone green with salt. There are narrow walkways crossing certain ravines, built from fitted slabs. They remain intact even where the surrounding cliffs have fallen away, spanning empty air and plunging water with no visible supports.

A few structures are still occupied, though not by people. Their interiors contain bowls filled with fresh water, coils of plant fiber, and walls covered in hand-cut lines. The marks repeat the changing height of the sea, but some lines rise higher than the surrounding mountains. At intervals, the builders carved a human figure beside them. The figures have no faces.

The old inhabitants may have lived here before the flooding, or may have arrived after it. Their camps suggest a culture adapted to constant submersion: platforms on high posts, sealed stone chambers, ladders descending into the water, and small rafts tethered to roots. Yet there are no bones, no refuse, and no signs of struggle. Every useful object has been left in place. Even the fires remain arranged in circles, though they have not burned for generations. When wind passes over the ash, it sometimes produces a faint red glow beneath the surface, as if the fire has only gone down rather than gone out.

The rules of the place are not entirely reliable.

Water flows uphill along certain channels. A person who enters a submerged passage may emerge somewhere above the surface, on a ridge several miles away. Sounds can be delayed, repeated, or heard from beneath the ground before they are made. Reflections do not always show the present landscape. In still pools, one may see the plateau as it was before the water came, with dry valleys and distant settlements, or as it will be after another great change. The reflected plants are sometimes unfamiliar. The reflected sky is occasionally full of stars.

The darkness is not empty. It has layers. Close darkness belongs to rock, water, vegetation, and mist. Far darkness seems to belong to distance, though no distance should be so complete. On rare clear intervals, the horizon opens and reveals a ring of high land extending in every direction, all of it submerged, all of it green with impossible abundance. Beyond that ring is a second horizon, much higher than the first, as though mountains are standing upside down beneath the sea.

Nothing hunts here. Nothing flees. Nothing decays in the ordinary manner. Fallen leaves dissolve into clear threads that return to the roots. Dead wood becomes stone without losing its grain. A person who remains too long in the water may find moss beginning beneath the nails, but there is no pain, and no certainty that this is harmful. The landscape does not attack visitors. It incorporates them gradually, according to its own patient processes.

The most dangerous event is the arrival of the deep tide.

It begins with a low vibration and a change in the plants. All of them, on land and underwater, lean toward the same distant point. The water draws away from the plateau, exposing terraces no one has seen before: staircases, gardens, foundations, and long channels cut into the bedrock. Then the dark water returns over the horizon, not as a wave but as a rising surface. It covers the lower valleys first, then the shelves, then the old structures. The sound grows until it drowns out thought.

When the tide reaches its greatest height, only the highest ridges remain above water. Their plants shine through the blackness. The scattered towers and shelters appear as tiny islands, and the whole drowned country seems less like a place being flooded than a place being awakened.

Then the water begins to fall again, leaving no wreckage behind. The channels resume their courses. The plants straighten. New growth appears in the cracks.

The plateau returns to itself—cold, dark, green, and loudly alive without a single animal anywhere in it.
