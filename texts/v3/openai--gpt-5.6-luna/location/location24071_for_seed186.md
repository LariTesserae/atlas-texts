The plateau begins above the last trees.

It rises in long, dark shelves of stone toward a country of immense distances: not a mountain peak so much as the upper body of a mountain range, broad enough to contain weather of its own. The ground is an old, settled thing. Glaciers have passed over it, rivers have worried at its edges, and frost has opened the same cracks for thousands of years, but nothing here feels freshly broken. The cliffs are rounded at their shoulders, polished on their faces, and furred in black mineral crust. In the hollows, the rock holds shallow soils like cupped hands.

The air is cold enough to make every breath visible. It comes thin and clean over the ridgelines, carrying the faint iron taste of high stone. Clouds move below the plateau more often than above it. They gather in the valleys as a white inland sea, spilling upward in slow, torn masses until they touch the lower slopes. When they do, the world condenses.

Moisture appears in brief, improbable places. Beads of water form along the undersides of overhangs and tremble there for hours before falling. A dark seam in the rock may produce a narrow runnel for a few days, then go dry. Meltwater collects in circular basins, each no wider than a room, black and perfectly still. Their surfaces are sometimes sealed with ice, sometimes covered by a floating skin of green growth. There are no dependable rivers, only the memory of them: pale channels crossing the stone, filled with moss, lichens, and the bones of weather.

Wherever water lingers, vegetation is unexpectedly abundant.

Low mats of blue-green moss spread over the rock in thick, velvety islands. They are interrupted by wiry shrubs with silver leaves and red stems, by rushes as tall as a person, by pale grasses that grow in dense, wind-flattened fans. In sheltered clefts, the plants become almost extravagant. Ferns unfurl beneath ledges. Small trees—crooked, ancient things with black bark—cling to the ravines, their branches crowded with waxy leaves. Their roots disappear into cracks too narrow to admit a hand, and their foliage remains green even beneath a rim of frost.

The vegetation does not form a continuous blanket. It gathers in separate concentrations, isolated by huge expanses of bare stone. From a distance these green places look like spilled pigment: a basin of moss here, a dark thicket there, a whole ravine bright with yellow lichen. Between them lies emptiness, miles of exposed plateau and broken escarpment, with no path, fence, cairn, or shelter to suggest that anyone has ever crossed it.

There are no buildings. No roads. No marks made by tools.

Yet the plateau is not wholly untouched by industry.

In certain valleys, half-buried machines stand among the grasses.

They are not ruins in the ordinary sense. There is no rusted factory, no collapsed tower, no scattered framework. Instead, the machines appear as isolated presences, as if the industrial world had been reduced to its essential organs and placed far apart. A cylindrical engine lies on its side in a hollow, its casing black and smooth beneath decades of mineral accretion. A row of heavy wheels protrudes from a cliff face, each one taller than a house, turning perhaps once every few hours. Far away, on a shelf of rock, a narrow chimney rises from the ground with no building beneath it. It releases a thread of warm vapor only when clouds pass overhead.

The mechanisms are mostly silent. Their power is felt rather than heard: a tremor beneath the soles, a brief dimming of the stars, a pulse that travels through the stone and wakes every pool in the vicinity. Some of them seem to use the mountain itself as fuel, drawing heat from deep below. Others respond to weather. When condensation gathers, valves open in the rock. When the plateau freezes, buried pistons shift. Once in a long while, a machine begins to move—not loudly, but with immense deliberation—and the surrounding landscape adjusts around it.

A ridge may rise by the width of a hand.

A spring may vanish.

A field of moss may turn its leaves toward a sound too low for human hearing.

The darkness is unusually complete. There is little atmospheric glow at this elevation, and the clouds below absorb the faint illumination from distant settlements, if any exist at all. Under the open sky, stars crowd the blackness. They seem close enough to cast a fine, colorless grain across the rocks. In cloud, visibility contracts to a few yards. The fog is not evenly white but stratified: blue at its edges, gray within, and occasionally threaded with a dull amber glow from beneath the ground.

That glow travels.

It appears first in a pool or crack, then moves through the landscape in branching lines, following old channels, root systems, and buried conduits. Plants lean toward it. Insects emerge from the moss. The larger animals begin to descend from the ridges. By the time the light reaches the far end of a valley, the entire ecosystem has become briefly alert.

The plateau is rich with fauna, though most of it is difficult to see.

There are broad-winged birds that fly without flapping, circling for hours in the thin air above the cliffs. Their feathers are dark on top and translucent beneath; when they pass over a pool, the water shows a moving skeleton of light. Small herd animals graze among the rushes, long-legged and woolly, with faces adapted to looking downward into ravines. Their hooves are split into several flexible points, allowing them to stand on surfaces that seem nearly vertical.

Predators leave almost no sign. A pale, many-jointed cat moves through the shrubs without disturbing them. Long-backed scavengers wait beside condensation basins, perfectly still until something drinks. In the deeper valleys, something larger moves beneath the vegetation. Its passage appears as a slow displacement of green, a wake traveling through the moss from one rock to another. No body is ever plainly visible.

There are also animals associated with the machines. Small, blind creatures nest in warm vents and feed on metallic salts. They gather in silver knots around opened valves, their bodies pulsing faintly in time with the buried engines. Certain moths lay their eggs in the insulation of old housings. When the machines stir, the moths hatch all at once and fill the air with pale wings, so quiet that their presence is registered only as a softening of the stars.

Sound is scarce, but never absent.

Most of the time there is only wind: a thin pressure moving across the plateau, too steady to be called a gust. It slides over the stone and through the grasses with a dry whisper. The clouds below make no sound at all. Animals move in brief, nearly inaudible bursts. Water falls one drop at a time from a ledge.

Then the ground gives a low internal note.

It may last for several minutes, or for a whole night. The note is not a roar but a vibration that enters the ribs and teeth. During it, distant wheels turn. Cliffs shed a little dust. Birds lift from the ravines in silence, and every animal on the plateau faces the same direction, listening to something beneath the audible world.

The weather is active despite the quiet. Frost advances and retreats in patterns. Condensation blooms across the rock, feeds the moss, and disappears beneath the sunless clouds. Sudden snow squalls pass over one ridge without touching the next. In sheltered valleys, the plants grow rapidly during brief humid intervals, then lock themselves into stillness when the air dries. The whole landscape alternates between motion and suspension.

Nothing here is crowded. There may be no living thing within sight, and then, around one wet hollow, hundreds of lives reveal themselves at once: grazing forms between the reeds, insects under the leaves, birds dropping from the dark, roots tightening in the soil, blind creatures stirring around a warm pipe. Abundance is concentrated, hidden, and temporary.

At the far edge of the plateau, the land falls away into a series of immense steps. Each step is green in its creases and bare along its faces. Beyond them, the cloud sea opens in a single long fracture, revealing distant ranges beneath: black summits, glacier-blue ravines, and pinpricks of artificial light far below.

No light can be seen moving up toward the plateau.

But sometimes, after midnight, a line of lights moves across the stone by itself.

It follows no road. It crosses the empty spaces between the machines, passes through stands of ancient trees, and descends into the dark valleys. Wherever it goes, the moss brightens, the pools tremble, and the animals turn to watch. Then it sinks beneath the ground, leaving the plateau once again to its stars, its cold, its breathing plants, and the patient industrial rhythm hidden inside the mountain.
