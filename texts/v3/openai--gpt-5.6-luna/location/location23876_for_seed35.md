The chamber lies below the surface, though “below” is difficult to define here. There is no soil above it, no rain-bearing sky, no buried river, and no pressure of ordinary geology. The place seems to have been formed all at once: a cavity punched into a dark mineral mass, its walls still holding the sharp, unsoftened edges of their making.

It is not large—perhaps the size of a cathedral nave, perhaps smaller than that, depending on how close one stands to the walls. Perspective behaves poorly in the chamber. The ceiling is low in some places and impossibly high in others, though the floor remains level enough to walk. Every surface is black, brown, or the dull red of heated iron. Fresh planes of stone jut from one another like broken blades. Their faces have not weathered. There are no rounded corners, no sediment, no dust carried in by wind. The chamber looks young, newly emerged from pressure, but it has no sense of becoming older.

Warmth rises from everything.

The floor is a dense pavement of fused mineral plates, each one separated by hairline seams glowing faintly from within. In some places the plates have lifted into low ridges, forming narrow aisles and trenches. In others they have collapsed around vertical vents. These vents do not exhale steam—there is no water to make steam—but release pulses of hot, dry gas that smell of scorched metal, bitter salts, and something animal. The gas shimmers in the dimness. It carries a static charge strong enough to raise the hair and make teeth ache.

The chamber’s only light comes from these seams, from furnace mouths in the walls, and from the pale blue filaments of the machinery scattered through the space. Nothing shines brightly. Illumination gathers close to its source and dies a few steps away. Between the lit places are thick zones of brown-black shadow, not empty but crowded: bodies, pipes, mineral growths, and the overlapping silhouettes of things that have learned to remain still.

The air is loud.

A continuous industrial thunder fills the chamber, produced by no single engine. Banks of piston assemblies pump in the walls. Heavy wheels turn behind grilles. Cables tremble in bundled sheaths, transmitting power or signals or some other pressure for which there is no human term. Every few seconds a metal ram descends somewhere out of sight with a concussion that travels through the floor. The impact does not echo normally. The chamber seems to absorb the first sound and return it from the wrong direction, so that a hammer striking east may be heard directly behind the listener.

Beneath this machinery is the rasping and clicking of fauna.

They are abundant, though they do not fill the chamber in the obvious way. The animals occupy layers. Small, pale scavengers live beneath the mineral plates, their jointed legs tapping in dense swarms against the underside of the floor. Their shells are translucent and threaded with amber nerves. They feed on heat, apparently, gathering around the brightest seams and clustering so tightly that they resemble wet sand—though nothing here is wet.

Larger creatures cling to the walls.

They have flattened bodies and too many gripping limbs, with circular mouths that open and close without visible teeth. Some are the size of hands; others are broad as doors. Their bodies are matte gray, but each bears a narrow strip of living orange along its spine. They do not move much. When the machinery pulses, they all rotate toward it in perfect unison, as though listening through their bones. A few have inserted themselves into the gaps between industrial components. Their limbs have become indistinguishable from brackets, braces, and conduits. It is unclear where the factory ends and the colony begins.

In the darker reaches, there are taller animals. They stand folded against the walls, six or seven feet high, narrow as scaffolds, their heads tucked beneath their chests. Their hides are covered in short mineral needles. They seem blind, but they track movement through changes in vibration. When approached, they do not flee. They unfold slowly, one segment at a time, and produce a deep resonant tone that can be felt in the sternum. The sound is answered from every other part of the chamber.

There is sparse vegetation, though it does not resemble vegetation from the surface.

Thin black fans grow from cracks in the floor, each one no wider than a hand. They are made of flexible carbon-like sheets and open toward the warmest vents. Their edges bear tiny silver hairs that gather static from the air. They do not photosynthesize. They draw energy from the machinery’s electrical leakage and from the chemical difference between the chamber’s gases. Around them, the smallest animals gather in shining knots. The fans never sway except when a machine starts or stops—which never happens.

Nothing changes here.

The engines are locked into one exact rhythm. Their pistons strike at the same intervals, their belts maintain the same tension, their heated cores remain at the same temperature. A damaged gear has been broken in the same position for so long that a new tooth has grown around it, mineralizing the fracture. One of the wall-creatures is caught halfway through a turn, preserved in a posture that is neither stillness nor motion. The gas vents open and close without exhausting themselves. The animals breed, feed, molt, and die, but the population never seems to increase or diminish. Their movements return, eventually, to the same arrangement.

The machinery is scattered rather than organized into a true factory. A drilling tower stands alone near the chamber’s center, its three legs anchored into the floor, its head buried in solid stone. It vibrates constantly but has made no visible progress. Along one wall sits a row of squat furnaces, only two of them active; the others are sealed with smooth plates bearing no symbols. A narrow conveyor emerges from a crack in the rock, travels several yards, and disappears into another crack. It carries small black objects shaped like seeds. None ever fall off. None ever reach a destination anyone can see.

There are occasional humanlike constructions: a ladder ending at chest height, a door set into a wall with no room behind it, a control panel whose switches have been replaced by teeth, a narrow observation booth overlooking nothing. Their surfaces are clean of dust. Their edges are sharp. They do not appear abandoned so much as interrupted.

At the far end of the chamber, a vertical scar divides the rock from floor to ceiling. It is only a few inches wide, but an immense brightness exists behind it—not light that illuminates, only the idea of brightness held under pressure. The scar never opens. The animals avoid it, except for the smallest ones, which disappear into it by the thousands. No sound comes from the other side. Yet whenever the chamber’s hammers strike, the brightness behind the seam briefly takes on the shape of an eye.

The place has rules, though they are not written anywhere.

Heat is a kind of gravity. The creatures drift toward it, and the plants unfold toward it, and the machinery seems to have been arranged by it. Sound has weight and can move bodies. Shadows are occupied spaces. Metal grows if left vibrating long enough. Anything brought into the chamber acquires a faint internal hum, then begins to match the chamber’s rhythm. After a time, its edges become too sharp to touch.

There is no water. No condensation beads on the pipes. No dampness softens the mineral. The animals carry their fluids in sealed sacs, and the black fans exude a dry crystalline powder rather than moisture. Even the air seems to have been stripped of anything that might flow. Yet the chamber is not barren. It is saturated with life, mechanism, heat, and noise—an enclosed ecosystem packed so densely that every organism is also a surface, a signal, a tool, or a structural component.

From a distance, the whole place resembles the inside of a machine that has mistaken itself for a habitat.

Up close, it feels more like a habitat that has learned to manufacture machines.

And beneath the ceaseless pounding, beneath the clicks and resonances and electrical whine, there is a quieter certainty: the chamber is complete. It has reached the exact form it was always going to have. No stone will round. No vent will cool. No creature will evolve beyond its allotted shape.

Nothing is waiting to happen.

Everything is already happening forever.
