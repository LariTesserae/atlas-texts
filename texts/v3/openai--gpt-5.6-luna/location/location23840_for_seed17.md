The sea lies above the clouds.

It covers a high continent—an immense table of newly fractured stone lifted so far above the surrounding world that the ocean has become a second sky. From the lower atmosphere, where the old weather still moves, the continent appears as a white-edged darkness in the blue: a plateau holding a sea that should have spilled over its margins long ago. It does not spill. The world’s gravity is weak here, or altered, or simply obedient to an older arrangement. Water rests on the high land as though in a bowl without walls.

There is no coast in the ordinary sense. The margins are cliffs miles high, with the ocean running to them in a perfectly level sheet. At the edge, it falls in a continuous curtain—not a waterfall, but a vertical ocean, broad enough to have its own weather. The falling water freezes into translucent veils, breaks into silver dust, and descends through the cloud layers for days before reaching whatever lies below. Its sound fills the world. It is not heard as a roar so much as experienced as a permanent pressure in the bones.

Above the plateau, the atmosphere is thin and intensely clear. Light has a normal, uncolored source—sunlight, perhaps—but the water turns it blue-green before it reaches the depths. The upper ocean is bright enough to show the terrain below: long ridges of black basalt, pale shelves of freshly exposed mineral, and ravines with edges as sharp as broken ceramic. Farther down, the light becomes a diffuse metallic dusk. There is no warm darkness. Even the deepest regions retain a weak, directionless gleam, as though the stone itself remembers the sun.

The sea is not deep by oceanic standards. It is shallow over much of the continent, but the continent is so high and so broad that its shallow water extends beyond curvature, beyond any recognizable horizon. A person standing on a submerged ridge may see the next ridge as a dark line, and then another, and another, until the whole world becomes a system of blue-black folds beneath the surface. The ridges are young. They have not been rounded by age or softened by life. Their faces are splintered, glassy, and raw, with vents and fault-lines opening like cuts in cooling metal.

Nothing grows.

There are no kelp forests, no moss on the stone, no drifting green haze. The mineral surfaces remain naked. In places, colorless films cling to the rock—not plants, not quite organisms, perhaps only colonies of chemistry that feed on pressure and electric difference. They are too thin to alter the landscape. The sea belongs to stone, water, and animals.

The animals are everywhere and almost nowhere.

Across the vast open water, the distances between individual creatures are enormous. A single pale swimmer may pass for hours beneath a survey vessel without another sign of life. Then the vessel enters one of the migration lanes, and the emptiness breaks open. Thousands of animals move through the water in precise, silent formations: long-bodied things with jointed fins; translucent shapes like folded sails; black, many-eyed forms that maintain a constant depth above the seabed. They do not school so much as occupy coordinates. Each follows an unchanging route through the sea, and each route intersects others without collision.

Their abundance is statistical, not intimate. There are many of them because the sea is vast. They do not crowd one another. They leave miles of water between bodies, as if every creature carries an invisible territory of pressure.

Some are enormous. Slow white masses drift over the ridges, their undersides lined with sensory organs that glow faintly when they pass above a vent. Their calls are too low to be heard as sound, yet the water trembles around them. Others are small and metallic, no larger than a hand, appearing in vast bands that flash once beneath the surface and vanish. There are predatory shapes, but predation is rare. The ecosystem has settled into a strange economy in which most creatures feed on dissolved minerals, pressure gradients, electrical discharge, or on the shed tissues of larger animals. The few that hunt do so at fixed intersections, waiting in places that never cease to be productive.

The water is deafening.

The permanent fall at the plateau’s rim supplies the first layer: a continent-wide thunder, continuous and without rhythm. Beneath it lies the sound of the sea itself, amplified by the high basin and its hard boundaries. Currents pass through trenches with the force of engines. The animals add pulses, clicks, infrasonic groans, and sudden detonations of compressed water. Every exposed structure rings. Every cable vibrates. Speech is useless outside sealed chambers; even inside them, voices arrive through the walls as a faint tremor rather than a sound.

Yet the noise never changes.

There are no storms. No tides. No seasons. No weather fronts travel across the water. The falling ocean maintains the same volume, the same width, the same white descent. Currents circle in permanent loops. The animals follow routes that may have been laid down before the plateau rose. Even the cracks in the young stone seem arrested at the instant of their formation. Water enters them, flashes to vapor under pressure, and returns through neighboring vents in a cycle that never develops into erosion.

The world is not still. It is locked.

A current can move with incredible force and never alter its channel. A creature can swim for centuries and never reach a new region. A fracture can remain bright and open without widening. The landscape possesses motion without history.

Scattered across the submerged ridges are the remains of industry.

They stand far apart—sometimes hundreds of miles between one site and the next—so that each looks like a solitary accident. A drilling tower rises from a shelf of basalt, its upper levels sealed behind thick glass and its lower machinery disappearing into the blue. A line of pylons crosses a trench, each tower leaning at the same angle, connected by cables that hum with stored electricity. Here and there are cylindrical factories anchored to the seabed, their hulls furred with mineral deposits but still functioning. Pumps draw water from the rock and return it warmer by a fraction too small to matter. Turbines turn in currents that have not changed since they were installed.

There are no roads between the facilities. No towns. No visible workers. The buildings were made for scale, but the world supplied only distance. Their windows shine with steady interior light. Their doors open and close according to schedules no living visitor could understand. Conveyor belts move ore from one chamber to another. Robotic arms perform repetitions whose purpose has outlasted every maker. Some structures have been broken cleanly in half by the rising stone, while others are embedded in it as if the plateau grew around them.

The machines do not dominate the landscape. They are too sparse for that. They appear instead as punctuation marks in an enormous sentence written in water and rock.

At high points, the ocean thins over the ridges and sunlight reaches the bottom. Here the stone is white, blue, and black, scored by fresh channels. The water presses down with a visible distortion, making distant structures waver and bend. At low points, the sea darkens, and the fauna become more numerous. Their bodies pass beneath the observer like fragments of night, each maintaining its appointed course.

The absence of plants makes every surface legible. There is nowhere for a thing to hide except in shadow, depth, or the bodies of the water itself. A dropped tool may remain visible for a thousand years on a clean slope. A footprint in sediment may never be covered. A dead animal slowly mineralizes where it falls, becoming a pale ridge among the sharper natural ridges.

At the rim, the world becomes impossible to measure. The ocean runs toward the edge in a broad, unbroken plane. The horizon is a straight line of light. Beyond it, the water falls upward or downward depending on where one stands, and the distinction loses meaning. The curtain catches the sun and divides the sky into moving columns. Creatures migrate through it without hesitation, passing from the level sea into open air and then vanishing into cloud, as though water and atmosphere are merely neighboring densities of the same medium.

Nothing here is being born. Nothing is wearing away. The plateau, the sea, the machines, and the animals have entered an equilibrium so complete that even catastrophe has become a permanent condition.

The landscape is young in shape and ancient in behavior: a freshly broken world that has already decided never to change.
