The place is called the **Green Aperture**, though there is no aperture and nothing green in the ordinary sense of the word.

It is a small high basin, no more than a few hundred paces across, cupped in the summit of a dark plateau. The plateau rises above a plain of pale mineral flats, though the basin itself is hidden from them by a rim of stone and thorn. From outside, it appears to be a shallow, weathered bowl of charcoal rock. Inside, it is unexpectedly crowded with life.

There is no water here.

The stone is dry enough to split the lips. Dust lies in the cracks like flour. The shallow depressions that might once have held rain are filled instead with fine metallic powder, and when the wind crosses them they give off brief blue sparks. Nothing pools. Nothing drips. Even the black, glossy surfaces of the rocks are not wet; they are vitrified mineral, polished by centuries of abrasive dust.

Yet the basin is lush.

Its vegetation grows in dense, isolated colonies around seams in the rock. Tall fronds rise in fans, their blades translucent and jointed like the wings of enormous insects. They are colored in deep greens, bruised violets, and a yellow that seems to contain its own dim light. Some plants are rooted in the ground. Others cling to the stone by hundreds of pale gripping filaments and hang outward over empty space, their roots exposed and busy as the legs of spiders.

They do not drink. They feed on the basin’s exhalations.

At irregular intervals, the ground releases a warm breath of mineral gas through vents no wider than a finger. The gas is invisible at first, but wherever it passes through the vegetation, leaves unfold and flowers open with violent speed. Their petals are not soft. They are thin plates of amber, mica, or horn, and they snap open with the sound of small doors. The plants draw nourishment from the gas, from the electrical charge in the rocks, and from the bodies of things that die among their roots. In the hottest seams, they grow so quickly that a bare crack can become a thicket between morning and dusk.

The air is warm, thin, and constantly in motion. It smells of hot stone, crushed leaves, ozone, and something sweetly rotten. The dimness is not caused by cloud. The sky above the basin is usually clear, a hard, dark blue that fades almost to black at the zenith. The light comes weakly from a sun that never seems to rise fully, as though the world has been placed at the far end of a long shadow. Along the western rim, however, the rock gives off a faint internal glow. It is enough to outline leaves and moving bodies, not enough to soften any darkness.

The shadows are thick and occupied.

Sound is the first thing a visitor notices. The basin is loud enough to feel in the ribs.

The vegetation clicks and chatters as it grows. Long seed-pods knock together in the warm currents. Hollow stems whistle at different pitches, and entire groves can begin to roar when the vents below them open. The ground gives occasional deep reports as hidden chambers collapse or expand. Over everything comes the fauna: shrieking, drumming, rattling, barking, buzzing, and the wetless scraping of claws over stone.

The animals are abundant but widely spaced, each presence separated from the next by sudden pockets of emptiness. A creature may leap from a thicket, cross half the basin, and vanish beneath a slab without ever being joined by another of its kind. There are no herds. There are swarms, but they occupy only brief, precise moments.

The smallest animals resemble pale beetles made from folded skin and mineral splinters. They travel in spirals around the gas vents, striking the ground with their abdomens. Their collective drumming causes dormant plants to open. Larger things hunt them: six-legged runners with long, flexible necks and mouths like shears; squat burrowers whose backs are covered in living moss; and tall, thin grazers that move as if blown by wind, though the air is still around them. Their bodies are almost weightless, supported by internal sacs that fill with the basin’s gases. When startled, they rise several feet into the air and drift sideways, limbs dangling, until they strike another plant or rock.

There are also things that do not appear to belong to the landscape’s ordinary ecology.

At the northern edge stands a cluster of black columns. They are not ruins, exactly. They may have grown there. Their surfaces are carved with shallow grooves, and the grooves are occupied by small white animals that resemble ears without bodies. Whenever someone speaks near the columns, the animals repeat the voice several seconds later from different heights. A whisper may come back from the ground. A shout may answer from the sky.

Near the center of the basin is a settlement of five structures.

They are made from slabs of local stone, lashed together with braided plant fiber and held upright by the curved ribs of some enormous dead animal. The roofs are low and angled. No doorway is large enough for a tall person to enter comfortably. Outside each structure hangs a line of hollow seed-cases, bone fragments, and polished mineral disks. The wind moves them constantly, making a rough, uneven music.

The inhabitants—if they still live here—have built their lives around the basin’s cycles. Their tools are stone blades, digging sticks, hooked poles, bone needles, and vessels made from the hardened skins of gas bladders. They collect shed plates from the plants, trap small animals in woven funnels, and chip the resonant black stone into knives that hum when held. Their structures are scattered rather than fortified. There is no central fire, because flame behaves strangely in the thin, mineral-rich air: it burns blue, climbs downward, and sometimes ignites the shadows beneath a person.

The rules of the place are not written, but they are strict.

Do not stand on a vent when the ground begins to sing.

Do not cut a flowering plant after dark; its roots will follow the blade.

Do not answer the columns.

Do not sleep beneath the hanging gardens. The plants release spores at irregular intervals, and the spores do not settle on the body. They settle on the body’s shadow.

The basin is old and, in most ways, settled. Its cliffs are rounded by long abrasion. Its paths have been worn into the stone by generations of feet and claws. The colonies of plants occupy the same seams they occupied long ago. The scattered dwellings lean, patched and repatched, but do not collapse. Every species seems to know its boundaries.

And yet the whole place is turbulent.

The vents open without warning. A stand of vegetation can burst into growth, split the rock beneath it, and topple itself within an hour. A flock of wingless, airborne animals may pour over the rim in silence, then fill the basin with a storm of cries. Sections of the ground shift a few inches at a time, not through earthquake but through slow, restless rearrangement. The plants react. The animals react to the plants. The mineral dust rises, catches the weak light, and turns the air into a dim, glittering violence.

Sometimes the entire Green Aperture seems to inhale.

Every frond bends toward the same direction. Every creature stops. The hanging ornaments outside the dwellings swing inward. The black columns begin to hum.

Then the hidden vents beneath the basin open together.

A wave of warm, invisible gas crosses the floor. Leaves unfold. Burrowers erupt from the ground. Pale beetles rise in clouds. The tall grazers lift into the air. The stone flashes with blue fire, and the sound becomes so immense that it is impossible to distinguish thunder from animal cries, rockfall from singing, or the living from the dead.

Afterward, the basin is changed only slightly.

A new thicket stands where there was bare ground. One of the old structures has fallen inward. A fresh crack runs along the southern rim. The animals have redistributed themselves. The dust has settled into a different pattern.

The place remains balanced, but not still.

It is a garden that has never learned gentleness, flourishing in the absence of water through heat, pressure, mineral breath, and ceaseless appetite.
