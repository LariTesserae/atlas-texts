The basin is no larger than a village, though it sits at an altitude where the horizon ought to be immense.

Instead, the land has folded inward around itself. A ring of black, newly raised rock encloses a shallow hollow perhaps half a kilometer across, its walls sharp as broken pottery. Beyond them, the world falls away into white cloud. Peaks appear and disappear in the vapor at eye level, but the basin remains above the weather, receiving the sun with a fierce, nearly vertical heat.

The rock is young. It has not yet learned the softness of age.

Its surfaces are dark basalt, blue-black and faintly metallic, split into clean planes and narrow blades. In places, the stone has cooled around bubbles of glass, leaving translucent blisters underfoot. Elsewhere, pale seams run through it like frozen lightning. Water leaks from these seams constantly—not in springs, but in fine, silver threads that gather on the rock, tremble, and sometimes climb briefly uphill before spilling into shallow channels.

There has been rain recently, though the sky is clear.

The air is hot enough to make the wet stone steam. A low mist hangs in the hollows, not thick enough to obscure anything, only enough to give every object a second outline. Condensation forms on the shaded faces of rocks and on the undersides of leaves. Droplets gather in midair around certain black stones, suspended in loose beads, then fall all at once when a shadow crosses them.

The vegetation is scattered rather than abundant. It occupies cracks, ledges, and the dark edges of watercourses, leaving broad expanses of bare stone between each green presence. Most plants are low: mats of waxy leaves, red-brown reeds, small ferns with translucent stems. Their colors are intensified by the heat—copper, blue-green, bruised violet. Some grow in perfect circles around warm vents, while others have no visible roots and drift a few centimeters above the ground, held there by a faint pressure that lifts dust and loose seeds beneath them.

At the basin’s lowest point lies a pool the size of a small house. It is not deep, but its bottom cannot be seen. The water is perfectly still except when something moves below it. Now and then, a pale shape passes underneath, too slow to be a fish and too deliberate to be a current. The pool has no inlet. Its level rises and falls with the position of the sun, though not in any recognizable tidal rhythm. At noon, it is nearly full. By late afternoon, a broad shelf of damp black stone has emerged around it, covered in tiny shells that open and close without making a sound.

There is little animal life. A few long-legged insects hover over the reeds, their bodies divided into bright segments that turn dark when they enter shadow. They do not seem to fly so much as choose successive points in the air and appear between them. Small, glassy creatures cling to the wet stone—perhaps mollusks, perhaps something that only resembles them. They leave behind trails of dry stone rather than slime.

Once, from somewhere beyond the ring wall, comes the cry of a bird.

The sound arrives before the bird does. The empty air gives a soft metallic shiver, and then a narrow, black-winged animal drops from the clouds, passes over the basin, and vanishes behind a ridge. Its shadow remains for several seconds afterward, moving independently across the rock. No other animals respond.

The basin is mostly quiet. Water ticks into channels. Steam sighs from fissures. The reeds rub together in the hot wind with a sound like paper being folded very slowly. Far below, hidden by the cloud layer, there is an occasional dull concussion—perhaps rockfall, perhaps thunder, perhaps machinery operating in the depths of the mountain. The sound is so distant that it is felt through the soles before it is heard.

Scattered around the hollow are structures that do not quite qualify as ruins.

One is a narrow tower planted at an angle in the basalt, no more than ten meters high. It has no visible joints or openings. Its surface is the color of wet ash, and faint lines move beneath it in slow, branching patterns. The tower leans toward the sun but casts a shadow toward whatever object is nearest, regardless of the light. When clouds pass far below, the lines inside it brighten, as though the tower is listening to weather that cannot reach it.

A second structure rests beside the pool: a low frame of pale material, assembled from three arches and a floor that never touches the ground. It resembles a shelter, though it provides neither shade nor cover. The space inside it is always a few degrees cooler. Water droplets suspended nearby bend toward the arches and arrange themselves into brief geometric lattices. If someone speaks within the frame, the structure stores the voice and releases it several minutes later from a different direction.

There are no roads, but there are marks. Thin silver tracks cross the basin in straight lines, disappearing under vegetation and reappearing on the other side. They are not grooves. They are regions where the stone has been persuaded to remain slightly warmer than its surroundings. A foot placed on one will trigger a faint pulse beneath the skin, as if the ground has recognized a weight it remembers.

The strongest rule of the place concerns distance.

Objects remain where they are, but their relationships do not. A rock near the pool may be reached in ten steps from one direction and fifty from another. A person walking away from the leaning tower can see it grow larger. The basin is small enough to cross in minutes, yet certain points refuse to approach each other. The water, the tower, and the pale arches form a triangle whose three sides can never be traversed in the same amount of time.

This does not feel hostile. It feels unfinished.

The land is still assembling its laws. Fresh fractures widen and seal in the same hour. A sheet of mineral grows over a puddle, then dissolves when the puddle reflects the sky. Plants shift their roots during the heat of the day, searching not for water but for changes in gravity. At dusk, when the sun reaches the rim of the basin, the black rock gives off a brief green radiance, and every living thing becomes sharply outlined.

For a few minutes, the pool rises into the air.

It lifts as a single trembling column, held between the ground and the open sky. The water does not spill. Inside it, the pale submerged shape becomes visible: long, jointed, and full of lights like distant windows. The creature—or mechanism—turns once in the suspended water, then descends with it. The pool settles. The lights vanish.

Night comes quickly at this elevation. Heat remains trapped in the stone, while the air above cools and fills with stars. The stars are unusually bright, but some are too low and too large, hanging just beyond the cloud bank like illuminated structures. During the night, the scattered plants close their leaves, the silver tracks go dark, and the leaning tower begins to emit a low vibration that can be felt more clearly than heard.

By morning, new water will be seeping from the rock.

A new crack may have opened in the basin wall. A reed may have moved several meters. One of the arches may have acquired a fourth side.

The place is small, sparse, and almost calm—but nothing in it is finished being what it is.
