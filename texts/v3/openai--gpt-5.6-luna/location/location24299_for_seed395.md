The plain begins where the horizon has not yet learned to become a line.

It is low and broad, almost level for hundreds of miles, a continent-sized sheet of warm mineral under a pale, ordinary sun. There are no rivers. No lakes shine between the rises, no rain darkens the ground, no wetness gathers in the roots. The surface is made of pale ochre clay, exposed basalt, and broad fields of salt-colored crystal that are not salt exactly, but the dried structural matter of the world: powdery, fibrous, faintly warm to the touch. In places it has folded upward into low ridges, as though the land were still being pressed out of a mold.

Nothing here has finished hardening.

The ground cracks without breaking. Ridges sag and rise again. Fresh mineral terraces spread in slow, almost imperceptible fans from shallow depressions, not deposited by water but extruded from the earth in sheets. Their edges are sharp and translucent, like the layered growth of a shell. The continent is still assembling itself. Every slope is provisional. A hill may be no more than a wrinkle in the planetary crust, a broad pressure-wave paused in its movement.

Yet the plain is crowded.

Vegetation covers it in a dense, low, green-gold growth. The plants do not drink. They draw nourishment from the air, from the mineral beneath them, and from the dim chemical warmth that rises through the ground. Their roots are not roots in the familiar sense, but branching contact-organs spread flat beneath the surface, gripping crystals and extracting the elements from them. They form mats, fans, cushions, and tightly packed groves. Some stand upright in stalks as tall as houses, their leaves arranged like overlapping blades of bronze and green. Others grow as dark, glossy ropes that knot themselves around every projection of stone.

There is little bare land. Even the open ground is occupied by thin films of lichen-like growth, by tiny mineral flowers, by pale filaments that tremble when the air changes. The vegetation has no need to leave space between itself. It grows into every vacancy, though it does so quietly, with an almost architectural patience.

Across the plain stand the settlements.

They are not constructed from quarried stone or shaped by tools. They have grown from the same biological mineralization as the plants, but under the repeated pressure of generations. Their walls are ribbed, vaulted, and warm-colored: ochre, cream, rust, and dark green. A settlement may cover twenty square miles, spreading outward in concentric terraces, then narrowing into towers and clustered chambers at its center. The buildings resemble termite cities imagined by a patient geologist—enormous, seamless, and full of openings too small to see into.

There are streets, though no one laid them down. The inhabitants have walked the same routes until the vegetation withdrew and the ground compacted beneath them. There are plazas where several root-mats have ceased growing, leaving smooth mineral floors. There are bridges grown between towers, shaded passages beneath arched leaves, and tall communal chambers with open roofs through which the sunlight falls in pale columns.

Nothing is made. Everything is cultivated.

The city and the landscape are not separate. Walls extend into groves. Groves press through walls. A residence may be a hollow in a living buttress, sealed by a curtain of hanging leaves. A public way may become a tunnel simply because a colony of shade plants has grown over it. The oldest settlements have the density of forests and the order of cities, with thousands of chambers layered over one another and narrow routes threading between them.

There is no metal, no glass, no wheel, no visible device. The only infrastructure is living tissue: load-bearing stems, mineralized membranes, root-like conduits carrying warmth and chemical vapors, and immense pale fronds that open and close to regulate the temperature of whole districts. In the hottest part of the day they furl themselves, lowering the streets into a green-brown half-light. Later they unfold again, exposing their undersides to the air.

The population is abundant but rarely seen all at once.

Small six-legged grazers move beneath the vegetation, their bodies covered in overlapping plates that match the colors of the soil. They scrape mineral growth from the ground with blunt mouths and leave behind faint, orderly furrows. Long-bodied animals climb the walls and live among the leaves, feeding on the sweet resins exuded by the larger plants. There are burrowing animals whose passage makes the surface pulse in slow waves, and high, delicate creatures that drift between towers on membranes of skin, carried by the warm updrafts.

Predators are present, though the plain does not announce them. They wait in the shaded recesses of the built groves, nearly motionless, or move in sudden, coordinated bursts through the vegetation. A whole bank of leaves may shiver and settle. A flock—or something like a flock—may lift from a roof in absolute silence, turning together beneath the sun before vanishing into the green.

The silence is one of the first things noticed.

There is no water to run, and therefore no constant murmur of channels, falls, or surf. The plants have no broad, wet leaves to slap against one another. The animals are soft-footed, and the mineral ground absorbs their movement. From a distance, the settlements seem abandoned.

But the quiet is full of small sounds. Dry stems click against one another. Seed-cases open with tiny snaps. The walls make faint settling noises as they grow. Beneath the plazas, something scratches through the compacted earth. In the high groves, suspended pods knock together at long intervals, each note separated by several breaths of silence. When the weather shifts, the entire plain gives a low, almost inaudible creak, as if the continent were flexing in its sleep.

The air is warm and clean, with a faint taste of chalk and green resin. It moves in broad, slow currents across the continent. There are weather systems here, though not clouds in the usual sense. Vast translucent veils of dust and mineral spores form far beyond the settlements, crossing the plain like pale fronts. They dim the sun without obscuring it. When they arrive, the vegetation closes its surfaces and the animals retreat into chambers. Fine particles settle over the roads and roofs, then are drawn into the plants by capillary organs that reach upward from every wall and leaf.

At other times the air becomes charged with dry heat. The surface brightens. The mineral terraces expand with soft, granular sighs, and new ridges begin to rise in the open spaces beyond the city. The inhabitants do not need to flee these changes; the settlements have grown around them, incorporating each new pressure and fracture. A district may be half buried by a fresh fold of ground, its upper chambers becoming caves. Another may be lifted on a slow swelling of rock, its streets tilting by degrees over a century.

The world is active everywhere, but rarely violent.

Growth is the dominant force. Plants thicken the roads. Animals open paths through plants. The earth pushes up beneath both. Walls mineralize, crack, and send out new living buttresses. Old towers lean into one another until they become a single vaulted structure. A settlement is not preserved against time; it is continuously absorbed by it.

At the edge of the inhabited plain, the architecture thins but never quite ends. There are isolated shelters, then groves without walls, then fields of low vegetation climbing over raw crystal. Beyond them, the young land extends toward a horizon blurred by warmth. The surface is pale and unfinished there, scored with shallow folds and dotted with the first dark clusters of life.

From a high place—though there are few true heights—the city appears less like a city than a dense patch of organized ecology, a vast green-brown organism spread across the mineral continent. It has no smoke, no machinery, no signal fires. Its avenues do not gleam. Its towers do not glitter. Under normal sunlight, everything is matte, tactile, and close-grown.

And beyond it, in every direction, the plain continues making itself.
