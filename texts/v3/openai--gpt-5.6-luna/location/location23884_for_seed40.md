The country is called the **Stillworks**, though no one remembers what it once made.

It occupies a high basin between mountains that are not quite mountains. They rise in long, blunt tiers, each tier an enormous engineered shelf, their faces plated in pale concrete, glazed ceramic, and sheets of corrugated metal gone white with age. Beyond them, the horizon is open and severe: distant ridges, transmission towers, suspended roads, and the faint verticals of other works disappearing into cold brightness.

There is no natural ground.

Every surface has been poured, bolted, tiled, riveted, or grown into place. The basin floor is a continuous industrial plain, sloping almost imperceptibly toward a central depression. Its material changes by district. Here it is gray concrete crazed with hairline seams; there it is black rubber, softened and folded by ancient heat; farther on, great fields of interlocking ceramic plates have buckled upward into low, sharp waves. Rails emerge from the floor and vanish into it again. Staircases lead to doors set in blank walls. Pipes cross one another at knee height, shoulder height, and far overhead, making a dense second terrain through which passage is possible only by memory.

The original geometry has not failed so much as continued.

Walls lean toward one another without falling. Floors climb in slow spirals. A factory tower, seen from a distance, may be several kilometers away; when approached, it becomes a narrow building at the edge of the same yard. Corridors occasionally open onto the sky, though no one can determine whether they are rooftops or interruptions in the structure. Some stairwells descend for hours and arrive at a level higher than the one they left.

The works are cold, but not frozen. A continuous chill lies over everything, the kind found inside a vast machine room before the machinery has been switched on. The air smells of wet stone, iron, old insulation, and a faint mineral sweetness like snowmelt running over fresh-cut glass. Moisture comes in episodes. For weeks, the sky may remain clear and intensely bright, the surfaces drying to a brittle powder. Then a veil of condensation gathers on every object at once. Beads appear on bolts, windows, cables, and the backs of signs. Water wells in the grooves between plates and flows in narrow, perfectly straight streams toward the basin.

Rain falls only in certain places.

It may descend in a square ten meters wide while the surrounding yard remains dry. It may fall upward beneath a bridge, or hang motionless in the air as a transparent vertical curtain. In the lower districts, the gutters are deep enough to walk through. They contain a slow, dark water that reflects not the structures overhead but places elsewhere in the Stillworks: a lit platform, a red door, a field of frost, a room with someone standing in it.

Vegetation is scarce and disciplined. Small gray-green growths occur where they have been permitted by fracture: mats of lichen on concrete seams, thin reeds around condensation drains, pale moss inside unused bearings. Their roots do not enter soil, because there is almost no soil. They thread into insulation, cable housings, and the porous undersides of tiles. Occasionally a whole bank of metal is covered by a fine, velvety growth that resembles frost from a distance. It is warm to the touch and withdraws from shadows.

Most of the visible life is animal.

The fauna has found abundance in the density of the works. There are flocks of long-legged birds nesting in conveyor hoods, their feathers dark and reflective like oil. Their calls are almost inaudible, but their shadows make a dry clicking sound as they cross the ground. Pale mammals with jointed backs move in groups through service tunnels, squeezing between pipes and waiting at crossings for signals that no longer function. They have broad, soft feet and transparent eyelids. At night—though the Stillworks has very little night—they gather beneath maintenance lamps and face the same direction.

There are insects in the warm spaces: silver-bodied things living in switch cabinets, wingless moths that feed on printed labels, and small bright beetles that carry sparks between disconnected terminals. In the reservoirs, something large moves beneath the water. Its passage raises no wave. Once in a while, a smooth back emerges among the concrete pylons, bearing rows of little windows that look briefly lit from within.

The creatures are not wild in the ordinary sense. They observe schedules.

At the beginning of each condensation season, the birds abandon one level and occupy another. The pale mammals cross the basin in single file whenever a distant siren sounds, although the siren has not sounded for centuries. Herds of narrow, antlered animals wait beside inactive gates, sometimes for days, until the gates open. The gates do not open for them. The animals pass through anyway, becoming momentarily flat and dark as ink beneath the threshold.

The Stillworks is bright without seeming warm.

The sky is a hard, luminous blue, often without a visible sun. Light comes from everywhere: from cloud, from the concrete, from the white faces of the mountains. Shadows are clean and thin, and sometimes point in different directions on the same object. Inside the factories, rows of ceiling panels burn with a steady, shadowless radiance. No dust motes cross the beams. Workers’ lamps—where there are workers—are unnecessary.

Far overhead, gantries span the basin like bridges between separate eras of construction. Some carry freight cars that advance one meter every few hours. Others support immense cylindrical vessels wrapped in frost. Their warning lights pulse at such a slow interval that one can mistake them for stars appearing and disappearing behind the daylight.

The factories are still operating, but at a rate too slow for ordinary observation.

Pistons descend once every few months. Presses close and remain shut through several seasons. Belts move so gradually that birds build nests on them, and then wake to find themselves carried hundreds of meters from where they slept. In one district, a line of furnaces produces a single pane of blue glass every twelve years. In another, automated arms sort screws according to a classification no living mind has recovered. The machines do not announce their labor. Their movements are nearly silent, accompanied only by the occasional settling groan of metal far below.

Sound is scarce and therefore immense.

A footstep may travel across the basin and return a minute later from a direction the walker has not faced. Water ticking into a drain can be heard from three buildings away. Wind passes through antenna fields without stirring the air, producing a low vibration in the teeth rather than in the ears. Every few days, somewhere in the distance, a great mechanism completes one small part of its cycle. The sound is a muted impact, more felt than heard. Birds stop moving when it happens. Condensation trembles on the rails.

There are inhabited sections, though habitation appears secondary to production. Rooms have been attached to the sides of factories in layers: dormitories, observation cabins, kitchens, clinics, offices, and rooms with no identifiable function. Their windows face inward toward other walls. Some contain chairs arranged for meetings that last longer than a human lifetime. Some have clocks with hands that rotate only when nobody is looking at them.

The people, if they can be called people, wear pale insulated clothing and move through the works with quiet confidence. They do not seem surprised by the distortions of distance. They know which doors are solid today, which staircases will lead downward, which pools of water are actual water. They leave offerings at certain junctions: lengths of copper wire, handfuls of salt, folded diagrams, small warm stones brought from somewhere outside the basin.

No one builds anything new. Yet new structures appear.

A retaining wall may be longer in the morning. A fresh flight of stairs may descend from the side of a tower, its metal still bright and sharp. A set of factory windows may reveal rooms that did not exist the day before. The materials are clean-edged, recently manufactured, and free of corrosion. They are not ruins being uncovered. They are additions being made by a process that has forgotten its purpose but retained its habits.

This is why the landscape feels young despite its age. The concrete has weathered, but the breaks are sharp. The towers are ancient, but their newest surfaces gleam. Erosion occurs as emergence: pressure from below, expansion from within, structures forcing themselves into the open. Where a natural cliff might crumble, the Stillworks extrudes a platform. Where a river might cut a valley, an endless drainage channel appears, lined with fresh white tile.

At the center of the basin stands the largest structure, a ring-shaped plant surrounding a hollow several kilometers across. Its outer walls are crowded with pipes, elevators, ladders, windows, vents, and sealed entrances. Inside the ring, the ground falls away in concentric levels. Each level contains a different weather. One has constant mist. One is dry and bright. One is covered in frost. On the lowest visible tier, rain falls upward from a black reservoir into a ceiling of clouds.

The hollow is too large to see across clearly. Its opposite side shimmers, not from heat but from uncertainty. Sometimes the ring appears complete. Sometimes a section is missing, exposing a distant landscape of brown grass and low hills. Sometimes, across the gap, one can see the Stillworks itself from above: the whole basin reduced to a diagram of pale surfaces, moving lights, and slow black waterways.

There is a rule here, though it is not written anywhere:

**Nothing may remain exactly where it was found.**

A tool left on a step will be incorporated into the step. A sleeping animal will wake in another room. A pool left undisturbed will deepen into a shaft. A person who stands motionless too long becomes difficult to distinguish from the surrounding machinery—not transformed, exactly, but assigned a function. Their outline acquires hinges, vents, access panels. Their thoughts continue in the form of indicator lights.

Movement prevents this, but hurried movement is dangerous. The Stillworks favors patience. Doors open for those who wait. Bridges align after several hours. The safest route is the one that seems to be going nowhere.

So people cross the open basin slowly beneath the white light, between the silent rails and the breathing towers. Birds pass through the bright air. Water gathers and drains. Machinery completes its nearly invisible work. Across the high, manufactured country, every surface is occupied by some presence—animal, mechanical, architectural, or uncertain—yet the whole place remains hushed, as if the world is holding its breath for the next small adjustment.
