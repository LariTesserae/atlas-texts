The city occupies the inside of a mountain that has not finished becoming a mountain.

It rises above a broad, pale horizon in terraces of black stone and rust-red mineral, each terrace too level to be natural and too rough to be called finished. The elevation is extreme: the upper districts sit above the cloud layer, where the air is thin, hot, and faintly metallic in the throat. Beyond the city, the land falls away in enormous steps toward a lower world hidden by glare. There are no distant trees, no green valleys, no signs of animal habitation. Only unfinished ridges, uplifted shelves, and long planes of stone still bearing the smooth, swollen surfaces of their formation.

The mountain is young in the geological sense, though it may be older than any living memory. Its slopes have not yet been carved into familiar shapes. Erosion has begun only in places: a few narrow channels cut into the rock, some shallow gullies around foundations, a succession of hairline fractures where heat and moisture have worried at the surface. Elsewhere, the stone stands as if poured yesterday. Edges remain sharp. Entire escarpments descend in clean vertical faces, and the exposed layers do not crumble so much as slowly loosen, one plate at a time.

The warmth comes from below.

The rock is hot enough that bare metal discolors in the open air, and the streets radiate heat long after the sun has passed over them. Yet moisture is always arriving from somewhere. A narrow band of cloud occasionally rises from the lower atmosphere and catches on the mountain’s upper shelves. Condensation beads along pipes, window frames, cables, and the undersides of bridges. Once or twice in a long day, a brief rain passes over the city—not a storm, but a veil of water that darkens the streets, fills cracks, and vanishes in steaming threads before it can gather into a stream.

The city has been designed around this contradiction: heat without dryness, water without rivers.

Every roof is a collector. Every wall is ribbed with gutters. Condensation towers stand above the districts like skeletal organs, drawing damp air through miles of perforated metal. Their inner surfaces are cold enough to make the water precipitate, and their reservoirs feed the city through sealed conduits. The rain that falls is caught before it reaches the ground. The city wastes nothing. Even the dampness in a worker’s breath is recoverable in the lower ventilation chambers.

From a distance, the settlement resembles a dark mineral reef clinging to the mountain. Its towers are squat and heavily braced, built low against the thin atmosphere and the occasional tremors from below. Factory blocks press against apartment blocks. Railways pass through walls and emerge on roofs. Storage cylinders are stacked in vertical fields. Gantries cross the streets at several levels, carrying pipes, electric lines, ore carts, and enclosed pedestrian tubes. There is no clean boundary between infrastructure and architecture. A residence may be built around a boiler. A transit station may contain a furnace. A public square may be the roof of a reservoir and the floor of a machine hall.

Everything is close to something else.

The lower districts are dense with heat exchangers, drilling rigs, slag presses, ceramic kilns, and plants for separating the mountain’s fluids into useful components. Their chimneys do not produce great clouds. The air is too thin and the factories too efficient for that. Instead, they exhale nearly invisible plumes that distort the view and make the far buildings shiver. At night, faint orange light leaks from vents and inspection seams. The machinery works continuously, but quietly by necessity. The city’s industrial rhythm is a low vibration passed through stone: a steady pressure in the soles of the feet, more felt than heard.

The principal sound is not noise but restraint.

Fans turn behind insulated walls. Pumps click at long intervals. Rails hum when a load is moving somewhere above the streets. Deep in the mountain, large mechanisms engage with a measured succession of metallic knocks, each one separated from the next by several seconds. The wind makes almost no song. It crosses the exposed ridges in thin, dry currents and slides over the buildings without finding leaves, grass, or loose forest to disturb.

There is no vegetation.

Nothing grows in the cracks. No moss darkens the shaded foundations. No lichen marks the old walls. The city’s damp surfaces remain bare, filmed only with mineral residue. Water leaves white fans of salt wherever it evaporates, and orange blooms of iron stain the drainage channels. In the warmest places, the stone develops a dull, glassy skin. In the coolest, it is coated with a translucent crust that trembles when touched and breaks into flakes like thin ice, though it is hot enough to burn.

The absence of plants is not experienced as emptiness so much as a strict local law. Seeds brought from elsewhere become inert. Organic material dries, chars, or mineralizes within hours. The city imports food in sealed cartridges and manufactures most of what it needs from mineral substrates, chemical slurries, and compressed atmospheric compounds. There are no gardens, not even ceremonial ones. In a few interior courtyards, people maintain arrangements of colored glass, wire, and polished stone where other cities might keep trees.

Animal life is rare and usually industrially entangled. Small pale things have been seen near the condensation works—jointed, soft-bodied creatures that emerge only during the brief rains and retreat into warm conduits afterward. They leave no tracks that last more than a few minutes. Some workers say the creatures are native. Others say they are leaks from an old process, something produced accidentally by the city’s chemistry. No one has found a nest, a carcass, or a breeding pair.

Large fauna are absent. The city’s only moving multitudes are machines, vehicles, cables, doors, elevators, and people.

People occupy the upper terraces in stacked neighborhoods connected by inclined lifts. Their windows are narrow, pressure-sealed, and often fogged from within. Because the city is built on a steep face, there are few horizontal streets. Most routes climb, descend, or pass through the mountain. An address is given not by district alone but by elevation, pressure, and heat: a place might be described as “above the third condenser, two levels below the red rail, where the air burns less.”

At the highest inhabited rim, the buildings thin out but do not end. There are observation decks, antenna fields, and old construction platforms extending over the drop. From these places, the whole region can be seen when the air is clear. The city spreads across the mountain in bands and knots, connected by bridges so heavily loaded with pipes that they appear to be sagging under the weight. Beyond it lie the neighboring peaks: not a range in the familiar sense, but a series of broad, newly raised forms, their surfaces still smooth and unweathered. Some bear distant lights. Some are entirely dark. One, far to the east, slowly increases in height over the course of generations.

The world is still building itself.

This is visible in the quarries, where extraction is not merely the removal of material but the revelation of structures that seem to have been waiting inside the rock. Miners cut into a wall and find vertical chambers, perfectly cylindrical, their surfaces warmer than the surrounding stone. Engineers open a seam and uncover a buried rail line running downward into solid ground. A drill may encounter a layer that answers with a faint, delayed pulse, as though the mountain has a circulation of its own.

The city’s industries are partly factories and partly accommodations. They do not simply produce goods; they stabilize the place. Deep pumps relieve pressure beneath the districts. Counterweights slow the mountain’s uplift. Cooling stations keep certain strata from expanding too quickly. Survey towers measure changes so gradual that an individual reading is meaningless, but over decades the numbers describe a landscape shifting under its inhabitants with patient certainty.

Nothing here happens quickly except the briefest events: a pipe rupture, a fall, a flash of rain, the sudden appearance of a creature in a drain. The greater motions are nearly imperceptible. A road tilts by a few centimeters over a century. A foundation settles into a shape no architect predicted. A ridge becomes more pronounced. A ventilation shaft gradually points toward a different star.

The city accepts these changes without alarm. Buildings are made with adjustable joints. Bridges contain slack. Every major structure has a second alignment already marked inside it, an anticipated future position. Doors can be raised. Rails can be re-leveled. Streets are layered over rather than repaired, so that old pavements remain beneath the new ones like geological strata. In some districts, people walk through abandoned streets suspended inside newer buildings, their signs still legible beneath mineral dust.

Light is ordinary here—hard daylight, pale shadow, the occasional amber wash of industrial lamps—but the height alters it. The sky is too close and too empty. Sunlight strikes the raw stone with little softness, while cloud shadows move below the city and never pass over it. During the intermittent rains, the world briefly becomes reflective: black streets, silver roofs, orange walls, wet rails. Then the water disappears, leaving steam and a clean mineral smell.

At dusk, the city does not glitter. It glows in small, controlled places. Windows become squares of muted warmth. Signal lamps mark the rails. Factory doors open and close like slow mechanical breathing. Across the mountain, thousands of conduits carry water, heat, power, and information, but most are hidden behind plated walls. The visible settlement is only the outer skin of a much denser organism—though no one agrees whether the organism is the city, the mountain, or the process by which the two are becoming inseparable.

At the edge of the highest terrace, where construction has stopped for now, a line of survey stakes descends into bare stone. Beyond them is open air, hot and thin, and the immense unfinished slope. Occasionally a droplet gathers on one of the stakes. It trembles in the wind, reflecting the city upside down.

Then, over the course of several minutes, it runs downward and vanishes into the new rock.
