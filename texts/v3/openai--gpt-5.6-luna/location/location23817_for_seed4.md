The city exists inside a hollow stem.

It is not a great stem, not a trunk or a vast green cathedral, but one narrow chamber in the body of a plant no taller than a house. From outside, the plant appears as a low, pale reed emerging from a flooded basin, its leaves folded close against the cold. Its surface is ridged with fresh growth, still sharp and soft-edged, as though it pushed itself out of the mud only recently. Rain gathers in the grooves. Water beads beneath the leaves and falls upward again in the plant’s slow breathing.

Inside, there is room for a city.

The hollow is no wider than a small room, but its walls rise beyond sight. Darkness makes height out of everything. The settlement clings to the interior in scattered shelves, ladders, bridges, and galleries, all fashioned from living matter: braided roots, dried tendrils, split seed husks, the translucent ribs of leaves. Nothing is truly built. Every wall has been persuaded to grow in a direction. Every stair is a root that thickened where feet passed. Every doorway is a wound trained open and lined with white, fibrous tissue.

The people have not cut the plant so much as taught it to hold them.

Their dwellings are seed-pods wedged between ribs of the stem. They are small, dark, and sealed with waxy leaf membranes. At night, which is nearly all the time inside, a household opens its pod by peeling back a living flap. The flap closes behind them with a faint muscular shiver. There are no lamps. Fire is unknown or useless here; the air is damp, and the plant dislikes flame. Instead, the inhabitants navigate by touch, by the pale coolness of root-veins beneath their fingers, and by the brief blue glimmer of certain beetles that nest in the walls.

The city is sparse. Long stretches of the hollow are empty, wet, and black. A bridge may lead for several body-lengths to a platform where three people sit beneath a hood of leaves, and then continue no farther. A market might consist of a single basket hanging in the dark: moss cakes, fungus eggs, strips of bitter inner bark. A stair can descend into a pool and vanish. The settlements are organized, but not crowded. Their order is the order of a plant: spaced according to access to water, warmth, and light, with large silences between necessities.

Water is everywhere, though rarely in motion that can be seen. It presses through the walls in dark beads. It hangs in strings from the ceiling. It gathers on the floors and slowly finds cracks to descend through. At the lowest level, a shallow black stream runs around the inner circumference of the stem, passing under roots and beneath houses. It is cold enough to numb the hand. When the plant drinks from the marsh below, the stream rises. When the plant exhales, it lowers again, leaving a shining line on the walls.

The air smells of crushed leaves, stagnant rain, and the faint iron scent of fresh growth.

The vegetation does not merely surround the city. It is the city’s only enduring material. Moss forms carpets over the platforms, but the moss is cultivated in clear lanes so that people can walk. Vines hang down in loose curtains, each one marked by knots that indicate direction, ownership, or warning. Broad leaves are used as roofs, doors, sails, blankets, and records. A message is not written; it is folded into a leaf’s veins, or tied into the growth of a stem. Children learn to read the plant’s changes before they learn the old hand signs: a tightening tendril means pressure below, a silvering leaf means cold water rising, a sudden flush of red means something has entered the hollow.

Something is always entering the hollow.

The animals are overwhelming, though the city itself is quiet.

They come through cracks and breathing pores, through the soft roots at the base, through holes made by worms and enlarged by generations of use. Most are small enough to hide in a palm, but there are so many that the darkness seems populous. Pale mites travel in shining continents along the wall. Long-legged water insects stand on the underground stream, their feet dimpling the surface without breaking it. Blind moths drift through the upper dark, brushing the ceilings with soft wings. They are harmless unless they descend in a mass, in which case they strip a leaf-hut overnight and leave the inhabitants crouched in the bare ribs of their home.

There are predators as well.

Thin, translucent eels live in the wet seams. Their mouths open sideways. They hunt by sensing warmth and will follow a hand for a long time beneath the water. Root-mice move in colonies through the walls, their bodies white, their teeth brown with plant resin. They are eaten by the hook-jawed crickets, which are eaten by the lantern beetles, which are gathered by people when food is scarce. There are squat amphibians with leaf-shaped heads that wait motionless in the pools and swallow anything that casts a shadow. There are furry things that cling upside down above the inhabited shelves, silent until they drop.

The people understand that the plant is not a shelter from the animals. It is a concentration of them. The hollow is a nest, a throat, a wet corridor in an ecosystem that has no concept of human boundaries.

So the city maintains rules.

Do not leave a door-flap open after the second condensation.

Do not touch a root that is warm.

Do not cross the stream when the water is carrying white petals.

Do not answer a voice from above unless you can feel the person’s feet on the bridge.

Never kill a house-beast unless it has entered the sleeping chamber. Dead things attract the larger things, and the larger things remember paths.

The most important rule is never to pierce the outer wall. The stem is young, still forming, and its skin is under tension. A cut might open into the marsh; it might collapse an entire district; it might cause the plant to fold shut around the inhabitants. No one knows what lies beyond the pale green wall except that it is cold, wet, and full of reeds that move when there is no wind.

Sometimes the wall opens on its own.

A fresh seam appears, bright as raw flesh. Water spills in. Marsh-light trembles beyond it—gray, distant, almost imaginary. For a few breaths the city sees the outside world: a low plain under a black sky, pools reflecting nothing, ranks of young plants thrusting from mud with their leaves clenched. Then the seam seals. The inhabitants are left with wet floors and a new direction in the maze.

The plant is growing quickly.

This is why the settlement has no ancient districts. The oldest platforms are already being swallowed by new tissue. Paths narrow, bend, and vanish. A home may become part of a wall. A bridge may thicken into a root. The dead are placed in soft hollows where the plant can absorb them, and sometimes, months later, their hair or fingernails appear in the pale fibers of a new stair.

Nothing is stable except the darkness.

Yet it is not still. The stem contracts with the weather. Rain travels downward in sudden internal floods. Roots flex beneath the floor. Leaves unfurl somewhere high above and make a sound like distant cloth tearing. The animal population shifts in waves: one night the walls are alive with clicking, another night every creature has gone silent, hiding from a predator whose presence is known only by the faint trembling of the platforms.

In those silences, the city hears itself.

Water tapping into water.

The soft rasp of someone sharpening a bone tool.

The breath of sleepers through leaf membranes.

A bridge creaking under the weight of one unseen animal.

Far below, in the black stream, something large turning over.

And above all of it, too deep to be called a sound, the slow wet labor of the plant growing around them.
