The world below the surface is called the **White Green**, though there is almost no green in it.

It is a continent-sized hollow beneath an ordinary-looking crust of stone, a space so large that its far boundaries are not known and perhaps do not exist. The ceiling is lost in brightness. It is not a roof in any familiar sense, but a high, continuous membrane of pale plant tissue stretched across the dark like the underside of a leaf. It shines with a cold, diffuse light—brilliant enough to cast shadows, too even to reveal a sun. The brightness seems to come from within the membrane. Veins cross it in slow-moving patterns, and sometimes whole districts of the ceiling flare blue-white, as though something enormous has turned in sleep.

Below this ceiling lies a nearly empty expanse.

The floor is not level. It consists of new-made ridges, shelves, trenches, and sudden walls of mineral pushed upward through the living growth. The forms are sharp because they have not had time to soften. Fresh basalt stands in black blades. Chalky shelves crumble into powder at their edges. Glassy deposits, formed where ancient heat met plant sap, jut from the ground in translucent planes. Between these features stretch distances so broad that the eye loses all sense of scale. A ridge may be a day’s walk away or a hundred miles away. A distant wall may be stone—or only a fold in the illuminated air.

The air is cold and thin-feeling, though breathable. It carries a permanent wetness without ever becoming humid. Condensation gathers on the surfaces of everything and then vanishes. At irregular intervals, moisture falls from the ceiling in immense, silent curtains: not rain exactly, but the release of water stored in the membrane above. The drops descend for minutes or hours, scattering across the hollows and collecting in narrow channels. For a short time, these channels become rivers. Then the water is drawn away through the roots, swallowed into the floor, or lifted again as a fog that climbs rather than sinks.

There are no ordinary trees here. The vegetation is not an assortment of things growing in the landscape; it is the landscape’s primary material.

The floor is made of root-mats hardened into terraces. Cliffs are trunks, though many are too broad to recognize as trunks, rising like pale escarpments from the plain. Bridges form when tendrils grow toward one another across a gap and fuse. Whole valleys are lined with the ribbed sides of leaves, each rib as thick as a tower. In some places the plant matter has collapsed into fibrous dunes, and in others it has been pulled taut between stone outcrops, creating immense greenish membranes that tremble in the unseen air.

Yet this abundance does not feel crowded. The plants are monumental and widely spaced. There may be a single stalk three miles high in an otherwise empty basin, its upper branches disappearing into the bright ceiling. A solitary vine can run for hundreds of miles before touching another living structure. The distances between these presences are part of the ecosystem. Nothing grows quickly toward its neighbor. Growth proceeds in long, searching gestures, with decades or centuries of empty space between one living mass and the next.

Most of the vegetation is pale: wax-white, blue-green, gray, or the color of old bone. Its tissues contain suspended flecks of light. Some surfaces glow steadily; others pulse only when touched by condensation. The brightest organisms are low-growing carpets that spread over the floor in perfectly circular fields. Seen from a height, these circles resemble lakes. They brighten when moisture falls and dim when it has been absorbed.

The plants do not merely grow. They migrate.

A forest may uproot itself over the course of a single night, dragging its root systems across the mineral floor and leaving behind long, wet furrows. A wall of stalks can bend flat beneath a pressure wave moving through the ground, then rise again in a different shape. There are flowering events that resemble explosions: the sudden opening of thousands of armored blossoms, each releasing a cloud of glowing dust. The dust hangs in the cold for weeks, making distant objects appear to swim.

Other changes are less visible and more consequential. A root network may abandon a region, and within a few days the stone beneath it will begin to crack. A hollow may fill with new growth in a season, or an entire vegetated shelf may rot inward and fall away, leaving a clean cliff with fresh, wet layers exposed. The White Green is young in the geological sense. Its features have the raw edges of things still being made.

The motion is turbulent, but the place is mostly quiet.

There is no constant animal noise, no insect-thrum, no rustle of crowded leaves. Sound travels far across the open spaces and is softened by the plant surfaces. A single falling stone can be heard for several minutes as it strikes distant shelves. The dominant sounds are occasional and enormous: the groan of a root withdrawing from stone; the deep tearing noise of a membrane splitting overhead; the whisper of moisture moving through a billion narrow channels.

Sometimes the whole landscape clicks.

This happens when the ceiling brightens. Across the plains, mineral plates contract, stalks tighten, and the roots make small, hard sounds like knuckles being bent. The clicking passes outward in waves. Then, often without warning, one of the great plants begins to shed itself. Its outer skin peels away in sheets, drifting down like enormous translucent feathers. The exposed interior is darker, wet, and threaded with living light.

There are animals, but they are rare enough to seem like mistakes.

Most are long-bodied things that spend their lives in the root substrate and emerge only when rain falls. They are almost colorless and have no visible eyes. Their bodies are sensitive to vibration, and they move by passing waves of contraction from head to tail. One may cross an open plain in a single day, leaving behind a temporary trench that fills with water.

Other creatures hang from the ceiling, where they resemble dead leaves until they unfold. They descend on strands of plant fiber to drink from the rain curtains, then climb back into the brightness. Their calls are so low that they are felt more readily than heard: a pressure in the ribs, a faint alteration in balance.

The rarest animals are the walkers. They appear at great distances, one at a time, crossing the open ground with no apparent destination. Their bodies are jointed and narrow, built for cold and emptiness. They sometimes carry pieces of plant architecture on their backs—small shelters, baskets, poles, or bundles of glowing tissue. Whether they are native creatures or the descendants of an older people is unknown. They do not approach settlements. They do not seem to fear fire, though fire rarely survives here.

Signs of construction are scattered across the continent.

A hut may stand on a stone shelf beside a dry channel, made from layered leaf-ribs and bound with root cord. A ladder of carved bone may descend into a crack. Small bridges cross some of the narrower voids, though most end halfway across, their far ends pulled loose by later growth. There are marker-stones arranged around pools, and low walls built to direct condensation toward planted beds. The tools found near these places are simple: stone blades, hooked poles, vessels of fired clay, wedges made from hardened resin.

Nothing built here remains unchanged for long. Plants grow through walls and swallow doorways. Floors buckle as roots pass beneath them. A hut may be preserved on the outside while its interior becomes a hollow chamber of living tissue. The inhabitants, if they still exist, must repair their homes by cutting, pruning, burning, and persuading the growth around them. Their technology is primitive not because materials are scarce, but because the material is always moving.

The world has one especially important rule: **living things answer to pressure**.

Touch a leaf and it may close. Strike a root and the disturbance can travel for miles. Walk repeatedly along one path and the plants may gradually harden it into a road. Stand still long enough and small tendrils will orient themselves toward the warmth of your body. Noise, heat, weight, and falling water all alter growth. Even thought may matter—not through magic, exactly, but because the organisms are exquisitely sensitive to the minute electrical changes of living nerves. Strong emotion causes nearby surfaces to brighten. Sleep encourages the growth of fine, enclosing fibers.

A person who remains in one place becomes part of the place.

The open plains are therefore crossed quickly. Travelers move from one stone outcrop to the next, from one dry channel to another, avoiding the large plant fields except during rain. They carry cloth screens against the ceiling’s glare, though the light is cold and never warms the skin. They carry knives less for defense than for cutting away new growth. Their camps are marked by small piles of black stones, because any other marker may root overnight.

There is weather here, but it is generated by the plants.

When a major canopy-organism exhales, cold winds sweep across the continent. They rise from low places and blow upward along the walls, carrying moisture, spores, and loose mineral dust. The wind can last for weeks. It may bend miles of vegetation in one direction, then abruptly reverse. During these periods, the bright ceiling becomes indistinct, and the immense hollow takes on the appearance of an inland sea turned upside down.

The storms are quiet at first. Their approach is announced by the disappearance of condensation. Every surface dries. The pale plants draw tight against themselves. Far away, a line of brightness moves across the floor as dormant growth wakes in sequence. Then the air begins to move.

At the center of a storm, plants are torn from their foundations and carried through the open space. Root masses tumble like drifting islands. Sheets of leaf tissue rise into the ceiling and fuse there. New rivers cut through the floor. Old landmarks vanish. When the wind ends, the landscape is always slightly larger, or slightly less complete, than before.

Nothing in the White Green is fully dead. Fallen trunks continue to feed light into the ceiling. Empty husks sprout roots. A carcass dissolves into a flowering field within days. Even mineral surfaces become colonized by thin, patient films that slowly translate their shapes into living tissue.

And yet there is an immense sense of vacancy.

The brightness reveals distances rather than filling them. The vast ceiling gives no warmth. The sparse plants stand apart like isolated thoughts. Between them are cold plains, black stone, dry channels, and the faint trails of things that passed long ago. A traveler may walk for days hearing only their own steps, the far-off drip of water, and the soft internal movements of the continent.

Then, somewhere beyond sight, an entire forest will begin to rise.

The ground will heave. Pale columns will push upward. The ceiling will brighten in response. A wind will cross the empty plain, carrying the smell of wet roots and newly split stone.

For a while, the White Green will be making itself.
