The city lies beneath a warm, motionless sea.

Not at the bottom of an abyss, but in a broad drowned basin where the water is deep enough to erase the sky. Above it there may be weather, islands, birds, sunlight; none of that reaches here. The sea presses down in a dim blue-black weight, and the city receives its light only from living things.

Its streets are laid out across a shallow continental shelf, descending so gradually that direction is difficult to feel. There are plazas, embankments, courtyards, stairways, and long avenues whose upper stones have been worn smooth by centuries of current. Most buildings stand only a few stories high, though many have lost their roofs or collapsed inward. Their walls are made from pale limestone, green-black basalt, and blocks of shell-rich concrete that have become part of the reef. Doorways open onto rooms full of silt. Windows frame the dark water. Bridges connect towers that lean together like old teeth.

The city has not been abandoned in any ordinary sense. It has been inhabited by the sea.

Soft corals occupy the corners of buildings. Fan-shaped growths spread across balconies and seal narrow passages with living lace. Long ropes of kelp descend from rooftops, sparse enough that the architecture remains visible, but numerous enough to sway through every open space. There are no forests here. Vegetation comes in isolated clumps: eelgrass in courtyards, red algae on shaded steps, pale floating bulbs tethered to broken statues. Much of the color is mineral or animal rather than green.

Every surface is crowded.

Small fish flicker through cracks in masonry. Transparent shrimp gather in the hollows of carved faces. Eels sleep in drainage channels and slide out when shadows pass. Squat lobsters pick through the seams between paving stones. Schools of silver fish turn together in the empty windows of houses, filling them briefly with reflected motion and then leaving them vacant again.

Larger animals move along the avenues with the slow confidence of residents. Broad-backed rays settle over entire sections of pavement, their wings covering mosaics and doorways. Pale sharks patrol the outer districts, nosing through collapsed markets. There are seal-like amphibious things, warm-skinned and whiskered, that wedge themselves between columns to sleep. Their young gather in the old public fountains, where water still rises—not from pipes, but from the pressure of underground springs.

The fountains are among the few structures that still function.

They stand in plazas paved with black stone, each basin filled with dark water warmer than the surrounding sea. From cracks below, mineral-rich springs rise in steady columns. The upwelling carries food and heat, and so the plazas have become the busiest places in the city. Tubeworms form dense red thickets around the vents. Blind fish hover in layers. Crabs, snails, and small scavenging creatures pack the basin edges so closely that the stone beneath them is difficult to see.

Yet the city is mostly quiet.

Water absorbs distance. A predator can cross a square without producing more than a tremor in the silt. The movement of thousands of animals becomes a soft, continuous hush: fins brushing against stone, shells ticking as they are dragged, the faint wet rasp of grazing mouths. Farther away, something large strikes a wall, and the sound arrives as a pressure through the ribs rather than a noise in the ears.

Sometimes the old buildings groan.

Their foundations settle by fractions of an inch. Sand pours from high ledges. A cornice breaks and falls through the water in a slow procession of fragments. Whole streets have tilted, but the tilting has become part of the city’s geometry. Long ago, the main avenues were straight. Now they slope toward one another, forming channels where currents concentrate and fish travel. Stairs end in open water. Courtyards have become basins. A temple has become a vertical reef.

Erosion here is not violent. It is patient, established, and complete in its patience. Water has rounded every exposed corner. Carved letters have blurred into shallow tracks. Statues have lost their hands, faces, and names, but their bodies remain, worn into useful surfaces for coral and shellfish. The sea does not destroy the city so much as continuously revise it.

There are rules to the darkness.

No light comes from above, not even on the clearest day. The upper water is too deep and too sedimented, and a permanent layer of warm, cloudy current hangs above the basin. At the lowest streets, the darkness is absolute. In the middle districts, life makes its own weak illuminations: blue sparks from disturbed plankton, green pulses inside transparent jellyfish, slow amber glows along the flanks of passing animals. These lights do not reveal the city. They only announce fragments of it—a column, an eye, a stair descending nowhere.

The brightest places are not safe places. Predators learn the patterns of illumination. Small creatures flash when frightened; larger creatures follow. A sudden blue flare in a courtyard may draw a hunting shoal from several streets away. The animals of the city navigate as much by pressure, scent, and vibration as by sight. The blind fish know the shape of walls by the changes in current around them. Rays bury themselves beneath familiar paving stones. Eels recognize the warmth of particular fountains.

The water is warm enough that nothing here feels preserved. It feels active, fertile, and close to decay.

There is no machinery, no electrical glow, no artificial signal. Whatever infrastructure survives is made from stone, slope, and the bodies of organisms. Channels once intended for water have become migration routes. Cisterns are nurseries. Sewer tunnels are denning grounds. The great seawalls, built in a time when the city still stood under air, now divide neighborhoods into distinct ecological zones: one side crowded with soft corals, the other swept clean by a cold, fast current. The old harbor is a forest of masts, but the masts are encrusted and dead, and among them move enormous transparent siphon creatures that feed on the current.

At the city’s center is a low district of broad buildings arranged around a sunken basin. Here the foundations are intact, and the density is greatest. Columns almost touch. Arches overlap. Fish pass through one room into another without noticing the boundary. The streets are carpeted with shells. A thin layer of silt lies everywhere except where the animals keep it stirred.

At intervals, the basin exhales.

The entire water column lifts by a breath. Silt rises from the pavement. Kelp bends. Schools of fish compress into silver clouds. Somewhere beneath the city, a geothermal chamber releases a pulse of warm water through the ancient foundations. The pulse moves outward through drains, wells, and cracks, changing the currents for several hours. Animals respond immediately. Hunters leave the dark edges. Grazers flood the newly warmed stones. Tubeworms open. The city seems, for a little while, to wake.

Then the pulse passes.

The streets settle back into shadow. The animals resume their routes. A ray folds itself over a mosaic. A shark disappears behind a leaning tower. Tiny lights wink on beneath a bridge, one after another, like windows in a settlement too deep to belong to the living world.

From a distance, the drowned city is almost invisible. Its structures blend with the shelf: a low, sprawling interruption in the seabed, extending across several miles, neither ruin nor reef but something between them. Only the towers and the densest walls rise clearly from the floor. Beyond them, the plain continues into darkness, marked by scattered avenues, broken monuments, and the faint drifting glow of life.

The whole place feels settled into itself.

It does not wait to be reclaimed, and it does not remember the surface. The sea has supplied it with inhabitants, weather, food, predators, and time. Every chamber has become a habitat. Every collapse has made a new passage. Every surviving wall serves as shelter, boundary, perch, nursery, or ambush.

The city is not beneath the ocean.

It is an ocean that has learned the shape of a city.
