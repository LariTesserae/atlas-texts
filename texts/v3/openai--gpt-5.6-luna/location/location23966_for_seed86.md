The landscape begins below the surface, but not in the close, crushing sense of a buried tunnel. It is a country beneath the country: a long, low cavern system opened into a single broad basin, its roof lost in dimness overhead. In places the ceiling rises beyond sight; in others it hangs low enough to show its raw underside, black and uneven, as if the earth had only recently been split apart. The air is cool and wet without being cold. Moisture gathers in beads along the stone, runs in narrow threads down the walls, and collects in shallow hollows between the rock shelves.

Nothing here is polished by age. The stone is young in appearance—freshly broken, angular, and pale where it has been exposed. Great ribs of limestone and dark volcanic rock thrust from the floor in unfinished ridges. Their surfaces are sharp with frostlike mineral growths, fractured ledges, and thin plates that flake away under the weight of passing animals. The basin floor is covered in gray scree, damp clay, and bands of fine white grit washed down from the higher galleries. Water has begun to make its paths, but has not yet completed them. It runs in straight, impatient-looking channels, drops from ledge to ledge, and disappears into cracks that are still too narrow to have become proper streams.

There are pools everywhere, but no lake. They lie in separate pockets: black water gathered in stone bowls, silver puddles in the hollows of the rubble, long shallow mirrors beneath dripping walls. Their surfaces tremble constantly. Some tremors come from the water itself, some from the movement of the ground, and many from the thousands of bodies crossing nearby. In the dimness, the pools reflect the faint gray illumination that filters down through distant shafts, though the source of that light is rarely visible. It may be daylight entering through collapsed portions of the upper earth, weakened and scattered until it becomes no more than a dull ceiling-glow. It is enough to distinguish the broad shapes of the land, not enough to provide warmth or clear color.

Vegetation is sparse and low. There are no forests, no hanging gardens, no continuous green. Instead, life gathers in isolated damp zones: mats of dark moss around seepage, pale grasslike blades growing from cracks, small fans of fern along the banks of the slowest channels. These plants are thin, widely spaced, and often pressed flat by traffic. Their colors are muted—blue-green, brown, gray, a little yellow at the tips. Between one patch and the next may be several hundred paces of bare stone.

The emptiness is deceptive.

The basin is full of animals.

They occupy every distance: solitary shapes near the pools, herds moving across the far shelves, swarms passing in front of the walls like smoke. Most are ordinary in form, though adapted to the underground country. Long-legged grazing animals pick their way over the scree, their hooves splayed for balance and their coats dusted with pale mud. They feed on the scattered moss and the sparse shoots, then move on in patient lines, leaving almost nothing behind. Smaller burrowing creatures erupt from the gravel in sudden bursts, scatter, and vanish into fresh holes. Thick-bodied amphibians crouch half-submerged in the pools. They are nearly motionless until something comes close, when they lunge with abrupt, wet violence.

The larger animals travel along established corridors without walls or paths. Their passing has worn broad, shallow lanes into the floor, though these are not made things; they are simply the repeated consequences of movement. Herds funnel between the stone ridges. Flocks cling to the higher ledges. Winged creatures wheel in the vault overhead, too numerous to see individually, their wings making a continuous, leathery rushing. Now and then a mass of them descends as one, filling a shelf, a pool edge, or the mouth of a side passage. The cavern darkens beneath them.

There are predators as well, but the abundance of prey makes them less secretive than surface predators. They wait in exposed places. A long, low catlike animal lies beside a runnel with its head on its paws, surrounded by the noise of smaller life. Pale, many-jointed hunters stand among the rocks, still as mineral pillars until the instant they move. Heavy animals with blunt jaws patrol the edges of the herds. Their hunting is brief and startling. A rush, a collapse of bodies, a burst of cries—and then the movement of the greater population closes over the event.

The sound never stops.

Water ticks from the ceiling at different distances, producing a scattered, irregular percussion. Shallow streams hiss over gravel. Feet strike stone in overlapping rhythms: soft pads, hard hooves, claws, dragging bellies, the rapid patter of small animals. Wingbeats pass overhead in waves. Calls travel across the basin and return from unseen walls—low booming notes, sharp warning cries, throat-deep bellows, the constant chittering and clicking of creatures too small to identify.

Because the cavern is so large, the noise does not resolve into a single roar. It has layers. Nearby sounds are harsh and immediate; distant ones become a steady, almost oceanic resonance. Every exposed rock face throws something back. A call made at one end of the basin may be answered by a dozen echoes from directions that cannot be judged. Yet the sounds are not mysterious. They belong to hunger, warning, mating, territorial display, and the simple work of moving through the world.

The landscape is remarkably stable. Nothing storms through it. No great river changes course. No frost heaves the ground, no tides rise, no seasons are apparent. The same dampness returns to the same hollows. The same herds follow the same routes. Water drips at a nearly unchanging pace, and the animals seem to have fitted themselves into its rhythm so completely that their movements resemble part of the terrain.

Even violence leaves little visible history. A carcass is stripped quickly, bones scattered, and the empty place is occupied again. Fresh fractures remain bright on the stone, but they do not noticeably widen. Mud dries at its edges without shrinking the pools. Plants are eaten down and reappear in much the same sparse patches. The cavern is not dead or frozen; it is alive in overwhelming quantity, but held in an equilibrium so old and complete that change becomes difficult to perceive.

There are no structures, no tools, no signs of deliberate shaping. A few animals use stones to crack shells or scrape tubers, and some carry sharpened fragments of bone in their mouths or claws, but nothing has been arranged into shelter or monument. The only marks of intelligence are tracks, feeding places, and repeated routes. The walls remain untouched except by water and bodies.

From a high shelf, the whole region can be seen in fragments: the dim roof, the pale mineral ridges, the separated pools, the sparse islands of vegetation, and the constant migration of creatures through the open spaces. The scale is wide enough to lose sight of an animal before it reaches the far side, wide enough for several climates of dampness to exist at once—dry scree beneath one wall, dripping ledges beneath another, mist gathered in a distant depression. Still, it is enclosed. The horizon is always a wall, a rise of stone, or darkness where the floor continues beyond the reach of the weak light.

The place feels newly made but already ancient. Its edges are sharp, its channels unfinished, its rock still raw; yet its animals move with the certainty of inhabitants who have never known another world. The earth has opened, water has entered, and life has filled the opening—not delicately, not sparsely, but in a vast, noisy pressure against the bare stone.
