The settlement occupies a shallow bowl in the high country, a place no larger than a village if measured by walking, though its walls and avenues were built to the proportions of a city. The surrounding mountains have long since worn themselves down. Their slopes are broad and gray, neither crag nor cliff, but immense old backs of stone descending toward the bowl in patient folds. Nothing here appears newly broken. Every edge has been softened by centuries of frost, thaw, wind, and the slow rasp of blown mineral dust.

At this elevation, the air is thin and cold enough to make distance look clean. The light is ordinary daylight—pale beneath a high, washed sky—but it falls across the place without warmth. Shadows gather blue in the hollows between buildings. On clear days, the sun reveals a faint silver sheen on every surface: not snow, but condensation, beaded on stone and iron-dark soil. When cloud descends, it does not pass overhead. It settles into the streets and rooms as a low, wet vapor, and the city begins to drink.

The buildings are mostly intact in the way bones are intact. Walls stand without roofs. Roofs lie collapsed in courtyards, their slabs half-sunk into moss and black grass. Long arcades run along the basin’s sides, supported by pillars swollen and rounded by age. Doorways open onto nothing. Staircases climb to vanished floors, then stop at the sky.

There are no roads in the usual sense, only paved channels and broad terraces. The paving stones are pale volcanic rock, worn almost flat, though the repeated passage of water has carved narrow grooves through them. The city was arranged around water that is no longer permanently present. Every street slopes toward a cistern, every courtyard contains a basin, and every public square is ringed with shallow gutters. Most are dry. A few hold dark, trembling pools no deeper than a hand.

Water comes irregularly. It gathers first as mist in the highest galleries, where it condenses on the undersides of stones and falls in slow, clear drops. Then it appears in walls: a dampening stain, a black seam, a line of beads. After several hours, or several days, the gutters begin to mutter. Small streams emerge from cracks in the masonry and run downhill with startling force, as if the entire settlement has been holding its breath. They may vanish by morning. They may continue for a week, flooding the lower rooms and making mirrors of the streets.

The inhabitants built for this behavior, though no inhabitants remain.

Their construction is layered and peculiar. Beneath the visible city lies an older one, and beneath that perhaps another: foundations protrude through courtyards, stairways descend into sealed chambers, and walls divide spaces that no longer correspond to any surviving plan. There are arches within arches, plazas built over plazas, and narrow corridors that end at solid stone where some earlier structure was filled in. In places, the city seems less built than thickened, as if generations had added themselves to it like mineral deposits.

Vegetation has entered the lower layers. Cold-loving grasses grow in the cracks, their blades dark green and stiff as wire. Mats of moss cover the north-facing walls, bright in the wet season and gray-green in the dry. Small flowering plants cling to ledges where condensation collects: white, star-shaped blossoms, blue cups, tiny leaves furred against the cold. In the deepest courtyards, where the stones retain moisture, there are waist-high stands of reedlike plants. They are not quite reeds. Their hollow stalks are translucent, and when the wind passes through them they give off a dim internal gleam, as though each plant contains a thread of trapped daylight.

The vegetation is not lush enough to hide the city. It gathers in islands, seams, and low green constellations. Vast stretches of paving remain bare. A single cushion of moss may occupy an entire square of stone, while ten neighboring squares hold nothing but dust. The emptiness is part of the place’s character: generous distances between one living thing and the next, broad courtyards with one tree, one pool, one wall of ferns.

The trees are rare and low. Most grow from ruined cisterns, their roots descending into waterlogged chambers. Their trunks are pale and twisted, their crowns sparse, bearing narrow leaves that turn black before they fall. When the wind rises, the leaves strike one another with a hard, dry clatter. There is no birdsong to answer them. A few small animals live here—pale burrowing things in the old drainage works, long-legged insects beneath the stones, a dark animal that appears only at dusk beside the water—but they are seldom seen. The city is not dead, exactly. It is simply too large for the life that remains in it.

Sound fills the emptiness.

Wind pours through the avenues and broken windows, swelling and dropping like breath in a vast instrument. The surviving arches produce different tones: a low booming note from the western arcade, a thin whistle from a collapsed tower, a rhythmic knocking where loose slabs move against one another. When rain arrives, it strikes thousands of surfaces at once. Dry basins ring. Gutters roar. Water drops fall through roofless rooms and land on stone with the precision of tapping fingers.

At times the whole settlement seems to speak in separate, overlapping voices. A stream runs beneath a pavement and can be heard but not seen. A wall cracks with a report like distant thunder. Somewhere below, a sealed reservoir gives a hollow pulse. The sound travels strangely in the cold air: a footstep may return from a quarter mile away, while a shout disappears into the mist at arm’s length.

The world here follows an old rule. Nothing is permitted to remain completely dry for long, but neither is anything allowed to become permanently wet. Water moves through the city in brief, powerful occupations, then withdraws into the stone. Moss dies back. Dust returns. Plants wait. The abandoned structures endure by alternating between saturation and exposure, expansion and contraction, growth and retreat. Even the ruins seem to participate in this cycle. A wall that has stood for centuries may suddenly shed a smooth outer layer, revealing older masonry beneath, not through collapse but through quiet unmaking.

When the weather is active, the place feels almost crowded. Mist advances between the buildings. Green growth opens. Water spills from the upper terraces. The reeds shine. The wind drives loose leaves and pale grit along the streets. Yet the eye can still travel through enormous empty spaces, from one solitary remnant to another: one arch, one tree, one flooded stairwell, one figureless square.

And then the weather clears.

The city settles into its final shape beneath the cold normal light. The terraces become gray planes. The mountain basin opens around them. Drops evaporate from the stones. The sounds diminish to wind, distant water, and the occasional sharp click of something living beneath the pavement.

Nothing moves in the avenues, but the place is not still. It is continuing—slowly, loudly, and without witnesses—to erode toward whatever form comes after a city.
