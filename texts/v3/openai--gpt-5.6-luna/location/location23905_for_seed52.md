The city lies in a shallow basin where the ground has only recently learned to be ground.

Its edges are sharp. Low ridges rise out of the plain in broken shelves, their faces dark and clean as if cut with a blade. Between them, streets and foundations spread across pale sediment that has not yet settled into any recognizable geological age. The soil is thin, gray-green, and glassy in places. Underfoot, it gives slightly—not like mud, but like a surface remembering that it was once fluid.

There is little standing water. Rain comes in brief, warm episodes, falling from clouds that gather without wind and dissolve almost as soon as they have emptied themselves. Afterwards, water remains in unlikely places: in the seams of the pavement, in shallow dishes formed by collapsed terraces, in the hollows of roofs. Condensation pearls on the shaded sides of buildings and runs upward along certain walls, drawn by hidden gradients. Every few days the city’s drainage system wakes and makes a soft internal churning, moving water from one temporary basin to another.

The climate is warm without being bright. The air holds the heavy softness of a summer evening, but the sky is usually dim—blue diluted almost to gray, or a long violet dusk that never quite becomes night. There may be a sun behind the cloud cover, though its presence is felt more as a pressure in the atmosphere than as illumination. Shadows are broad and indistinct. The city’s own lights are sparse, carefully placed, and low in intensity: green lines beneath bridges, amber pools around doorways, a few suspended signs whose letters rearrange themselves too slowly to be noticed.

Beyond the central avenues, the settlement thins quickly. Towers stand far apart, each surrounded by open ground. Their surfaces are pale ceramic, dark metal, or something translucent that seems to have been grown rather than assembled. Some buildings lean at impossible angles without appearing unstable. One has no visible entrance and is wrapped in a veil of fine droplets, falling continuously from its uppermost level and vanishing before they reach the ground. Another is a long, low structure half-buried in the basin, its roof covered by a forest of thin antennae that bend toward passing weather.

The city was built with more confidence than understanding. Its infrastructure is orderly, but not entirely obedient to ordinary space. Roads run straight for several kilometers and then arrive at themselves from another direction. Elevated transit lines cross the basin in broad arcs, carrying no visible vehicles. Occasionally, a silent shape passes along them: a dark capsule, a ring of cold light, or a distortion in the air that briefly makes the buildings behind it appear closer. The routes are always active, though the stations may remain empty for days.

Most of the built environment is quiet. Doors open before anyone approaches. Street surfaces repair their own cracks with slow blooms of metallic material. Maintenance machines move beneath the pavement, sensed only by a faint tremor under the soles. In the open districts, there are long stretches where no person can be seen and no machine can be heard. Yet the city does not feel abandoned. Something is always measuring the air, adjusting a wall, changing the direction of a drain.

Vegetation occupies the intervals left between these systems.

It is lush in isolated patches, and nowhere else. Ferns spread beneath the transit pylons in dense, glossy fans. Broad-leaved plants grow in the shadow of tower foundations, their leaves dark enough to absorb the dimness around them. Vines climb unfinished buildings and produce clusters of translucent fruit that contain no seeds. Along the drainage channels, low reeds form bright green bands, trembling even when the air is still. They draw moisture from the stone and release it as a fine mist at dawn—or what passes for dawn here.

The largest growths are not trees exactly. They rise from the basin in scattered columns, branching only near their upper reaches. Their trunks are jointed and faintly warm, with bark like layered mineral skin. They may be native, or they may have been introduced as part of some forgotten reclamation project. Around them the ground is softer, and small pools appear more often. Their crowns hold a permanent dim glow, not enough to illuminate anything, but sufficient to outline their leaves against the sky.

Fauna is rare. A few pale, many-jointed creatures occupy the wet margins of the city, moving in sudden coordinated bursts between stones. They have no obvious eyes and seem to navigate by changes in humidity. In the vegetation, something clicks at irregular intervals, then falls silent whenever the sound is answered. High above, small gliding shapes sometimes pass through the atmospheric haze. They are too distant to identify and leave no visible shadow.

The most common living presence is microscopic. Green films spread over flooded concrete and retreat when the surface dries. Fine colonies gather around warm conduits. In some districts, the walls themselves support slow ecologies: lichen-like patterns that rearrange overnight, feeding on electrical leakage and airborne minerals. A building may be bare one week and furred with silver growth the next.

The landscape is young in a way that has little to do with age. Its forms appear newly exposed, abruptly cut from some earlier condition. The ridges around the basin have not been rounded by long weathering. Their stone shows internal colors—rust, pearl, black-blue—along fresh fractures. Some slopes end in hanging shelves of rock that seem to have separated only moments ago. After rain, the plain changes subtly. New channels appear. Old ones close. Once, in the far eastern quarter, an entire vacant district shifted several meters during a quiet afternoon, leaving its buildings standing but rearranged on their foundations.

No one seems alarmed by such events. The city has rules for them.

At certain hours, marked by a change in the tone of the overhead haze, all movement stops. Transit pauses. Doors seal. Water is drawn from the streets. The vegetation folds its leaves and becomes almost indistinguishable from sculpture. During these intervals, the basin listens to something beneath itself. The ground emits a low vibration too deep to be called a sound, and the dim light gathers along the ridges as though the terrain were being scanned from below.

Then the systems resume. A bridge extends by several hundred meters. A tower opens a new window. Rain falls in one narrow strip across the western plain and nowhere else.

The air smells of wet stone, warm metal, leaves, and the faint mineral sharpness of freshly opened earth. Wind is uncommon, but when it arrives it moves across the basin in a single broad sheet, lifting mist from the channels and bending the tall vegetation all at once. For a few minutes the city becomes visible as a whole: sparse buildings, green islands, exposed ridges, empty roads, and the dim geometry of mechanisms too advanced to reveal their purpose.

Then the wind passes, and the place returns to its quiet activity—growing, draining, measuring, settling into a world that has not finished deciding what it is.
