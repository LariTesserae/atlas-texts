The settlement occupies a high shelf of the mountain, a shallow basin no larger than a village square if the village square had been stretched between cliffs and roofed with cloud.

Nothing here has finished becoming land.

The ground is a pale, dark-veined stone, newly lifted from the mountain’s interior. It lies in broad plates and blunt terraces, with seams still visible where the earth has folded and cooled. The edges are sharp but not yet broken down; there are no rounded boulders, no deep ravines, no slopes softened by centuries of falling water. Instead, the terrain has the tentative geometry of something assembled in haste: shelves, wedges, upright fins, shallow hollows. In places the stone is warm beneath the hand, though the air is cold enough to silver the breath. Thin white mineral blooms push out along the seams, and water collects in them as if the rock were still learning how to hold a surface.

The basin is wet without being flooded. Mist gathers close to the ground, especially in the early hours, and beads along every exposed face. Water rises from below in slow, clear threads. It seeps through the stone, appears at the lip of a terrace, pauses in a trembling pool, then slips over the next edge. Some walls weep continuously. Others produce a single drop at long intervals, each one falling with such precision that the people have built little basins beneath them to count the mountain’s breathing.

Green life has taken advantage of all of it.

Moss lies thick in the sheltered cracks, vivid as wool. Low ferns spread over the flat stones, their fronds dark and glossy with condensation. There are mats of blue-green growth that resemble lichen but feel soft and warm, and pale grasses rooted in pockets of fresh grit. Small flowering plants rise directly from the mineral seams: white bells, rust-colored cups, translucent leaves. They are not arranged as a meadow. They occur in isolated islands, each one surrounded by clean, empty stone.

Above the basin, the vegetation thins abruptly. The lower slopes are hidden beneath cloud, but the upper rim is nearly bare: black teeth of rock, patches of green, and long tongues of vapor pouring over the crest. The air tastes metallic and wet. The cold is not severe, but it is persistent, a coolness held in the stone and returned at midday. Sunlight reaches the settlement plainly when the clouds open, though the brightness seems filtered by the height. Every surface is sharply visible: the wet shine of a stair, the grain in a timber beam, the pale trails where water has dried and left salt.

The settlement is built around the basin rather than across it.

Its houses stand apart from one another on narrow stone platforms, each connected by plank walks, rope bridges, and carefully fitted steps. There are perhaps forty structures, though from a distance they seem fewer because of the great intervals between them. Empty terraces separate one building from the next. Grass grows in the spaces. Mist passes freely through the streets.

The buildings are made from simple materials: local stone, split timber, woven reed, hide, and sheets of hammered copper or dark clay. The stone blocks are large and roughly shaped, their corners still raw. Some walls have been raised only halfway, leaving rooms open to the weather. Others are complete but strangely tall, with narrow doors and roofs pitched steeply to shed the constant damp. No surface bears much ornament. Construction is treated as a practical negotiation with the unfinished mountain. A wall ends where a seam of water begins. A stair bends around a living patch of moss. Roofs are supported by trunks sunk into sockets that the rock itself seems to have grown for them.

The largest structure is a long, low hall built over a natural fissure. Its floor is a lattice of cedar slats. Below it, invisible in the vapor, water rushes through the mountain. The entire hall trembles faintly. At its center hangs a row of wooden plates, each one suspended over the opening by cords. When the water below changes its pressure, the plates knock together.

They knock almost constantly.

This is a loud place, despite its emptiness. The falls are not large, but they drop through slots and shafts that magnify them into tremendous voices. Water striking a lower basin booms like a distant gate. Wind entering the stone fins produces long, low notes, while the plank walks creak and slap against their supports. Roof tiles click under condensation. Loose copper sheets chatter whenever a gust crosses the ridge. Conversation is conducted close to the ear, and bells are used not for ceremony but to locate one another in the fog.

The loudest sound comes from the eastern wall, where a newly opened channel descends through a sequence of black steps. It is only a narrow stream, but the rock beneath it is hollow. Every few seconds the water gives a deep, resonant cough, as if something enormous were turning over in sleep. The inhabitants have built no bridge there. They have built listening posts instead: small roofed platforms where people sit alone, wrapped in felt, recording the changing rhythm with charcoal marks.

There are few animals. A handful of small, long-legged birds nest under the eaves, though no one sees them fly; they seem to appear and vanish in the fog. Pale insects live among the warm mineral seams, moving slowly and without visible wings. Now and then a dark shape crosses the upper rim—perhaps a goat, perhaps a creature adapted to the thin air—but it never comes down among the houses. The people keep no livestock. They cultivate moss, roots, reedlike stalks, and a low succulent whose clear interior is used for drinking water and lamp fuel.

Nothing grows quickly here. A garden bed may remain unchanged for a season, then produce all at once: three leaves, a flower, a cluster of edible bulbs. The settlement’s food stores are therefore built into the architecture. Clay jars line the walls. Roots hang from rafters. Every household maintains a damp chamber where cuttings and spores are kept alive. The people are patient because the place requires it. They mend a bridge before it fails, reinforce a wall before the crack widens, and mark new springs before deciding whether to divert them.

The mountain is still rising.

This is the central rule of the landscape: stone does not merely weather here; it arrives. New shelves emerge from the ground with almost imperceptible slowness. A ridge may be a handspan higher by the end of a year. Fresh rock pushes through moss in clean, unfractured surfaces. Sometimes a door becomes difficult to open because the threshold has lifted. Sometimes a path ends against a smooth wall that was not there the previous winter. The inhabitants call these events births, though they approach them with the caution one might give an advancing animal.

They do not quarry the mountain. To cut into new stone is considered both dangerous and rude. Buildings are allowed to gather around it, and roads are adjusted as the ground changes. The oldest paths are not the straightest; they are the ones that have learned the basin’s habits.

At the farthest edge of the settlement stands a tower under construction. It has no walls yet, only four upright stones and a platform lashed between them. From its top one can look down through gaps in the cloud and see nothing of the world below. The lower valleys are completely obscured. The village seems suspended above a white ocean, a small arrangement of roofs and green patches on an island of raw geology.

At times, a new sound rises from beneath the basin: a slow grinding, deep enough to be felt in the ribs. The people stop what they are doing and wait. Water trembles in the counting bowls. Moss shivers. The wooden plates in the hall begin to knock in a new pattern.

Then the sound passes.

The stone settles into its latest shape. The paths remain. The mist closes in again. And the sparse settlement, loud with water and wind, continues its careful occupation of a place that has not yet decided what it will be.
