The city occupies a high basin where the mountain has not finished becoming a mountain.

Its floor is a broad, black mineral shelf, tilted slightly toward a horizon hidden by weather. The rock is young in the geological sense: glossy planes of basalt, pale seams of newly crystallized salt, soft banks of gray sediment that still remember the pressure beneath them. Nothing has been worn smooth by centuries of rain. Edges remain sharp. Ridges rise like unfinished walls. In places the ground has the appearance of poured material, as if the landscape were being assembled from below and had cooled before the work was complete.

Water is everywhere, though very little of it flows quickly.

It hangs in the air as cold vapor. It beads on every surface. It gathers in the seams between paving plates, in the hollows of machinery, beneath the ribs of bridges. Entire cliff faces weep in thin, shining threads. The water is nearly black at night, reflecting light only when some distant system pulses beneath it. Shallow channels cross the city in a dense, branching network, carrying meltwater and condensation toward the lower basin. They do not rush. They creep with the patience of ice, making a soft mineral whisper as they pass under grates and through porous stone.

The darkness is not empty. It is crowded with small lights.

The settlement is built in terraces across the basin, a city of towers, trenches, pressure vaults, suspended roads, and immense structures whose purposes are not apparent from their shapes. Buildings stand close together, not for warmth—there is no warmth to preserve—but because the available ground is unstable and the city is continually bracing itself against the unfinished mountain. Their walls are made from dark ceramic, transparent ice-metal, and materials that appear to have been grown rather than manufactured. Some structures are hollow frameworks surrounding columns of water. Others are sealed, windowless masses with slow bands of blue radiance moving through their interiors.

There are no plants. No moss softens the masonry. No root breaks the pavement. The only things resembling vegetation are mineral formations deliberately cultivated along the drainage channels: branching fans of conductive crystal, gray-black and translucent, spreading millimeters at a time. They open and close according to pressure changes deep underground. Their fronds make no sound, but when touched by runoff they transmit faint signals through the city, causing distant towers to wake, turn, or alter their posture.

The city’s roads are not roads so much as negotiated surfaces. Some are broad plates floating a finger’s width above the stone, held up by invisible fields. Others are narrow cuts through the bedrock, roofed in clear material to keep the condensation out of the machinery below. There are vertical streets, too: shafts through which cargo platforms climb for hours, and long inclined transitways where entire neighborhoods appear to slide slowly uphill.

Nothing moves fast.

A tower may rotate by a few degrees over the course of a day. A bridge may unfold itself over a month. A sealed district may migrate across the basin on buried traction rails so slowly that its departure is recognized only when its shadow falls somewhere else. The city is in constant motion, but most of that motion belongs to processes too gradual for the eye: foundations settling, reservoirs filling, pressure being redistributed through the mountain, new layers of infrastructure knitting themselves into old ones.

Against this slowness, the fauna are overwhelming.

They fill every level of the place.

There are dense swarms in the air, though “swarm” suggests insects and these are not insects. They are small, many-jointed bodies made of flexible shell and wet reflective tissue, each carrying a dim internal glow. They drift through the streets in thick, coordinated sheets, flowing around pillars and through maintenance apertures. When they gather, the air seems to have acquired a second architecture. They cling to walls in layered carpets, then peel away all at once, leaving the ceramic briefly dry beneath them.

Larger creatures move through the drainage system. Pale, blind forms with flattened bodies slide beneath the grates, their backs marked by rows of sensory pits. They are too numerous to be hunted and too useful to be exterminated. The city has been designed around them. Their feeding channels are lined with access doors. Their passage clears sediment from the waterworks. When one becomes trapped, entire sections of infrastructure pause for days while a rescue system slowly reshapes the surrounding rock.

In the open basin, herds of tall, jointed animals stand almost motionless among the terraces. Their limbs are thin and numerous, supporting bodies like suspended bundles of dark glass. They face into the prevailing wind. Condensation collects on their bodies and runs downward through channels in their skin, nourishing internal organs that shine faintly through their translucent sides. They do not graze; there is nothing to graze on. They filter the atmosphere, draw minerals from the rain, and exchange electrical signals with the city’s foundation.

Their calls are not calls in any familiar sense. A herd may produce a single tone that lasts several hours, rising and falling so slowly that it seems at first to be part of the weather. Other animals answer with impacts, pulses, and long vibrations transmitted through the ground. The combined sound is enormous.

The city never becomes quiet.

Water ticks against roofs. Pumps throb in buried chambers. Pressure valves release with deep, foghorn-like exhalations. Transit plates grind over their rails. The swarms make a dry, continuous rasp as their bodies touch. Herds call into the darkness. Beneath everything is the low, almost inaudible concussion of the mountain’s unfinished interior—a sound felt in the teeth more than heard.

Because the air is so wet, sound travels far. A single metallic strike in one district can continue echoing between the terraces for minutes. Distant machinery seems close. Nearby creatures vanish into the fog without becoming silent. The result is a landscape of confused distances: a roar from below, a tapping from within the wall, a chorus apparently moving across the sky.

Above the city, the high basin is enclosed by ridges and hanging shelves of stone. Some are natural; some are the exposed edges of older construction. They rise into darkness and disappear. Water descends from them in veils, freezes in places, melts in others, and is caught by enormous collection membranes stretched between towers. These membranes tremble under the weight of condensation. Every so often one contracts, wringing itself out into the reservoirs below.

There is no sun visible from the inhabited levels. It may be hidden behind cloud, or behind the mountain, or simply irrelevant to the city’s systems. Illumination comes from infrastructure and fauna: narrow strips beneath walkways, status lights behind wet walls, blue-white veins beneath the paving, the amber eyes of creatures waiting in alcoves. Some districts maintain a permanent twilight glow. Others are completely black, navigated by pressure, sonar, and the touch-sensitive surfaces of the walls.

The rules of the world are not openly displayed, but they are strict.

Water must be returned cleaner than it was received. No structure may cast a permanent shadow across a migration route. Certain sounds are forbidden because they disturb the slow orientation of the basin herds. No excavation can proceed without first asking the mountain what it intends to become. This is done through arrays of sensors driven deep into the rock, where they listen for changes in stress and temperature. Construction is permitted only when the predicted shape of the future ground can accommodate it.

The city does not dominate its environment. It negotiates with it, sometimes successfully.

Some buildings have already been abandoned to the fauna. Their interiors are packed with layered nests, wet shells, and the thin clicking of thousands of feeding mouths. Others have been absorbed into the emerging geology, their lower stories sealed beneath new mineral growth. A transit line disappears into a ridge and emerges far away with its geometry subtly altered. A whole residential quarter has been left empty because the stone beneath it began producing a slow, regular pulse. The inhabitants did not dismantle the quarter. They simply built around it, allowing the pulse to pass through foundations and streets.

At the margins, the distinction between settlement and organism becomes difficult to maintain.

A retaining wall develops pores and begins filtering water. A maintenance swarm nests in the joints of a bridge, causing its load-bearing capacity to improve. A reservoir grows a membrane that responds to weather before the weather arrives. The creatures seem to use the city, but the city also uses them, and both may be participating in a process neither understands: the gradual formation of a new environment from wet stone, engineered matter, animal bodies, pressure, and darkness.

Far beyond the last towers, the basin continues.

There are no fields, forests, or open wilderness in the old sense. There are only terraces fading into fog, black channels shining between them, and innumerable bodies moving patiently through the cold. The city extends past sight, not as a single continuous mass but as a chain of dense, connected habitats, each built around some local feature of the unfinished mountain. Here a tower cluster surrounds a warm fissure. There a sunken district shelters a breeding aggregation. Further on, lights descend into a flooded canyon where the fauna are so numerous that the water appears to move against its own current.

Everything is wet. Everything is cold. Everything is occupied.

And beneath the loud, crowded, slowly changing world, the ground is still rising.
