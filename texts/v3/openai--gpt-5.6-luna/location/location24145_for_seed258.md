The plateau begins where the mountain forgets how to become stone.

It is high country, but not cold. Warm air gathers in the basin and stays there, carrying the smell of wet bark, mineral water, and green matter beginning to ferment. Below the rim, the land opens into a broad, unfinished country of terraces, channels, halls, and isolated towers. Nothing appears naturally placed. Every surface has been made, though no maker can be found.

The ground is a continuous construction of living material.

Great mats of root-fiber spread across the plateau in broad, level sheets, their upper surfaces smooth enough to walk upon and their undersides hanging in curtains through the ravines. Trunks have been braided into retaining walls. Hollow stems form long arcades. Thick, pale runners have been pressed together into bridges and stairways, each step grown at a comfortable height, though the staircases lead nowhere anyone would recognize. The vegetation is not merely covering the landscape; it is the landscape’s masonry, timber, soil, plumbing, and roof.

There is very little bare earth. Where the construction has not reached, the mountain shows through in raw fragments: fresh gray stone, blunt shelves of red mineral, seams of black glassy rock that look newly exposed. These outcrops are strangely unweathered. Rain falls on them, but does not carve them. Water beads in their hollows and remains there, clear and perfectly still, as if erosion has only just been invented and has not yet learned what to do.

The open spaces are large. Stands of vegetation rise at a distance from one another, leaving wide courts of damp plant-floor between them. A single tree may occupy an entire rise, its trunk swollen into a cylindrical building and its crown spreading above the mist like a many-roomed roof. Farther off, another tree stands alone, perhaps half a kilometer away, connected to the first by a thin green causeway. Between them, nothing grows higher than the ankle.

The emptiness is deliberate-looking, though no boundary marks it.

Water is everywhere, but rarely in the form of ordinary streams. It collects in the architecture. Gutters made from curled leaves carry rain along the edges of terraces. Veins of translucent vine pulse with water beneath their skins. Rock faces sweat continuously, producing narrow silver falls that descend into hanging gardens. In the lower courts, shallow pools occupy the depressions between root-tiles, and their surfaces are so calm that they reflect the sky without interruption.

Rain comes often, but it does not seem to arrive or depart. Fine precipitation hangs in the air beneath the upper terraces. Droplets cling to every leaf, every rail, every cord of braided root. One can pass through a veil of water and emerge no wetter than before, while the plants nearby tremble under the weight of it. Sometimes the mist condenses upward, lifting from the pools in slow, luminous threads and disappearing into the undersides of leaves.

Nothing floods. Nothing dries.

The whole plateau exists in an exact, ongoing wetness.

The plants are immense, but the place is not crowded. Their forms are separated by intentional intervals, as though the vegetation has been arranged according to a plan that values silence, distance, and clear sightlines. There are meadows of low, glossy growth where no blade rises above the knee. There are enormous vacant plazas roofed by leaf canopies, their floors made from overlapping plates of yellow-green tissue. There are towers with windows cut into them, each window opening onto another interior chamber rather than the outside.

In some places, the architecture has been left incomplete. A wall ends in a cluster of pale tendrils that have not yet joined. A bridge extends several yards into open air, its far end thickening but never touching another structure. A staircase emerges from a mound of roots and climbs toward a blank vertical face. These unfinished edges do not decay. They remain tender and bright, held forever at the moment before completion.

The air is bright with ordinary daylight. There is no supernatural glow, no colored sun, no darkness beneath the leaves except the natural shade of their overlapping forms. Yet the light behaves strangely around the living structures. It enters a chamber through one opening and comes out through another at the same strength, even when the two openings face different directions. Deep inside the plant-built halls, broad patches of daylight lie on the floor in the shape of windows that are not there.

The sound of the plateau is enormous.

Water makes most of it: a constant fall and hiss from the terraces, a thousand small impacts of droplets, the heavy breathing noise of condensation running through hollow stems. The great leaves clap against one another in the warm air. Root-bridges creak, though they do not sway. Seedpods knock together high overhead with the hollow resonance of bells. In the largest courts, the sound gathers and becomes almost orchestral—a low, sustained booming underlaid by rapid ticking and wet, papery rattling.

The volume suggests weather, but the weather never changes.

There is no storm front. No gust rises and passes. No shower intensifies or fades. Each sound continues at its established strength, as if the landscape has settled into a single chord. A fall of water can be deafening beside one’s shoulder and inaudible three steps away. In certain passages, a person’s footsteps are swallowed entirely; in others, the same step returns from every wall at once, multiplied into a crowd.

Fauna is scarce. An occasional long-legged creature stands in a pool, thin as a reed and jointed in too many places, drinking through its feet. Small winged things sometimes cross the open courts, but they do not land and cast no visible shadows. Once, a pale animal may be seen far out on a terrace, motionless among the low growth. If approached, it is found to be a folded leaf. If left alone, it may eventually move.

The plants do not respond to visitors in any obvious way. They do not turn toward footsteps or close around a body. But they maintain the place with a quiet precision. A path remains clear. A handrail thickens where a hand has rested. A wet floor develops a raised pattern of ridges exactly where people prefer to walk. No wound remains open. No fallen branch stays fallen. If a piece of the architecture is removed, the surrounding growth closes the absence without haste or struggle, returning the structure to its previous shape.

The world here has no tools because tools would be redundant. Every function has been absorbed into growth. Water is carried by stems. Rooms are hollowed by living pressure. Walls support themselves. Bridges know their own span. The plateau is made, but its making is biological, patient, and absolute.

At the center of the open basin stands the largest construction: a ring of enormous trunks surrounding a flat, empty floor. The trunks are white inside and green outside, their surfaces scored with regular horizontal marks like the lines of a calendar. Above them, their crowns join into a roof so high that clouds drift beneath it. Rain falls through the roof in straight columns and stops at the level of the floor, suspended there as thousands of vertical threads.

Nothing grows in the ring.

Nothing enters it. Nothing leaves it. The water remains hanging, the air remains warm, and the thunderous sound of the living plateau presses against the boundary without crossing.

The marks on the trunks suggest that the place has existed for a very long time. Their spacing suggests that time has not passed here at all.
