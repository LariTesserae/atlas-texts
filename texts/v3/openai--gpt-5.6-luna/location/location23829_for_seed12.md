The city rises through a warm inland sea that has not yet learned its proper shape.

At the highest points, black stone terraces protrude from the water like the unfinished ribs of a mountain. They are not islands in the ordinary sense. The sea lies over them in layers: ankle-deep on the lower streets, waist-deep in the markets, and far above the roofs in the central districts. The uppermost towers stand beneath a suspended surface, a dim, wavering ceiling of water held in place by no visible shore. It stretches from horizon to horizon, a blue-green vault under which rain falls upward in slow threads.

The whole region is elevated—an underwater plateau or the crown of a rising volcanic continent—and this gives the flooded city an odd verticality. Streets climb steeply toward ridgelines. Bridges pass above one another. Staircases descend into water and emerge again on the sides of houses. A person may leave a square by walking uphill through a curtain of warm water, cross a roof garden, and find themselves looking down into another neighborhood submerged beneath them.

The stone is young. Its surfaces are raw, angular, and only beginning to settle into recognizable forms. Basalt pushes through the streets in folded black shelves. Pale mineral seams shine along walls where the rock is still cooling. There are broad red fields of porous stone, sharp as broken pottery, and chalky deposits gathered around vents. Nothing has been gently worn smooth. Corners remain corners. Slopes end abruptly. The city has been built around geology that is still in the act of becoming.

In places, the ground trembles—not with earthquakes, exactly, but with slow rearrangements. A stair will acquire one additional step over the course of a season. A wall will tilt upward by the width of a hand. New stone wells into existence in the lowest plazas, hot and dark, and the inhabitants simply place boards over it, then build a room above.

The water is warm enough to enter without hesitation. It carries the taste of minerals, leaves, and distant rain. It is not clear. Green suspended matter gives it a perpetual twilight, and long veils of aquatic plants drift through the avenues, rooted in roofs, balconies, and the crevices between paving stones. Their leaves are broad and translucent, like thin leather. Some grow in the air and trail their roots down into the flood; others float just beneath the overhead surface, making a second ceiling of crowded green.

Vegetation is everywhere, but not wild in the usual sense. It has found the city before the city was finished.

Vines loop around towers and pass through windows. Low gardens spread across the tops of submerged houses, their plants bending sideways in the water. Reed forests occupy old courtyards. A tree with a pale, swollen trunk grows from the center of a public bath and divides into branches that disappear into the upper water. Its leaves are dark violet underneath, adapted to the weak light. The people harvest its fruit by climbing down from above, moving slowly through the buoyant green.

The plants make the city dense without making it loud. Their stems brush against stone. Their roots click faintly as they expand. Fronds tap on shuttered windows. In the deeper channels, entire gardens sway in a single current, all moving together with the patient rhythm of breathing.

There are few animals. The water is too young, or too mineral-rich, or perhaps the city has driven most life away. Small silver things shelter under stairs and vanish when approached. Once in a while, a broad shadow passes above the streets, seen only as a dim interruption in the blue ceiling. At night—or what serves as night—long-legged amphibious birds stand on roofs with their heads tucked beneath their wings. They do not call. They are as quiet as the stones.

Most sounds are made by water and human labor.

A steady dripping falls upward from pools into the suspended sea. Ropes creak. Wooden wheels turn beneath the surface. Somewhere a hammer strikes a wedge into fresh rock, the blows spaced far apart. Voices carry strangely through the flooded streets, arriving softened and directionless, so that a person may hear their name from a house that has not yet been built.

The settlement itself is primitive in its tools but ambitious in its arrangement. Its people use timber, woven fiber, fired clay, bone, shell, and simple iron brought from somewhere beyond the visible water. They have built platforms on stilts, sealed chambers with waxed cloth, floating walkways, cisterns, ladders, sluice gates, and immense woven nets stretched across whole districts. Their construction is practical and provisional. Every building looks capable of being dismantled, raised, or abandoned.

There are no straight roads for long. The ground interrupts them. A street might narrow into a channel, then become a staircase, then pass through the open interior of a tower. Homes are joined by covered bridges and flexible walkways that rise and fall with the water. Markets occupy the upper floors of buildings because the lower floors are too wet; kitchens are built on rafts; workshops cling to the dry sides of cliffs.

The city is organized around height rather than distance. People speak of the Lower Salt, the Middle Gardens, the Black Rise, and the Roofwater. Bells are hung at different elevations so that their tones mark the levels of the city. But the bells are rarely rung. The atmosphere encourages patience. Nothing here changes quickly enough to reward alarm.

Light filters through the water above in a dull, green-gold wash. There is no clear sun. At times a brighter patch drifts across the ceiling, illuminating the streets with the vague impression of daylight seen through deep ocean. At other times the entire city darkens for hours, though no cloud or eclipse is visible. The inhabitants keep lamps in shallow bowls of oil, protected by clay shades. Their flames burn low and blue, and are reflected in the water until every street seems to contain a second, inverted settlement beneath it.

The governing rule of the place is that water does not always fall.

It gathers where it pleases. It can remain suspended over a courtyard, flow uphill along a wall, or pour from a doorway and stop in midair. Rain sometimes rises from the flooded streets in thin silver columns, collecting overhead. Objects dropped into the water descend slowly, then may drift sideways for miles before touching bottom. A stone thrown from a tower can travel upward, lose its momentum, and hang there for several minutes before continuing into the sea above.

The people have learned not to fight these behaviors. Their bridges are built with slack. Their walls are perforated. Their gardens are tied rather than planted. They leave spaces in everything for the world to continue forming.

Beyond the inhabited districts, the plateau extends into open water and unfinished terrain: black ridges under green depth, warm fog banks, raw cliffs, and broad mineral flats where no road has yet survived. Far off, new mountains are beginning to lift themselves beneath the sea. Their upper faces are barely visible through the dimness, ribbed with red heat and threaded with the roots of future forests.

The settlement is not a completed city, and perhaps never will be. It is a place built in the interval between emergence and drowning, where architecture, vegetation, water, and stone occupy the same patient process. Every surface is temporary. Every foundation is also a seed.
