Beneath the surface lies a country without sky.

It is not a cavern in the usual sense, not a hollow carved through ordinary stone, but a continent-sized interior: a warm mineral chamber extending farther than sound can travel and farther than any expedition has mapped. Its ceiling is lost in darkness. Its floor is a broad, uneven plain of black glass, ash-gray crystal, and newly hardened stone. Here and there the ground rises into long, sharp-backed ridges, as if the world had only recently cooled and had not yet learned to soften its edges.

There is no standing water. No seepage darkens the stone, no stream cuts a valley, no dampness gathers in the cracks. The mineral surfaces are dry enough to drink the warmth from exposed skin. In some regions the floor is covered with pale salt-like dust, though it is not salt and dissolves in nothing familiar. In others, the ground is layered in thin plates of iron-black material that ring faintly when stepped upon. The plates lie close together, packed like scales, and the slightest pressure may make an entire slope tremble with a subdued metallic whisper.

Warmth comes from below and from everywhere. It is not the oppressive heat of a furnace but the constant temperature of a living body. The air is thick, dry, and faintly charged. It carries the smells of hot stone, scorched metal, old ash, and something sharp and sweet that clings to the throat. There are no seasons, no storms, no fronts of weather. The atmosphere does not move in winds so much as settle into immense, nearly motionless layers. Near the floor it is warmest. Higher up, invisible strata of denser gas hang beneath the ceiling. The boundary between them is so stable that it resembles architecture.

Darkness fills the chamber completely.

There are scattered lights, but none are bright enough to reveal the whole. A seam in the stone may glow with a dull red line. A cluster of mineral shelves may carry a powder-blue sheen beneath their surfaces. Passing animals sometimes leave brief trails of amber radiance where their bodies brush the dust. These lights do not flicker like fire. They persist with the calm, unwavering quality of stars seen through a sealed window, and they illuminate only the nearest few feet. Beyond that is the same vast blackness.

The land is crowded despite its emptiness.

The floor appears bare from a distance, but up close it is densely inhabited. Low, many-jointed creatures lie folded in the grooves between mineral plates, their bodies the color of ash and old bronze. They feed by pressing their mouths—or perhaps their mouths are simply specialized openings—against the stone and drawing out heat, pressure, and trace elements. They rarely fight. Resources do not seem to diminish. The chamber maintains a perfect and ancient balance, and the creatures have adapted to it by becoming patient.

Some resemble bundles of black wire. Some are broad, pale things with dozens of articulated legs, moving sideways in complete silence. Others are almost indistinguishable from rocks until they unfold a fan of sensory filaments or open a vertical eye. There are immense forms embedded in the walls, their bodies extending through the strata for hundreds of yards. Their surfaces rise and fall at intervals too slow for the movement to be noticed directly. A ridge may be breathing. A mound may be listening.

The fauna is abundant but not lively. Nothing darts. Nothing cries out in alarm. The creatures proceed according to old routes and old intervals, crossing one another without urgency. Their density gives the plain a hidden motion: a stirring beneath the plates, a procession of silhouettes in the dimness, the gradual opening and closing of things that had seemed to be stones.

The loudest sound is usually the sound of walking.

Boots—or bare feet—make brittle clicks against the mineral crust. Far away, some pressure passes through the ground and returns as a deep, almost inaudible note. At long intervals, the chamber produces a sound like a great bell struck under earth. It does not echo. The darkness seems to absorb it before it can travel.

There are signs of inhabitants who use tools, though their works are sparse. A low shelter stands beside one of the glowing seams, built from stacked slabs and bound with strips of leathery mineral. Its roof is flat and heavy, designed not against rain but against the slow settling of dust from above. Another structure is a ring of sharpened black pillars around a shallow pit. The pit is empty, yet the pillars have been polished by repeated handling.

Paths connect some of these places. They are not roads so much as cleared strips through the crust, each marked with small upright stones. The markers are simple, uneven, and made by hand. Their arrangement suggests practical knowledge: where the floor is thin, where the air is poisonous, where the embedded creatures are likely to wake. No settlement is large. Buildings appear singly or in little clusters, separated by enormous distances of dark terrain. Between them, the makers travel with lamps of dim mineral glow and hooked implements for testing the ground.

The rules of this place are simple and absolute.

Nothing grows. There are no leaves, roots, spores, or green surfaces. Life does not depend on light, and nothing here truly dies in the way surface organisms die. Bodies are dismantled, absorbed, or mineralized. Remains become part of the floor. The distinction between organism and geological formation is uncertain; some creatures are born from eggs, some from fractures, and some appear to have been present in the stone before the stone cooled.

Nothing changes quickly. Cracks remain open for centuries. Dust lies where it falls. A footprint may persist until the foot beneath it becomes part of the ground. The sharp ridges are young, but they are not actively eroding. They seem to have emerged all at once from a pressure event long finished, their faces still clean and angular because the chamber possesses no rain, no river, and almost no wind to wear them down.

Distance behaves strangely here. The chamber is vast enough to possess horizons, though no horizon can be seen. A glow may remain visible for days of travel, always appearing no nearer. Voices do not carry, but heat sometimes does. A living body can be sensed from far away as a slight alteration in the surrounding pressure, and the buried fauna respond to that disturbance with slow, collective attention.

At the center of the known plain stands a forest of stone needles, though “forest” is only an old word for any dense vertical growth. The needles rise from the floor in thousands, close-packed and perfectly still. They are warm to the touch. Between them, creatures cling in layers, some hanging upside down, some sealed into the gaps, some extending delicate feelers toward the invisible currents above. No path crosses this region. The inhabitants leave offerings at its edge: worked fragments of metal, carved plates, small heaps of colored dust.

Nothing is known to happen there.

That may be why the place is feared. Not because it changes, but because it does not. The darkness, the heat, the innumerable quiet bodies, and the unweathered stone all belong to an equilibrium older than memory. Even the living seem less like inhabitants than temporary motions within it.

The landscape does not wait for discovery.

It has already settled into itself.
