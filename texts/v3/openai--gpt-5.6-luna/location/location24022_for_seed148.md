The place is a city only in the way a lung is a city.

It occupies a low basin no wider than a few streets, though no streets remain visible. The ground is black, wet in patches, and unfinished. It has the appearance of stone before it has decided to become stone: soft shelves of mineral, translucent seams, crusts still lifting themselves out of the mud. Here and there, the earth has split open along clean, deliberate lines, exposing pale interiors that pulse faintly with blue-green light. The cracks are narrow but immeasurably deep. Cool air rises from them in slow breaths.

Almost everything else is plant.

Not a forest. Not an overgrown settlement. The plants have replaced the distinction between landscape and infrastructure. Columns of bundled stems stand where towers once might have been, their surfaces layered in bark, ceramic-looking lichen, and old metal plates absorbed into living tissue. Walls curve inward like the chambers of a seed. Bridges extend between them as braided roots, though some are clearly made of corroded girders that have been softened and re-grown. Windows occur at irregular heights, black openings sealed by membranes of leaf. Doorways bloom and close according to no visible schedule.

The settlement is small enough to cross in a few minutes, but its proportions are wrong. A single chamber may contain the vertical depth of a skyscraper. A narrow green corridor opens into a hollow so vast that its far side cannot be seen, yet the walls remain only a few steps apart. Space folds inward around the vegetation, making room without making distance. From one angle, the place is a hamlet of sheds and gantries. From another, it is the inside of a root.

The light is nearly absent. Day and night have little meaning here. Darkness lies in layers: soft black beneath the leaves, denser black inside the old factories, and a deep, polished darkness in the gaps where the world has failed to continue. The plants provide their own limited illumination. Some carry dim silver veins. Some exhale clouds of green sparks when touched. A low canopy of translucent fronds hangs overhead and gathers condensation, dropping it in slow, irregular taps. Water is scarce but never wholly absent. It appears as beads along cables, narrow streams in the grooves of roots, puddles in rusted machine housings, and sudden rain from leaves that have no visible source of moisture.

The air is cool enough to preserve everything. It smells of wet iron, crushed stems, mineral dust, and the sharp green odor of sap. Fog accumulates near the floor but does not drift naturally. It gathers in corners, forms around certain objects, and sometimes rises in columns as though remembering a vanished chimney.

There are industrial remnants throughout the growth.

A line of squat engines stands beneath a vaulted trellis, their flywheels locked in vines. Their pistons move occasionally, not with enough force to perform work, but with the slow, involuntary rhythm of sleeping animals. Each downward stroke draws moisture from the ground. Each return stroke produces one dim pulse in the surrounding roots. Pipes emerge from the soil and disappear into trunks. Gauges have been grown over by bark, their needles still trembling. Conveyor belts carry small white seeds from one dark aperture to another. They run continuously, though no one seems to load or unload them.

The machines do not appear abandoned. They appear incorporated.

A factory wall has become the outer skin of a giant stalk. Its brickwork can be seen beneath the plant tissue, but the bricks have been rearranged into spirals. A transformer hums inside a flower the size of a room. Cable bundles hang down like roots and terminate in pools of black water. When the pools ripple, lights briefly appear beneath the surface: a street grid, an unfamiliar coastline, the interior of a human eye.

The ecology is abundant, but not crowded. The emptiness is part of its design.

Between one living structure and the next are broad, silent intervals where nothing grows. These spaces are too clean to be clearings. They resemble missing portions of the world. Dust hangs motionless in them. Sound thins there. A dropped object may fall for several seconds before striking the ground, though the distance is visibly shallow. Animals avoid the emptier zones unless pursued.

The fauna is everywhere in the margins.

Small pale things move through the roots: many-legged grazers with soft bodies and transparent armor, feeding on the mineral crusts. They leave narrow wet trails that evaporate almost immediately. Dark birds roost upside down in the factory rafters, each one with a long, flexible neck folded into its chest. They do not call. Instead, they periodically open their beaks and release a brief absence of sound, a pocket in which every nearby noise vanishes.

There are fox-sized creatures with leaflike ears and jointed forelegs. They travel in silence through the undergrowth, stopping to listen to the breathing of walls. Their eyes reflect not light but places: an open field, a flooded basement, a room full of chairs. Larger forms move behind the green architecture, too slowly to be seen clearly. Their passage bends stems and makes condensation fall. Once, something with the outline of a deer steps through a wall without damaging it. Its body is made of roots on one side and exposed machinery on the other. It pauses beneath a dead lamp, then unfolds into several smaller animals and scatters.

Predation occurs without much noise. A cluster of bright, flowerlike organisms opens around a mothlike animal and closes it into darkness. A vine withdraws from a patch of ground just before a pale mouth rises from below. The struggle is often more architectural than physical. A plant will alter the shape of a corridor, narrowing it around its prey. A hunting animal will step into a doorway and emerge from a different doorway several meters away, having crossed an angle that should not exist.

The place is active at all times, but quietly so.

There is the constant minute movement of leaves turning toward unseen sources of warmth, of roots tightening around stone, of insects excavating walls. Condensation ticks from high surfaces. An engine coughs once and then remains silent for an hour. Somewhere in the settlement, a pump begins to work, pushing a thin flow of water uphill through a transparent stem. The water glows as it rises and falls again as rain inside another chamber.

Weather happens locally. A cloud may form beneath a roof. Rain may fall in a single shaft no wider than a person, soaking one patch of moss while the surrounding ground remains dry. Frost appears on warm machinery. Wind moves through only one corridor, carrying leaves in a circular current. Occasionally, a pressure change causes every plant in the settlement to bend toward the same unseen point, and for a moment the whole place resembles a crowd bowing.

The rules are not stable, but they are consistent enough to be learned.

Living things can absorb manufactured objects, but not all objects can be absorbed. Steel enters roots readily. Glass is rejected and slowly extruded in clear petals. Paper becomes skin. Electricity behaves like a scent: it attracts certain plants and causes others to close. Fire does not spread through vegetation. Instead, burning material produces roots, and the roots grow toward the flame.

Names have physical consequences. If a structure is called a bridge, it will eventually connect two things. If called a room, it will develop an inside. If called empty, it may become dangerous to occupy. The animals seem to understand these rules better than anything human-made. They never cross a threshold without pausing, and they never look directly at the spaces between buildings.

At the center of the settlement is a shallow basin filled with gray water. A single tree grows from it, though “tree” is an inadequate word. Its trunk is a bundled tower of roots, pipes, bones, and stairways, all fused into one pale column. The branches disappear into darkness above. They may continue for miles, or they may end just beyond sight.

Every few minutes, the tree sheds one leaf.

The leaf descends slowly, turning through the black air. Its surface is marked with diagrams: factory plans, animal anatomies, maps of rooms that do not exist yet. When it touches the water, the basin briefly reflects a different version of the settlement—sometimes flooded, sometimes burning, sometimes entirely human and empty. Then the reflection closes, and the water returns to gray.

No river enters the basin. No drain leaves it. Still, the water level changes.

The city is not decaying. Decay would imply that it had reached completion. It is being made, continuously and uncertainly, by plants, machines, weather, animals, and errors in reality. Its foundations are still deciding whether to be geological or architectural. Its streets are still growing memories of where they might lead. Its darkness is not the absence of light, but a material accumulating in the hollow spaces.

From outside, if outside exists, the place would look like a low mound of green-black vegetation under a cold sky.

From within, it feels like standing inside the first thought of a world.
