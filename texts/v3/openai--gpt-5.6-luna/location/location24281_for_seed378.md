The city stands high above the cloud shelf, on a broad plateau of black stone and pale mineral crust.

There are no trees, grasses, mosses, or even weeds in the cracks between the streets. The ground is too hot and too chemically rich for anything recognizable as a plant. Rainwater gathers anyway. It beads on every surface, runs in narrow shining threads down the slopes, and wells up through the pavement in clear, warm pulses. The plateau is wet not because storms are constant, but because the air is always close to saturation. Clouds form below the city, rise in torn white masses against the escarpments, and leave behind a fine condensation that settles over everything: roofs, rails, statues, machinery, and the exposed bones of the hills.

The sun is fierce. It turns the wet stone silver, makes shallow pools glare like sheets of glass, and bleaches the city’s concrete to a color almost blue. Heat rises from the streets in wavering columns, while water slips steadily through gutters and channels beneath them. In places, the rock itself weeps. Long mineral faces shine with moisture that has traveled up from deep underground, emerging through seams in the plateau as if the mountain is sweating.

The settlement is spread thinly across the open highland. It is not a dense city but a collection of districts separated by expanses of bare ground, evaporation basins, freight yards, and old quarry scars. Its buildings are squat and practical, built from poured gray aggregate, glazed brick, rusted steel, and panels of translucent ceramic. Most have sloping roofs, not for snow, but to catch and direct the endless films of water. Towers are rare. Where they exist, they are industrial: cooling stacks, pressure columns, lift shafts, and tall frameworks carrying pipes from one district to another.

The roads follow the natural folds of the terrain. They are raised above shallow drainage fields, their edges lined with channels and grated trenches. The largest avenues carry almost no traffic. A few electric haulers move slowly between warehouses, their wheels shedding water. Conveyor belts cross the empty distances on high gantries. Pump stations open and close their valves according to the water table. Far away, a factory exhales a pale column of vapor that hangs motionless in the brilliant air before thinning into the glare.

The machinery is old enough to have become part of the landscape. Rust has settled into the joints of bridges and staircases, but the main surfaces are constantly washed clean. Pipes descend into the ground, disappear under neighborhoods, and reemerge on distant ridges. Some are hot enough to steam. Others carry cold water gathered from the underside of clouds. The city’s infrastructure has the look of an enormous circulatory system built for a body that no longer remembers its original shape.

Sound is strangely absent.

There is no wind in the ordinary sense, though the air moves. It presses, loosens, condenses, and climbs. No leaves stir. No insects whine. The machinery has been insulated, its engines housed underground or muffled behind thick walls. From a distance, the only sounds are the soft ticking of cooling metal, the occasional rush of a sluice gate, and water tapping into a basin one drop at a time. Footsteps carry extraordinarily far over the empty stone, but even they seem to be absorbed by the brightness.

The animals are everywhere once the eye adjusts.

They gather in the wet margins of the city, beneath culverts, around warm outflows, along the abandoned rail lines. Most are low to the ground and pale, their bodies adapted to the mineral glare. Some resemble long-legged shore birds without feathers, balancing on jointed limbs over the drainage channels. Others are broad, many-segmented creatures that move beneath the surface film, leaving temporary raised lines in the water. There are translucent forms clinging to the undersides of bridges, opening and closing their bodies against the damp steel. They do not make calls. They communicate through ripples, flashes of color, and minute changes in posture.

Their abundance does not make the place feel alive in a familiar way. There are no nests in the roofs, no swarms above the water, no vegetation for anything to browse. The animals feed on bacterial films, industrial runoff, mineral deposits, and one another. Around the oldest pumping stations, they cluster in dense but silent congregations, their bodies arranged in rings around warm vents. At noon they retreat into shadows beneath platforms and drainage grates; in the cooler hours before dawn, they spread across the open ground to drink condensation.

Some of the larger ones cross the city without fear. A herd of six-limbed, shell-backed grazers may pass between warehouses, scraping the damp crust from the pavement with blunt mouths. They leave no visible tracks, only clean arcs where their feeding has polished the stone. High above, narrow-winged creatures circle the cooling towers, almost invisible against the white sky. They descend suddenly when a pressure valve opens, riding the upward column of heat.

Beyond the settlement, the plateau extends for many kilometers. Its surface is marked by shallow basins, ridges, and terraces left by ancient quarrying or by water moving slowly through the rock. The farther slopes descend toward a broad interior valley filled with cloud. Peaks rise through it like islands, dark and sharply cut, their upper faces bright with water. Below the cloud line there may be rivers, forests, or older settlements, but from the plateau they are hidden. Only the smell of wet stone rises from below.

The high ground is not barren in the sense of being dead. It is occupied by processes rather than growth. Water is always finding a route. Heat is always migrating upward. Mineral crusts form, crack, and are dissolved again. Animals arrive at the warm places and leave when the chemistry changes. Buildings sag, are reinforced, and become permanent. Old roads are flooded and rerouted. New channels are cut through streets. The city does not expand so much as adjust its position within the moving boundaries of heat and water.

After rain, the whole plateau changes character. The sky becomes even brighter, almost white, and every hollow fills with shallow water. The industrial districts appear to float above a continuous reflective surface. Steam lifts from vents in the ground. The animals emerge in numbers, crossing the roads and gathering in the basins. For a few hours, the city’s scattered structures are connected by glimmering paths, and the empty spaces between them seem less like emptiness than an immense, carefully managed reservoir.

At night the heat remains trapped in the stone. The sky clears above the cloud deck, crowded with stars, while the wet ground continues to shine with reflected lamps. There are few lights, because few are needed: the pale mineral surfaces hold the day for a long time. Pumps murmur under the streets. Water moves through hidden pipes. Somewhere in the dark, a large animal steps into a drainage channel, and the resulting ripple travels outward through the city, past concrete walls and silent factories, until it reaches the far edge of the plateau.
