The plateau begins where the lower world ends.

There is no gradual rise to it, no foothills worn into comfortable slopes. The land has been lifted in one enormous act and then left for ages: a continent-sized table of dark mineral, its rim broken into escarpments thousands of meters high. From below, if below can be seen through the permanent veils of dust, the upper country resembles a suspended slab. Its underside is lost in ocher atmospheric haze. Its upper surface lies beneath a broad, pale sky and stretches farther than weather can be followed.

The elevation has made its own climate. The air is thin but warm, dry enough to sting exposed metal and draw heat rapidly from shadow. At noon, the rock radiates a deep furnace warmth; at night, it gives that warmth back to a sky crowded with hard stars. There is no dew. No rain has ever fallen here in any recognizable form. The shallow basins contain old deposits of salts, carbon compounds, and glassy minerals, but nothing softens them. They are not mudflats or dry lakebeds. They are fields of powder-fine crystal, crusted over by centuries of wind and tremor.

The wind does not blow continuously. It arrives as a broad, almost imperceptible migration of air: a pale horizontal pressure moving across the plateau, lifting dust from one region and laying it down in another. A storm may take weeks to cross the country. Its front is visible long before it is felt, advancing as a dull veil over the horizon. When it reaches the city, loose grains tick against towers and grind slowly into their joints. The sound is less like weather than machinery running in another room.

The terrain is mostly open, but not simple. Long ridges of basaltic stone run for hundreds of kilometers, their sides polished by ancient atmospheric flow. Between them are mineral plains, rust-red in some light, violet-black in others, with seams of coppery ore rising in straight, improbable lines. There are towers of pale ceramic stone, formed where buried gases once escaped and baked the surrounding ground. Some stand alone. Others occur in immense orderly forests, though their arrangement is geological rather than biological: columns, fins, and hollow chimneys grouped around old vents.

The plateau’s greatest irregularity is the Furnace Belt, a chain of warm fractures crossing its center. They do not erupt. They breathe. Their heat leaks steadily through the stone, and around them the mineral crust has become glossy and dark, as if softened and frozen repeatedly. Thin plumes of sulfurous vapor rise from cracks. In the open air they spread low across the ground, too sparse to obscure the landscape but dense enough to stain the horizon yellow. At night, certain fissures glow faintly from within—not red, but a subdued blue-white, the color of hot ceramic under pressure.

Life gathers there.

Nothing grows. There are no leaves, roots, grasses, or stalks, and no green has ever established itself in the plateau’s soil. The abundant fauna is built instead from shells, filaments, articulated plates, and translucent membranes. Some creatures use heat gradients as others might use rivers. Some feed on the slow oxidation of exposed minerals. Others graze on colonies of black, hair-fine mineral threads that spread across warm rock in branching sheets. These threads are not plants, though from a distance their mats can resemble scorched moss. They are more like living circuitry: colonies of simple organisms that store charge, divide along crystal boundaries, and retreat underground when the surface becomes too bright.

The larger animals move slowly, but there are many of them. Herds of six-legged stoneback grazers cross the flats in lines so wide and patient that they alter the dust around them. Their bodies are low, armored, and jointed, with vents along the spine that open and close to regulate internal pressure. Their passage makes almost no sound. A herd may take an entire day to pass a fixed point, each animal placing its feet into the shallow depressions left by the one ahead.

At the thermal shelves, long ribbon-bodied predators lie folded against the ground. Their outer surfaces are matte and pale, reflecting the sun, while their undersides absorb warmth from the stone. They do not chase. They wait beside mineral-thread colonies, sensing electrical disturbances through their limbs. When a smaller creature crosses the field, the predator unfolds with startling speed, then settles again. The event is usually too far away to hear.

There are aerial forms as well, though the thin atmosphere does not support ordinary flight. They rise on buoyant envelopes filled with hot reactive gases, dragging filamentous bodies behind them like loose cables. In the distance they look like scraps of smoke caught beneath invisible ceilings. Their movements are slow enough to seem geological. At dusk, they descend toward the city’s heat plumes, where they gather in loose, silent congregations above the exhaust stacks.

The city is called Veyr by its inhabitants, though its name is older than any surviving language. It occupies a shallow mineral basin near the Furnace Belt, where the ground is stable and the deposits are rich. From far away it appears sparse: a scattering of towers separated by broad expanses of bare rock. Up close, the spaces reveal roads, rails, buried conduits, pressure gates, maintenance trenches, and old foundations beneath new ones. Veyr has been built in layers, not because the old city was abandoned, but because nothing here is ever fully removed.

Its buildings are squat where they must resist the wind and tall where they must reach the deep atmospheric currents. Industrial towers rise in clusters, ribbed with heat exchangers and wrapped in dull metal scaffolds. Conveyor bridges cross the basin at great heights. Their belts carry crushed stone, crystallized gases, and black mineral biomass from one processing district to another. Some factories work continuously, their cycles measured in months rather than hours. Great presses close once every few minutes. Kilns breathe in long pulses. Storage silos fill so gradually that their changing levels are visible only over several days.

The city is electrified, but not brightly. Light is used sparingly: amber guide-lines along roadways, white inspection lamps under gantries, blue status signals on sealed doors. At midday the ordinary sun is sufficient. At night, Veyr becomes a constellation of low, steady points scattered across the basin. There are no glowing windows in the usual sense. Most inhabited spaces are buried, pressure-sealed, and lit from within by broad panels that imitate the neutral color of daylight.

Traffic moves with the same patience as the landscape. Autonomous haulers cross the flats in widely spaced convoys, their wheels or tracked feet leaving dark parallel marks that remain for years. Maintenance machines stop for migrating herds. The animals have priority, not by law alone but because the roads were built around their routes. A vehicle that strikes one is considered damaged even if the machine survives.

For all its industry, Veyr is mostly quiet. The thin air carries voices poorly. The factories are insulated against their own vibration, and the broad distances swallow what escapes. Standing on the outer rim, one hears only the faint clicking of dust, the occasional low shift of a distant transport, and the dry creak of cooling metal. Beneath these sounds is a nearly continuous tremor from the plateau itself: deep pressure traveling through the lifted stone, too slow to be felt as movement but present in the soles of the feet.

The world changes here, but on a scale that resists attention. A ridge loses a millimeter. A vent migrates the width of a hand. A colony of mineral threads advances across a plain by several meters in a generation. Towers are repaired, expanded, and enclosed until their original purposes become archaeological questions. Roads fill with dust and are uncovered again by the next atmospheric cycle. The plateau does not feel dead. It feels settled—an ancient system that has found a balance between heat, pressure, industry, and the patient abundance of strange life.
