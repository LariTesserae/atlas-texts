The plateau begins abruptly, as if the land has been lifted out of the earth and left unfinished.

Its edge is a long procession of dark, newly exposed rock: black basalt, gray-green schist, faces cleanly split and still sharp enough to hold the light in narrow blades. There are few rounded stones. The ground has not yet learned the patience of weather. Cliffs stand in angular steps, gullies descend as straight cuts, and whole shelves of stone lean outward over the valleys below. In places, pale mineral seams shine through the dark rock like frozen streams.

The elevation is high enough to keep the air cool, though the sun is unusually bright. Light fills the plateau without much warmth. It comes down cleanly through a thin, almost colorless sky and settles on every wet surface: on the black stone, on the silver roofs of distant structures, on the dense vegetation crowding the cracks. Shadows are sharp and blue. Even at midday, the ravines retain a coldness that feels older than the rest of the landscape.

Water is not abundant, but it is everywhere in small, temporary forms. Moisture gathers in hollows along the rock shelves, beads beneath overhangs, and condenses on the undersides of broad leaves. A rain may fall for an hour and leave no river behind, only hundreds of darkened channels and bright pools caught in the stone. Most of these disappear slowly into moss, gravel, and porous black earth. A few join into narrow streams that follow the new fractures downhill, their courses too young to have become smooth.

The vegetation is thickest where the rock breaks. Ferns fill the gullies in overlapping layers, their fronds bright as painted metal. Low shrubs with red stems grow in mats over the slopes, and long grasses lean together in dense, wind-combed sheets. Moss covers the northern faces, not as a soft carpet but as a swelling green crust that climbs over stones, bolts, drainage pipes, and the foundations of old machinery. In the open stretches, the plants are low and tough, shaped by cold and exposure. Their leaves are waxy, dark, and crowded close to the ground.

There are no tall forests. Nothing here has had enough time—or enough shelter—to become immense. The largest plants are isolated stands of pale, jointed growth that resemble young trees but carry their leaves in concentric fans. They rise from the folds between ridges, their upper branches all turned toward the same invisible direction. When the weather changes, they turn slowly, almost imperceptibly, as if following a light source beyond the sky.

The plateau is crossed by the remains of an industrial system. It is not ruined exactly. Much of it still functions, though the purpose is difficult to understand.

A narrow rail line runs along the highest shelf, its tracks disappearing beneath vegetation and reappearing farther on. Small electric cars move over it at long intervals. They have no visible drivers and make almost no sound. Their wheels are enclosed, their motors buried under layers of insulation. From a distance they resemble black insects traveling patiently along a silver thread.

There are pumping stations in the ravines, each one a squat concrete building with a single cylindrical tower. Pipes emerge from the rock, pass through walls, and vanish again under the ground. Some carry water. Others pulse faintly with a dull amber light. The machinery inside never seems to start or stop; it merely continues, with the immense, indifferent steadiness of something designed to operate longer than any living memory.

Here and there, transmission towers stand among the plants. Their cables sag between the ridges, but not with gravity. They hang in perfectly shallow curves, as if the distance between towers has been measured against a different force. On bright days, a faint radiance travels along them in slow intervals. It takes several minutes to pass from one tower to the next.

Buildings are scattered rather than gathered. A control hut on a rise. A worker’s barracks half-sunk into the moss. A concrete arch with no wall attached to it. Their windows are dark or clouded. Some doors open onto rooms filled with clean water to the ankles. Others reveal staircases descending only a few steps before meeting solid rock.

The industrial marks do not dominate the place. They have been absorbed into it. Grass pushes through grating. Ferns grow from ventilation shafts. A rusted crane supports a colony of white, branching lichen that glows faintly in shadow. The oldest pipes have become warm corridors for roots, and the plants above them form unusually dense, dark-green bands. In some areas, the boundary between engineered channel and natural ravine has disappeared entirely.

The air is quiet.

There is no constant wind, only the occasional movement of it through the grasses, a broad soft exhalation that travels across the plateau and fades. Water ticks from ledges. Condensation gathers and falls inside the hollow pipes. Somewhere far below, a turbine turns with a sound too low to be heard directly, but the rock beneath one’s feet gives a slow, regular tremor.

Birds are rare. Small pale insects appear after rain and vanish before the ground dries. Now and then a long-legged animal can be seen on a distant slope, standing perfectly still among the grasses. Its outline is difficult to retain in the eye. It may have antlers, or branches behind it, or nothing at all. Nothing hunts in the open. Nothing calls.

The landscape’s strangeness is quiet and procedural. Water sometimes collects on the uphill side of objects, even when the surface slopes away. Shadows under the transmission lines point in different directions from the shadows of the towers. A pool may reflect a sky that is slightly darker than the one overhead. These things do not announce themselves. They are noticed only after prolonged stillness.

Most changes occur slowly. A channel deepens by a finger’s width over a season. A pipe disappears beneath moss. A concrete wall tilts another degree toward the valley. The electric cars continue their patient routes. The plants thicken around everything.

Beyond the plateau, the land opens into a broad succession of ridges and green-black basins, all cut from the same recent upheaval. Brightness rests over the entire region. There is no haze, no city, no obvious settlement—only the scattered infrastructure, the dense growth, the exposed stone, and the long, nearly silent work of water, machinery, and time.
