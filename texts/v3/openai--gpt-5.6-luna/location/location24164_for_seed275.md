The city lies beneath the earth in a chamber so broad that its far edges are softened into haze.

There is no water here. No streams under the streets, no seepage darkening the walls, no condensation beading on the leaves. The ground is a dry mineral floor: gray, ocher, and faintly metallic, split into plates where the chamber is still settling. In some places it has the unfinished look of stone that has not decided what shape to become. Ridges rise only a few meters, then stop. Raw seams of pale ore show between the paving slabs. Dust gathers in the angles but is not blown into dunes; the air is too still for that.

Everything alive is plant.

The vegetation has taken the place of soil, timber, masonry, insulation, plumbing, and much of the city’s machinery. Low mats of black-green growth spread over the mineral floor, their surfaces hard and matte like felted metal. Tall stems emerge from them in widely separated stands, pale as old bone or blue-gray as gunmetal. Their leaves do not hang in the usual way. They project in layered fans, broad enough to form roofs, bridges, awnings, and suspended walkways. The leaves are cool, leathery, and faintly warm at their veins. When cut, they do not bleed water or sap but release a dry yellow vapor with the smell of crushed pepper and hot stone.

The settlement has been built among these plants, and from them.

Its streets are corridors between planted towers, mostly straight, mostly empty. Some are paved with slabs of compressed rootwood, though rootwood is a misleading name: the material is closer to a ceramic grown in darkness, with a grain running through it in branching electrical patterns. The buildings stand apart from one another, leaving broad mineral squares between them. Their walls are assembled from brick-sized blocks of harvested stalk, kiln-hardened and fitted around steel frames. Their windows are made from translucent leaf membranes stretched across rigid lattices. Behind them, the city glows with the muted order of industry: indicator lights, moving belts, elevator cages, the occasional orange flare of a furnace.

The tallest structures are not buildings but cultivation shafts. They descend into the floor and rise toward the unseen ceiling, carrying bundles of plant tissue between levels. Their outer surfaces are ribbed with iron catwalks and maintenance ladders. Along them, conveyor arms move slowly, carrying clipped fronds to processing halls. The fronds become insulation, fuel, paper, cable sheathing, pharmaceuticals, filters, and food. Nothing in the city is fully dead. Even waste is fed into sealed beds where pale fungal-plant hybrids break it down and return it as mineralized growth.

The chamber is illuminated as though it were an ordinary day somewhere aboveground, though no sun is visible. A diffuse white light falls from the ceiling, evenly spread across the whole immense vault. It has no obvious source. The shadows are soft and short. They gather beneath machinery and beneath the larger leaves, where the darkness has a greenish cast. At intervals, the plant canopy opens into circular clearings, and in those places the light brightens sharply, as if the buried world has a sky after all.

The ceiling is high enough to disappear. It is crossed by roots, support struts, pipes, and great horizontal fronds that have grown across the darkness like suspended continents. Some of the plants have reached the upper rock and continued into it, making their own channels. Others have stopped midway, their tips sealed and dormant. Thin pale shoots are appearing everywhere—in cracks, beneath paving, from the sides of old towers. The city is not complete. It is still being made.

This is visible in the infrastructure. New streets end abruptly at raw ground. Factory walls have been extended by mismatched generations of greenish blocks. A railway runs for several kilometers through the settlement, then disappears into a low arch where the tunnel mouth is still edged with scaffolding and uncured plant material. Survey markers stand in the open squares, red-painted stakes surrounded by circles of chalk. At the chamber’s western side, whole districts have been outlined but not built: foundations, cable trenches, and rows of young structural plants waiting to thicken.

The air is cool and dry. It carries almost no humidity, yet it is not harsh. The plants maintain themselves through chemistry that does not require liquid water: they draw gases from the stone, exchange mineral dust through their roots, and circulate their internal fluids as dense gels and vapor. Every so often a vent opens somewhere in the floor, releasing a breath of cool, scentless air from deeper strata. The plants lean toward it—not visibly, not all at once, but enough that over weeks their avenues curve in its direction.

There are animals, though they are mostly small and difficult to see.

Dry-bodied pollinators move between the tall flowering structures, their wings clicking rather than buzzing. They resemble moths made of folded paper and dark wire. They emerge at the beginning of the artificial day and vanish into cracks when the lights dim. Along the mineral margins, long jointed grazers feed on the low mats. Their mouths are fitted with rasping plates, and their bodies are armored against the abrasive dust. Larger creatures inhabit the spaces beneath the city: six-legged browsing things with narrow heads, brought up through freight gates to clear invasive growth from the rail lines. At night—or whatever the city calls the interval when the lights are reduced—they return below, leaving only many-toed tracks in the dry substrate.

Predators are present too. They are narrow, patient animals that mimic the vertical stems of the vegetation. One may stand among a row of plants for hours, indistinguishable except for the slight turning of its head. Their movements are quiet and economical. The city’s inhabitants have learned to mark safe corridors with strips of silver leaf, which predators dislike. In the less settled areas, warning bells are unnecessary; instead, the plants themselves contract into bright, rigid postures when a hunting animal passes nearby.

Most of the city’s sound is mechanical and distant.

A compressor ticks behind a wall. A chain runs over a pulley. Somewhere far below, a drill works in slow, measured bursts, and the vibration travels through the floor more than through the air. The factories do not roar. Their machinery is insulated, and their engines have been designed around the living materials they process. A transport carriage glides along its rail with a dry whisper. Large leaves flex overhead with a sound like paper being folded once.

Between these sounds are long intervals of stillness.

In the open squares, a person can hear their own clothing move. They can hear the fine crackle of mineral grains settling beneath their feet. They can hear a plant opening: a brief series of tiny clicks followed by the release of a cooler pocket of air. In the cultivated districts, workers pass without speaking, connected to their tools by hoses, straps, and bundles of living fiber. Faces are lit by the pale leaves and the steady white illumination above.

The city’s rules are botanical before they are civic.

No structure may be built without a root allowance. Every foundation must leave space for growth, and every road must include a planned route for future stems. Cutting a mature plant is regulated more strictly than dismantling a house. A tree-tower is considered both infrastructure and citizen: it has a registry, a maintenance schedule, and a history of prior forms. Some are old enough to have grown around abandoned buildings, enclosing them in thick green walls. Others are young and aggressively vertical, supported by temporary steel frames until their tissues become load-bearing.

Fire is forbidden except in designated furnaces. Open flame kills the air-filtering growth and can trigger rapid, dangerous flowering. Water is equally forbidden—not because it is scarce, but because it is disruptive. The plants do not know what to do with it. A spill causes tissues to swell, split, and ferment. The city has no drains. It has absorption beds, vapor traps, and mineral sinks.

Beyond the settled avenues, the chamber becomes increasingly unfinished. The floor rises in shallow shelves. Raw stems push through unprocessed stone. Factories thin into survey camps and isolated maintenance sheds. The light remains normal, but the order of the city loosens. Plants grow at stranger angles. Some are transparent, displaying internal currents of amber gel. Others produce leaves that resemble ladders, hooks, or jointed fingers. Machinery has been abandoned where the vegetation overtook it, and now belts move on their own as tendrils turn their wheels.

Farther still, near the chamber’s edge, the distinction between landscape and construction disappears. A half-built industrial district merges with a forest of structural plants. Steel pylons emerge from root masses. Ventilation towers are wrapped in flowering vines that have no flowers but open into complex mechanical apertures. New ground is rising beneath everything, extruded slowly from the dark by colonies of mineral-feeding growth. The city is not merely occupying the cavern. It is manufacturing a habitable world inside it.

And beneath the streets, beneath the factories, beneath the oldest planted foundations, something is still moving.

The vibrations are too slow to be machinery. They pass through the stone in broad pulses, causing dust to lift from the paving and leaves to turn in unison. Each pulse is followed, somewhere in the distance, by the sound of new roots cracking open the mineral floor.

The settlement pauses to listen.

Then the conveyors resume, the pollinators return to the flowers, and another district begins to grow.
