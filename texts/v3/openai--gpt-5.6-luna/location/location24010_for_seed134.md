The place is a high basin beneath the sea.

Not a trench, and not a plain: a small, newly broken bowl of black mineral rising from the surrounding ocean like the summit of a drowned mountain. Its rim is only a few hundred meters across, but the water above it continues upward into blue distance. From the basin floor, the surface is invisible. Daylight arrives anyway—filtered through an immense depth into a steady, lucid gray—so that everything is clearly seen, though nothing is brightly lit. There are no shadows sharp enough to trust.

The basin’s walls are young and angular. They have not yet learned the softened shapes of old stone. Ridges stand in splintered ranks, their faces glazed with basalt, salt, and pale metallic deposits. Some surfaces are red-brown with iron. Others shine like wet graphite. Cracks run through the floor in straight, improbable lines, not made by tools but by cooling and pressure. Along these cracks, the earth exhales.

The water is cold enough to slow the hands and stiffen the joints, but not cold enough to freeze. It presses from every direction with the quiet force of a closed fist. Currents move through the basin in broad, gradual turns. A whole cycle may take a day, or a month, or longer: dense water descending through one fissure, warmed water rising through another, the basin breathing with the patient rhythm of a machine too large to have been built.

There are no plants. Nothing waves in the current. Nothing roots, branches, flowers, or makes a leaflike attempt at catching the dim light.

Instead, life occupies every available surface.

The black shelves are furred with pale organisms no larger than fingernails, though their bodies are too jointed and metallic to be called shells. They cling in overlapping scales, packed so tightly that the rock seems to have grown a second, moving skin. When disturbed, they open and close in sequence, exposing concentric mouths or polished internal plates. The motion travels across them like a signal through a factory floor.

Between them live long, translucent animals shaped like cables, their bodies gathered in loose coils around warm cracks. They do not swim so much as unspool. Their heads are blunt and eyeless; their sides pulse with blue-white organs that ignite one after another. Thousands of these pulses pass through the basin each minute, giving the impression of a vast electrical diagram being slowly redrawn.

Larger creatures occupy the narrow spaces between the rocks. They are dense, muscular, and nearly colorless, with fins folded close to their bodies like the blades of industrial fans. Their movement is economical. A single flex carries them across the basin, where they wedge themselves into gaps and wait. Some bear clusters of rotating disks along their backs. These disks grind against the current, extracting something from it—heat, charge, dissolved metal, perhaps simply the force of water itself. The animals are not using machines. Their bodies are machines, evolved past any distinction between organ and mechanism.

The whole basin is loud.

Water booms against the rim in slow, subterranean impacts. Vents roar from below, not with the explosive fury of volcanoes but with the continuous sound of immense pumps. Thin sheets of gas rise from the cracks and collapse into curtains of silver bubbles. The bubbles strike the overhanging stone with a sound like rain on a roof, though there is no roof and no rain.

Everywhere, smaller sounds layer over the greater ones: clicking plates, rasping mouths, the hollow knock of bodies against rock, the rapid metallic chatter of swarms changing direction. Some creatures produce tones so low they are felt in the ribs before they are heard. Others answer with sharp, bell-like notes. The basin seems to be communicating with itself, though no message is ever urgent.

Nothing here moves quickly. Even predation is gradual.

A broad, many-limbed animal may settle over a colony and remain there for hours while its feeding surfaces open by fractions. The colony does not flee. It closes, reopens elsewhere, and slowly withdraws into the cracks. A cloud of small silver bodies may take an entire afternoon to pass from one wall to another. Their density makes the water seem full of drifting filings, but each individual keeps a precise distance from every other, as if held in an invisible lattice.

The industrial character of the place comes from this coordination. There are no towers, cables, roads, furnaces, or manufactured objects. There is not even a tool worn smooth by use. Yet the basin performs all the functions of an enormous plant.

Living bodies sort minerals from the water. Others crush them, dissolve them, or transport them toward the vents. Heat is gathered and redistributed. Pressure is converted into motion. Waste from one species becomes feedstock for another. The smallest creatures maintain the surfaces on which the larger creatures depend. Nothing is assembled, but everything is processed.

At the center of the basin stands a single formation: a column of pale stone thrust upward from the floor, sharp-edged and unfinished, like a broken tooth. It is not especially tall—perhaps thirty meters—but the surrounding space is so compact that it dominates the entire landscape. Around it, the current turns in a slow spiral. Animals gather along its sides in dense, shifting bands. Their bodies scrape the mineral surface clean, then cover it again with luminous residue.

The column emits a steady vibration. It is too regular to be geological and too deep to be called a song. The creatures respond to it without appearing to listen. When the vibration changes, they all alter their posture. A minute later, the cracks brighten. An hour later, the water begins to circulate in a new direction.

This is the closest thing the place has to a rule: the basin is always becoming more itself.

Fresh mineral layers accrete along the vents. Edges sharpen as they fracture. New cracks open and remain raw and glassy. Animals colonize them almost immediately, packing the empty seams with pale bodies. Nothing has had time to erode into familiarity. The landscape is young, but its life is abundant; it feels less like a wilderness than like the first production floor of a world that has not yet decided what it is manufacturing.

Above the rim, the ocean continues without boundary. Below, the black rock continues downward into warmth and pressure. Within the basin, every surface is occupied, every current used, every sound absorbed into the general working noise.

The place is not dead, despite the absence of green.

It is busy, cold, submerged, and patient—an unfurnished chamber of the deep where biology has learned to resemble industry, and where the machines are still growing.
