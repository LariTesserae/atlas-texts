The high country begins above the last ordinary trees, though “begins” is not quite the right word. It has no edge. The land rises for days through blue-gray weather until the horizon loses interest in distinguishing mountain from cloud, and then continues upward in broad, ancient shelves.

This is a plateau the size of a continent, or perhaps a continent that has been pressed flat beneath an invisible hand. Its ridges are worn almost smooth. Peaks survive only where the stone has remembered how to resist. Their sides descend in enormous, shallow folds, scoured and polished by ages of ice, wind, and something slower than both. From a distance the whole landscape resembles the sleeping body of a vast animal, though no animal has ever lived here, and the resemblance may be a failure of distance rather than a property of the land.

The rock is dark, green-veined, and old beyond useful measurement. In places it has softened into terraces of slate-colored soil. In others it rises in shelves as regular as masonry, each one hundreds of meters wide, each face marked by long vertical grooves. These grooves are not quite cracks. They are the traces left by water that fell upward, or by roots that grew through stone and were later removed. No geologist could say which. The exposed surfaces carry a pale mineral bloom, a powder like frost that does not melt. It gathers in the corners of ruined streets and along the bases of cliffs, where it occasionally lifts itself into the air and drifts against the prevailing wind.

Moisture comes rarely and unevenly. Most of the plateau is dry beneath its cold, thin atmosphere, but the land is full of hidden dampness. Every few weeks, or every few years—the interval changes depending on where one stands—a gray condensation settles over a valley. It beads on every surface, fills the hollows in the rock, and hangs from the vegetation in enormous, trembling drops. There are basins where the water remains after the mist has gone, narrow black pools threaded together by channels no wider than a hand. Some have no visible source. Some drain uphill.

Rain is a local event. A person might stand beneath a clear sky while, three kilometers away, a vertical curtain of water falls from a cloud that has formed directly against the ground. The rain makes almost no sound. It touches the soil and vanishes, or sinks sideways into the stone, leaving behind temporary green zones that can be seen from the far ridges: dark, spreading stains of growth that expand for a day and then settle back into the plateau.

The vegetation is surprisingly rich, but it does not gather in forests. It occupies the empty spaces with patience.

Low mats of blue-black moss cover the sheltered faces of the terraces. Pale reeds grow in the intermittent channels, though their roots are often exposed in air above the water, forming delicate lattices that resemble the branching of frost. There are broad, ground-hugging leaves with translucent veins. They fold shut at noon, not from light but from the approach of shadows. There are tall, rigid stalks clustered around old wells and broken foundations, each stalk capped by a dark cup that collects condensation. When the cups overflow, the water travels down the plant in one continuous sheet and enters the ground without wetting the surrounding dust.

Nothing here moves quickly. The plants lean, withdraw, flower, and shed according to pressures too gradual for ordinary observation. Whole meadows change arrangement between one visit and the next. A patch of silver fronds may have migrated fifty meters over the course of a season, leaving no trail except a faint softening of the stone. Root systems knit through abandoned roads and sometimes raise them in long, shallow waves.

There are no birds in the sky, no insects in the grass, no tracks in the mud except those made by weather and the occasional passing person. The flowers open without pollinators. Seed pods split and release their contents into the wind, but the seeds do not always fall. Some remain suspended for months, held in place by invisible currents, until the air itself decides to become soil.

The silence is not empty. It is an immense, well-insulated quiet, interrupted by processes rather than creatures.

At long intervals, the plateau exhales. A whole slope settles by a fraction of an inch, and the sound arrives minutes later as a low, distant folding. Mineral crust cracks with a noise like a fingernail drawn across glass. The reeds click together in vast stands when the invisible water beneath them changes direction. During condensation, thousands of droplets fall from leaf to leaf, but the sound is too soft to be heard directly; instead, one becomes aware of a faint alteration in the air, as if the world has begun whispering behind a wall.

The built places are scattered and enormous.

Cities stand along the old terraces, but they are not crowded cities. Each contains more empty space than structure: long avenues running between isolated towers, plazas broad enough to hold weather, bridges spanning dry channels, and neighborhoods in which the roofs have fallen away to reveal gardens of cold vegetation. The settlements appear to have been laid out for a population far greater than any that remains, or for inhabitants whose bodies required extraordinary distances between one another.

Their construction is primitive in its tools but patient in its labor. Stone blocks were shaped by abrasion, wedges, fire, and water. Timber-like growths were bent into frames and bound with braided root. Walls are made from slabs fitted so precisely that no mortar is visible, though the seams occasionally open and close with the temperature. There are towers of stacked slate, leaning slightly in different directions, connected by elevated walkways. Under the walkways, pale plants grow in uninterrupted bands, as if the city were a trellis built for them.

The people who made these places are absent, but their organization remains. Channels divide the streets according to a logic no longer understood. Doors are set into walls far above ground level, reached by stairways that end in open air. Courtyards contain stone basins that fill only during the rarest weather. At intersections stand waist-high markers covered in shallow incisions. The markings change when no one is watching. Sometimes they form maps. Sometimes they describe a person who has not yet arrived.

In the largest settlement, a road passes through a row of buildings and emerges several kilometers away, though the buildings along it are only a few steps deep. From the outside, their walls are continuous. Inside, their rooms contain different seasons. One may be full of dry summer light and the smell of dust; the next is dark with condensation, its walls sweating; another opens onto a field that cannot be seen from any window or doorway elsewhere in the city.

The city’s ruins do not decay in the ordinary direction. Broken walls become more stable. Fallen stones settle into structures that are older and more complete than the buildings they came from. A collapsed arch may be found standing again after a year, but in a place thirty meters from its original foundation. Staircases sometimes lose their upper steps while gaining lower ones. A house gradually becomes a hill, then a hill with rooms, then a house belonging to a different century.

The plateau has been eroding for so long that erosion here resembles completion. Rivers no longer cut deep valleys; they polish existing contours. Wind removes only what the land has already agreed to surrender. Cliffs have become ramps, mountains have become folds, and the oldest ruins have merged with the natural shelves until it is difficult to tell whether a terrace was carved by glaciers, by hands, or by a pressure exerted before either existed.

Yet the landscape remains active.

Weather moves across it in immense, slow systems. A bank of cloud may take three days to cross the horizon, dragging a shadow the color of iron. Frost advances over a valley in branching geometries, pauses at the edge of a wall, and then continues through the wall’s interior. In some districts, gravity strengthens and weakens in cycles. Moss grows upward during the weak phases, hanging from the undersides of bridges like green smoke. Loose stones rise a few centimeters, hover, and settle again without sound.

Distances are unreliable but not randomly so. They bend around old structures, pools of water, and concentrations of vegetation. A person walking toward a tower may reach it in an hour, or may spend a week approaching while the tower remains the same apparent size. Looking back, the road behind may have become a river, a stair, or a narrow strip of night.

The sky is usually an ordinary, washed-out blue beneath a thin veil of cloud. Light behaves normally until it touches something ancient. Then shadows may detach from their owners. Reflections may show the landscape at another hour. In the dark windows of a ruined hall, one can sometimes see weather occurring on the far side of the world—or on a version of the plateau that has not yet been eroded.

At night, there are few lights. The city’s mineral seams emit a muted green radiance when damp. Pools gather a gray sheen from the stars, though the stars themselves are not always visible overhead. Some plants open and reveal interiors full of dim, steady points, as if each one contains a small map of the surrounding plateau. They close again when approached.

Nothing hunts. Nothing flees. There is no animal presence to organize the silence around. The living world here is rooted, mineral, and distributed. The vegetation competes by altering moisture, stone, and time. One plant may cause a wall to remember its original shape. Another may draw warmth from the ground and leave a circle of permanent frost. A third spreads through abandoned streets, not as a vine but as a gradual correction: wherever it grows, crooked things become straight, and straight things begin to curve toward it.

The vastness makes every settlement feel provisional. A city might contain foundations, reservoirs, roads, gardens, and halls enough for a civilization, yet from the nearest ridge it appears as a handful of pale marks in an ocean of rock and weather. Between cities are distances that cannot be crossed by ordinary travel, though old milestones insist they once could be. Some paths pass through valleys that exist only when mist is present. Others lead into the blank spaces between one arrangement of the world and the next.

The plateau is cold without being barren, quiet without being still. It feels less like a place at the end of history than a place after history has hardened into geology. Life continues in sparse green concentrations. Water appears where the land permits it. Ruins hold their shapes, lose them, and hold different shapes. The sky crosses overhead. Far away, a mountain slowly flattens, and the sound of it will arrive tomorrow.
