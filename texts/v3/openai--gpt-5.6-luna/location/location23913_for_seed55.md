The landscape lies beneath a permanent sea.

Not an ocean with islands in it, but a whole country drowned to its foundations: a low, continental expanse of water, cold from surface to floor, extending farther than the eye could ever measure. There is no horizon. Above is darkness thickened by depth and weather. Below is darkness laid across stone.

The water is clear only in the way old glass is clear. Near the surface, if such a surface exists, there may be a gray dilution of daylight, but it does not reach here. Far below, the world is held in a blue-black suspension. Every object begins nearby and ends invisibly. Distances are judged by the time it takes for a sound to return, though sounds return weakly, after traveling through galleries, flooded courts, and the hollow undersides of roads.

The floor is not natural seabed. It is made.

Everything beneath the water was once shaped by hand: broad slabs fitted without mortar, terraces descending by fractions of an inch, immense foundations packed with rubble, long walls laid flat across the drowned plain. The builders used stone, fired clay, dark timber turned nearly mineral by age, and a pale concrete made from shell and ash. No piece is delicate. The structures were made for permanence, and permanence has reduced them to a single, low geometry.

There are no towers left. No spires, roofs, or walls stand high enough to interrupt the general level. Time and water have worn them down, softened their corners, filled their rooms, and spread their materials outward. Former buildings are now broad shelves under the growth: plazas became shallow basins, streets became channels, houses became mounds with their interiors exposed. The whole country has settled into a final form, not ruined so much as completed by erosion.

Here and there, the old construction rises into long ridges. These are causeways, perhaps, or the crowns of buried avenues. They run straight for miles beneath the water, their surfaces covered in a dense growth of submerged vegetation. Other lines intersect them at measured intervals, forming grids visible only from above, if above can be reached. The roads do not lead anywhere now. They simply continue, crossing one another in the dark with the calm certainty of things that were built to outlast their users.

The vegetation is immense and patient.

It grows from every seam. Green-black fronds stream in the weak current. Long ribbon plants lie flat against the stone, then lift together when the water moves. Pale stems rise from courtyards and spread into layered canopies, their leaves broad and translucent, their undersides crowded with smaller leaves. In the old drainage channels, mats of moss and bladderless waterweed form thick carpets. Root systems have entered cracks and widened them, but only slowly. They do not tear the masonry apart. They occupy it.

There are groves where the vegetation has covered whole districts. The trunks are short and thick, more like pillars than trees, rising from planted squares or from the mouths of ancient wells. Their crowns drift overhead in darkness, forming a low ceiling of leaves. The plants have no flowers that open brightly, no colors except shades of green, brown, gray, and the occasional white of a new root. Nothing flashes. Nothing calls attention to itself.

There is no animal life.

No fish move through the avenues. No worms loosen the soil. No shells cling to the walls, no insects hover at the surface, no hidden eyes reflect the dimness. The plants grow without pollinators, spreading by runners, spores, division, and the patient release of seeds into the current. Their reproduction is slow enough to be almost geological. A new stand may take a century to cross a courtyard. A fallen frond remains where it lands, softening into dark sediment and feeding the next generation.

The absence of animals makes the water feel unusually large. A field of vegetation can extend uninterrupted for miles, its leaves moving in a single response to a distant current. Nothing flees from a passing pressure wave. Nothing snaps or rustles with intent. The growth sways, settles, and sways again.

Most of the time, the only sound is water moving through the made world.

It passes under slabs and through cracked retaining walls. It gathers in chambers and releases itself through narrow openings with a low, continuous breath. In some places, the drowned city amplifies the smallest motion: a current entering a long tunnel becomes a tone, almost below hearing, while a shifting bed of leaves produces a soft, granular hiss. There are no sudden noises. Even the collapse of a wall happens gradually, stone sliding down stone until the sound is absorbed.

The currents are weak and broad. They do not scour the land anymore. They travel along channels that have been worn into smoothness, following routes established long before the flooding. A change in direction can take years to become noticeable. Water rises through one district, withdraws from another, and leaves behind a thin film of silt that settles over steps and inscriptions. The place changes, but only at the scale of generations.

Cold is present everywhere. It is not a sharp cold, because there is no wind and little exposed air, but a deep, even temperature that enters the material of the ruins. Stone, clay, root, and water all seem to possess the same chill. In the deeper chambers, the cold is almost dry in its sensation, despite the surrounding water: a stillness that numbs thought and makes time feel physical.

The flooding may have come from the sea, from a broken basin, or from a long failure of the old waterworks. There are signs of all three possibilities, and none need be true. At the edge of the drowned expanse, if one travels far enough, the floor begins to slope upward by degrees so small they are difficult to perceive. The constructions continue there as well. Fields of stone, levees, cisterns, and low platforms extend into the gloom, all covered by the same water and the same vegetation. The built world does not end at a border. It fades into increasing depth.

The old rules remain visible in the arrangement of things.

Channels are always paired. Steps descend in exact counts even where the destination is gone. Walls turn at repeated angles. Every broad basin has an outlet, though many outlets are blocked by root masses or silt. There are thresholds beneath the water, doors that still open into empty rooms, and narrow passages too small for a person but large enough for the original flow of water. The builders seem to have trusted gravity, repetition, and maintenance. Their work is plain, practical, and made with tools that left marks: chisel grooves, drilled holes, beaten edges, hand-sized impressions in wet clay.

No mechanism moves now. No lamps burn. No metal gleams. The technology of the place is exhausted and submerged, but not mysterious. A crane was a wooden frame. A lock was a fitted gate. A road was stone. A reservoir was a reservoir. The wonder lies only in the extent of the labor: a whole visible world made by simple means, then left to endure after the people disappeared.

In the deepest sections, vegetation grows from the ceilings of flooded halls. Its roots hang downward in pale curtains, and its leaves spread along the upper surfaces where faint nutrients collect. Beneath them are benches, pillars, collapsed staircases, and rows of identical niches. The rooms are dense with presence but empty of inhabitants. Plants fill the openings; water fills the plants; stone fills the distance between them.

Sometimes a current carries a sheet of leaves through a great avenue. It moves as one dark body, brushing walls and passing beneath broken arches. Then the avenue is empty again.

There is no dramatic center to the landscape. No palace, mountain, abyss, or surviving monument rises above the rest. The place has been flattened by age into continuity. Its grandeur is horizontal: one constructed district dissolving into another, one drowned garden becoming a reservoir, one road vanishing under roots and reappearing far away. The scale is too vast for a single ruin. It is a submerged civilization reduced to terrain.

In this world, water does not destroy quickly. It preserves, carries, fills, and slowly revises. Plants do not conquer stone so much as inherit it. Darkness is not an interruption of the landscape but its permanent climate. Quiet is not emptiness; it is the accumulated sound of a place that has stopped expecting anything to arrive.

Far overhead, unseen weather may move across the sea. Its pressure travels downward as long, faint pulses. The vegetation bends together. Silt lifts from the terraces and settles again. Somewhere, an ancient gate shifts by the width of a finger.

Then the water becomes still, and the drowned country waits in its final shape.
