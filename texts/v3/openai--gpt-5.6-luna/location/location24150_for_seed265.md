The plain begins where the last hard ground gives up.

It is broad and nearly level, a low basin filled with black water and pale mud, though “plain” is not quite right. The surface has the unfinished look of something recently uncovered: ridges with knife-clean edges, plates of cooled mineral thrust through the silt, long angular shelves that have not yet been softened by weather. Water lies everywhere, but rarely in the same form twice. It gathers in shallow tarns, threads itself through cracks, beads on vertical faces, and rises in the hollows with no visible source. In places the ground sweats steadily, making little clear wells in the dark.

There is no sky.

Above the basin hangs a ceiling of darkness so complete that it cannot be called cloud. It has depth, perhaps, but no stars and no discernible surface. Now and then a dimness passes through it—not light exactly, but a loosening of the black—and the distant terrain appears in flat gray layers, as if seen through several sheets of wet glass. The dimness fades before anything can be examined. Shadows do not respond to it. They remain where they are, attached to rocks, machines, reeds, and animals according to older rules.

The warmth is constant and close. It comes from the water and from below the ground. The air smells of iron, wet stone, hot rubber, and the faint medicinal sharpness of ozone. Mist hangs knee-high over the basin, thickening around the machinery and thinning over open water. It does not drift with any wind. It gathers in particular places, as though remembering them.

Vegetation is sparse, but not absent. Low stands of dark reed grow in dense, precise clumps along channels and around the warmest pools. Their stems are jointed like tubing, black-green when wet, and their leaves are narrow enough to resemble strips of metal. They make no sound when touched. A few pale mats spread across the mud, more like lichen or spilled insulation than plants. They take root in geometric patches, forming squares, circles, and interrupted lines. Where they grow, the mud beneath them becomes smooth and hard.

The open spaces between these patches are crowded with animal life.

Most creatures are small and difficult to distinguish from the ground until they move. Many travel in close files along the water’s edge, their bodies low and segmented, their surfaces shining with a dull mineral coat. There are pale, many-legged things that collect around warm pipes and black amphibious shapes that float just below the surface with only their nostrils showing. Some animals possess too many joints; others appear to have been assembled from repeated versions of the same limb. They are abundant without being noisy. The basin is full of feeding, nesting, shedding, mating, and migration, but these activities occur with the concentration of a silent factory floor.

Larger animals stand motionless for long periods in the mist. They may be herbivores, though there is little for them to eat. They may be listening. Their backs rise in ridges like overturned boats, and water runs from their hides in narrow streams. When one finally moves, it does so slowly and with alarming certainty, crossing several hundred meters without seeming to take more than a few steps. Their reflections do not always follow them. Sometimes a reflection remains behind, standing in the water after the animal has gone.

Scattered across the basin are the remains of industry.

There are pumping stations on concrete plinths, isolated control towers, sections of elevated conveyor, and low sheds with their roofs collapsed inward. None form a town or a coherent facility. They seem to be fragments from a much larger system that has been dismantled, buried, or spread apart. Rust has not consumed them. Instead, their metal is dark and smooth, polished by condensation. Bolts turn slowly in their housings without falling out. A valve wheel may rotate one quarter-turn over the course of a day. A dead generator occasionally warms itself, though no fuel enters it and no current leaves.

The machines are not loud. Their operation can be felt more easily than heard: a soft pressure in the teeth, a tremor passing through the mud, a momentary tightening of the mist. From a distance, an engine might produce one low note every few minutes. The note does not echo. It seems to be heard at its source and nowhere else.

Cables run between some of the structures, sagging almost to the water. They disappear into mud, reemerge on the far side of a pool, and sometimes continue upward into the darkness overhead. Their surfaces are beaded with warm moisture. Along them grow tiny translucent organisms that pulse in sequence, not emitting light but making the cable seem briefly more present.

The water is mostly shallow and opaque. Its surface carries films of bronze, green, and violet that fold and refold without being disturbed by movement beneath them. In the deepest channels, the water is perfectly still. Looking into it gives the impression of looking upward through a shaft, though there is no reflected ceiling and no bottom. Objects lowered into these channels are not lost immediately. They remain visible for a long time, descending at a steady, impossible rate, until they pass behind something that should not be there.

The terrain changes gradually, but its changes are not reliable. A ridge may be sharp today and rounded tomorrow, not through erosion but through a kind of revision. A path crossed many times can become a shallow trench, then a wall, then a strip of dry ground suspended a handspan above the mud. Nothing shifts quickly. The basin has the patience of geology, but geology here does not always preserve cause and effect.

Distances behave similarly. The far shore is visible across the open basin, marked by a line of broken towers. Walking toward it takes hours. Walking away may bring the same towers closer. A structure glimpsed through mist can have three sides from one direction and four from another. The machinery seems especially vulnerable to this. A pipe may enter a building on its eastern face and leave through the same face several minutes later. Doorways sometimes open onto the interior of the building one would expect, and sometimes onto the wet plain, seen from a height that does not exist.

There are rules, though they are difficult to learn because they operate slowly.

Water remembers the shape of anything that rests in it. A hand placed in a pool leaves behind a pale impression that may remain for days. Footprints fill with clear water before they fill with mud. Animals avoid certain channels not because of sound or scent, but because those channels retain the outline of every creature that has drowned in them.

Metal attracts weather. Around old machinery, condensation falls upward, gathering on undersides and climbing into the air as droplets. Near active engines, the darkness overhead becomes slightly denser. Organic things, by contrast, lose definition at the edges. Reeds blur when watched directly. Animals seem more solid in peripheral vision than face-to-face.

Nothing decays at an ordinary rate. A carcass may remain fresh for months, while a living animal may shed a layer of skin that turns to ancient dust before it touches the ground. Rust forms in perfect circles. Mud dries from the center outward, leaving a raised ring. Pools evaporate without becoming smaller; their surfaces simply grow farther down.

The basin is quiet because sound is being absorbed by something beneath it. Voices travel only a few meters. A shout becomes a pressure, then a vibration, then nothing. Water makes almost no splashing. The reeds do not rattle. Even the abundant creatures move in muted ways, their feet and bodies passing through the wet ground with the soft sound of cloth drawn over stone.

Yet there is always a deeper noise.

It is not continuous enough to be called a hum. It comes at long intervals, perhaps once every hour, perhaps once every season. The whole landscape seems to inhale. Water lifts in all the channels. The animals stop. The cables tense. Far-off machines wake with a single coordinated shudder. For several seconds, the plain becomes intensely still, as if awaiting an instruction from below.

Then the water settles. The animals resume their slow movements. Somewhere beyond the visible basin, a factory begins to operate in the dark. Its unseen mechanisms turn patiently, producing something at a scale no scattered ruin could explain. No smoke rises. No lights appear. Only the warmth increases by a fraction, and the mist rearranges itself into long, orderly lanes leading toward the horizon.
