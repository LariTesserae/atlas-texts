The landscape is hidden inside the summit of a mountain that has not finished becoming a mountain.

From outside, the peak appears as a narrow black tooth rising far above the cloud layer—too steep for snow, too young for soil, its sides made of fresh basalt and sheets of dark volcanic glass. No river reaches it. No lichen has found a hold. The wind at this elevation is thin and violent, but the mountain’s uppermost chamber is sealed from it by a skin of engineered stone and folded light. The city inside cannot be seen from below. Even radar returns the mountain as a single, unremarkable obstruction, as though the interior were a thought the instruments have been instructed not to complete.

The inhabited space is small: a vertical hollow no wider than a town square, punched through the summit like a borehole and widened into a series of ledges. It is less a city spread across land than a city clinging to the walls of a shaft. Bridges cross the emptiness at irregular heights. Narrow streets run along the rock face, ending at doors, vents, elevators, or sheer drops. Buildings are thin, angular things—black ceramic shells, pale metal ribs, translucent pressure membranes—attached to the mountain rather than founded upon it.

There is almost nothing in the volume between them.

A tower may stand alone on one ledge, its nearest neighbor a hundred meters away across the dim shaft. A transit line hangs in the dark with no visible support, its cabins moving silently along a filament that disappears into the stone. Platforms are connected by stairs designed for machines, not bodies: precise inclines, shallow steps, and handholds that glow only when touched. The emptiness is not waste. It is clearance. Everything here has been built around the possibility that the mountain will shift.

And it does shift.

The summit is newly emerged from the deep crust. Its walls are sharp, unsoftened by weather, still carrying the blunt geometry of fracture. At intervals, the rock makes a slow internal adjustment. A vertical crack opens by the width of a finger, then closes. A ledge rises a few centimeters overnight. The whole chamber gives a nearly imperceptible tilt as molten pressure moves far below. The inhabitants have built their structures to slide, flex, and unfasten themselves from one another. Buildings do not resist the mountain; they listen to it.

The heat comes upward.

The lower walls are warm enough to haze the air. In some places, the black stone shines with a dull red radiance beneath its glassy skin, though no flame is visible. Heat gathers in strata: a hot layer at ankle height, a cooler pocket near a ceiling, a sudden furnace draft from a vent that was sealed moments before. The city’s cooling systems do not remove heat so much as move it, carrying it through the architecture in luminous channels and releasing it beyond the summit in nearly invisible plumes.

Moisture is intermittent and local. Most of the chamber is dry, its surfaces powdered with metallic dust and pale mineral scale. Then the high air cools abruptly, or a pressure seam opens in the mountain, and condensation begins.

It forms first as a darkening on the walls. Beads gather in the seams of the buildings and along the undersides of bridges. Within minutes, a fine rain falls from the ceiling—not from clouds, but from the chamber’s own breathing. It is warm, faintly acidic, and rich with dissolved minerals. The droplets strike platforms and run in narrow silver threads toward collection gutters. Some descend through the shaft in glittering curtains, vanishing into vents far below. Some are caught by hovering plates that unfold from the walls like metal leaves, though there is no plant life here and nothing organic in their design.

The rain never lasts long. It leaves behind salt-white fans, green-black stains of copper chemistry, and brittle deposits that the city’s maintenance systems cut away before they can become weight. The moisture is stored in sealed tanks, used to cool the reactors, wash the dust from the transit rails, and feed the machines that fabricate new wall anchors. Nothing grows from it.

There are no plants, no animals, and no recognizable remnants of either. The only moving forms are mechanisms: inspection spheres sliding along cables, jointed repair frames unfolding from recesses, and thin swarms of autonomous tools that scatter when light touches them. They do not behave like insects, though from a distance they may resemble a congregation of metallic gnats. They are fragments of the city’s nervous system, each carrying a small task and almost no identity.

The built environment is similarly incomplete. There is no continuous street grid, no central square, no visible civic heart. Instead, the settlement is a collection of occupied ledges and suspended rooms, distributed through the shaft according to heat, pressure, and access to the mountain’s interior. Some structures are sealed and dark. Others contain dim rectangles of habitation light. A few have been abandoned while still intact, their doors open onto rooms with no furniture, no dust, and no explanation.

The technology is quiet enough to be mistaken for geology.

Elevators move without cables, rising through the shaft on controlled distortions in local gravity. Their destinations are not always fixed. A platform that leads to a lower district may, after a seismic adjustment, open onto an observation chamber or a blank wall. The city’s maps are therefore probabilistic: they show where a place is likely to be, not where it must be. Corridors can lengthen by several meters when the mountain is under compression. A door may refuse to open because, according to the building’s prediction, the room beyond has not yet finished existing.

Light is kept low. There is no sun inside the chamber, only a dim gray radiance leaking through the summit membrane and the occasional red pulse from the rock. Most illumination comes from edge-lines embedded in floors and handrails. They glow softly when movement approaches, then fade behind it. In the upper reaches, the darkness is nearly complete. Structures suspended there appear as partial diagrams: a railing, a window, three pale steps, the underside of a bridge. Beyond them is not blackness so much as an absence of reflected light.

The air is thin, hot, and mineral-scented. It tastes of iron, wet stone, and the faint electrical sharpness of active machinery. In the deepest sections, the atmosphere is managed rather than naturally breathable, and the ventilation currents can be felt as warm bands passing through the streets. When condensation begins, the air carries a smell like struck flint.

Sound rarely travels far. The chamber absorbs it in layers of porous ceramic and vacuum gaps. A footstep produces a small, dry click. A distant door closes with the softness of a pressure change. The transit cabins make no audible noise at all, but their passage is marked by a slight displacement of dust and a tremor in the nearest handrail.

More often, the mountain speaks.

It gives off low pulses as hot material shifts below. It clicks as glass fractures in the walls. It exhales through narrow vents, producing a long muted note that rises and falls over several minutes. During a pressure event, every metal surface in the city may hum at once. The sound is not loud, but it occupies the body: a vibration in the teeth, a faint trembling in the ribs, a suggestion that the entire settlement has become an instrument being tested.

The city is active in ways that do not resemble life. Its empty spaces are constantly measured. Wall anchors retract and redeploy. Bridges alter their tension. Heat migrates through the foundations. Construction units remove a newly formed shard of glass, seal a crack, and leave before the crack has fully stopped glowing. The settlement is always repairing itself from the future effects of the mountain’s next movement.

At the highest ledge, beyond the last inhabited platform, there is a narrow opening in the summit membrane. It reveals the world outside: a white ocean of cloud far below, lit from above by a sun that appears painfully bright even at a distance. The air beyond is turbulent, and moisture flashes into vapor against the heated stone. Sometimes a cloud rises high enough to touch the peak. For a few minutes, the hidden city receives a vertical rain from the sky.

Then the cloud falls away.

The opening seals. The dimness returns. Water ticks through the gutters. Somewhere below, a new fracture forms, and the settlement quietly changes shape around it.
