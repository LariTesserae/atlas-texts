The plateau has no name because names do not carry far here.

It occupies most of the visible world: a high, cold expanse under a white-gold sky, rising in broad shelves and tilted basins until the horizon becomes indistinguishable from the upper air. There are no rivers, no lakes, no glints of moisture caught in distant folds. The low places are not watered valleys but mineral depressions, pale as old bone, and the high places are long backs of black stone running for hundreds of kilometers beneath the living cover.

Everything else is plant.

Not a forest. Not grassland. The word *vegetation* is too small and too orderly. The plateau is a continent-sized organism spread thinly over itself, a green-black tissue in which roots are foundations, stems are towers, leaves are weather, and flowers are temporary factories. It does not form a continuous carpet. Great distances remain bare between its presences: expanses of ash-colored mineral, wind-polished and cracked, with isolated growths standing in them like islands in a dry sea. But wherever life has taken hold, it has taken hold completely.

There are mats several kilometers wide, low and leathery, ribbed with parallel veins as thick as roads. There are groves of hollow stalks rising from the stone in sparse ranks, each one hundreds of meters tall and no wider than a train carriage. Their crowns are not leaves but rotating arrays of dark blades, turning constantly toward the bright sky. Other plants spread sideways instead of upward, lifting broad, translucent sheets between ridges. The sheets are cool gray on one side and copper-green on the other, and they tremble even when the air appears still.

The air is never still.

A wind comes over the plateau with the force of machinery. It moves in long, visible fronts, carrying dust, seeds, brittle fragments of bark, and clouds of pale reproductive powder. The powder gathers along the edges of the mineral flats, then rises again in spirals and columns. From a distance these columns look like smoke from an industrial city. Up close, they are alive: millions of seed-grains turning in coordinated vortices, each grain equipped with minute cilia that open and close like fans.

The brightness is immense. The sky is an almost colorless blue, but the ground throws back a hard glare. The plants have learned to drink light directly and violently. Their surfaces flash with mirrors, scales, silver hairs, polished wax, and fields of black pigment. Some leaves are so reflective that they erase the landscape around them, giving back only the sun and the racing sky. Others are dark enough to appear burned.

The sun itself is not warm. Heat arrives in brief, local surges where a colony has arranged its blades toward it, but most of the plateau remains cool. Frost forms in the hollows, though there is no water to freeze. It is a mineral frost: fine needles of crystallized salts and volatile compounds, growing overnight across the bare stone. By morning the plants have dismantled it. Root-fibers rake the crystals inward, and the stems pulse with the dry chemical traffic of digestion.

There is no water cycle here. There is an exchange cycle.

The organisms draw nourishment from the atmosphere, the stone, the radiation, and one another. Their fluids are oils, solvents, brines too concentrated to flow, and translucent gels held under pressure in their tissues. When a stalk dies, it does not rot. It collapses in a shower of hot flakes and oily vapor, and the surrounding plants rush to harvest it. Roots unfasten mineral layers. Fungi-like threads—if fungi is the right word for things that grow without moisture—burn through the remains with cold chemical light. The dead are not returned to soil. They are stripped into components, separated, refined, and incorporated into the next generation.

Nothing is wasted because nothing is allowed to remain inert.

In the broadest basins, plant colonies have become industrial.

They construct no buildings, yet the land is full of processes that resemble factories. A grove of tall, hollow stems draws gases from the upper atmosphere and compresses them in enormous internal chambers. Every few minutes the grove exhales. The release is thunderous, a concussion that travels across the flats and sends loose stones skittering for kilometers.

Another colony extrudes long cables of woody fiber and lays them in loops around exposed mineral seams. The cables heat, tighten, and split the stone. Within years—though the plants count time differently—the seams have been quarried open. Needle-shaped roots descend into the fractures and draw up metallic elements in glowing threads. Their surfaces are etched with tiny repeating patterns, not written instructions but inherited operating structures: biological circuits grown generation after generation.

There are leaf-fields that function as solar arrays, their thousands of overlapping blades adjusting themselves in perfect sequence. There are stalks that act as pumps. There are bulbous growths that sort particles by charge. There are forests of pale, jointed stems that produce rhythmic electrical discharges, their blue sparks jumping from crown to crown across gaps of several meters.

The electricity makes the air taste metallic, though there is little air for taste to cling to. It also makes the sound.

The plateau is deafening.

Wind booms through hollow vegetation, turning entire valleys into resonant chambers. Tall stems knock against one another with the report of artillery. Root-pressures release underground in rolling detonations. The great exhalations of the gas groves arrive as walls of noise, followed by a deep vibration that can continue through the soles of the feet long after the blast has passed.

Some plants sing, though the sound is not deliberate. Their leaves are tuned by shape and tension; when the wind crosses them, they produce immense chords. A single colony can make a note that lasts for an hour, so low it is felt in the ribs rather than heard. Neighboring colonies answer with higher tones. The result is a continuous, shifting roar, like an ocean made of metal and leaves.

At intervals the whole plateau changes key.

A storm begins, but not a storm of rain. The air darkens beneath enormous airborne sheets of reproductive tissue. These sheets detach from distant colonies and sail overhead, sometimes covering thousands of square kilometers. Their undersides are lined with cilia and mouths, and their passage strips loose material from the ground. Mineral dust rises. Small organisms flee or are caught. Sunlight is reduced to a greenish twilight.

Then the sheets descend.

They adhere to the bare flats and unfold into new mats, while older growths react violently. Stalks bend toward them. Root networks contract. Chemical fires run along the ground in branching lines. Whole stands may be consumed—not by flame, but by rapid digestion—while the surviving portions launch themselves into the wind as hard, black seeds.

The plateau is old enough that these disturbances have carved their own stable patterns. Erosion is not absent; it has been absorbed into the living system. The wind has polished the stone for ages, but roots have learned to enter every weakness before it can widen. Cliffs are draped in hanging membranes that catch fragments before they fall. Ridges are reinforced from below by mineralized root lattices. The land looks weathered, but not abandoned. Every scar has become a niche.

The sparse spaces are therefore as important as the living ones.

A person could travel for days across a mineral flat and see only three or four major growths. Yet each one would be enormous, visible from the curvature of the ground: a solitary black tree with a crown of turning vanes; a low colony spreading like a cracked continent; a tower that vents a white plume into the upper atmosphere. Between them lies silence only in the visual sense. The emptiness is crossed by pressure waves, chemical signals, drifting spores, and subterranean electrical pulses.

Fauna moves through this emptiness in sudden abundance.

Most creatures are small and angular, built for the dry chemistry of the world. They shelter in the shade of root shelves and emerge during the brief cool periods when the plants reduce their activity. Many have mineral shells, glassy membranes, or bodies coated in reflective scales. Some leap across the flats on jointed limbs, leaving trails of charged dust. Others cling to the sides of the great stalks and feed from exposed valves, moving in black swarms whenever a plant opens.

There are larger animals as well, though “animal” is only a convenient division. Long, six-legged grazers comb the mats with toothed mouthparts, scraping away surface tissue and excreting concentrated chemical pellets. Winged things ride the thermals above the colonies, their wings thin as torn leaves and their bodies bright with internal electricity. When the gas groves exhale, these creatures dive into the pressure fronts, using the blasts to cross hundreds of kilometers.

Some animals are symbiotic machines grown from plant tissue. They carry no tools, but their bodies contain cutters, pumps, grinders, and catalytic chambers. They travel between colonies in slow, purposeful migrations, dismantling damaged growth and transporting its useful parts. Their movements have the scale and coordination of industrial logistics. A single herd can take apart a dead tower in a week.

Nothing here has been made, but much of it has been manufactured.

There are no roads. The paths are pressure grooves worn by repeated migrations, or pale bands where the plants have chemically sterilized the ground. There are no towers except the living ones, no wires except those grown in mats and roots, no engines except the pulsing chambers that pump gas through stalks and the compressed muscular organs that propel the larger fauna.

The boundary between organism and apparatus has been erased by time.

A plant may spend a century becoming a refinery. A colony may become a power grid. A reproductive structure may rise like a factory chimney and produce, in a single season, enough seed to cover an entire province. None of this is directed by a visible intelligence. The systems are inherited, revised, and tested by collapse. The plateau’s “technology” is not invention but selection operating at continental scale.

From the highest ridges, the landscape reveals its true vastness.

The living zones form dark braided bands across the mineral surface. Their edges move. A colony advances in one direction while retreating in another, consumed by its neighbors or abandoned to the wind. Bright fields of reflective leaves flare and go dark. Distant storms lift whole ceilings of dust. A line of airborne organisms crosses the horizon like a migrating cloud, but it takes three days to pass.

The horizon is too far away to be trusted. Weather forms there and arrives transformed. A clear region may become black with spores. A quiet plain may begin to tremble as underground root systems synchronize. A solitary stalk may split open and release a pressure wave capable of flattening every low growth for kilometers.

At night—if night can be called the period when the light merely thins—the plants glow in restrained colors. Blue electrical veins travel through the mats. Red points pulse along the tall stems. Pale columns exhale luminous gas. The mineral flats shine with frost and scattered biological sparks. The sky remains bright near the horizon, and the entire plateau seems to continue upward into it, as though the land is trying to become atmosphere.

There is no water to soften the world, no mud, no mist, no reflecting surface that holds a stable image. The landscape is dry, elevated, cold, and open. It is also crowded with life—not crowded in the human sense, but saturated with activity separated by vast distances.

The emptiness is occupied.

The silence is impossible.

And beneath the constant thunder of wind, chemistry, migration, growth, and collapse, the plateau is always making more of itself.
