The landscape begins above the weather.

There is no mountain range in the ordinary sense—no succession of peaks, no sheltered valleys, no snow collecting in folds. Instead, the entire horizon is occupied by a continent-sized elevation: a broad, uplifted mass of mineral crust rising into a thin, cold atmosphere. Its upper surface has been worn for so long that it has become almost level, but not smooth. The land is a plain at the scale of a planet, interrupted by trenches, shelves, and immense isolated remnants of older strata. Here and there, whole regions stand above the plain as tablelands miles high, their sides cut into nearly vertical faces by erosion so ancient it seems less like weathering than the final state of matter.

From a distance, these heights appear inverted. The cliffs are not dark below and pale above; they are lit evenly by the sun, every layer exposed in bands of iron gray, bone white, dull violet, and the green-black of metals that have never known water. Their upper surfaces are flat enough to resemble abandoned floors. Along their edges, the rock has been reduced to knife-thin cornices and suspended shelves. Some have survived for ages beyond counting, held together by forces that are not quite structural. A person can walk beneath one and see the underside trembling very slowly, as though the plateau were remembering an earthquake.

The air is clear, cold, and insufficient.

Sound does not travel far. A voice becomes a dry flutter and disappears. Footsteps are felt more often than heard: little impacts transmitted through the stone, arriving at the bones before the ears. The wind makes no continuous roar. It comes in immense, silent movements, pressure fronts that pass over the land and lift whole veils of mineral dust into the sky. At the boundary of one of these fronts, isolated grains begin to rise and orbit one another. A column may form without warning, perfectly straight, reaching from the plain into the blackening blue above, its contents turning in total silence.

The storms are not made of rain.

They are made of charged dust, powdered glass, metallic vapor, and fragments of the upper crust. They appear as walls of pale gold or bruised purple advancing across the plateau. Their lightning does not fork through clouds; it runs along the ground, finding ancient seams and illuminating them in branching lines. Sometimes the entire horizon flashes beneath a storm, revealing buried geometry: roads wider than countries, foundations that extend beneath the cliffs, and enormous circular scars whose edges are still moving.

The storms are turbulent in a way that weather alone cannot explain. Pressure, magnetism, and gravity seem to negotiate with one another. A dust front may flow uphill. A canyon may briefly become weightless. Stone slabs drift a few inches above the ground, rotate, and settle in a new arrangement. On rare occasions, a section of plateau folds—not collapses, but folds—its ancient layers flexing like cloth before locking into a new, equally final shape.

The land has been eroded almost to completion, yet it is never still.

Everywhere, the slow exhaustion of the world is crossed by sudden violence. A plain that appears unchanged for ten thousand years may split open in a single hour. A cliff may shed no fragments for centuries, then disintegrate into a cloud of dark plates that hang in the air, suspended by some transient alteration in the local field. New ravines are cut not by water but by abrasive currents of dust moving at impossible speed. They are clean-sided, dry, and immensely deep. Their bottoms are often hidden by darkness rather than shadow.

There are no rivers. There are no lakes, even in the largest depressions. The low places contain salt-like mineral blooms, pale crusts, and fields of brittle nodules that crackle when the temperature changes. In some basins the surface is covered with a powder so fine it behaves like smoke. It gathers in drifts against the cliffs, though the wind may lift the entire drift at once and carry it for hundreds of miles. Other basins are paved in black plates, each one separated from the next by hairline gaps glowing with a faint internal red.

Vegetation exists only as a scattering of low, stubborn forms. Some cling to cracks in the stone, resembling gray moss or flattened lichen, though they have no softness. Their surfaces are mineralized, their roots—if they are roots—threading into thermal gradients rather than soil. They open during the brief local warming that follows a storm, spreading translucent fans no larger than a hand. Within minutes they close again and become indistinguishable from the rock.

Farther across the plateau, isolated groves stand in shallow hollows. They are not trees but vertical lattices of pale material, each one grown around a narrow shaft of warmth rising from below. Their branches are rigid and crystalline. When the pressure changes, they alter their geometry by a few degrees, producing forests that seem to rearrange themselves between glances. Nothing lives beneath them. Their presence is more like a chemical event than an ecosystem.

Animal life is scarce enough to feel accidental. A few things move across the open ground, traveling in long, precise arcs between zones of heat. They are thin-bodied and many-jointed, with broad feet that distribute their weight over the dust. Their outer surfaces mirror the sky, making them difficult to see except when a storm passes behind them. They do not call to one another. They communicate through brief changes in reflected light, small shutters of color that vanish before the eye can interpret them.

Most journeys cross no living thing.

The emptiness is enormous. Distances cannot be judged by familiar landmarks because the land itself is too large. A cliff visible in the morning may still be visible at night, its illuminated face having moved only slightly across the horizon. A structure can stand alone for a thousand miles of plain. Its scale may be domestic—a narrow doorway, a low tower, a shelter with a single opening—or it may be so large that it takes a day to walk around one wall. There is no consistent relationship between purpose and size.

The scattered constructions are remnants of an intelligence that did not build cities, or whose cities have been erased. A black needle rises from one plateau, perfectly smooth, with no entrance and no reflection. Around its base are dozens of shallow circular depressions, each occupied by a small sphere of blue light. The lights do not illuminate the stone. They mark locations in a space that may no longer exist.

Elsewhere, a bridge extends across a canyon too wide for any visible support. It is made from a substance like smoked glass, and its surface remains level despite the changing gravity around it. No footprints remain on it. Beneath the bridge, the dust storm flows upward through the chasm, breaking into luminous ribbons as it encounters the structure.

There are shelters half-buried in the plateau, their walls composed of layered material that repairs itself when scratched. Their interiors are empty except for air held at a different pressure and small mechanisms that continue to perform tasks without witnesses. One such chamber may contain a table, a lamp, and a view through a sealed window. Outside the window, the same cliff has been changing for centuries. Inside, nothing has aged.

At intervals, the old technology reveals itself in the landscape rather than in objects. A line of stones begins to move in sequence, passing a vibration over several miles. A ravine closes without dust or noise. A dead field of mineral crust becomes temporarily transparent, showing a second landscape beneath it: roads, towers, and enormous shapes moving far below the surface. Then the crust becomes opaque again.

The sky is ordinarily blue, though thinner and darker toward the zenith. The sun is hard-edged and white. Shadows are sharp enough to seem cut into the ground. Under normal light, every surface has an austere clarity: no haze, no softness, no color made richer by moisture. The distant plains are bronze, gray, and white. At stormfall, the colors deepen strangely. The air fills with suspended particles that turn the sunlight green, then copper, then nearly black, while the land below continues to shine as if lit from underneath.

Night brings no relief from scale. The stars are numerous but not bright enough to make the plateau feel inhabited. Far across the horizon, atmospheric events continue: a silent ring of dust rising into orbit, a continent-length line of static, a slow bloom of pale radiance where something beneath the crust has opened. The darkness is interrupted by the occasional flare of a remote installation, visible for a few seconds or several years.

The world’s rules are not hostile so much as indifferent and incomplete. A person can cross a plain for days and remain perfectly safe, then step onto a patch of stone where gravity leans sideways. A metal tool left in the open may become weightless, transparent, or older than its maker by morning. Heat does not always rise. Dust does not always fall. The most reliable law is that every stable thing is temporary, and every temporary thing can last a very long time.

This is a high place at the end of erosion: a dry, ancient continent almost reduced to its final shape, stretched across a scale too large for any single view. It is quiet because the air is thin and the distances are vast. It is empty because life has withdrawn into rare seams of energy. It is turbulent because the old forces have not finished rearranging what remains.

The plateau looks dead from afar.

Up close, it is constantly becoming something else.
