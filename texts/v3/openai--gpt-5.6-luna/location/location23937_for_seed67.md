The lowlands of **Morrow Array** extend farther than the eye can follow, a warm, shallow world assembled rather than formed.

There is no soil. No stone in its original state. No place where a root could once have entered the ground, because there was never ground here in the ordinary sense. The entire continent is a continuous construction: poured ceramic, ribbed alloy, black glass, pressure-sealed concrete, and enormous plates of a pale material that resembles bone but rings like porcelain when struck. The surfaces meet at precise seams, though precision has not made them smooth. The land rises and falls in broad manufactured swells—basins, ramps, terraces, heat-sinks, spillways—each one too large to read as architecture from the ground. From above, it may be a machine. From within, it is a country of hard horizons.

Water lies close beneath everything.

It seeps from joints. It beads on the warm metal. It gathers in long, shallow channels where the upper surfaces have been designed to slope by fractions of a degree. A gray film covers the lower terraces, rarely deeper than an ankle, though some broad depressions hold dark water to the waist or higher. The water is clean in some places, oily in others, and faintly luminous where it passes over active conduits. It does not flow with the urgency of a river. Most of it advances in slow, prescribed sheets, drawn by pumps beneath the plates and returned through grates the size of plazas.

Warm vapor hangs everywhere. Not a fog thick enough to conceal the world, but a constant softening of distance: the far factories become smudged towers, the stacked causeways lose their edges, and every light acquires a pale halo. Condensation runs down walls in hundreds of narrow threads. It falls from overhead galleries as a patient artificial rain.

The atmosphere smells of wet iron, mineral salts, hot insulation, and the faint sweet odor of the creatures that fill the water.

They are everywhere.

Fauna is the dominant fact of Morrow Array. The shallow channels are crowded with low, many-jointed things that move by coordinated ripples of their bodies. Some are plated in translucent shells; some are soft and dark, with lamp-like organs recessed beneath their skin; some resemble long bundles of cables dragging themselves through the water. They gather beneath warm vents in dense, layered masses, each individual pressed against the next. Their bodies form living mats over service roads and floodgates. When a machine arm descends, the animals part around it—not in panic, but with the practiced obedience of a population that has lived beside machinery for generations beyond counting.

Larger forms occupy the open basins. They stand on six or twelve narrow legs, their bodies suspended high above the water, their backs supporting racks of dormant instruments, filter membranes, or nests. Their heads are difficult to distinguish from their tools. Many have no visible eyes. They detect changes in pressure, heat, salinity, and electrical charge, but the Array changes very little, and so they remain almost perfectly still.

One may stand for a century beside a sluice gate, its limbs submerged to the joints, its flanks furred with smaller organisms. Another may lie across an entire drainage trench like a collapsed bridge. Smaller fauna move over them in millions: pale scavengers in the seams, red-black mites around heated bolts, ribbon-bodied creatures that cling to vertical walls and drink condensation. The animals do not seem to compete much. There is little hunger in the usual sense. They graze on residue, mineral films, electrical leakage, warm water, and one another’s cast-off surfaces. Their ecology is less a chain than a dense, interlocked occupancy. Every exposed surface has a tenant.

Nothing grows. There are no leaves, stems, spores, or recognizable colonies of fungus. The living things here are animal-like in their insistence on bodies, appetites, and response, but their world has no green interval, no soft wilderness between constructions. They have adapted to manufacture so completely that the distinction between organism and machine is unreliable. Some creatures carry replacement valves in their bodies. Some lay eggs inside inspection housings. Some secrete insulating foam around ruptures in the walls. The factories feed them, the animals maintain the factories, and neither process appears to have begun first.

The industrial districts rise from the lowlands in enormous ranks. They are not cities, though they possess the density of cities and the verticality of industrial architecture. Towers squat rather than soar, built broad against the warm, wet atmosphere. Their sides are crowded with gantries, pipes, lifting frames, conveyor trunks, and sealed windows glowing with normal white light. Most machinery is active but quiet. Motors turn behind walls. Pumps pulse under the floor. Fabrication lines slide without clatter, producing identical plates, joints, shells, filters, limbs, and sections of track. The scale of production is difficult to comprehend because there is no visible destination for the things being made.

Some outputs vanish into the foundations. Some are carried by tireless crawler platforms toward the horizon. Some are lowered into the water, where dense animals gather around them and begin to alter their surfaces. Nothing appears to be expanding. Nothing is visibly repaired. The Array manufactures replacement parts for a structure that does not seem to decay.

This is the central law of the place: **everything remains within tolerance**.

Water never rises far enough to drown the lower works. Heat never builds beyond the threshold the organisms can bear. No section is allowed to rust through, collapse, dry out, or become truly dark. Storms may occur, but they are not changes so much as rehearsals: the same warm rain, the same pressure, the same veils of vapor, arriving at intervals too regular to call weather. A failed machine is isolated before failure becomes an event. A dead animal is absorbed almost immediately by the surfaces around it, leaving only a clean patch of metal and perhaps a few newly active maintenance forms.

The world is dynamic in detail but static in identity. There is constant motion—legs stirring in the water, belts turning, droplets falling, valves opening, bodies feeding—yet nothing develops. No process leads anywhere. The landscape performs itself.

The sounds are correspondingly subdued. Water makes the clearest noise: a thousand tiny falls, the soft dragging of shallow currents, the wet ticking of condensation on metal roofs. Beneath this is a low industrial hum, so broad and continuous that it is felt more than heard. Machines operate at frequencies chosen not to disturb the fauna. The animals themselves are mostly silent. Their communication passes through vibration, changes in body heat, chemical traces, and small pulses of light beneath the water. Occasionally one of the great standing creatures releases a long, soft tone, and every nearby animal turns toward it at once. The response can travel across the basin in waves without ever becoming loud.

There are no natural horizons. Every distant line is a wall, berm, intake, embankment, or production platform, though their size makes them resemble geography. A raised strip may continue for hundreds of kilometers before dipping beneath the vapor. A shallow channel may divide two regions like a sea. Elevated transitways cross one another overhead, carrying silent freight masses and herds of slow, six-legged transport fauna. Under them, the warm water remains crowded and nearly motionless.

Light is ordinary daylight, but its source is not always apparent. The sky above the Array is a broad pale brightness, filtered through layers of vapor and industrial particulate. Sometimes the ceiling opens onto a white, empty sky. Sometimes it reveals other structures overhead: suspended reflector fields, maintenance platforms, or the underside of a second constructed level. There is no true night. At the dimmest interval, the factory lamps become more visible, and the water turns black around their reflections.

The materials are young. Edges remain sharp. Breaks show clean internal layers, as if the continent were still recently made. There is little weathering beyond mineral staining and the polishing effect of countless bodies passing over the same routes. Corners have not softened into ruins. Pipes do not sag. Foundations have not settled. The entire place possesses the unsettling freshness of something finished only moments ago—despite the animals whose lineages fill its every channel and the machines that have been running longer than memory can measure.

To stand there is to feel surrounded not by wilderness, but by an immense and stable metabolism. The land is fabricated, inhabited, maintained, and utterly without solitude. Even an empty platform is only temporarily visible beneath its film of water and the creatures pressed into its seams. The silence is not absence. It is the sound of an overwhelming population making no unnecessary disturbance, each organism and mechanism holding its assigned place in a world that has forgotten how to become anything else.
