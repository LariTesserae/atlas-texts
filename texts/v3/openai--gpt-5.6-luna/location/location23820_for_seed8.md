The land lies low and open beneath a warm, pale sky.

It is a basin that has been wearing itself down for longer than anything alive can remember. The old hills around it have nearly surrendered their height, reduced to long backs of stone and shallow ridges, their surfaces rounded smooth by rain, floodwater, and the slow grinding of mineral against mineral. Nothing here rises sharply. There are no cliffs, no ravines with hidden depths—only broad shelves, shallow depressions, and gradual slopes that seem to continue even after the eye has lost them.

Water is close to the surface everywhere.

In the lower ground, it gathers in dark, irregular sheets between islands of mud and pale stone. Some pools are only a handspan deep, but their surfaces are so still and reflective that they appear bottomless until a fish stirs or a bird steps through them. Narrow threads of water move almost imperceptibly across the flats, slipping through cracks, joining, separating, and joining again. After rain, the basin becomes a patchwork of silver water and black earth. Even in dry weather, dampness shines from the hollows.

The stone is old limestone, softened and honeycombed by ages of wetness. Here and there it has become chalky and white; elsewhere it is stained ochre, green, or the deep brown of tea. Water seeps from its faces in small persistent tears. The rocks do not form dramatic outcrops. They lie exposed in low shelves and scattered plates, as though the whole region had once been submerged and was now only barely remembering how to stand above the water.

Vegetation is sparse, but not absent. Low mats of salt grass cling to the edges of pools. Small, leathery shrubs grow in widely separated clumps, their leaves dull and waxed against heat and damp. Reed beds occupy the deepest channels, though they are thin enough that the open land can still be seen through them. Their stalks are yellow-green and stiff, and when the wind passes they do not ripple like a field; they knock together in dry, hollow bursts.

There are no trees.

The emptiness between plants is part of the character of the place. Mud, water, stone, and sky dominate. In some directions the surface extends for miles, broken only by low islands, distant ridges, and wavering bands of heat. The horizon is not hidden by forest or architecture. It remains broad and exposed, giving the basin the feeling of a world reduced to its oldest materials.

The air is thick and warm. It carries the smell of wet mineral, stagnant water, mud opened by rain, and the faint green bitterness of crushed reeds. A haze hangs low over the farther flats, not quite fog and not quite heat shimmer. Moisture gathers on the shaded sides of rocks and beads along the sparse vegetation. At dawn, the whole basin may seem to sweat.

The place is loud despite its emptiness.

Water drips constantly from stone into stone. Shallow channels hiss over gravel. When wind crosses the open flats, it produces a broad rushing sound, and the reeds answer with their dry clacking. The animal life provides the greater noise: frogs calling from hidden pools, insects vibrating in the grasses, waterbirds crying from the marsh, and sudden explosions of wings when something approaches. The calls travel far across the level ground. A single sharp note from a distant bird can seem close enough to touch, while the continuous murmur of thousands of insects rises and falls like the breathing of the basin itself.

Fauna is abundant, though scattered according to the water.

Small fish move through the clearer channels, their bodies flashing silver beneath the brown surface. Crabs and mud-dwelling creatures leave delicate fans of tracks around the pools. Long-legged birds patrol the shallows, stepping carefully among submerged stones. Others stand motionless for long periods, then strike with startling speed. Lizards warm themselves on pale rock, while frogs occupy every degree of wetness—from the saturated mud to the shallowest rain-filled hollows.

At dusk, bats may pass low over the water, and insects rise in dense, shifting veils above the reed beds. Their numbers make the air seem granular. The larger animals remain mostly quiet and concealed, moving through the basin by routes known to them: along the backs of old ridges, between the pools, through the sparse vegetation. Nothing rushes unless it must. The land encourages patience. Predators wait. Water travels slowly. Mud settles. Stone continues to soften.

Even the weather feels gradual. Rain does not transform the place so much as reveal what was already there. Floodwater spreads through old channels; pools deepen; hidden springs become visible. Then, over weeks and seasons, the water withdraws again, leaving rings of mineral on the stones and a darkening of the mud. The basin does not regenerate dramatically or collapse. It persists, settling further into its final shape.

There is no sign that anything has been built here. No path remains distinct from a game trail, no stone has been arranged, no mark interrupts the natural surfaces. The only order is the deep order of water finding the lowest ground, roots following moisture, animals following both, and the ancient basin holding them all without haste.
