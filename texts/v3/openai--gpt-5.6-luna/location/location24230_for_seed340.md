Below the surface, the world is green in the way a furnace is red: not by color alone, but by total occupation.

There is no water here. No seepage darkens the floor, no underground river threads the low places, no bead gathers on the leaves. The air is warm and dry enough to crack ordinary wood within minutes. Yet everything lives. The living matter has found another chemistry—bitter mineral vapors, metallic salts, heated gases drawn up from the stone, and a syrup-thick internal fluid that is not water and does not evaporate so much as harden into amber, glass, or fragrant dust.

The cavern is called a cavern only because it is beneath the surface. It is too large to feel enclosed. Its roof is a distant black-brown vault, still rising in places, still being made. Columns of raw stone thrust upward through the green mass, but many are not stone yet. They are mineral spines wrapped in pale root-sheaths, widening by the hour as the plants accrete grit from the air. Whole sections of the ceiling remain unfinished: shelves of granular earth hang over the landscape, trembling, shedding slow avalanches of warm pebbles and red powder.

The floor has no stable shape. It is a country of ridges, pits, cones, and half-formed terraces, all occupied immediately by growth. Great cushions of root tissue mound themselves in the hollows. Bristling forests rise from the slopes—not trees, exactly, but vertical arrangements of stalk, tendon, leaf, and bark, each one growing into the next. Some trunks are hollow and ribbed like organ pipes. Some are braided cables thicker than houses. Others are flat, sprawling sheets pressed against the ground, their surfaces plated with mineral scales and their edges constantly folding, splitting, and rejoining.

The vegetation has no clear boundary. It is the ground, the wall, the hanging ceiling, the atmosphere’s filter, the roads, and much of the animal life’s food. A person crossing the region would never walk on bare earth for more than a few steps. The exposed mineral is quickly veined by roots, carpeted by hairlike filaments, or seized by squat, fleshy growths that resemble mushrooms made of fired clay. Even the stones are partially alive. Green-black films crawl over them in branching geometries, extracting heat and trace elements. Crystals swell inside flowerlike chambers, then burst outward in showers of splinters.

The colors are not gentle. There are sheets of bruised violet, copper orange, sulfur yellow, and dense, almost black greens. Some plants carry bright internal lines, like molten veins beneath translucent bark. Others are pale and powdered, their leaves coated in mineral dust. Here and there, enormous blooms open without petals: circular mouths of layered tissue, each ring rotating at a different speed. They exhale clouds of warm spores, iron filings, or glittering seeds that strike the surrounding growth like hail.

The light is ordinary enough to cast shadows, but its source is difficult to identify. It comes from everywhere and nowhere: a broad, ochre illumination reflected through the plant mass, as if daylight were passing through miles of stained flesh. The nearest surfaces glow faintly from within. High above, pale photosynthetic membranes stretch across gaps in the unfinished roof, harvesting heat from the deep geological dark. When they tear, the landscape below is briefly lit by a hard white flare from exposed mineral, and every creature in the vicinity reacts at once.

There are too many creatures.

They occupy all layers of the place: underfoot, in the foliage, inside the trunks, between the hanging roots, and in the air. Most are small, but smallness has become a form of abundance. The ground ticks and rustles continuously with six-legged grazers, burrowing mouths, armored seed-eaters, and blind animals that navigate by pressure waves. Pale things as thin as thread swarm over the leaves, stripping away old tissue while leaving the living veins untouched. When startled, they rise in clouds and cling to the body like dry ash.

Larger animals move with the relentless purpose of machinery. Long-bodied browsers push through the plant corridors, their heads protected by layered shields of horn and hardened root. They bite entire sections from the forest and carry them away, followed by dozens of smaller scavengers that live in the wounds they make. Heavy, low creatures with many jointed legs force tunnels through the vegetation, compressing the living floor into smooth ramps. Their backs support parasitic gardens. Some have flowering growths emerging from their sockets; others have become so thoroughly colonized that it is impossible to tell where animal ends and plant begins.

Above them, winged organisms beat against the hot air in enormous numbers. They are not birds. Their wings may be membranes, leaf fans, or vibrating clusters of tendrils. They fill the upper reaches with constant motion, diving into the blooms and scattering clouds of dust. Some cling to the ceiling and feed upside down. Others strike the stone deliberately, using their bodies as hammers to loosen mineral crusts for the roots below.

Predators are present, but predation is not the central drama. There is no quiet interval between hunts. A many-mouthed thing can tear through a herd while a vine grows through its ribs, flowers, and begins feeding on both animals before either has finished dying. Death is not a clearing of space. It is an alteration of texture. The dead become scaffolding, fertilizer, shelter, or movement. A carcass may stand upright for weeks, hollowed out and animated by the creatures nesting inside it.

The sound is deafening.

There is the grinding of mineral against mineral as the land expands. There is the thunderous collapse of root shelves and the deep concussion of animals striking the ground. Every plant makes some sound: clicks from opening seed cases, low pulses from communicating trunks, brittle snaps as leaves shed their mineral skins. Vast groves flex together with a noise like thousands of wooden doors being forced open. The winged swarms produce a continuous electrical whine. Grazers chatter through their jaw plates. Predators whistle, boom, and release subsonic calls that make the ribs ache.

At intervals, the whole region gives a single collective shudder. It begins far away, perhaps beneath the floor, and arrives as a series of impacts through the roots. Animals flee in every direction. Blooms close. Hanging growth drops in curtains. The sound rises beyond hearing into a physical pressure. Then a new ridge breaks through the ground, or a section of ceiling folds downward, or a forest accelerates into a sudden, violent maturity.

This landscape is still being formed. Erosion is not yet erosion in the familiar sense; there are no ancient channels, no polished slopes, no settled layers. Stone is being crushed, lifted, eaten, excreted, and rebuilt faster than it can become old. The floor convulses through cycles of construction. A mineral shelf rises on columns of plant tissue. Roots bind it, animals hollow it, spores coat it, and the entire structure collapses into a basin. Almost immediately the basin fills—not with water, but with pale root foam, crawling bodies, and angular shoots that force themselves upward.

The warm air carries no clean scent. It smells of hot resin, scorched leaves, stone dust, medicinal bitterness, and the coppery odor of living things opened too quickly. Breathing without protection is possible but unpleasant. The lungs are left coated with fine botanical powder. The natives use simple masks made from layered bark and packed fiber, their edges sealed with hardened sap. Their tools are mostly stone, bone, thorn, and chitin. They cut paths through the vegetation with hooked blades, wedge open root doors, and build small shelters in the hollows left by dead organisms.

Human construction is scattered and provisional. A watch platform may be lashed between three living columns, though the columns will slowly grow around its supports. A shrine may consist of painted bones arranged in a flower’s abandoned throat. There are narrow bridges of woven root across the deeper pits, but every bridge is also growing, feeding, and occasionally tightening until it throws travelers into the dark. Small camps hide beneath slabs of bark or within the hollow bodies of fallen animals. Their inhabitants mark safe routes with white mineral handprints, though the marks fade as the walls bud over them.

There are rules here, though they are biological rather than moral.

Stillness is dangerous. Anything that remains unmoving for too long is interpreted as a surface, a support, or an available nutrient. The plants begin to seal it in. Sound attracts some creatures and repels others; silence is not safety, only a different signal. Brightness causes certain blooms to open, and opened blooms change the air. Blood—or any warm internal fluid—can trigger explosive growth. Fire is possible, but it burns strangely, racing through dry tissue in silent lines before erupting all at once. Smoke is avoided by nearly everything, except the great blind animals that follow it to find newly exposed mineral.

The deepest rule is that nothing here keeps its identity for long.

A root becomes a wall. A wall becomes a feeding surface. A feeding surface becomes a nest. The nest becomes a predator. The predator dies and becomes a root. Even the empty spaces are temporary, occupied by heat, dust, spores, or the pressure of something about to emerge.

Far across the underground region, beyond the nearest forests and the red mineral ridges, the ceiling is splitting open. A pale architecture is pushing through: vast fans, translucent and folded, climbing toward the unseen surface. Their expansion sends slow waves through the entire green country. Each wave brings avalanches, migrations, new blooms, and frantic feeding.

The landscape is not mature enough to be called a wilderness.

It is an organism in the act of becoming one.
