The place is a small city at the bottom of a high mountain lake.

The lake occupies a hollow above the tree line, held in a bowl of dark, weathered stone. From the surrounding ridges it looks almost motionless: a sheet of blue-green water laid into the mountain, its surface often hidden beneath low cloud. There are no broad rivers feeding it, only thin falls, snowmelt, and the slow seepage of the heights. Yet below the surface the water goes down farther than the scale of the city suggests. The inhabited basin is only a few streets across, but the lake continues beneath it into a cold, lightless depth.

The city itself is not constructed on the lake floor so much as grown from it.

Pale vegetation rises from the silt in scattered towers and platforms. Some plants have thick, hollow stalks as wide as a person’s body; others spread into immense floating leaves, their undersides ribbed with air chambers. Their roots grip the old stone foundations beneath the mud. Their stems have been bent, braided, and trained over generations into bridges, doorways, stairs, and low vaulted chambers. A few of the largest plants form the city’s public structures: green columns leaning toward one another, joined by woven roots, with broad leaves stretched overhead like translucent roofs.

The buildings are sparse. There is a great deal of open water between them.

A walkway may run for several dozen steps through clear water before arriving at a single house. The houses stand apart like islands in a meadow, though the meadow is vertical and submerged. A rope of vine marks the route between them. A cluster of white shells hangs at an intersection. A stone basin, carved by hand, rests on a shelf of living wood. These signs are easy to miss in the blue-green vastness, and a stranger could pass through the settlement without realizing it was a settlement at all.

The people who made this place use little metal. Their tools are stone-edged, bone-tipped, shell-scraped, or made from the hard, dried parts of the lake plants. They cut only what they must. A stalk severed too deeply may bleed for years, and a root damaged at the wrong season can alter an entire neighborhood. Construction is therefore a patient practice of persuasion. Young stems are tied to frames. Vines are led around stones. Roots are encouraged into cracks. A wall can take a lifetime to become thick enough to call a wall.

The oldest structures are indistinguishable from the vegetation that supports them. One low hall has been growing since before the current paths were laid. Its pillars are dark with age and marked by rings where former generations tied their tools and sleeping hammocks. The floor is a mat of living runners, warm only by comparison with the water. Small pale flowers open along its edges, releasing strings of silver bubbles that drift toward the ceiling.

There is no true ceiling. Above the city, the water brightens and fades through layers of suspended silt. Daylight arrives in a broad, ordinary glow, broken into wavering bands by the surface far overhead. On clear days, sunlight reaches the upper roofs and turns them gold-green. Deeper down, colors simplify: red becomes brown, yellow becomes dull white, and the shadows under the plant bridges become almost black.

The temperature is cool everywhere. The water presses through every seam in the city, and clothing is made for insulation rather than modesty: layered fibers, felted plant down, skins of water-resistant leaves. Residents move slowly, conserving warmth and breath. They know which air pockets remain trapped beneath certain leaves and which stems can be opened without flooding a room.

The city is loud despite its emptiness.

From above comes the continuous thunder of the falls entering the lake. It travels through the water as a deep, bodily vibration, making the plant towers tremble almost imperceptibly. Leaf surfaces clap and fold in the currents. Hollow stalks knock together with the sound of distant wooden bells. In the open channels, water rushes along the lake floor, dragging pebbles and old branches. Fish strike the woven walls. Shells scrape over stone. Schools of small silver animals move through the streets in dense, flashing bursts, then vanish into the vegetation.

There are larger animals too. Long-bodied lake grazers feed on the outer gardens, stripping algae from the roots and leaving the cultivated plants untouched. Broad-winged creatures descend from the upper water, though they are not birds and do not fly; they steer themselves with membranes and rise in slow spirals toward the light. Thick, blind scavengers live among the foundations. Their movements are nearly silent, but their presence is announced by the sudden lifting of silt.

The fauna is abundant without making the place feel crowded. Life gathers in pockets: beneath a warm shelf of stone, inside a flowering grove, around a sunlit roof. Between those pockets is open water, dim and patient. A single animal may cross a whole square by itself. A person can stand on a bridge and see nothing moving for several breaths, then watch hundreds of tiny bodies emerge at once from the plants.

The vegetation fills the environment, but it does not form a continuous jungle. It is more like a constellation of living architecture scattered through a submerged ruin. Here and there, the old stone of the original settlement shows through: retaining walls, stairways leading nowhere, round foundations, a paved square drowned beneath moss and root. The stones are smooth from long erosion, but not collapsing. Everything has settled into its condition. The lake has not recently arrived; it has been here long enough to become an ordinary element of history.

Water has altered the meaning of every boundary. A door is a woven curtain of stems. A street is a channel kept clear by hand and by grazing animals. A roof is a layer of floating leaves. A watchtower is a tall plant whose upper chambers reach toward the brighter water. There are no sealed rooms, only spaces with different degrees of shelter, warmth, and current.

The rules of the place are simple and strict.

Nothing may be built without a living anchor. No plant may be cut during flowering. No channel may be blocked, because the lake’s slow circulation is believed to carry breath through the entire city. Fish are not owned, but particular families tend particular spawning hollows. Every household maintains a section of rootwork, and every bridge is periodically retied. If a plant dies, the structure built from it must be dismantled before the decay reaches its neighbors.

Change is visible only over long intervals. A new arch may be growing where there was once open water, but no one remembers its beginning. A foundation may sink by the thickness of a finger across a generation. The lake level rises and falls with cycles measured in centuries. The city does not expand so much as thicken, sending roots into abandoned spaces and allowing other spaces to return to the lake.

At the settlement’s center is a small open court, roofed by a single enormous leaf. Its surface is veined with channels that collect warm water from the shallows and distribute it through the city. In the center stands a rough stone pillar, not carved into an image but polished by repeated touch. Around it hang the oldest tools: hooks, knives, weights, and woven baskets, all suspended in the water like relics in a shrine.

Beyond the last cultivated structure, the lake floor slopes away.

The vegetation becomes taller and more widely spaced. Its leaves drift in the dark like slow flags. The sounds of the city thin into the larger sounds of the mountain: the pressure of water, the distant roar of falling meltwater, the creak of roots anchored in stone. Above, the ordinary daylight continues to shine through the surface. Below, the deep lake waits, cold and almost still, while the small green city breathes in its blue silence.
