The plain begins without a horizon.

It is low country, but not flat. The land has recently come into being—or recently been made visible—and its surfaces still have the abruptness of something that has not yet learned how to weather. Long shelves of black mineral rise a few meters above one another, their edges clean as broken glass. Between them lie shallow basins filled with pale dust, green filaments, and mats of vegetation so dense they resemble piled fabric. Nothing is rounded. Every ridge is a blade, every hollow a precise incision, every outcrop a fresh fracture.

There is no water anywhere.

The basins are not dry in the ordinary sense; they were never wet. Their soil is a powdered ceramic of calcium, carbon, and stranger elements arranged in microscopic lattices. It gives beneath the roots with a faint, fibrous resistance, then locks again as soon as the pressure is gone. The green growth does not drink. It feeds on gradients in the mineral itself: electrical strain, heat trapped beneath the crust, and a slow leakage of particles from the invisible machinery threaded through the ground.

From a distance the plain appears lush. It is covered in low vegetation, dense enough to hide the mineral between its leaves. There are fan-shaped fronds in metallic greens, translucent reed-clusters with no hollow stems, and broad mats whose surfaces are stippled with thousands of lightless pores. Some plants stand in rigid groves, their leaves angled identically toward the sun. Others crawl across the shelves in braided masses, gripping the stone with root systems that are more like soldered filaments than roots.

Their greens are not all the same. There is the dark green of oxidized copper, the yellow-green of old glass, the blue-black of deep forest seen through smoke. In the lower hollows, the vegetation becomes almost black and absorbs the ordinary daylight, while taller growth catches the light along its edges, outlining whole fields in thin, clean fire.

The air is warm and perfectly still.

It has the dry, mineral taste of a room sealed for centuries, though there is no room and no enclosing structure. The atmosphere is breathable, but only in the way a machine can be said to be alive: its composition remains within narrow tolerances, corrected continuously by processes too small and too numerous to observe. Gusts do not occur. Dust does not drift. The leaves do not flutter. A strand of grass may be bent beneath a passing shadow, but it returns to its exact position before the shadow has finished crossing it.

The sun is ordinary enough. It gives a warm, white-gold light, and objects cast shadows in the expected direction. Yet the shadows are slightly more definite than the objects making them. A thorn’s shadow can be seen as a crisp black line across a stone even when the thorn itself is hidden in the vegetation. In some places, the shadow of a plant extends a few centimeters beyond the plant’s actual reach, as if the light has retained a memory of where the organism was supposed to be.

The landscape is open on a regional scale. It extends through several successive shelves and shallow valleys, all green, all mineral, all without a road or wall or trace of human shaping. The farther features do not diminish as much as they should. A ridge two kilometers away appears only slightly smaller than one a hundred meters distant. This does not make the country feel cramped. Instead, distance loses its authority. The eye can look across the plain and feel that every place is nearby, while the body knows that walking there would take hours.

The density is in the life, not in the movement.

Growth occupies every available surface. Leaves overlap leaves, fronds emerge through fronds, and the mineral itself is visible only in sharp interruptions. There are no clearings except where the black shelves split apart. Even there, thin green threads have entered the cracks and filled them with delicate, luminous tissue. The plants seem to have grown all at once, not over seasons but in a single event, as though the plain had been covered by a green solution that crystallized upward into stems and blades.

Nothing appears to age.

There are no fallen leaves. No dead trunks. No brown margins, nests, burrows, tracks, or scattered remains. Individual plants remain at the same degree of maturity, neither flowering nor withering. Their surfaces are clean. Where one is broken, it does not bleed sap or dry out; it remains broken, a precise wound with a faint silver interior. After an hour, a century, or perhaps no time at all, the wound is unchanged.

The few animals are difficult to classify. Occasionally a long, jointed shape passes through the vegetation without disturbing it. It may be a creature, or a distortion traveling beneath the leaves. Once, something with six narrow legs stood motionless on a mineral shelf, its body no more substantial than a dark reflection. It had no visible head. When the light shifted, it was simply no longer there, leaving behind a small arrangement of plants pressed into a perfect radial pattern.

There are no insects. No birds. No calls from hidden things. The ecology has the quiet of a closed circuit.

Sound travels strangely across the plain. Footsteps are muted by the vegetation, though the dense mats do not compress. A stone struck against another produces no ringing impact, only a brief absence of sound around the point of contact. The dominant noise is a low, nearly inaudible pressure, felt more in the teeth than heard: a sustained vibration from beneath the shelves. Every so often it resolves into a distant chord, as if a vast glass instrument has been touched somewhere beyond the visible country.

The chord does not fade. It is either always present or has always already ended.

At intervals, the world misplaces itself.

A line of plants may repeat for several meters with identical leaves, identical angles, identical imperfections. A ridge seen from one direction can have two separate summits, while from another it has only one. A person standing in a basin might see their own back moving along a far shelf, though no reflection is present and the figure does not respond. The effect is not chaotic. It has the unsettling precision of a system maintaining an incorrect answer.

The strongest distortions occur around the exposed black mineral. Its faces are not merely reflective; they are computational. Patterns crawl through them without movement, visible only when the eye stops trying to focus. At one angle, a rock contains the diagram of the surrounding valley. At another, it contains a landscape of unfamiliar geometry: towers made of folded distance, a white sky beneath the ground, or a dark ocean made of solid shadow. These images do not seem to be visions. They seem to be neighboring solutions.

The technology of the place has no machines, stations, cables, or ruins. It is present as a property of matter.

The mineral shelves are engineered at scales too fine to see, their atoms arranged into structures that correct local gravity, regulate the atmosphere, and preserve the vegetation in permanent equilibrium. The plants may be biological, or they may be self-replicating instruments built from carbon and light. Beneath the ground, something vast and distributed keeps the entire region synchronized. It does not pulse, calculate, or awaken. It simply maintains.

No gradients remain for long. Heat equalizes. Pressure equalizes. Decay stops at the edge of possibility. Even light seems reluctant to change the place. The sun crosses overhead, but the warmth at noon is no stronger than at morning. Shadows lengthen only slightly, then hold. A leaf turned toward the east remains turned toward the east, regardless of the hour.

This is a young landscape frozen before erosion can make it gentle.

Its sharpness is not violence but incompletion. The shelves have not settled. The valleys have no history of floods because there were never floods. The vegetation has no inherited seasons, only a standing order to remain green. There is no architecture because the world itself has been given the functions of architecture: shelter, regulation, memory, calculation, boundary.

At the far edge of the visible plain, the green growth thins around a field of upright mineral shards. They rise from the dust in dense ranks, each one taller than a person, each one perfectly clear. Inside them, the ordinary daylight appears to be traveling downward. Their shadows fall upward into the sky and stop at an invisible ceiling.

Beyond the shards, the land continues.

The same warm light lies over it. The same silent vegetation presses against the same new-cut stone. Nothing moves except the slow passage of attention across the place, and even that may be an illusion produced by the landscape to reassure itself that time still exists.
