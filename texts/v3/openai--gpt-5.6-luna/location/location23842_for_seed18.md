The place is a high basin no wider than a village square, though the world falls away from it on every side.

It sits on the crown of a mountain whose true height is difficult to measure. The mountain is hidden beneath construction: terraces of black iron, retaining walls, riveted galleries, concrete buttresses, pipes as broad as houses. Beyond the basin’s rim there is no natural slope, only immense vertical faces descending into cloud. The edges have been weathered smooth by centuries of wind and occasional rain, but not broken down. Erosion here has become a settled architecture. Every crack has been plated, every hollow given a drain, every overhang braced. The mountain does not appear conquered so much as persuaded to remain in one shape.

The basin itself is a shallow, oval space enclosed by machinery. Its floor is a pale metal grating suspended over a depth that cannot be seen. Through the grating, blackness opens between the supports. Sometimes, far below, a slow gleam moves through the dark: a river, perhaps, or a line of lamps on an unseen service level. Nothing rises from that depth. Nothing falls into it. The air simply occupies the distance, cold and immense.

There is very little in the basin by volume. A few towers stand widely separated from one another, leaving long spans of empty air between them. A crane hangs motionless over the center, its cable descending straight through the grating for hundreds of meters. Three green-painted pressure vessels lean against the eastern wall. A narrow control house, windowless except for one strip of yellow glass, has been built into the western cliff. Between these objects are acres of open floor, silvered by weather and marked with small pools.

The pools are fed by condensation. Water gathers on the undersides of pipes and walks in beadlike processions along the seams, falling at irregular intervals through the grating. Rain arrives rarely and with no visible cloud: a sudden fine mist that moves horizontally across the basin, wetting only one side of every object. It may last six minutes or six hours. Afterwards, the metal shines, and green life wakes almost immediately.

Vegetation is abundant, but it has no soil in which to be abundant. Moss carpets the joints of the machinery. Ferns grow from cable housings. Long, waxy vines trail from inspection platforms and disappear down the mountain’s face, their roots threaded through insulation and mortar. A grove of narrow trees occupies a disused loading bay, each trunk pale and jointed like bundled electrical conduit. Their leaves are dark blue-green, broad as hands, and hold droplets that tremble without falling. In the center of the grove stands a flowering plant with a bell-shaped bloom large enough to shelter a person’s head. Its flowers open whenever the turbines below increase their load.

The plants do not spread randomly. They follow warmth, vibration, and the invisible paths of old power. Wherever a transformer has once overheated, there is a dense thicket. Wherever a machine has been silent for too long, the growth thins, retreats, and leaves bare metal exposed. The ecology has learned the factory better than any engineer could.

The air is warm at the basin floor and cold along the upper rails. It smells of wet stone, ozone, hot oil, and the sweet mineral scent of crushed leaves. In the brightest parts, a fine haze hangs around the pipes. Under normal daylight the place is neither beautiful nor bleak; it is plainly visible, with the hard clarity of a working environment. Yet the ordinary light seems unable to illuminate the depths. It stops at the edges of the grating, as though the darkness below were not an absence but a material.

The sound is enormous.

Turbines rotate somewhere inside the mountain, their low frequency passing through the metal and into the bones. Above that is the continuous concussion of exhaust vents, the hammering of pressure valves, the clatter of belts and chains, and the deep periodic impact of something massive completing a cycle far below. Voices cannot cross the basin. Warning horns are felt more than heard: a sudden pressure in the chest, followed by ripples moving through every hanging vine.

And still, in the pauses between cycles, there are other sounds. Water ticking into unseen channels. Leaves rubbing together. The small metallic chirps of animals.

The fauna are plentiful but widely scattered. Pale moths drift through the open space, each carrying a faint green glow in its abdomen. They gather around indicator lamps and sometimes vanish into the glass. Small six-legged creatures nest inside cable drums, emerging in synchronized bursts to drink condensation from the rails. A colony of long, red birds lives beneath the crane’s counterweight. Their wings are too short for ordinary flight, but they move effortlessly in the updrafts along the cliff, circling the basin for hours before landing on the same three bolts.

There are larger animals as well. Once or twice a day, a thin-bodied grazer comes up through a maintenance hatch from below. It has translucent skin, jointed legs, and a mouth adapted to scraping moss from steel. It crosses the floor in a straight line, never pausing at the vegetation, and descends through another hatch at the opposite edge. No one has found where the two passages lead. The creature is tolerated by the machinery. When it passes, sensors dim and doors unlock.

The rules of the place are old and mostly mechanical.

Nothing may be carried over the western edge. Loose objects placed there become weightless, hover for a while, and then drift upward into the clouds. Water always moves downward, except during the turbine reversals, when every drop in the basin rises at once and hangs in the air like a field of glass beads. Plants cannot be cut while the engines are running; their severed stems knit themselves together before the blade has cleared them. Any machine that is repaired three times acquires a new function not specified in its original design. A pump may begin to produce light. A door may begin to remember footsteps. A pressure gauge may show the weather on the other side of the mountain.

The workers, if there are workers, are not present now. Their signs remain: numbered ladders, faded stripes, stenciled arrows pointing toward emergency exits that have been sealed for decades. In a niche beside the control house, a bank of helmets hangs from hooks, each helmet filled with a little forest of moss. A calendar behind the yellow glass has stopped on a month that does not exist in any current year.

The machinery operates without urgency. Its movement is slow, ponderous, and exact. Pistons descend over several minutes. Gears turn with the patience of planetary bodies. A valve opens a fraction at a time, and the change in pressure takes an entire morning to travel through the mountain. Nothing breaks dramatically. Components wear down, bend, grow over, and are incorporated into the next arrangement. The whole installation seems to have survived by refusing to distinguish between maintenance and evolution.

At the highest point of the basin is a platform no larger than a bedroom. A railing encircles it, and beyond the railing the sky is open on all sides. Clouds pass below, not above. On clear days, distant peaks appear as small dark islands in a white expanse, though some of them are revealed to be other platforms, other isolated stations, each carrying its own trees, pipes, birds, and patient engines.

From here the landscape seems almost empty: a handful of structures suspended over immeasurable depth, green growth trembling in the exhaust wind, a few animals moving along prescribed paths. But the emptiness is crowded with distance. Every sound belongs to something hidden. Every wet surface holds a seed. Every silent machine is slowly becoming part of the living mountain.
