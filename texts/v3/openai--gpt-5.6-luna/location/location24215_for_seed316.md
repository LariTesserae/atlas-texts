The plain has no horizon.

It is too large for one. Weather is born somewhere beyond sight and arrives already exhausted, dragging itself across the land in long, low veils. Warm rain falls without falling: it hangs in the air as a dense suspension, beads on every surface, gathers in the hollows of stone, and travels sideways in shining sheets. The atmosphere is close to the skin, almost liquid. Breath tastes of minerals, old leaves, and the faint sweetness of something fermenting beneath the ground.

This is a basin, though “basin” suggests an edge. The land slopes so gradually that a person could walk for days and never understand they were descending. Once, perhaps, there were mountains here. Their last fragments remain as black ridges far apart, worn down to the stature of walls. Most of the world is mudstone, silt, pale clay, and broad plates of green-black rock polished by water. The stones are ancient beyond their own memory. Their surfaces are veined with tiny channels where rain has flowed for ages, each groove softened into a final, patient shape.

Nothing is sharp except the movement of the land.

The ground is packed. Not hard-packed—full. There is no clean space between things. Fine roots, drowned stems, shells, mineral threads, pale worms, pockets of warm water, and the remains of vanished organisms occupy every pore. Where the surface appears bare, it is only because the life beneath it has become too small to see. A footprint closes slowly behind the foot, not from elasticity but because the ground rearranges itself, as though making room for the memory of the pressure.

Sparse vegetation rises from this saturation in isolated growths. There are low reed-fields, but the reeds stand far apart, each one thick as a wrist and dark as wet bronze. Their leaves are narrow and translucent, carrying dim green light from their own interiors. In the open flats grow squat, many-limbed shrubs with no visible foliage, only clusters of damp, folded surfaces that open and close with the pressure of the air. Here and there, enormous root systems break the mud—pale, swollen structures like the ribs of buried animals. They do not belong to trees. They descend from plants that have long since ceased to grow upward, choosing instead to spread below the world.

The vegetation is sparse above ground because the surface is already occupied by other processes.

Water is everywhere, but rarely in the shape of a river. It wells up through seams in the stone and spreads in thin films. It trembles in shallow pans. It hangs beneath ledges in long drops that never detach. It leaks from the sides of ancient ridges, not in streams but in continuous weeping, the rock sweating out the accumulated pressure of deep time. In the dimness, the wet ground reflects the sky so perfectly that the basin seems filled with black, shallow holes. Some of these reflections are not reflections. They show clouds that belong to another weather, or landscapes that lie beneath the surface, or the movement of a figure walking somewhere several hours in the future.

The light is weak and diffuse. There is no visible sun, though the warmth of it presses down through the cloud cover. Day and night are distinguished less by brightness than by the direction in which the shadows fail. During the brighter interval, the clouds above become a low ceiling of pearl-gray vapor, and everything beneath them shines with a muted, underwater radiance. During the darker interval, the air thickens until the far distance disappears entirely. Shapes remain visible after they have moved away. Objects cast shadows before they are illuminated.

The weather is not merely weather. It behaves like a vast, unstable organism.

Storms form across the basin in silence for a while, gathering in coils around invisible centers. Then the sound arrives. It is not thunder but a continuous, deafening concussion: the roar of water passing through buried chambers, the grinding of mud beneath the plain, the bellow of pressure changing under thousands of square miles of stone. The noise has layers. Near the ground is a wet, muscular booming. Above it is a chattering rush, like rain striking an immense metal roof. Higher still is a deep vibration that can be felt in the teeth and sternum, a note too low to hear but too large to ignore.

At times the whole landscape speaks at once.

The reeds knock together though there is no wind. Mud bubbles in dense fields, each bubble bursting with a different tone. The black ridges groan as their interiors shift. Far away, a wall of rain falls upward, lifting silt and drowned vegetation into the cloud deck. The sound is so complete that small sounds vanish within it. A stone rolling down a slope, a bird calling, a body entering water—these happen without audible transition, swallowed by the planetary tumult.

Fauna is rare, but absence does not make the land empty. Most animal life has either retreated beneath the saturated surface or become indistinguishable from the processes around it. In the reed-fields, there are occasional impressions like the passage of something broad and heavy, though nothing is seen making them. Pale, jointed creatures sometimes surface in the shallow pools: blind, soft-bodied things with too many folded limbs, rising only long enough to drink the rain that is already covering them. They have no obvious eyes, mouths, or openings. After a few moments they flatten into the mud and become part of its pale veining.

The largest animals are encountered as disturbances. A distant section of the plain may begin to heave, not violently but with enormous purpose, as though a creature beneath the basin were turning in sleep. Water climbs the slopes of the disturbance. Reeds bend toward its center. Then the land settles, leaving behind a new depression filled with warm, opaque water. No tracks remain.

There are no insects in the air. Nothing flits. Nothing nests. Yet the sound of countless small movements comes from below: scraping, pulsing, burrowing, the innumerable work of organisms too old and too numerous to be individually alive. The earth seems to be digesting itself.

Scattered across the plain are the remnants of construction, though it is difficult to say whether they were built by people or by a species that merely arranged matter with unusual persistence. A low wall runs for half a mile and then disappears into a marsh. Its stones are fitted without mortar, each one rounded by age until the wall looks like a natural fold in the earth. There are isolated chambers with open roofs, their interiors filled to the threshold with black water. In one place stands a narrow tower, leaning slightly, made from layers of pale shell and hardened mud. Rain enters at the top and emerges from the sides as thin streams, as if the tower is still performing some forgotten function.

No roads connect these structures. No tools lie near them. The few marks that might be writing have been worn into arrangements of shallow grooves by water, wind, and time. Occasionally the grooves change between visits. They may be eroding, or they may be answering.

The world does not preserve boundaries well here.

A pool may be shallow at its edge and bottomless three steps farther in. A ridge can be approached for an hour and remain the same distance away, while the country behind the traveler becomes unfamiliar. Rain strikes one side of a stone and passes through the other. In certain stretches, the sky lies beneath the ground: looking down into wet clay reveals a dim, moving expanse of clouds, and looking up shows only the reflection of one’s own submerged face.

Distances have moods. Near things are reliable only while observed. Far things may suddenly become intimate. A low shrub at the edge of sight can fill the entire field of vision without growing larger, its wet limbs extending through the air like a diagram of a forest. The next instant it is small again, and the land between has changed color.

The plain is turbulent without progressing. It is not being destroyed, nor being created. It is trapped in an immense process of revision, each moment wearing away the last while preserving its pressure. Ancient erosion has not brought stillness; it has brought the removal of all forms except those capable of enduring endless change. The basin has been flattened almost to its final shape, yet that shape is unstable. Its rivers become rain, its rain becomes stone, its stone becomes mud, its mud rises briefly as living tissue and returns below.

There is no clear center to the place. Weather has one for a while, then abandons it. A sound begins at the far edge and becomes the whole world. A warm wind crosses the flats carrying the smell of wet mineral and crushed roots. The dimness thickens. Across the saturated ground, every reed bends in the same direction—not toward the wind, but toward something moving beneath the continent.

Then the plain opens.

Not dramatically. No crack, no collapse. A seam in the mud simply becomes wider than it was, revealing a depth filled with slow, luminous water. The water is crowded with shapes that do not belong to any known body plan. They drift upward, vanish, and reappear in the sky as dark patches beneath the clouds. Around the opening, the ground convulses with silent industry. New channels form. Old channels close. The distant ruins tilt by fractions.

The rain continues to fall sideways.

And the whole vast basin, packed with water, pressure, buried life, ancient stone, and impossible rearrangements, makes a sound like the beginning and ending of the same thing.
