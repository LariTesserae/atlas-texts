The city stands on a high, level basin where the mountain has been worn down to a broad shelf.

It is not a green highland. The rain comes often, but the soil is thin and mineral-bright, and most of the water refuses to enter it. It lies in shallow sheets over the rock, gathers in long black runnels, and disappears through cracks that have been widened by centuries of patient seepage. The basin floor is paved in places, not by streets but by geology: slabs of dark basalt, pale ashstone, and a dull metal-gray material that appears to have flowed there and cooled. Between these surfaces, the ground is folded into low ridges and shallow depressions, as though the whole plateau were a vast, weathered washboard.

The elevation is high enough that the horizon is open in every direction. Beyond the city, the land drops by gradual terraces rather than dramatic cliffs. Far-off mountain ranges sit under a warm, colorless sky, their upper faces softened by suspended vapor. Clouds are common but not fast. They gather, flatten, and remain. Rain arrives as a broad dimming of the air, sometimes for minutes, sometimes for days, and afterward every surface continues to sweat.

The warmth is unusual for the height. It lives in the stone.

At dawn, the streets release vapor. Drainage channels steam gently beneath their iron grates. Walls sweat in vertical lines. The undersides of bridges drip even when the weather is clear. Water beads on cables, window frames, smokestacks, and the exposed bolts of the rail lines. In the oldest districts, the buildings have been designed around this constant wetness: roofs slope inward toward cisterns; doorways are raised on narrow platforms; walls are pierced with little stone mouths that breathe out condensation. No one calls it rainwater. Rain is only the beginning. The basin itself is always giving something back.

The settlement spreads broadly across the shelf, never rising into a true skyline. Its buildings are heavy, low, and widely separated. Warehouses lie beside empty lots of exposed rock. Apartment blocks stand in rows around enormous drying yards, though nothing ever dries entirely. Factories occupy entire districts, connected by covered conveyors and elevated pipes. Their walls are stained in layers: rust at the bottom, white mineral bloom above that, then soot and old rain marks. The city appears settled rather than expanding, as if its present shape was reached long ago and no one has found a reason to alter it.

There are streets, but the older routes are channels first and roads second. During rain, water moves along them in measured currents. At the intersections, bronze gates rise and fall to direct the flow toward reservoirs, turbine houses, or the lower terraces. The gates operate slowly, with the deliberation of clock hands. They do not respond to sudden weather. They respond to averages, to accumulated pressure, to what the basin has been doing for a season.

The industrial machinery is equally patient.

Great pumps work beneath the city, though their pistons complete only a few strokes each minute. Their movement is felt as a steady bodily pressure more than heard as individual impacts. Conveyors carry stone, salt, metal ingots, and sealed drums from one district to another. They move so slowly that pedestrians use them as landmarks, watching the same crate pass for an entire afternoon. Along the eastern rim, a line of drilling towers extracts heat from the stone. Their heads turn at an almost imperceptible rate, drawing up warm water from deep chambers and sending it through the city’s pipes.

The factories do not seem to manufacture quickly. They refine, press, cure, anneal, and settle. Their products are things meant to last a very long time: bridge plates, pump housings, flood gates, ceramic insulators, thick glass, structural beams, containers for corrosive fluids. The city’s industry is not energetic so much as continuous. It has the rhythm of a geological process.

And it is deafening.

A low mechanical roar fills the basin from edge to edge. It is difficult to locate. Near the factories, it separates into grinding belts, hammering presses, turbine thunder, exhaust coughs, and the enormous breath of ventilation towers. Farther away, all those sounds merge into one dense note, a vibration in the ribs and teeth. Rain does not soften it. Thunder does not rise above it. Conversations take place by gesture or through signs painted on walls. Bells are useless. Sirens are replaced by rotating lamps and colored steam.

Even in the open spaces, silence does not exist. The drainage tunnels boom beneath the pavement. Water falls through vertical shafts and strikes hidden basins far below. On the western side of the plateau, a chain of spillways descends in broad steps toward the lower country. They are not tall waterfalls but immense, permanent curtains, each one spreading over a dark stone lip and breaking into white turbulence. The sight is calm from a distance. The sound is not. The whole western horizon is a wall of continuous impact.

Vegetation survives in scattered, deliberate places. There are low mats of copper-colored moss around warm vents, and narrow grasses growing in the protected seams between paving slabs. A few trees have been planted in deep concrete wells near government buildings, their trunks wrapped in heating coils and their sparse leaves coated with mineral dust. They look maintained rather than native. Beyond the city, the plateau is mostly open stone, with small black shrubs crouched in the hollows. Their roots are long, pale, and more like cables than roots. They grow slowly enough that a new branch is recorded by surveyors.

The native ecology is difficult to see. Small, pale animals live in the drainage works: blind things with soft jointed bodies and translucent shells, feeding on films of algae and industrial residue. They emerge after long periods of rain and gather around warm grates. No one knows how far they range. Larger fauna are rare. Occasionally, a broad-winged scavenger crosses the sky from the lower terraces, using the warm air rising from the city, but it may be weeks before another is seen.

The few birds that remain have adapted to the sound. They do not call. They communicate with changes in flight and flashes of reflective skin beneath their wings. At dusk, they settle on power lines in perfectly spaced intervals, each one separated from the next by the length of a hanging cable.

The atmosphere makes distance unreliable. In clear weather, the opposite edge of the basin appears close enough to walk to before noon. In wet weather, it dissolves into layered veils. Towers seem to stand in places they do not. Roads continue into blank vapor and return from another direction. The city’s lights are not bright, but they persist in the mist: amber windows, green indicator lamps, red aircraft beacons, the pale vertical glow of factory interiors. Under ordinary daylight, these lights remain on, not because they are needed, but because the city has been illuminated continuously for so long that no one remembers which systems depend on them.

There are peculiar rules here, though they are treated as engineering facts.

Water always finds the oldest path.

A wall built over a buried channel will be damp within a year, then soft within ten. A road laid across a former stream will gradually acquire a shallow groove down its center, even if the road is raised and reinforced. Engineers do not fight this. They mark the paths, enclose them where possible, and leave certain routes unbuilt.

Heat also remembers where it has been. A factory shut down for decades may remain warm inside. A hand placed against an abandoned furnace can feel the pressure of old production moving somewhere beyond the wall. The city’s oldest pipes carry water from chambers no map agrees on. Some reservoirs fill from below even when the pumps are off.

Nothing changes quickly, but nothing is entirely still.

A district may remain unfinished for generations, its cranes rusting in place while foundations settle. A staircase may acquire one extra step over the course of a century. Surveyors sometimes find that a retaining wall has moved several meters without cracking, its stones having flowed apart and reassembled in a more stable arrangement. The citizens accept such changes as the ordinary behavior of the plateau. Buildings are inspected not for damage, but for whether they have begun to migrate.

At the center of the settlement is a broad civic basin, dry by design but wet in practice. It contains old monuments, pump housings, and trees grown from cuttings of trees that no longer exist. During the rainy season, a skin of water covers the entire floor. The city’s reflected lights tremble there. People cross it on narrow raised walks while the industrial thunder rolls around them, and the surface shows a second, inverted city beneath their feet.

From the basin’s outer rim, one can see the whole place: the low blocks, the open yards, the pipelines and railways, the distant spillways, the smoke flattened under the warm clouds. Nothing is hidden by density. There are too many gaps between things. Empty lots extend for kilometers, occupied only by drainage markers, rusting equipment, or shallow water that reflects the sky.

The landscape feels old enough to have reached an agreement with itself.

The city does not dominate the plateau. It has been inserted into it, then partially absorbed. Streets become channels. Factories become warm caves. Buildings settle into the same patient erosion as the rocks. Rain moves through the foundations and comes out carrying traces of every material it has touched. The machines continue their low, deafening labor. The sparse plants cling to their seams. Far below, the waterfalls keep striking the terraces.

Nothing rushes.

Everything is being worn into its final shape.
