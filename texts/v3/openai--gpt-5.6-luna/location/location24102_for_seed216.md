The high country begins where the clouds end.

It is a plateau, though “plateau” is only the nearest available word: a level expanse raised far above any visible lowland, broad enough to have its own horizons and weather. Warm rain moves across it in long, translucent sheets. The air is heavy with moisture, but the light remains dim, as if the sun has been placed behind several layers of green glass. Sometimes a pale brightness spreads over the sky without revealing a source. Sometimes shadows fall upward.

Nothing here is naturally grown.

The ground is an immense work of construction, though its makers used no machines more complicated than stone blades, fiber cord, fired clay, and patient hands. Slabs of dark ceramic overlap one another beneath the vegetation, fitted without mortar and curved to follow the ancient contours of the plateau. Their surfaces have been scored into channels, hollows, steps, cisterns, and broad terraces. The construction is old enough to have settled into itself. Edges have softened. Joints have filled with black soil. Rain has polished the exposed faces. In places, entire hills are revealed to be built shells, their interiors hollow and bridged by ribbed vaults.

The builders did not make roads so much as leave corridors between living walls.

Vegetation occupies nearly everything else. It is not a covering laid over the constructed ground; it is the substance that makes the place habitable. Great mats of root and stem span the terraces, pale and fibrous where they are young, dark as old rope where they have thickened. Trees grow horizontally from the walls, their trunks braided into arches and stairways. Vines descend from the sky in curtains so broad they resemble cliffs. Flat-leaved plants overlap in shingle-like layers, catching rain and passing it downward from one green basin to another. Moss fills the seams in the ceramic and spreads over them in geometric patterns, preserving the outlines of buried rooms.

The plant life has been trained for so long that wildness and architecture have become indistinguishable. A grove may be a garden, a roof, a bridge, or the exposed outer wall of a settlement. A pale forest of hollow stalks may conceal a stairwell descending for miles. Some blossoms open only when weight is placed on their petals, and the old paths are marked by their slow, silent blooming. Other plants grow around the hands of the dead and preserve their shape for centuries.

The vegetation is total, but the landscape is strangely empty.

There may be no tree for half a mile, then a single enormous one rises from a basin of glazed stone. Its crown is wider than a village. Its roots have lifted entire platforms into the air, leaving open space beneath: a warm, dim vault crossed by rain and dangling gardens. In such places, distance becomes difficult. A small fern can be as far away as a mountain. A nearby wall may recede inward for an afternoon’s walk. The plateau seems to contain vast unoccupied intervals between its presences, not because the builders failed to fill them, but because space itself has been made generous.

The air is warm even at this elevation. Breath does not fog. Rain collects on the skin as beads of mineral-rich water, and every surface exhales a faint dampness. The high winds should be strong, but they arrive as slow pressure rather than movement, bending the tallest fronds without producing a rustle. Clouds enter through the vegetation and remain there, snagged in the upper branches. They form small weather systems among the leaves: a bank of mist drifting through one forest, a private drizzle under another, a clear interval where the dim sky hangs close and green.

Water is everywhere, but rarely in open quantity. It trembles in wall channels. It moves beneath transparent roots. It gathers in suspended bowls grown from thick leaves, then spills in thin threads into lower reservoirs. The ceramic surfaces are warm and wet, and the rocks—where any stone remains—weep continuously from seams that lead nowhere. A stream may run uphill along a carved groove, pause at a vertical face, and continue from the same point several yards away. Pools sometimes contain reflections of weather that is not occurring: lightning in a cloudless sky, falling snow, a red dawn that belongs to another season.

Fauna is abundant, though almost none of it announces itself.

Small six-legged grazers move through the leaf litter in herds, their bodies soft and translucent, their feeding mouths opening and closing without sound. Long-winged insects cling to the undersides of leaves, each wing patterned with the architecture of a different ruin. Pale birds nest in the hollow columns, but their calls are felt as a pressure in the chest rather than heard. Something large travels through the upper canopy at dusk, causing platforms to dip one after another in a line that crosses the horizon. It leaves no broken stems.

Predators are present too. They wait in the green, nearly motionless, with the patience of carved figures. One may resemble a folded branch until the surrounding vegetation turns slightly toward it. Another lives in the open spaces beneath the terraces, visible only as a change in the density of rain. The animals do not fight loudly. Pursuit is marked by sudden absences: a patch of leaves emptied of insects, a trail of warm water, a flock lifting without wingbeats.

Even the loudest events are muted. Rain makes a soft granular tapping on the ceramic roofs, but the sound seems to stop at the edge of each terrace. Falling objects land without impact. Thunder appears as a brief dimming of the light. When the wind passes, the plants lean and recover in silence.

There are rules here, though they are not consistent enough to be called laws.

A path walked often becomes shorter. A neglected stairway gains steps. Living things cast shadows only when watched. Water remembers the shape of the vessel that first held it, and may continue to flow in that shape after the vessel is gone. Doors open onto rooms that are directly below them, unless someone expects this, in which case they open onto the weather. The horizon occasionally folds inward, revealing that the plateau is surrounded not by air but by more plateau, suspended at an impossible distance.

The old built world persists beneath the living one. Every so often the plants withdraw from a surface and expose a wall covered in hand-cut marks. The marks may be instructions, prayers, measurements, or records of plants that have never existed. Some describe the landscape accurately. Others describe the person looking at them. No settlement appears inhabited, yet every few days a newly repaired bridge is found somewhere beyond the mist, its fibers still wet and its knots still warm.

Across this immense elevated garden, construction and growth continue at a pace too slow for ordinary notice. Roots tighten around foundations. Clay vessels form in darkness. A wall settles by the width of a finger. Somewhere, unseen hands—or organs resembling hands—are still shaping the place.

The plateau is ancient, but not finished. It has reached an equilibrium that only looks like stillness from far away. Up close, every leaf is drinking, every channel is carrying, every animal is searching, and every structure is quietly becoming something else.
