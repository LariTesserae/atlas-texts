The high country begins where the ordinary forest ends.

It rises in a broad, ancient swell above the surrounding world—a continent of plateaus, tilted basins, and long green escarpments, all lifted so high that the air is thin but the heat is fierce. There is no snow. The sun burns down through a pale, almost ordinary sky, and the stone holds that heat long after evening. At this elevation the clouds arrive low and exhausted, dragging their shadows across the uplands. They break against the ridges, spill a brief rain, and vanish. For most of the year the land is dry enough to crack.

Yet almost everything that can be seen is alive.

The vegetation does not grow upon the land so much as constitute it. It carpets the stone in layers: mats of leathery moss, swollen root-webs, pale fungal films, low braided shrubs, and tall jointed plants that rise like columns from the green mass. Their roots have entered every seam in the plateau. Over ages they have pried apart the rock, gathered dust, and woven the fragments into suspended soils. In some valleys, the ground is a resilient skin of living fibers, warm beneath the feet and faintly pulsing with the movement of sap. In others, the plant layer has grown into cliffs and bridges, its dead stems hardened into dark mineral-like timber, its fresh growth spilling over the edges in long curtains.

The forest is not continuous. It appears in immense islands separated by bare distances—wide shelves of ochre stone, open hollows filled with wind, and pale ravines where nothing grows but a few isolated towers. From a ridge, one can see green expanses stretching for days, each separated by emptiness so broad that the world seems sparsely inhabited. But every patch of life is crowded beyond reason. A single grove may contain more living bodies than a city, while the miles between groves remain nearly vacant.

The largest plants are visible from the horizon.

They stand on the high plains as dark, many-limbed silhouettes, some hundreds of feet tall. Their trunks are not solid trunks but bundled columns, each sheath growing around the last. Their crowns open only during the brief wet season, when they unfold enormous ribbed leaves that catch condensation from the night air. At dawn, the leaves sag under their collected water and release it in silver falls. The rain does not always reach the ground. It is intercepted by smaller plants, absorbed by hanging root curtains, or drunk by clouds of insects before it can sink into the soil.

Water exists in sudden, hidden abundance. There are basins beneath the vegetation where moisture gathers in black pools. There are long channels under the living floor, carrying brief torrents from one depression to another. There are hollows in the stone that fill during a storm and steam dry within hours. The plants remember these places. Their roots converge on them from every direction, and the animals know them too.

The air smells of hot sap, crushed leaves, wet stone, and the ammoniac tang of enormous animals. After rain, the whole plateau exhales. Mist rises from the green ground in low veils, carrying spores and the sharp perfume of opening flowers. Then the heat returns. Water beads on every surface, trembles, and disappears. The moisture is intermittent, but the life has adapted to interruption. Nothing here expects permanence. Everything waits, stores, burrows, seals itself, or grows quickly enough to take advantage of the next wet hour.

The sound is difficult to endure.

The high country is full of voices, and distance does little to soften them. Great herds move somewhere beyond the visible ridges, their calls rolling through the stone in low, concussive waves. They are long-throated grazers with plated backs and flexible mouths, animals that crop whole curtains of vegetation as they pass. Their feeding makes a sound like tearing cloth magnified to the scale of weather. Smaller creatures shriek from the upper canopies. Winged things beat among the leaves in dense, frantic clouds. Burrowing animals drum on hollow roots, communicating through the ground. Predators answer with whistles, barks, and sudden explosive roars.

At midday, the plateau seems to shout.

The largest animals are not necessarily the most dangerous. There are colonies of six-legged runners that move in coordinated sheets through the low growth, stripping an entire patch bare in minutes. There are lanky, nearly weightless hunters that climb the tallest plants and drop from the canopy without warning. There are broad, pale scavengers that follow the herds and exhale clouds of stinging dust when threatened. In the humid ravines, amphibious things lie in the pools with only their flower-like breathing organs visible. Some of the flowers are animals. Some of the animals root themselves in place for weeks. The distinction between grazing, flowering, nesting, and growing is not reliable here.

The strangest creatures are those that have become part of the vegetation.

Along certain ridges, the hanging vines shift against the wind because they are full of sleeping bodies. A grove may slowly relocate over decades, its tree-like organisms pulling their roots free one by one and replanting themselves farther downslope. There are immense shelled animals that carry gardens on their backs, their moss and flowering growth concealing the slow movement of their legs. Some predators imitate dead trunks so perfectly that the forest seems to contain extra pillars. Others imitate storms: they gather in high branches, darken the sky with their wings, and descend in a sound like breaking surf.

The light remains simple and recognizable. Morning is gold. Noon is white and hard. Evening stretches the shadows of every plant across the exposed stone. But the living mass complicates the day. Groves may close their leaves at the passage of a cloud. Whole hillsides turn their pale undersides toward the sun. At night, the ecosystem does not become quiet; it changes register. Root chambers glow with dull blue phosphorescence. Flowers open like mouths. The air fills with the clicking of small bodies and the deep, synchronized booming of creatures too large to see.

The rock beneath it all is old, dark, and weathered into broad settled forms. The terrain does not look newly broken. Its cliffs have softened, its ravines have found their courses, and its ridges have been worn into massive, patient curves. Erosion has not defeated the plateau; it has taught the vegetation where to grow. Plants bind the slopes, reinforce the ledges, and sometimes hold entire mountainsides in place. Elsewhere, their roots have won. A canyon wall may be a solid face of stone at the bottom and a woven green overhang above, as if the forest is gradually lifting the edge of the world.

There are signs of people, but they are few and easily swallowed.

On high, dry shelves stand small structures made from split stalks, clay, and slabs of dark stone. Some are watch platforms, some shelters, some water traps. Their builders have learned to harvest condensation from the great leaves and to cultivate certain root mats around their camps. They use sharpened bone, stone blades, cord, fire, and simple metal ornaments traded from distant regions. A few paths cross the open ground, marked by cairns or rows of bleached shells. Most disappear at the edge of the vegetation.

The old structures are stranger. In one basin, a ring of stone pillars rises from the plants, each pillar pierced by dozens of small holes. When the wind blows through them, they produce a deep chord that can be heard for miles. No one knows whether they were built as instruments, signals, or traps. In another place, the remains of a tower have been engulfed by a tree-like organism. Its upper rooms still project from the trunk, accessible only by climbing through warm, fibrous tunnels. The inhabitants left behind fragments of painted pottery, fishhooks, and the bones of animals too large to have lived in the surrounding valleys.

Travel across the high country is governed by the movements of life more than by terrain. A person can cross open stone quickly, but the empty shelves are exposed to heat, wind, and watching predators. The green expanses offer shade and water, but they are dense with unseen movement. A safe route may depend on the hour when a certain herd passes, or on whether the root-floor is asleep. Some plants fold their leaves when touched and reveal pits beneath them. Others open only for particular animals, and a traveler who forces a passage may trigger the synchronized release of spores, thorns, or alarm cries.

The world is not hostile in any simple sense. It is immensely occupied.

Every surface has a purpose, every pause attracts attention, and every sound is answered by something. The vast spaces between the living islands offer moments of silence only by comparison; even there, wind rasps over stone, distant herds pulse beneath the earth, and high above, solitary winged animals circle in the hot air.

From the highest ridges, the plateau seems almost empty: scattered green continents floating in a sea of ochre rock, clouds casting slow shadows, thin smoke rising from one human shelter far below. Then one of the great forests moves.

Not sways. Moves.

A dark line travels through the vegetation as thousands of bodies pass beneath it. Leaves rise and fall. The tall plants bend away in sequence. The sound reaches the ridge several breaths later—a tremendous, layered roar of feeding, alarm, wingbeats, snapping stems, and the deep calls of animals announcing themselves to one another across the immense heat.

The high country has stood in this balance for longer than memory. It is settled, weathered, and ancient, but never still. Its emptiness is only the space between overwhelming presences.
