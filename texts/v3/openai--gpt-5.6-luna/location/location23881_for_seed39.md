The land begins above the weather.

A broad, unfinished plateau occupies the high country, not quite a mountain summit and not yet a proper basin. It rises in long, pale shelves toward a serrated horizon, each shelf separated from the next by abrupt faces of raw stone. The elevation is great enough that the sky feels close and the valleys below have disappeared into a white ocean of cloud. Peaks emerge from that cloud in scattered islands, dark and angular, but the plateau itself is larger than a single overlook: a region of terraces, narrow ravines, shallow hollows, and open mineral flats extending beyond easy sight.

Nothing here has settled into its final form.

The rock is newly exposed, slate-gray in some places, rust-colored in others, with broad seams of black basalt and milky seams of quartz. Cliffs stand with unnaturally clean faces—not because they have been cut, but because they are still being made. Stone thrusts upward in slow, pressure-built ribs. Cracks widen and close with the temperature. New slabs lift by fractions of an inch, tilt, and fall. The ground is less a surface than an argument between what is rising and what is trying to come apart.

Water is everywhere, but rarely gathered in generous bodies. It lies just beneath the surface, trapped in the porous rock, and expresses itself as darkening patches and thin, cold seepages. Along the cliff walls, narrow threads descend from unseen sources, freezing at night into transparent cords and loosening again under the weak daylight. The rocks weep continuously. Some hollows hold tarns no wider than a house, their surfaces black and perfectly still except when a drop falls from the rim. Other depressions are filled with gray slush, shallow enough to cross but cold enough to numb the feet through leather.

Rain arrives in fine horizontal veils. Snow arrives wet and heavy, then vanishes into the stone. The air is saturated even when no precipitation is falling. Mist gathers in the ravines and hangs there, unable to rise through the thin atmosphere. It makes the plateau seem more populated than it is: a moving pale wall, a suggestion of distant land, a shoulder or wing appearing and disappearing among the vapor.

Vegetation is sparse, pressed low by cold and wind. There are black-green mats of moss around the seepages, lichen crusts spreading over the sheltered sides of boulders, and small, tough plants growing in cracks where warmth rises from below. Their leaves are narrow and waxy. Some bear red stems that remain visible after the leaves have been stripped by the weather. In the deeper hollows, sedges form thin colonies, each tuft separated from the next by bare gravel. There are no trees. Nothing reaches high enough to challenge the wind.

Yet the plateau is rich with animals.

They are not concentrated in herds or flocks. They are distributed through the emptiness, each presence made conspicuous by the space around it. Long-legged mountain grazers move across the distant shelves in lines of three or four, their bodies dark against the pale stone. They pick at moss, sedge, and the thin vegetation along the watercourses. Their hooves are broad and split, suited to unstable ground. When one animal slips, the others do not panic; they simply turn their heads and wait, still as stones, until it recovers.

Small white birds nest in the cliff cracks. In flight they are nearly invisible against the cloud, but their shadows pass over the rock in sudden, quick fragments. They gather around the seepages to drink and bathe, shaking ice from their feathers. Far above them, larger scavengers circle in silence, riding the few warm currents that rise from the dark fissures.

There are burrowing things beneath the plateau as well. Their tunnels show in the soil as lines of loosened gravel, then vanish under slabs. Occasionally the ground gives a soft internal thump, and a section of scree trembles. The animals below are not digging for food alone. They follow the heat.

Heat is the reason the high country remains alive.

Under the plateau, deep in the new stone, geothermal pressure is building. It moves through the rock in slow, powerful cycles. At intervals, vents open along the ridges and release steam with a long mechanical breath. The vapor comes out in dense white columns, scalding at the center and cold around its margins. Mineral deposits accumulate around the vents in stepped terraces: yellow sulfur, green copper stains, chalk-white crusts. They resemble the foundations of machinery, though no hand has built them.

The whole region has an industrial character without industry. There are no roads, towers, cables, engines, or ruins. Nothing has been made. But the forces at work are scaled like a factory: pressure chambers beneath the ground, heated fluids moving through channels, rock lifted and sorted, deposits laid down in repeated bands. The vents pound and hiss. Water runs through stone with the subdued rush of a pump. In the quietest places, one can hear deep vibrations passing underfoot, regular enough to seem deliberate.

The sound does not carry far. The thin air absorbs much of it, and the mist breaks what remains. Most of the time the plateau is almost quiet: a faint ticking as ice loosens from a wall, the soft scrape of hooves, a bird calling once from a ledge and then not again. Near the vents, the silence is replaced by low industrial murmurs and intermittent bursts of steam. These sounds seem enormous only because everything else is so widely spaced.

Light is weak and diffuse. The sun is present as a blurred brightness behind cloud, turning the wet stone pewter-gray. At certain hours, a narrow band of light reaches through a break in the weather and strikes one remote escarpment. For a few minutes the cliff glows red and gold, its fresh fractures sharply visible, while the surrounding land remains dim. Then the cloud closes and the color is gone.

There are no signs of habitation. The animals do not fear any absent civilization, and the wind finds every hollow without meeting a wall or roof. Tracks last only briefly. Rain fills them; frost fractures them; a new crust of mineral dust settles over them. Even the most durable marks are temporary because the plateau is still rising. A slope that is bare today may split open tomorrow, exposing warm black stone and a new line of water. A shallow ravine may deepen by a handspan in one season, then pause for years.

The erosion is not yet dominant. The land has not been worn smooth by age. It is raw and provisional, more concerned with emergence than decay. Its cliffs are young, its drainage uncertain, its soils barely begun. Water has started to choose its courses but has not yet fully committed to them. Rivers are only intentions: threads, runs, seepages, temporary falls. In places they disappear underground and reappear hundreds of yards away, having found a weakness in the rising rock.

Beyond the nearest shelves, the plateau continues in a series of open, half-formed features. A basin of dark water lies below one ridge. A field of pale stone tilts gradually toward the clouds. A line of vents marks a broad fault, each one breathing at a different rhythm. On the far side, the land drops away so completely that distance becomes indistinguishable from sky.

The place feels empty, but not dead. Its emptiness is the necessary space between processes: between one animal and the next, one seepage and the next, one pressure release and the next. Fauna moves through it like scattered sparks. Water circulates below and above. Stone rises, cracks, cools, and rises again.

Nothing has been built here because the landscape itself is still manufacturing the ground.
