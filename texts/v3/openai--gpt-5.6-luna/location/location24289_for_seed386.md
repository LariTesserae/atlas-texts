The high country is hot enough to make the rain feel warm.

It lies above the ordinary weather, a broad green basin held between immense slopes of dark stone. From a distance it resembles a plateau, though no level place remains level for long: the ground rises in long shelves, folds into ravines, and breaks away into precipices where the valley continues downward through blue haze. The enclosing mountains are so high that their upper faces are lost in cloud, but the basin itself is bright beneath a steady, pale sun. Rain gathers there and nowhere else. It arrives in brief, heavy veils, darkening every surface, then withdraws, leaving the air steaming.

Water is close to everything. It seeps out of the rock in threads and sheets. It beads along the undersides of leaves, wells up in black hollows, and moves beneath mats of moss with a soft, hidden urgency. The soil is thin on the slopes and deep in the flats, rich brown beneath a skin of fallen leaves. Small streams divide and reunite without settling into channels. In the lower parts of the basin, they spread into marshes where reeds and broad-leafed plants stand shoulder to shoulder, their roots submerged in warm, clear water. Pools collect in the old scars of the mountainside, though the scars themselves are no longer raw. Their edges have softened over centuries into fern banks, hanging gardens, and terraces of rooted trees.

The stone is volcanic, but not recently violent. It has been weathered into rounded pillars, shelves, and immense leaning plates, each surface furred with life. Basalt shows through in charcoal and blue-black bands. In places, pale mineral deposits spread over it like dried milk. These are warm to the touch. A hand pressed against one may feel a faint, regular vibration—not a tremor, exactly, but the deep cycling of heat and pressure below the basin.

Vegetation is thick without becoming impenetrable. Trees occupy every possible height: low, twisted trees along the exposed ridges; tall, straight-trunked forms in the sheltered hollows; and small, glossy-leaved growths rooted directly in the cracks of the cliffs. Vines hang across the ravines in long green curtains. Moss covers the shaded faces of the stone, while sun-loving plants bloom in abrupt, brilliant patches wherever the canopy breaks. The leaves are large, waxy, and dark, built to catch sudden rain and shed it toward the roots. After a storm, the whole landscape shines.

There are no paths, walls, poles, bridges, or ruins. Nothing has been cut, stacked, or shaped by a hand. Even fallen trees remain where they land, becoming wet corridors for beetles, fungi, and pale root systems. The absence of construction is not emptiness. The basin is crowded with natural arrangements so intricate that the eye keeps mistaking them for works of engineering: interlocking root arches, spillways formed by stone ribs, layered shelves that drain one into another, and groves aligned along invisible underground currents.

The industrial character of the place belongs to the earth itself.

Deep below, the mountain operates through chambers, pipes, valves, and pistons of stone. Superheated water rises through narrow conduits, expands in buried caverns, and drives slow movements in the crust. Every so often, a whole slope gives a minute, almost courteous adjustment. A ridge settles by the width of a leaf. A spring changes its outlet. A bank of mineral crust cracks open and begins to breathe steam. The sound is rarely loud. Far beneath the vegetation there is a muted thudding, a cyclic pressure like engines running behind several walls. It is strongest near the warm springs, where the ground gives a soft metallic hum despite containing no metal.

The air smells of rain, green matter, mineral water, and hot stone. Mist forms quickly in the ravines and remains suspended among the trees, though the open shelves can be sharply clear. Sunlight is ordinary and strong, filtered only by cloud and foliage. The heat collects under the canopy, where the atmosphere becomes close and wet. On exposed rock, it is dry enough for lichen and wiry grasses to survive, but never for long: some cloud always gathers against the heights, and water eventually returns.

Life is abundant, but quiet.

There are insects in the reeds and small winged things moving among the flowers, yet their activity is mostly a rustle and a brief flicker. Thick-bodied amphibians lie half-submerged in the warm pools. Small mammals travel through the root tangles, leaving polished tunnels in the mud. Long-legged birds stand motionless in the marsh until they suddenly unfold and cross a strip of water in silence. Larger animals occupy the forest margins and the steep gullies, but they are seldom seen directly. Their presence appears as a tremor in a fern, a slide of wet soil, or a set of deep impressions filling with rain.

Predators do not dominate the place. Nothing hunts continuously. The whole ecosystem moves in intervals: feeding, hiding, flowering, mating, shedding, flooding, drying, beginning again. Even decay is gradual and efficient. Fallen trunks disappear beneath fungi and moss, their wood hollowed from within while still standing. A tree may remain upright for years after its interior has become a moist, inhabited chamber.

The main sounds are water and distance. Rain ticking on broad leaves. Drips falling from one leaf to another long after the sky has cleared. The soft push of a current through reeds. Occasional wingbeats. A far-off crack from a warming cliff. Beneath these, almost below hearing, the buried mechanism of the mountains: a slow pulse, a pressure-release, a subterranean rotation.

At the basin’s center stands a raised island of stone, densely forested and ringed by shallow water. From its summit, the entire region can be seen: marshes shining between dark groves, waterfalls descending in thin white lines, warm vapor trailing from the ravines, and the great enclosing walls of the mountains disappearing into cloud. There is no sign that anyone has ever lived there, yet the place does not feel empty or untouched. It feels occupied by processes too large to call inhabitants.

Nothing in the landscape is still. Nothing is in a hurry.
