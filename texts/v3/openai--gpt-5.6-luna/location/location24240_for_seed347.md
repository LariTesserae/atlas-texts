The landscape begins below ground, but not in darkness.

A long, shallow basin has opened beneath the surface where a chain of sinkholes and collapsed chambers has joined together. Daylight falls through the breaks overhead in broad, pale shafts, softened by mist and the veils of roots hanging from the ceiling. The light is ordinary daylight—gray under cloud, white after rain, warm only for a brief hour when the sun reaches directly into the largest opening—but it arrives in fragments. Farther across the basin, the ceiling rises beyond sight, lost in vapor and hanging vegetation. The place feels open in the way a valley feels open, though its horizon is made of rock.

Water is everywhere.

It lies only a handspan beneath the dark soil, and in many places it lies on top of it. Thin streams run along the edges of old streets. Water beads on every wall and drips from every root. The rock faces are young and raw, recently exposed by collapse: pale limestone, blue-gray shale, surfaces sharp enough to cut skin. Fresh breaks show clean colors beneath their weathered skins. Slabs of stone have fallen into the basin and remain where they landed, dividing the ground into ridges, channels, and narrow wet valleys.

At the lowest point, a broad sheet of shallow water covers what may once have been a square or a market green. It is not deep enough to swim in, but deep enough to conceal broken paving, steps, and the tops of fallen pillars. The surface trembles whenever water enters from one of the many cracks in the walls. In the quieter corners, it reflects the ceiling openings and the branches of plants growing upside down from them.

The old settlement occupies much of the basin.

Its buildings are close-packed, built from local stone and clay brick, their walls pressed nearly against one another. Streets wind between them in narrow channels, some still paved, others swallowed by mud and moss. There are courtyards, stairways, retaining walls, bridges, cisterns, low towers, and whole rows of houses whose upper stories have collapsed into their lower rooms. Nothing was laid out for spaciousness. The town seems to have grown by adding one structure against another until there was no bare ground left.

Yet the settlement has not become a ruin in the simple sense. It has become habitat.

Moss carpets the roofs in thick, saturated layers. Ferns grow from chimneys. Pale grasses push between paving stones, their roots descending into the old drainage channels. Vines have taken the facades and pulled them inward, drawing loose stones down with them. In the courtyards, trees have established themselves in the accumulated soil: white-trunked, low and spreading, with leaves the color of deep water. Their roots enter windows and emerge through walls. In some houses, the trees have become the walls, their trunks filling rooms and lifting roofs apart.

The vegetation is lush but cold-loving. There are no broad tropical leaves, no hot perfume, no riot of bright flowers. Instead, everything is green, blue-green, silver, or nearly black. Thick liverworts cling to the masonry. Small reed beds grow wherever the water pauses. Clusters of horsetail rise from gutters and abandoned wells. In the dampest streets, a low forest of fern and sedge has formed, so dense that the old buildings appear as dark islands among it.

The air is cool enough to keep the skin damp without making the water freeze. A constant condensation gathers beneath the ceiling and falls as a fine, irregular rain. After storms on the surface, the underground basin becomes briefly louder and more dangerous. Water punches through new cracks, carries gravel down the walls, and spreads across the floors of the town. The fresh erosion is visible everywhere: raw gullies cut through gardens, sharp-edged channels slice across plazas, and new debris fans lie at the foot of cliffs that did not exist a season ago.

There is very little sound.

The place is not silent, but its sounds are close and subdued: dripping, trickling, the soft collapse of wet soil, the brushing of leaves against stone. Water moves through the settlement with the quiet persistence of breath. Every so often, a deep concussion passes through the rock when something collapses far away. It arrives as a pressure more than a noise, loosening a few grains of grit from the walls.

The animals make the basin feel occupied without making it loud.

Small black fish move through flooded streets, feeding in the moss along the curbs. Blind or nearly blind salamanders live beneath the stones, emerging after heavy rain to cross the paths in slow, shining lines. Water voles and long-legged marsh rodents nest inside the walls. They use old stairwells as burrows and old wells as nurseries. Pale bats roost in the upper chambers, leaving the daylight shafts in sudden, silent swarms when disturbed.

Birds enter through the sinkholes. Wagtails and gray marsh birds forage along the water’s edge, while larger birds nest in the broken towers and call only briefly before the sound is swallowed by the cavern. Insects are abundant: midges hovering over the pools, beetles shining beneath stones, moths feeding on the pale flowers that grow in cracks. Spiders have stretched webs between statues, balconies, and tree roots, making entire facades appear veiled.

Predation is present but muted. A stoatlike animal slips through the reed beds. Snakes lie along warmish drainage stones where surface heat lingers. The larger predators are rarely seen; their tracks appear in mud near the flooded square, and their absence is felt whenever the small animals go still.

There are no machines, wires, lamps, or manufactured sounds. The infrastructure is old and entirely physical: gravity-fed channels, stone sluices, stepped reservoirs, bridges pinned together by weight, and masonry arches thick enough to survive generations of water. Some buildings still contain carved gutters that guide condensation from roof to cistern. Some streets were deliberately raised above the marsh, though the marsh has begun to reclaim them. The people who made the town understood water, but the new water is arriving from directions they never planned for.

The rules of the place are simple and visible. Water follows the lowest path. Stone fails along its newest fracture. Plants occupy every surface that can hold a root. Anything abandoned becomes soil, shelter, or food. Nothing remains empty for long.

The settlement extends beyond the immediate basin into side valleys and half-flooded districts, all connected by tunnels, bridges, and sloping streets. In the distance, under the diffuse daylight, the town seems almost intact. Then a roof gives way beneath a mat of fern, or a whole block is revealed to be standing in a lake, and the apparent order breaks apart.

It is an urban landscape being steadily converted into a wetland, but the conversion is still young. The stone is raw. The channels are fresh. The trees are not yet old enough to dominate the buildings. For now, architecture and ecology are packed together so tightly that neither can be separated from the other. Walls hold back roots; roots hold up walls. Streets carry water. Rooms shelter animals. Gardens become ponds. The town remains recognizable only because the living landscape has not yet finished changing it.
