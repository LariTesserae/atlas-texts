The place is a low basin no larger than a village, enclosed on all sides by dark, gently rising stone. It may once have been a quarry, though the quarrying has been finished for centuries. Its walls are smooth from long weathering, not cut into steps but softened into broad, slumped faces, as if the rock had briefly been liquid and then remembered how to stand. Water moves everywhere at ground level. It gathers in shallow black shelves, threads through cracks, beads on the walls, and hangs in the air as a fine condensation that settles on every surface.

Nothing grows here.

There are no stems, leaves, mosses, or roots. The basin floor is covered instead by mineral films: dull gray plates, glassy nodules, pale porous crusts, and narrow ridges of something that resembles salt but flexes very slightly when touched. The surfaces are slick with a permanent wetness. In the dim light they seem almost organic, though their arrangement follows no recognizable pattern—rings around drains, fans spreading from warm pipes, scalloped deposits beneath overhangs. Some have been worn smooth by water. Others have been built up in dense, fragile layers, like the accretion of a shell.

The air is cool enough that breath would show, though there is little wind. A low ceiling of cloud or vapor rests inside the basin, not above it. It clings to the ground and gathers under the roofs of the scattered structures. Droplets form on the undersides of rusted beams, swell, and fall at irregular intervals. The rain here may come from the sky, or it may simply be the basin continually exhaling its buried water.

At the center stands the remains of a small processing plant. It is not large enough to dominate the landscape, but everything seems arranged in relation to it: channels slope toward it, pipes emerge from the surrounding rock and disappear beneath its floor, and the animal paths converge around its foundations. The plant consists of three squat buildings joined by narrow gantries, all of them stained nearly black by water. Their walls are made of riveted metal and poured concrete, their windows clouded opaque. One chimney still releases a thin breath of warmth. It does not smoke. It produces only a steady plume of vapor, which descends rather than rises and spreads in a white layer along the floor.

Inside, the machinery remains intermittently active.

A belt turns one quarter of a revolution, stops, then turns again. A chain trembles over a wheel whose teeth have been rounded by use. Deep below the plant, pumps labor slowly in flooded chambers, forcing water through old conduits. The motors do not roar. Their sound is reduced by the surrounding damp and stone to a patient, almost inaudible vibration, felt more often through the soles than heard. Every so often a valve knocks once. The sound travels through the basin walls and returns from several directions, making it difficult to tell where it began.

The quiet is thick with small noises.

Water ticks into water. Fine grit slides down the quarry faces. Something beneath the mineral crust makes a soft, rapid tapping as it moves. Along the edge of a shallow pool, pale bodies slip between the deposits—low, many-legged animals with translucent skins and no visible eyes. They are abundant, but seldom seen all at once. One appears beneath a sheet of water; another withdraws behind a pipe; several narrow shapes ripple through the black sediment in the instant before the surface closes over them.

They feed on the plant’s wastes, on chemical films, on one another.

The largest creatures are about the size of dogs, though they do not resemble dogs in any other way. They have flattened bodies armored in overlapping plates, with broad gripping limbs and delicate whisker-like organs that trail in the water. Their backs carry small vents that open and close with the rhythm of breathing. They gather in the warm runoff beneath the factory and lie piled together, nearly touching, each one stealing heat from the bodies below. When disturbed, they do not flee immediately. They turn toward the disturbance as a group, their bodies adjusting with a slow, coordinated ripple. Then the whole mass slides silently into the channels.

Smaller animals live in the seams of the ground. They are nearly transparent, or else so dark that they vanish against the wet stone. Some cling to vertical surfaces and drink the condensation. Some travel in lines beneath the mineral crust, making it twitch and settle behind them. Near the old settling tanks, dense colonies of tiny, hinged things open and close themselves in the water, filtering it. They create a faint silver movement across the surface, like rain falling upward.

Predation is quiet here. A long, narrow animal waits beneath a ledge with only its feeding tendrils exposed. The tendrils resemble threads of wet wire. When a smaller creature passes, they contract instantly, and the water closes with hardly a sound. In another pool, something large shifts under the opaque surface, displacing a dark mound of silt. The smaller animals scatter in all directions, though there is no splash.

The basin has no horizon. Its boundaries are close and certain: rock wall, plant wall, machinery, pool. A person could cross it in a few minutes, but would not experience it as a simple distance. There are too many layers pressed together. Pipes run above channels; channels run beneath grates; grates are furred with mineral deposits; the deposits conceal burrows; the burrows open into flooded chambers. Every surface has a second surface underneath it.

Old human marks remain, but they are sparse and subdued. A ladder descends into a flooded shaft. Numbers are stenciled on a wall, the paint blistered and softened. A warning sign leans against a concrete pillar, its letters obscured by pale growth—not plant growth, but a hard, branching deposit that has spread across the metal in the shape of frost. A small inspection hut stands apart from the factory, half-sunk in mud. Its door hangs open. Inside are a bench, a dead electrical panel, and a row of hooks from which nothing hangs.

The mineral formations have incorporated fragments of the machinery. A cable disappears into a pearly mound and emerges several feet away, its insulation replaced by a translucent sheath. Bolts are surrounded by smooth halos. Around the base of a broken pump, layers of gray material have risen in slow concentric folds, preserving the pump’s shadow beneath them. The place does not reclaim objects quickly. It grows around them, seals them in, and continues.

There is a faint illumination without a clear source. The sky above the basin is dim silver, but the factory windows give off a weaker blue-gray glow, perhaps from old electrical systems still discharging somewhere underground. The pools absorb what little light exists. Their surfaces are black except where the animals move or where thin mineral skins catch the glow and return it as a muted sheen. Near the warm machinery, condensation turns the air luminous, making every passerby appear incomplete: a shoulder without a body, a hand dissolving into vapor, the pale outline of a creature emerging briefly from the mist.

The rules of the place are not hostile so much as persistent.

Water always finds the lowest available route, even when the route leads upward through a pipe or into a sealed machine. Heat attracts life. Vibration attracts attention. Bright light causes the small animals to freeze and the larger ones to gather. The mineral deposits are slow but responsive; they thicken around prolonged sound and thin where water runs quickly. Anything left in the basin for long enough becomes wet, then stained, then partially integrated. Metal rusts in layers rather than flakes. Concrete darkens from within.

The old factory still follows its production cycle, though no finished product leaves it. Water enters, is heated, filtered, pressurized, and returned to the basin. The process has become its own ecology. The animals depend on it. The mineral formations record it. When the pumps pause, the basin changes immediately: warm pools cool, the clustered fauna unbind themselves, and the entire population begins moving toward the dark conduits below the plant. When the machinery starts again, the creatures return in dense, silent streams.

At intervals, the rain begins.

It does not fall heavily. It descends as a fine, cold veil, thickening the vapor and raising the water level by a finger’s breadth. Every exposed surface becomes reflective. The quarry walls shine. The metal structures gleam. The animals rise from their burrows and channels in such numbers that the ground appears to be flowing. For several minutes the basin is almost motionless except for this migration: thousands of pale limbs, plated backs, jointed bodies, and threadlike feelers traveling toward the plant, toward warmth, toward whatever buried mechanism keeps the water moving.

Then the rain stops.

The creatures disappear beneath the crust. The pools settle. A valve knocks once in the distance, and the sound passes through the stone.

Nothing grows, but the place is not barren. It is crowded with processes—filtering, feeding, condensing, corroding, pumping, shedding, and slowly becoming mineral.
