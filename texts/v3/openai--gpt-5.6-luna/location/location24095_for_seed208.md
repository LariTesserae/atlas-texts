The basin lies low beneath a ceiling of cloud.

It is not a valley in the ordinary sense, because nothing rises high enough to deserve the name of mountain. The land has been worn down for so long that its boundaries have become uncertain: broad shelves of gray stone descend by imperceptible degrees toward a dark, shallow inland sea. Far away, the horizon is interrupted by low ridges, smooth-backed and ancient, their sides folded into one another like layers of cooled ash. Between them, the open ground is flooded in long silver sheets.

Water is everywhere, though rarely deep. It seeps through the stone, stands in saucer-shaped hollows, gathers beneath overhangs, and moves in narrow black channels that have no visible source. The rock itself sweats. Beads of water hang from the undersides of ledges, joining and falling with a patient, ceaseless ticking. In places, entire walls weep in thin curtains. The surface of the basin is never entirely dry; even during the briefest clear interval, the stone shines as if freshly polished.

There is no plant life.

No grass softens the flats. No moss fills the cracks. There are no roots holding the mud, no leaves interrupting the sky, no color of green anywhere. The world is made of mineral, water, and animals—though whether the animals are truly animals is difficult to say.

They are abundant.

Across the distant shelves, pale bodies move in scattered streams. Some are low and many-legged, their limbs jointed like the fingers of a buried hand. Some drag themselves through the flooded channels, leaving wakes that shine with a faint blue residue. Others cling to vertical stone and descend slowly, upside down, their shells the color of old bone. They have no eyes that can be seen, but they turn toward disturbance. They react to one another. They hunt, flee, swarm, mate, divide, and die.

Their remains form much of the landscape.

The flats are covered in thin scales, curved plates, translucent spines, and hollow, ribbed casings. Underfoot, the fragments crunch and grind into a chalky paste that washes away with every rain. In some depressions they have accumulated into low banks, pale as salt. In others, the bodies of the dead have fused with the stone, making ridges that look almost architectural. The oldest layers are black and glassy, compressed over ages into smooth shelves. The newest are still wet, still twitching.

The most common creatures are the size of a hand, though some grow as large as a door. They travel in loose congregations rather than herds, spreading across the basin with no apparent leader. Their movement is noisy beyond reason. Thousands scrape their shells over stone at once, producing a continuous rasp like a continent being filed down. When startled, they strike their limbs against the ground in a rapid, clattering burst. The sound travels over the water and returns from the low ridges, multiplied until a single disturbance becomes a rolling concussion.

Nothing here is quiet.

The falling water makes a constant white roar. Every channel hisses and chatters over its stony bed. The larger animals emit deep pulses through their hollow bodies, sounds felt more in the ribs than heard with the ears. At irregular intervals, whole colonies begin to call together: a rising, tremulous vibration that swells into a deafening drone. The air seems to thicken beneath it. Water trembles in its pools. Loose shells hop and turn on the flats.

Then, as suddenly as it began, the call stops.

For a moment the basin is left only with rain, seepage, and the small sounds of feeding.

The atmosphere is cool and dim, but not dark. Light filters through the cloud ceiling in a diffuse gray, with no clear direction and almost no shadow. The wet stone reflects what little brightness exists, so that the ground appears lit from within in patches: a dull pewter here, a blue-black sheen there, a milky gleam around the shallowest pools. The creatures are mostly pale, dark, or colorless, but some bear bands of muted orange and violet beneath their translucent shells. These markings appear brighter when the bodies are disturbed.

There is a strange pressure to the weather. Rain does not fall in storms so much as arrive in long, patient episodes. A veil descends over the whole basin, thickens, slackens, and returns. Sometimes the cloud lowers until the far shelves disappear entirely. Sometimes the air clears enough to reveal the full reach of the basin: watercourses crossing one another in branching patterns, isolated stone towers, and the occasional small structure standing alone in the emptiness.

The structures are primitive and scattered.

Most are little more than windbreaks made from stacked slabs, their joints sealed with a dark mud that has dried hard as ceramic. A few consist of shallow pits roofed with rib bones and plates from the larger creatures. There are narrow walkways raised above the wettest ground, though many have collapsed and lie half-submerged. Along some channels, crude traps have been set: hinged frames of stone, weighted with shells, designed to catch the smaller crawling animals as they follow the water.

No road connects the structures. No settlement gathers around them. They belong to people—or to something that once had hands—but the builders have left no obvious signs of their presence beyond the arrangements of stone. A fire pit may be found cold and filled with rainwater. A tool of chipped black rock may lie beneath a ledge. Once, on a high shelf, there is a row of standing slabs, each marked by a single shallow groove.

The grooves all face the inland sea.

The sea itself is not truly open water. It is a vast, flat expanse spread over a depression in the old stone, perhaps only waist-deep at its center. Its surface is usually obscured by mist, rain, or the movement of what lives beneath it. Long backs occasionally break the water—segmented backs, ridged backs, backs covered in soft mouths that open and close as they pass. The largest forms move with such slowness that they can be mistaken for islands until one of them exhales.

When they exhale, the entire basin hears it.

A column of vapor rises from the water. The surrounding creatures scatter. Waves cross the shallows and climb the banks. The sound rolls outward like a distant explosion, though nothing is broken except the surface.

The land is ancient in a way that does not feel peaceful. Its age is visible in the near-completeness of its erosion. Every sharp edge has been softened. Every slope has settled. The ridges are not being shaped so much as finished. Rain, seepage, and the constant passage of bodies are reducing the basin toward a final, level form.

Yet the process is not finished, and life—if that word applies—keeps it active.

Water loosens the stone. Animals grind it down. Their shells are worn away and replaced. Their dead become new ground, then are washed into the channels, then returned to the sea. The basin is a slow circulation of mineral and flesh. Nothing grows upward. Everything accumulates, collapses, dissolves, and moves on.

There is no obvious sun, but the dimness changes. The creatures know these changes. Before the light weakens, they retreat from the open flats toward cracks and flooded hollows. Before it brightens, they emerge in their thousands, spreading across the wet ground to feed. Predators—larger, quieter things with long flexible jaws—follow them at a distance. Their presence is marked by sudden gaps in the crawling streams and by the violent clatter of smaller bodies escaping in every direction.

At the edge of the basin, where the stone rises only slightly, the ground is crowded with old shells and broken tools. From there one can look across the entire open region: the low drowned shelves, the black channels, the isolated shelters, the pale congregations moving like spilled beads, and the inland water breathing under the cloud.

The world offers no horizon of green, no warmth, no high place from which to escape the noise.

Only water, worn stone, dim light, and the tireless abundance of things that have learned to live without leaves.
