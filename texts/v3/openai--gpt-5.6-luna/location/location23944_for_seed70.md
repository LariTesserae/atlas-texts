The water begins above the trees.

It does not fall from a sky, nor gather in a recognizable lake. It occupies the whole upper world: a warm, clear ocean suspended over a high country of newly risen stone. Its surface is far above the mountain ridges, and its pressure settles downward through every hollow and fissure. The land is submerged, but not drowned in darkness. Sunlight travels a long way through the water and arrives green-gold, softened but ordinary enough to cast shadows.

The highest ridges stand like islands in a deep interior sea, their summits still hundreds of meters below the water’s skin. From above—if above is meaningful here—the terrain would resemble a chain of broad, broken uplands. From within it, the country feels open. Long valleys extend beyond sight, their floors descending in terraces toward blue distances. Cliffs are fresh and angular, not yet rounded by time. They look cut from the earth with a single immense stroke: black volcanic shelves, pale shelves of newly exposed mineral, red seams that glow dully where the water passes over them.

The stone is young. It has not acquired the patient softness of an old landscape. Ridges end abruptly. Boulders stand balanced on narrow points. Whole walls of rock lean at impossible angles, held for now by pressure and friction. Fine fragments drift constantly from the faces—not in avalanches, but in a slow, nearly imperceptible shedding—and collect in pale fans at the foot of every escarpment. A touch, if anything still touched here, might send a slope whispering into the valley.

The warmth is pervasive. It does not come only from the distant sun. The earth itself breathes heat through cracks and seams. Around the vents, the water is almost silky, full of minerals and minute suspended life. Columns rise from fissures and spread beneath the overhanging shelves like inverted smoke. They do not boil. Nothing here moves quickly enough to seem violent. The warm plumes ascend for days, perhaps years, before thinning into the surrounding water.

Vegetation has taken the new ground with astonishing confidence.

On every ledge, mats of green growth lie thick as moss, though many are not moss at all. They are broad, low forests of translucent fronds, each blade ribbed with silver channels. Their roots thread into cracks in the rock and into one another, making living nets beneath the sediment. In the valleys, vegetation becomes taller. Stalks rise in clustered groves, flexible and jointed, their crowns opening like umbrellas toward the light above. Their colors remain mostly green, but deep water has altered the meaning of green: some leaves are nearly black, some blue as glass, some yellow enough to seem lit from within.

There are hanging gardens on the undersides of cliffs. Long curtains of vine descend into the ravines, each strand beaded with bubbles that do not rise. The bubbles remain attached to the plants, shifting slowly as the water moves, and inside them small white blossoms open and close. Between the shelves grow fan-shaped colonies whose surfaces ripple though there is no visible current. They are plants, or something close enough to plants that the distinction has no use here. When sediment settles over them, they lift themselves by a few centimeters and wait.

The density of life makes the landscape feel crowded even though animals are scarce. Every stone is furred, filmed, rooted, or webbed with growth. Pale filaments spread across bare faces in branching patterns, joining one patch of vegetation to another. Some of the mineral seams are actually living deposits: slow-building crusts that thicken around warmth and turn the cracks into raised, branching ridges. A newly emerged valley may be empty of trees yet still be packed with thousands of quiet processes.

Fauna appears only as interruption.

A long, ribbon-bodied creature may pass far overhead, its outline broken by the light, too distant to tell whether it swims or is carried by the water. A cluster of transparent shapes drifts between the fronds, contracting once every several minutes. In a forest of tall growth, something large has pressed a passage through the leaves, but no track continues at either end. The plants close over it slowly.

There are no swarms, no constant flicker of small mouths and fins. Animal life is rare enough that each presence becomes an event, though the landscape itself does not react. A creature can move through a valley and leave no alarm, only a temporary darkening among the stems. If it dies, it is taken apart gradually by the growth around it. The bones, if bones they are, become pale supports for new leaves.

Sound travels strangely in the submerged airless world. The place is mostly quiet, but never silent.

There is the low pressure-song of the water against the cliffs, almost below hearing. There are faint clicks from stones settling in the young slopes. Vents make a soft, continuous exhalation. The tall plants flex against one another with a dry, papery whisper despite being underwater, as if their surfaces carry their own pockets of air. Occasionally, from somewhere beyond the visible ridges, comes a broad, distant tone: not an animal call, but the sound of an entire shelf cracking and shifting by the width of a hand.

Light remains normal in its behavior, yet the water gives every hour a second atmosphere. Near the upper reaches, sunlight is warm and direct. Farther down, it becomes green, then bronze, then a blue so deep that shadows begin to look solid. On clear days, the submerged mountains cast enormous shadows across the valleys. On days when the suspended water thickens with silt, the world narrows to a few dozen meters, and the plants loom like trees in fog.

There is no weather in the ordinary sense. Instead, pressure fronts pass through the water. The vegetation bends long before the current can be felt. Whole forests tilt in one direction and remain that way for weeks. Mineral dust travels slowly across the high country, settling on leaves and ledges in delicate drifts. When the pressure changes, the dust lifts again and flows along the valleys like smoke.

Time is visible here.

New terraces form where sediment gathers. Green crust advances over a black rock face by the width of a fingernail. A cliff loses one stone, then another, each fall separated by seasons. The warmest valleys become denser each year, their plants layering over old growth until the floor rises toward the hanging gardens. Nothing is sudden except the original making of the place, and that event is already past.

The world has no roads, shelters, tools, or signs of intention. Its only boundaries are gradients: warmth thinning into coolness, light sinking into blue, bare stone becoming living surface, solid ground descending into trenches where even the plants grow sparse. Somewhere beyond the open ridges, the submerged country drops into true deep water. The valley edges do not end there; they simply become less visible, their vegetation reduced to luminous threads, their young cliffs descending through green darkness.

At high elevation, beneath an ocean that should not be there, the land is beginning to settle into itself. It is lush without being old, crowded without being loud, and strange without appearing broken. Every feature seems temporary—the sharp ridge, the suspended bubble, the leaning forest—yet the whole landscape moves with the patience of something that expects to remain.
