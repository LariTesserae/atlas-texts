The land begins above the clouds.

Not on a sharp summit, nor on a range of proud peaks, but on an immense, weathered highland whose upper surface has been worn nearly level over ages beyond memory. It rises so far above the surrounding world that the lower valleys are seldom visible. They appear only in fragments: a blue incision between stone shelves, a pale glint of distant water, a cloudbank flowing below the escarpments like a second, moving ground.

The plateau is not flat in any gentle sense. It is a continent of steps, terraces, tilted tables, and old ruptures. Great shelves of dark stone descend toward the horizon, each separated from the next by cliffs hundreds of meters high. Time has rounded the edges, powdered the faces, and filled every crack with soil. Erosion has done almost all it can do here. The land has entered a final form—ancient, immense, and softened without becoming smooth.

In places, the rock stands in remnants of its former shapes: arches with their centers fallen away, columns worn hollow, ridges like the ribs of buried animals. Their surfaces are black, green, and rust-red, stained by mineral seepage and years of wind. Some formations look melted. Others seem to have been carved by deliberate hands, though no tool marks remain sharp enough to prove it. At the edges of the plateau, whole portions of stone peel away without warning. A slab the size of a village may lean for centuries, groaning softly, and then collapse in a single tremendous descent.

The sound of that collapse is not heard so much as inhabited.

Wind dominates the high country. It crosses the open terraces with nothing to slow it, pouring through gaps and down stair-step valleys in long, continuous blasts. It makes the cliffs roar. It enters cracks in the rock and emerges as tones—deep organ notes, shrill whistles, booming pulses that seem to come from underground. During the strongest gusts, conversation becomes impossible even at arm’s length. Loose stones skitter uphill. Grass lies flat for minutes, then springs upright as the wind shifts direction.

Far below, unseen water contributes its own voice. Rivers run through the chasms, swollen briefly by melt and storm, then reduced to threads in the dry seasons. Their falls repeat through the landscape as a deafening, distant thunder. Rain does not arrive often, but when it does, it arrives violently. Clouds strike the plateau rather than pass over it. The world vanishes into white vapor, and water streams through channels that may have been dry for years. It drums on stone, hammers the vegetation, fills hollows, and tears fresh scars through ancient soil.

Then the storm is gone.

Warmth returns quickly under a pale, ordinary sun. Mist clings in sheltered places: beneath overhangs, in narrow ravines, around the mouths of caves. Condensation gathers at night on the cold faces of the cliffs, beading in lines and dripping into hidden basins. These small waters sustain almost everything alive. A patch of moss may occupy a crack no wider than a hand and still spread for hundreds of meters along the shaded wall. Pools collect in the basins of eroded stone, green at the edges and perfectly dark in their centers.

Vegetation is unexpectedly lush, but never crowded. It grows in islands separated by great expanses of bare rock and wind-scoured gravel. Tall, flexible grasses occupy the protected terraces, their blades silver underneath and dark green above. Low trees grow where ancient dust has accumulated in hollows. They have broad, waxy leaves to preserve moisture and trunks bent permanently away from the prevailing wind. Their roots descend through cracks rather than soil, gripping the plateau like fingers.

In the wetter ravines, vegetation becomes almost excessive. Ferns spill from the cliff faces. Vines descend in curtains, trembling constantly in the air currents. Thick mats of flowering plants cover the banks of temporary streams, blooming all at once after rain and collapsing into brown threads within days. Their flowers turn toward sound rather than light. A passing rockfall can make an entire slope open in color.

There are few animals. The emptiness is conspicuous. A winged creature may cross the sky once in an afternoon, riding the thermals without flapping. Small pale things live under the stones and emerge only during rain. Their tracks appear in the mud and are erased before anyone can follow them. In the grasslands, large grazing shapes sometimes stand motionless for hours, too distant to identify. They are thin, long-legged, and almost without visible heads. When approached, they do not flee. They simply become difficult to see, as if the wind has carried them farther away.

The plateau is not still despite its age. It is constantly in the process of changing—just not by ordinary growth. Wind-driven dust migrates across whole regions. Stormwater cuts new temporary rivers. Cliffs shed their outer layers. Patches of vegetation advance and retreat in response to moisture, leaving the land mottled with green one season and ocher the next. Dust devils form without warning and travel in coordinated lines, crossing the terraces like herds. At night, faint electrical storms flicker inside the clouds below, illuminating the undersides in silent, irregular flashes.

Occasional human traces mark the openness. A low shelter of stacked stone stands beside one of the reliable springs, its roof long gone. Narrow paths, more remembered than visible, connect water pockets across the plateau. Here and there are circles of stones, rusted metal wedges, scraps of woven fiber sealed into old mud. No settlement persists. The distances are too large, the weather too abrupt, the available water too uncertain. Those who come here build only what they need and leave it to be absorbed by the land.

One structure remains more complete than the others: a small watchtower on a shelf overlooking several valleys. It is made of dark blocks fitted without mortar, each block rounded by age. Wind passes through its empty windows with such force that the tower produces a continuous, shuddering note. From a distance it resembles a tooth, a marker, or a remnant of some much larger construction buried beneath the plateau.

There is no single horizon here. The highland extends outward in overlapping surfaces, each fading into haze, cloud, or distance. The eye finds green, then stone, then open air. Everything is exposed. Everything is far apart. Yet the whole place is loud with forces acting upon it: wind in the hollows, water in the deeps, rock giving way, plants opening after rain, clouds boiling beneath the world.

It feels less like a landscape at rest than like the last surviving layer of a vast, ancient machine—worn nearly smooth, sparsely inhabited, and still operating with terrible power.
