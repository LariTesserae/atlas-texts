The place lies beneath the ground, though not in a cave.

It is an old cistern-city, a chambered landscape made entirely by hands that have been gone for centuries. Its floor, walls, terraces, channels, stairways, buttresses, and overhead vaults are all built from the same honey-colored stone: soft enough to cut with bronze and harder tools, hard enough to endure. There is no raw earth anywhere. Even the mud is manufactured—fine grit washed from worn masonry and gathered in shallow basins, where it lies in dark, damp seams between paving slabs.

The city is small by the measure of cities, no larger than a hilltop village compressed into a single broad room. Its boundaries are sharply felt. At one end, a wall of fitted blocks rises into darkness. At the other, the floor slopes toward a series of low arches and vanishing drains. The ceiling is close enough to be part of the terrain, ribbed with beams and old supporting ribs. In places, it descends so low that the inhabitants must stoop; elsewhere, it lifts into a warm, smoky vault where echoes gather and refuse to die.

Everything is crowded.

Narrow walkways cross one another over water channels. Stone shelves have been fixed into the walls at every height. Small chambers open behind curtains of woven reed, beneath staircases, inside buttresses, and under the raised floors of workshops. Clay jars stand shoulder to shoulder in alcoves. Rope ladders hang beside carved footholds. There are no empty corners, only corners not currently being used.

Moisture arrives irregularly. Sometimes it comes as a sudden infiltration through the ceiling, a cold silver thread that becomes a torrent for ten minutes and then ceases. Sometimes the walls sweat all night, beading along their joints. Warm air moves through hidden shafts and carries condensation from one chamber to another. Water gathers in basins, in grooves worn down the center of steps, in the hollow feet of statues, in the mouths of carved animals. It is never allowed to remain where it falls. Every drop has a route.

The channels are the city’s true streets.

They run in parallel lines beneath grates of slotted stone, around the bases of buildings, beneath sleeping platforms, and through the workshops. Some carry clean water toward cisterns. Others remove waste, carrying it with a steady brown rush into lower chambers. A few are ritual channels, kept empty until the rains come aboveground, when the people open their gates and let the buried city listen to the storm through its plumbing.

The stone is worn but not ruined. Its edges have softened, its corners rounded by hands, feet, rope, paws, and water. Long grooves shine on the stair treads. Doorways have swollen into irregular arches from generations of passage. Where the masonry has cracked, someone has packed the fault with pebbles, resin, fired clay, or braided fiber. Every repair has been repaired again. The place does not decay so much as accumulate its own history in layers of practical correction.

There is little vegetation. A few pale creepers grow from cracks where wet grit has gathered, their leaves small and thick, their color the muted green of old glass. Mats of moss cling to the shaded sides of water troughs. In the warmest chambers, edible fungi are cultivated in stacked clay trays, their caps crowded together like folded ears. The people keep bundles of dried root and reed, but these are brought from elsewhere. Nothing grows freely for long. The stone is too occupied, the light too scarce, the passages too constantly used.

The light is ordinary but displaced. It enters through shafts, vents, and gridded openings far overhead, breaking into pale rectangles that crawl slowly across the walls. Some chambers receive a warm amber glow from oil bowls and charcoal braziers. The flames are low, shielded, and carefully rationed. Smoke stains the vaults black, but currents of air carry it toward certain chimney passages, making those passages valuable and continuously crowded with hanging meat, damp cloth, and clay vessels.

In the main chamber, the air is warm enough to soften wax and raise sweat beneath clothing. It smells of wet stone, boiled grain, animal musk, smoke, resin, and mineral water. Condensation gathers on the skin. The inhabitants wipe their faces with strips of cloth, then hang those strips from lines stretched across the walkways. The cloths drip onto the people below.

Sound is everywhere and has nowhere to go.

Water hammers through the channels. Wooden mallets strike stone. Hand drills rasp. Loom weights knock against one another. Children shout from one level to another, and their voices return from three directions at once. Goats complain from a pen beneath a stair. Bats flicker through the high vaults, their wings making soft, rapid claps. Somewhere, a wheel turns continuously in a stream, creaking at each revolution. Somewhere else, a woman sings a work-song in a low register meant to carry through masonry.

Even quiet activities produce noise. Feet scrape on grit. Jars knock together. Rats rustle in the walls. Water ticks from a ceiling seam into a bronze bowl. The city’s silence, when it briefly occurs, is not peaceful. It is an alarm.

The fauna is abundant because the built environment provides countless niches. Small, long-bodied rats live in the drainage galleries and have learned to avoid the traps set by hand. Pale cave swallows nest under lintels and feed on insects drawn to the lamps. Fat beetles flourish in the refuse channels, their shells polished dark by constant moisture. Thin-legged spiders suspend webs between every high beam, making silver veils that tremble whenever the air shafts breathe.

There are domesticated animals as well: small goats with clipped horns, narrow-backed pigs, pigeons kept for eggs, and a species of burrowing fowl that spends most of its life under raised floors. The people breed them in dense enclosures, using every scrap of food and returning every useful waste to the proper channel. Nothing is simply discarded. One creature’s refuse is another creature’s feed, fuel, dye, plaster additive, or bait.

At certain times, the channels boil with silver fish.

They arrive during the intermittent floods, carried down from an unseen upper reservoir. The fish are small, blind or nearly blind, and so numerous that the water seems to move against itself. People lower woven baskets into the grates and lift them writhing into the light. Children wade knee-deep through the overflow while adults shout warnings from the ledges. The fish vanish within hours, salted, smoked, or packed into clay jars. The few that escape retreat into the darkness beneath the city, where they remain until the next water event.

The inhabitants understand their world through maintenance. They do not speak of nature and construction as separate things. A wall that leaks is sick. A channel that runs clear is well-fed. A fungus bed that fruits too quickly is frightened. A collapsed stair is not a ruin but an interruption in the city’s circulation. Their laws are mostly about flow: water must not be blocked, smoke must not be trapped, animals must not be cornered near a cistern, and no person may close a door that another person has marked with wet clay.

The oldest rules concern sound.

No one may strike iron after the third watch, because the vibration travels through the vaults and wakes the nesting bats. No one may sing in the eastern drain, where voices become confused with the sound of incoming water. If a person hears their own footsteps following them, they must stop and wait; this means a maintenance passage has opened somewhere behind them, or that something has come out of it.

The place is not haunted, exactly. It is too inhabited for that. But it retains impressions. Some chambers amplify certain words. Some staircases answer pressure with a dull internal knock. A sealed doorway occasionally exhales warm air smelling of rain, though no one has opened it for generations. These things are regarded with caution rather than wonder. The city is old, and old structures have habits.

Life here moves quickly in small bursts: a flood, a hatch of insects, the sudden birth of kids in a crowded pen, a roof leak opened by shifting stone, a quarrel over a dry storage shelf, a rush to clear a drain before water reaches the sleeping tiers. Between these events, the city settles into its long-established balance. People patch. Animals feed. Water descends. Smoke rises. The stone receives the pressure of everything and gradually rounds itself around it.

Above, the surface may be changing—storms passing, grasses bending, dust collecting in the sun—but down here the world is enclosed, warm, loud, and made. Its horizon is a wall. Its weather is condensation and flood. Its forests are scaffolds, ropes, hanging fungus, and the dense branching of corridors.

Nothing is natural, yet nothing feels finished.
