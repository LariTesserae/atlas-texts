The landscape is a low country without a horizon.

It has been built rather than formed: an immense level of dark masonry extending farther than distance can remain meaningful. The ground is assembled from fitted slabs, wedges, channels, vaults, and broad plates of fired mineral, all of them made by hand or by tools only slightly more capable than hands. No seam is straight for long. Courses buckle. Foundations rise unexpectedly through the surface like the knuckles of something buried, then stop. Whole districts of pavement end in clean, unfinished edges, beyond which there is only a gray-black substrate waiting to be given a shape.

It is hot here, but not brightly so. Heat comes up from the constructed ground in slow breaths. The air is heavy with moisture, and every surface sweats. Water gathers in the joints between stones, in shallow rectangular depressions, along the undersides of lintels. It runs in narrow channels cut into the paving, then pauses, as if uncertain where downhill is. The channels are numerous but rarely connected. Some fill steadily from unseen sources. Others drain upward into the dim air.

There is no soil, and nothing grows.

No green interrupts the darkness. No root has cracked a wall. No moss has taken hold in a shaded corner. The few organic-looking stains are mineral: soft black blooms spreading over warm stone, translucent films that tighten and loosen with the humidity, pale crusts shaped like collapsed petals. They may be life in a form too early, too late, or too strange to deserve the word. They do not feed, reproduce, or move in any recognizable way. They simply occur, and sometimes occur elsewhere.

The buildings are sparse across the vastness. A tower stands there, then another several kilometers away, and between them a plain of unfinished foundations and empty reservoirs. The distance is not wilderness. It is absence inside a project.

Everything has been made for something, though much of it no longer remembers what.

There are staircases descending into shallow water. Arches with no walls beneath them. Long raised roads that lead toward blank faces of masonry. Cisterns the size of inland seas, each containing only a hand’s breadth of dark water. There are circular platforms studded with sockets, abandoned blocks still bearing the marks of chisels, and immense avenues whose paving stones have been laid one at a time across the flat country. Some stones are raw and angular; others have been polished until they reflect a dull, bruised version of the sky.

The tools remain in places. Wooden mallets swollen with moisture. Stone wedges. Bronze-colored blades gone soft at the edges. Ropes that continue to tighten when no one pulls them. Scaffolds of black timber stand around half-built structures, though there are no builders visible anywhere. Occasionally a hammer falls in the distance. It strikes once, and the sound travels for minutes through the low country, diminishing without quite becoming silence.

The air is dim beneath a ceiling of cloud or vapor. It is difficult to tell which. The sky has the color of watered charcoal, with faint expanses of reddish illumination behind it. There is no clear sun. At irregular intervals the whole vault brightens from within, not as lightning does, but as if some enormous object has opened its eye behind the atmosphere. Shadows become visible then—thin, sharp, and pointing in different directions from one object to the next. When the illumination fades, the shadows do not always return to their owners. They remain cast across the ground, sometimes moving slowly on their own.

Rain is frequent, though it does not fall everywhere at once. Curtains of warm water move across the constructed plain in broad, distant bands. Under them, the architecture darkens and gleams. Water strikes roofs, ledges, paving, and empty tanks with a soft and almost continuous ticking. Where rain has passed, surfaces steam. The vapor hangs low, hiding the bases of towers and making the far structures appear to float.

The rain sometimes falls sideways. Sometimes it falls from the ground into the sky.

In those places, puddles lift themselves into thin columns, trembling as they rise. They gather beneath low clouds and vanish. A wet channel may empty upward over the course of an hour, leaving behind a clean, dry groove. Elsewhere, moisture condenses in the air above an avenue and drops as a perfect sheet, descending from no visible cloud. The water is warm enough to blur the boundaries of things. Through it, a wall may appear to have been built in another location.

The geography is young.

Not young in the sense of being fresh or fertile, but young as a thought that has not finished deciding what it means. The land is still selecting its arrangements. A plain can acquire a slope while watched. A foundation can sink several meters into the paving without breaking any stone. A road may continue beyond its previous end, its new blocks appearing one by one from the fog, each already worn at the corners. The changes are not violent. Nothing collapses with drama. Instead, the world revises itself with patient turbulence.

A wall built in the morning may contain a door by evening. A door may open onto the same wall from the other side. A staircase may have one more step each time it is climbed, though the climb never takes longer. In one district, every column leans slightly toward the nearest source of water. In another, all masonry leans toward the sound of the last person who spoke.

The terrain is mostly low, but “level” is unreliable. Broad basins lie among shallow rises, and the basins are flooded with a few centimeters of black water. Beyond them are slabs raised just high enough to keep the feet dry. There are no mountains, but the scale makes mountains unnecessary. The architecture itself supplies the vertical measure: parapets receding into haze, towers disappearing into the dim vapor, bridges crossing empty air at heights that cannot be reconciled with the ground beneath them.

The built world is so extensive that it has developed weather of its own.

Warm fronts move along avenues. Pressure gathers in enclosed courtyards and bursts through keyholes. Clouds form inside unfinished halls, drift through windows, and dissolve against exterior walls. In the open plains, gusts move in perfect circles around isolated structures, carrying water droplets, dust, and the faint odor of hot mineral. The storms are not large enough to be majestic. They are too close, too low, too local. A whole weather system may turn around a single basin for days, while ten kilometers away the air remains still.

There is little sound apart from water and construction.

The wet ground clicks as it contracts. Distant slabs grind against one another with the slow complaint of tectonic plates, though they have been cut and placed by tools. In the empty halls, moisture falls from ceiling to floor in widely spaced drops. The echoes are weak and reluctant. Sound seems to lose interest after traveling a certain distance. A shout becomes a murmur, then a pressure in the air, then nothing.

Sometimes the quiet is interrupted by a collective adjustment: every door in a district closes at once, or every loose stone shifts a fraction toward the east. The event produces a broad, soft shudder. Water trembles in the channels. The dark films on the masonry contract. Then the landscape settles into a slightly different version of itself.

There are marks of habitation, but no inhabitants.

Small alcoves have been carved into the walls at regular intervals, each large enough for a crouching body. Some contain bowls, cords, sharpened stones, or bundles of reeds that have never been part of a plant. In the largest chambers, hundreds of low platforms face one another across empty floors. Their surfaces are worn smooth, though nothing seems to have knelt or sat upon them. A few footprints appear in wet dust, always single prints, as if someone had taken one step and then ceased to occupy the world.

No animal calls come from the dark. No insects gather around the moisture. Nothing flees from the approaching weather.

Yet the place is not dead. It is too active for death.

At long intervals, a line of masonry will begin to extend across the plain, laying itself down with the measured logic of a primitive road crew. Blocks emerge from the wet ground, rise, turn, and lock against one another. The work may continue for a hundred kilometers, leaving a fresh avenue behind. Later the avenue will bend toward an impossible destination, or narrow until it becomes a single line of stone, or pass through a wall that has appeared across it.

Elsewhere, structures unmake themselves. A tower loses its upper half without falling; the missing courses simply cease to have been placed. A bridge becomes a row of disconnected piers. An entire courtyard turns inside out, so that its walls stand on the floor and its floor hangs overhead. The materials remain solid. It is only the agreement between them that fails.

Reality breaks most visibly at the edges of things.

A doorway may show the dim, wet landscape, but the rain in that view falls upward. The reflection of a tower in a flooded basin may be taller than the tower itself, or may show a structure that has not yet been built. A person standing near a wall—if a person ever stands there—might cast a shadow with an extra limb, a second head, or no body at all. Perspective loosens in the haze. Near and far trade places without warning. A distant bell can be seen vibrating before it is heard.

The materials are mostly stone, fired clay, dark brick, and a metal that resembles tarnished copper but flakes like bark. Timber appears in beams and scaffolds, though it has no grain. It is grown in molds, perhaps, or assembled from layers. When cut, it releases warm water instead of sap. The water smells faintly of rain on dust.

There is no evidence of ornament except where function has become indistinguishable from ritual. Repeated grooves, nested circles, thresholds too narrow to pass through, walls built in pairs with a person-sized emptiness between them. The simplest objects are often the strangest. A bowl may hold water that reflects the interior of a room located thousands of kilometers away. A length of rope may lead into the stone and tug gently at regular intervals. A small stone weight, placed on the ground, may cause the surrounding paving to rise toward it.

The vastness is not empty in the way a desert is empty. A desert belongs to weather, distance, and the old patience of natural things. This place belongs to intention, even where intention has vanished. Its emptiness is measured. Its silence has been left in designated spaces. The scattered towers and unfinished roads imply a civilization of extraordinary reach and very little presence, a people who could prepare a continent but could not—or would not—fill it.

In the dimness, the built landscape seems to be waiting for instruction.

Then the ground changes again.

A shallow basin begins to deepen, one imperceptible layer at a time. Water gathers there, warm and black. Across the basin, a line of new columns rises from the paving. They are crude, square, and damp, each placed slightly farther from the last. No hands lay them. No machine moves beneath them. They emerge as though the world has remembered, briefly, how construction is done.

Beyond the columns, the low country continues beneath its vaporous sky: wet, hot, unfinished, and almost entirely made. Rain crosses it in silent bands. Far-off structures revise their shapes. Shadows detach and travel. The architecture trembles with the effort of becoming permanent, though permanence has not yet been invented.
