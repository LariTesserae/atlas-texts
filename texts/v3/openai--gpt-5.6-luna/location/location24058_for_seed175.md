The place is a high hollow no larger than a village green, though no village has ever stood there.

It rests on a broad shoulder of dark stone beneath a sky that seems unusually near. The elevation is enough to sharpen the distance: beyond the hollow, ridges rise and fold away in long blue-green layers, their upper slopes softened by cloud. Yet the hollow itself is enclosed, almost private, held between three low shelves of rock and a fourth wall of mist. It has the feeling of a room whose roof has been removed.

Rain is not falling, but everything remembers it.

Water lies just beneath the ground. It gathers in shallow, clear basins between the stones and threads itself through the moss in narrow silver lines. The rock faces weep from seams no wider than a finger. Droplets emerge, pause, and descend with such steadiness that they seem less like falling water than beads being counted. Along the lowest edge of the hollow, a thin marsh has formed: not broad enough to be called a wetland, but deep enough to conceal the darkening earth beneath mats of floating green.

The stone is volcanic, though age has made it soft-looking. It is charcoal, rust-brown, and green-black, with broad planes worn smooth by centuries of water. Its cracks have been enlarged into shelves and pockets. In some places, erosion has carved little circular hollows into the rock, each holding a disk of rainwater. These pools are remarkably still. No insects skate them. No larvae stir beneath their surfaces. A pale film of minerals slowly gathers around their rims, delicate as frost.

Vegetation grows generously, but never thickly. Ferns rise in scattered fans from the damp gravel, each separated from the next by a surprising amount of bare ground. Low plants with waxy leaves spread in islands. Their leaves are broad and translucent, veined with faint amber, and hold beads of water long after the surrounding stone has dried. Slender grasses occupy the higher shelves, their seed heads bending not in wind but in the slow release of moisture from the soil.

There are trees, though only a few. They are short, twisted things with silver trunks and crowns like dark green umbrellas. Their roots are exposed along the banks, gripping the ground in patient loops. Nothing nests in them. Nothing moves among their branches. Their flowers open and close over spans of days, pale cups that turn toward the changing brightness even when the sky remains clouded.

The air is warm for such a height. It carries the damp, mineral scent of rain trapped in stone, along with the green smell of bruised leaves and something faintly sweet from the marsh. Mist forms near the ground in the morning and retreats only a little by midday. It does not rise or drift as ordinary mist should. It gathers in certain hollows, maintaining its boundaries, as if each patch belongs to a particular stone.

Light is normal, though softened by moisture. The sun passes over the hollow without transforming it. Morning is gray-gold. Noon is greenish and clear enough to reveal the ridges beyond. Toward evening, the weeping rock faces catch a dull copper glow. Shadows remain shallow. The place never becomes fully stark, never fully bright.

There is almost no sound.

Water makes the most of it: the thin tick of drops into stone basins, the subdued threading of runoff, the occasional soft collapse of a saturated bank. Plants rub together only when the air shifts. Once in a long while, a reed gives a dry, hollow knock against another reed, though no visible breeze passes through the marsh.

The silence has depth. It does not feel empty of animals; it feels as though animals were never included in the design. There are no wingbeats, no calls, no hidden rustles in the undergrowth. Even the smallest movements belong to water, leaf, stone, and light. The absence is not ominous exactly. It is complete.

At the center of the hollow stands a solitary slab of pale rock, waist-high and narrow, rising from a bed of moss. Its surface is marked by shallow grooves. They might be natural striations, except that they are too evenly spaced, running from top to bottom like the lines of a crude instrument. A person with stone tools could have made them, perhaps, but there are no flakes, hearths, paths, or other signs of habitation. The marks may predate any hand. They may be the residue of water following a pattern no longer visible.

When the sun reaches the slab, the grooves darken with moisture. The stone seems to breathe—not visibly, but through a gradual darkening and lightening, as though water is moving inside it. The change takes several hours.

This is the closest the place comes to activity. Nothing happens quickly. The marsh advances by the width of a finger over a season. A stone basin fills and empties according to weather that may have passed days earlier. Roots lift gravel grain by grain. The trees alter their posture over decades. Every process is slow enough to be mistaken for stillness.

The hollow appears established, settled into its own long equilibrium. Water has worn the rock without destroying it. Plants have claimed the wet places without closing them over. Mist, warmth, and height coexist without struggle. Even erosion seems less like damage than an old form of maintenance.

There may once have been a rule here, though no one remembers who made it: water may remain, plants may spread, stone may change, but nothing with a heartbeat can cross the hollow.

If that rule is true, the landscape obeys it effortlessly.
