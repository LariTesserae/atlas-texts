The land is called, by no one who lives there, the **Low Country of the New Rain**.

It lies nearly at sea level, though the sea is not always visible. Much of the country is a fresh upheaval: black basalt ridges, raw ochre banks, shelves of gray clay and pumice thrust up from the marsh in long, unfinished folds. The ground has the look of something still cooling. It is sharp in places, glassy in others, and nowhere entirely settled. A path that exists at dawn may be gone by dusk beneath a slumped bank, a burst of mud, or the sudden opening of a water-filled crack.

There are no roads, walls, bridges, or towers. Nothing has been straightened. The only lines across the country are made by water, roots, migrations, and fire.

The country is broad enough to have horizons, but the horizons are difficult to see. At a distance, the land dissolves into dense growth and hanging darkness. Forest rises from black mud in layered masses: broad-leafed trees with smooth, pale trunks; thorn trees that grow in tangled crowns; ferns taller than a person; vines as thick as a wrist; flowering creepers that climb, collapse, and climb again. The vegetation is lush because it is opportunistic. It grows wherever a little dampness remains—in cracks between new stones, in the hollows of fallen trunks, on the backs of other plants, in pockets of condensed mist.

Water is scarce in the manner of something that has not yet learned where to stay.

Rain comes hard and briefly, hammering the leaves until the whole forest seems to tremble. Then the clouds pass, and the water races away in silver-brown sheets, leaving only shining beads caught in the foliage and narrow streams hidden beneath mats of grass. The larger channels are unstable, braided and re-braided by every flood. They run through the open flats in several directions at once, dividing around islands of reeds and rejoining wherever the mud allows. Some vanish into porous black ground. Some emerge again hundreds of paces away, bubbling up warm from below.

At night, condensation gathers heavily. It slicks the rocks and darkens the bark. It falls from leaves in a constant, irregular dripping, so that even under a clear sky the forest sounds as if rain is still coming. In the hollows, warm vapor rises from the ground and lies low over the marshes. It does not conceal the landscape so much as rearrange it. A thicket becomes a wall. A watercourse becomes a pale road. Something large moving through the fog appears briefly, then is only a pattern of disturbed reeds.

The darkness is deep.

The sky is often hidden by cloud, smoke-colored mist, or the immense overlapping crowns of the forest. Even when the moon is present, its light reaches the ground only in broken fragments. There are no fires except those started by lightning or by the slow self-heating of buried vegetation. The people who sometimes pass through the country carry small resinous flames in shells or hollowed gourds, but their lights do not conquer the dark. They make small, trembling rooms inside it.

The dark is not empty. It is layered.

Leaves brush against leaves overhead. Insects rasp in the bark and under the stones. Winged things strike the canopy like thrown handfuls of gravel. Frogs call from every depth of the wetlands, each species answering in a different register: hollow knocks, metallic chirps, throaty pulses, shrill whistles that seem to come from the air itself. The sound does not form a chorus so much as a pressure. It fills the ears until silence becomes unimaginable.

At irregular intervals, the entire country erupts.

A flock of black birds bursts from the trees. Hundreds of small bodies rise through the canopy, then separate into ribbons. A herd of six-legged grazers crashes across the flats, their hooves punching into the soft ground. Something in the river gives a deep, booming bellow, and all along the bank animals answer—some from burrows, some from branches, some from beneath the water. The forest shudders with movement. Leaves fall. Mud splashes. The noise swells past hearing and becomes physical, a vibration in the ribs.

Then, as quickly, it subsides into another kind of tumult.

The fauna is overwhelming not because every creature is enormous, though some are. It is overwhelming because everything is occupied. Every surface has a resident or a visitor. Small shelled animals cling to wet stones in clusters. Pale crabs burrow through the soft banks, their tunnels collapsing behind them. Long-bodied amphibians hang from roots and wait with open mouths. The branches are crossed by gliding mammals, bright-feathered reptiles, and soft, many-jointed things that move in loops along the vines. The mud is printed with the marks of feeding mouths, dragging tails, clustered feet.

Large herbivores travel in restless groups through the reed beds. They are low-slung, warm-bodied animals with flexible trunks used to uproot water plants and strip bark. Their young hide beneath the adults when predators come. Predators are common. Some run low and fast on four legs; some descend silently from the trees; some remain motionless in shallow water until an animal steps within reach. They do not maintain territories so much as currents of presence, flowing after prey, flood, scent, and opportunity.

At night, some of the plants open.

Their blossoms are dark red, blue-black, or a faint translucent green, and they release scents powerful enough to make the air seem thick. Small animals swarm over them. Larger animals arrive to feed on the swarm. The flowers close abruptly when touched, trapping insects for a few moments before releasing them dusted in pollen. In the wet season, whole stretches of forest seem to breathe: petals unfolding, fronds uncurling, seed pods splitting with little explosive cracks.

The country is young, and its youth is visible everywhere. The cliffs have not softened. The rivers have not chosen their beds. Thin crusts of mineral skin the mud, breaking beneath a foot to reveal warm sludge below. New islands form overnight in the channels, built from branches, pumice, roots, and the bodies of plants torn loose upstream. They hold together for a few days, perhaps a season, before the current dismantles them.

The earth is warm at depth. In certain places, a person can kneel and press a palm to the ground to feel a faint, steady throbbing—not a heartbeat, exactly, but the slow passage of heat and pressure beneath the crust. Steam vents breathe through mats of moss. Mud pots tremble and release bubbles with the smell of sulfur, wet stone, and decaying leaves. These places are avoided by large animals, but not always by the small ones. Birds gather around the warm pools in the dark, standing on one leg while steam pearls on their feathers.

There is little metal. Those who enter the country use sharpened stone, fire-hardened wood, shell, bone, and braided fiber. Their tools are practical and temporary. A spear may be abandoned after one hunt because retrieving it would mean stepping into a nest of biting things. Knives of black volcanic glass are wrapped in leaves when not in use. Nets are weighted with stones and set in the channels, though the catches are unpredictable: fish, eels, snapping turtles, drowned branches, once in a while something that has no business being in a river that shallow.

No artifact remains long in the Low Country. Rain rots fiber. Roots lift stones. Mud swallows fire-hardened wood. Animals carry away anything edible or interesting. Even footprints are erased quickly, first by dripping leaves, then by insects, then by the patient settling of the ground.

The most reliable landmarks are living ones.

A tree with a split crown leans over a channel that moves every week. A colony of white fungus marks a warm seam in the rock. A particular flock of red-throated birds nests in the same drowned forest each season, though the forest itself is slowly sinking. There is a ridge of black stone shaped like a sleeping animal, and around it the water always runs backward for a few breaths before continuing toward the marsh.

The rules of the place are simple but not gentle.

Water seeks the lowest ground, but the lowest ground changes. Heat rises. Anything still for too long becomes shelter, food, or a place to lay eggs. Noise attracts attention, though no one can say whose attention. A bright color may mean poison, ripeness, warning, or nothing at all. The safest ground is often the newest ground, because the older ground has had more time to accumulate burrows, nests, predators, and hidden water.

Storms cross the region without warning. The air thickens. The leaves turn their pale undersides outward. Animals vanish into holes and branches. Lightning walks along the horizon, illuminating the entire country in blue-white fragments: flooded flats, smoking vents, crowded trees, a sudden wall of rain advancing across the open land.

When the storm arrives, it does not fall so much as strike.

The channels rise in minutes. Trees bend until their crowns touch the mud. Birds and insects are thrown from the canopy. Water pours from every surface, and the country becomes one immense moving skin of blackness and reflected light. New cuts appear in the banks. Old paths dissolve. Plants are torn free and carried into the dark, where they become islands, rafts, and food.

Afterward, the Low Country is altered but not quiet. The animals return almost immediately. Frogs begin calling from the flooded grass. Winged scavengers descend. Small creatures inspect the new debris. Somewhere in the forest, something large begins to feed.

By morning, the land has made room for itself again. The water retreats into its temporary channels. The vegetation lifts its leaves. Steam hangs above the marsh. The black stones shine. And across the open flats, among the reeds and fresh scars of erosion, every living thing resumes its urgent, crowded movement.
