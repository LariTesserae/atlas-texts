The city stands on a high plateau where the mountains have worn themselves almost smooth.

They are still mountains, technically: long, immense backs of stone rising from the cloud basins, their slopes folded into one another like the knuckles of an ancient hand. But erosion has been working here for so long that every sharpness has gone. The ridges are rounded, the gullies broad and shallow, the cliffs reduced to leaning shelves. Rock shows only in occasional exposures beneath the vegetation, and even there it appears softened, furred, and half-digested.

The air is cool enough that breath briefly whitens in the mornings. Moisture arrives irregularly. A storm may pass without rain, leaving only a film of condensation across every surface; then, weeks later, water will fall in a single gray afternoon and collect in the hollows of the plateau. There are tarns between the districts, black and shallow, their margins indistinguishable from the surrounding growth. Water travels underground through roots and fibrous mats, appearing and disappearing at the will of the living landscape.

There is no bare ground.

The entire plateau is covered by plants, though “plants” is an insufficient word for what occupies it. The vegetation has become soil, pavement, wall, roof, insulation, rope, fuel, and sometimes machinery. A continuous green-brown substrate stretches from horizon to horizon: thick mats of low growth, braided stems, waxy leaves, root tissue, moss, and pale fungal threads. In some places it is firm enough to bear a freight engine. In others, a boot sinks through several meters of layered vegetation before meeting anything resembling earth.

Trees grow in scattered congregations, not forests. Their trunks are vast, pale cylinders, each one the width of a building and hollow in places. They rise a hundred meters before branching, their crowns flattened by the high winds into enormous horizontal shelves. Between them, there are long open distances—meadows of dark, close-cropped plants, interrupted by drainage channels and the occasional tower. The trees are sparse, but the living matter is not. It fills every crack. It hangs from the undersides of bridges. It grows inside abandoned vehicles and threads through the joints of old factories.

The city was built into this growth rather than upon it.

Its avenues are elevated causeways made from layered stalks, resin, concrete, and rusting steel. They cross the plateau in straight lines, some still used, some ending suddenly where the living substrate has lifted or folded over them. Buildings stand apart from one another in broad clearings, their lower stories enveloped by vegetation. Industrial halls have been swallowed up to their windows. Apartment blocks emerge from lawns of red bladder-plants and gray tendrils, their upper floors connected by suspended walkways whose cables have been replaced with living vines as thick as train cars.

At a distance, the settlement resembles a sparse city abandoned in a green ocean. Closer, it is clear that the city is not abandoned. It is operating, though not entirely according to human intention.

Old factories still work in the northern quarter. Their smokestacks release no smoke, only clouds of warm spores that drift sideways in the wind. Turbines turn in enormous concrete housings. Conveyor belts carry bundles of cut vegetation from one building to another, though the bundles sometimes twitch, re-root, or climb back onto the belt in the opposite direction. Presses stamp plant fiber into panels, pipes, beams, and sheets of translucent material. The production is slow and irregular. Some machines run for days with nobody near them; others stop whenever a person looks directly at them.

Electric lines cross the open spaces on high pylons. They hum loudly in the cold air. In places, the cables have been braided with vines, and the current travels through both. Blue-white sparks move beneath the leaves like small trapped thunderstorms. At night—when night is allowed to arrive—the lines illuminate entire corridors of vegetation from within, revealing root systems hanging in the air where there should be darkness.

The city’s sound is disproportionate to its emptiness.

Wind roars over the plateau and through the hollow trunks. The industrial district pounds and clanks. Pumps throb beneath the streets. Somewhere, a siren has been sounding for decades, but its pitch changes according to the weather. The broad leaves of the high trees strike one another with the noise of metal sheets. Root masses grind slowly against foundations. When condensation forms, it gathers on millions of leaves and falls all at once, producing a tremendous whisper like distant surf.

There are long periods when no animal is visible. Fauna is scarce here, and what exists is often difficult to distinguish from vegetation. A few black, jointed grazers move through the meadows, their bodies armored in bark and their mouths opening like flowers. Pale birds nest in factory vents but rarely fly; they unfold their wings only to catch passing gusts and let themselves be carried across the plateau. In the drainage canals, something large occasionally displaces the water without surfacing. Its passage leaves a temporary line of green glass behind.

Most motion belongs to the plants.

A field may lie still for hours, then ripple as roots contract beneath it. A stand of reeds can migrate several meters overnight. The walls of a building slowly thicken toward one another, sealing windows and creating new interior chambers. Vines grow toward sources of sound rather than light. Seedpods roll uphill. When rain comes, entire neighborhoods open themselves: shutters unfold as leaves, gutters bloom, and the roofs of warehouses cup downward to drink.

The rules of the place are not entirely stable.

Distances alter subtly in the open. A factory seen across the plateau may take an hour to reach, or a day, or only a few minutes if approached while carrying something made of living wood. Roads occasionally meet themselves. A tower can be visible from every direction and still have no single location. Inside certain buildings, gravity leans toward the nearest tree. Water climbs walls if left unattended. Shadows sometimes belong to plants that have not yet grown.

The most disconcerting changes are quiet. A familiar street may acquire an extra intersection. A bridge may cross a gap that was not there the day before. An industrial plant may contain rooms larger than its exterior, with ceilings lost in green vapor and machinery arranged around a central root descending farther than the plateau should allow. People who enter these places return with leaves in their hair and a precise memory of having stood beneath a second sky.

Yet the landscape does not feel chaotic. Its strangeness has settled into age. Nothing here is newly broken. The impossible has been weathered, incorporated, made structural. Reality has eroded alongside the mountains, wearing down its edges until contradictions fit comfortably together.

At the highest point of the city is a broad square, though the paving has long since become a single continuous sheet of dark plant tissue. It is nearly empty. A few lamps stand around its perimeter, still burning in ordinary daylight. At the center rises a shallow basin filled with condensation, replenished by no visible source. Around it, the city’s towers, trees, smokestacks, and open green distances can all be seen at once.

Every few minutes, the basin trembles.

The vibration travels outward through the square, into the roots, the streets, the factories, and the plateau beyond. Machines answer with a deep mechanical groan. The trees release their leaves. Far across the high country, entire fields bend in the same direction, though there is no wind.

Then everything resumes its separate motion: the engines, the roots, the slow water, the clouds passing below the city, and the ancient work of becoming something else.
