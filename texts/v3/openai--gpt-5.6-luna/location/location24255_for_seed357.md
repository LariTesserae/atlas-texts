The plateau is built, but not by hands.

It rises above the cloud layer in a broad, broken country of terraces and sudden clefts, a high place where heat has nowhere to go. The sun burns with ordinary brightness, yet the air is perpetually saturated. Water hangs in the atmosphere as a silver film. It gathers along every edge, beads on every surface, and falls in sudden warm veils from skies that are otherwise blue. Nothing here is truly dry. The stone—or what resembles stone—sweats.

The entire landscape is made from plant matter.

Not a forest growing over rock, but a world in which rock never formed. The ground is a vast arrangement of root-bone, braided stems, hardened sap, leaf plates, and translucent vascular sheets. It has the geometry of construction: embankments, buttresses, avenues, retaining walls, stairs too wide for any body, platforms suspended over ravines. Yet every structure is alive. The plateau appears to have been designed, then abandoned to its own growth, though no maker is implied by the design. Its walls continue to thicken. Its bridges lean toward one another. Its stairs sprout new steps and swallow old ones.

The material is pale in the high sun—green-white, yellow as old ivory, flushed in places with blue and wine-red. Some surfaces are fibrous and soft enough to dent under rain. Others have been cured into a glassy hardness, like giant leaves mineralized without becoming mineral. When these hard plates split, they do not break cleanly. They open along shining seams, revealing wet layers beneath: cords of sap, folded membranes, chambers full of warm vapor. Water courses through the architecture as if through plumbing, though the channels are veins and the cisterns are swollen bulbs.

There are no animals. No wings rise from the reeds. No insects stipple the water. No tracks cross the mud because there is no mud and no creature to make tracks in it. The plants are not inhabited by a hidden ecology of small lives; they are the ecology. Growth, decay, irrigation, and construction occur as one continuous process.

Across the open plateau, vegetation is sparse in the manner of a monumental garden. Great distances separate the presences. A single tower may stand alone for half a kilometer, its crown lost in the low cloud. A field of knee-high blades may extend to a clean horizon without one shrub or stone interrupting it. Then, all at once, the emptiness will end at a ravine crowded with enormous structures, as though a whole city had erupted there in a single season.

The towers are called nothing, but they resemble organs enlarged beyond any useful scale. Some are hollow stalks, ribbed and slightly translucent, breathing clouds from their upper vents. Some carry flat shelves of leaves, each shelf broad enough to roof a house, though no houses exist. Some rise as spirals, their surfaces planted with hanging gardens that pour water down in continuous curtains. In the hot wind, the towers flex. Their roots draw tight beneath the ground, and the whole plateau answers with a slow, visible heaving.

The high elevation makes the distance enormous. Ranges of constructed vegetation fold away toward the horizon, blue with atmospheric haze. The valleys between them are not deep enough to be valleys in any ancient geological sense. They are fresh cuts, raw and steep, made by recent upheaval and still deciding what shape to keep. Their walls are sharply crenellated, with clean fractures where new growth has forced old masses apart. The erosion is young: every edge is too crisp, every slope too unstable, every channel still bright with the color of its exposed interior.

Rain fills these cuts almost immediately. Water pours from terraces, gathers in hanging pools, and runs through ravines in broad, foaming sheets. Some streams climb briefly before falling, pushed upward through living pressure. Some puddles tremble without wind and rise into the air as ropes, pulled toward unseen openings in the vegetation. A stream may vanish beneath a mat of leaves, then return far away as a warm mist that condenses on the underside of a bridge.

The atmosphere has a physical presence. It presses against the skin. It smells of crushed greenery, hot resin, rain on fresh-cut stems, and something faintly metallic that does not come from metal. In the low places, vapor moves in long horizontal bands. It wraps the bases of the towers and leaves the upper structures blazing in sunlight. The clouds below are so near that the plateau seems to float above a white ocean, but the heat of the day boils columns of cloud up through the ravines. Moisture rises from below and descends from above until the whole region is part of one unsettled circulation.

The light is normal, which makes the strangeness harder to escape. There are no colored suns, no permanent dusk, no supernatural radiance. Shadows fall where they should. The sun warms the platforms and turns water into glare. At midday, the landscape looks almost comprehensible: a vast elevated infrastructure of living green material, built according to principles one might recognize if one were willing to mistake growth for engineering.

Then the changes begin.

A wall bends inward over the course of an hour. A staircase lengthens by several meters, its lower steps dissolving into pale pulp while new steps unfold at the top. A field of tall leaves abruptly lies flat, not from wind but because the whole field has decided to become a roof. The roof may remain for a day, sheltering nothing, before lifting itself into hundreds of upright blades again.

The sound is constant and loud.

Water strikes leaf roofs with the roar of a crowded market. Hollow towers boom when pressure moves through them. Long membranes snap and drum in the heat. Far-off structures crack open with reports like splitting timber magnified across the plateau. Beneath everything is a wet, muscular grinding: roots shifting through the substrate, channels widening, walls settling into new forms. Sometimes a vibration passes through the ground and every vertical plant leans at once, as though the whole region has heard a command.

There is no speech, but there are calls in the broadest sense—repeated tones generated by growth. A column may emit three deep pulses whenever it opens a vent. A basin may hum as it fills. Whole valleys can resonate together, their walls answering one another with hollow notes. The sounds are not communicative in any recognizable way, yet they produce the uneasy impression of coordination. The landscape is never quiet enough to reveal whether it is thinking.

In the sparse zones, the emptiness is severe. Long strips of bare-looking surface extend between growths, but even these are constructed: woven mats of dormant root, sealed with a thin skin that shines under rain. They are not dead ground. Veins move beneath them. Tiny ruptures appear and close. A person crossing such a plain would feel warm pulses underfoot, irregular and widely spaced, as if walking across the back of something sleeping.

The largest open area is a high basin surrounded by tilted green escarpments. At its center stands a single immense structure, perhaps a tree, perhaps a civic monument, perhaps the first and only thing that learned to grow upward. Its trunk is divided into seven white columns, each leaning away from the others before joining again near the summit. Between them hangs a lattice of leaves, terraces, reservoirs, and dripping chambers. Water falls through the interior in multiple stages, striking shelves below with a thunder that can be heard across the basin.

Nothing lives in the basin except the structure and the ground that supports it. Yet the place is crowded with events. Tendrils unroll across the empty floor. Pools appear, boil gently, and sink. New buttresses thrust from the walls. A whole slope may green over in one afternoon, then collapse into steaming ribbons by evening. The basin does not preserve its forms long enough for them to become ruins. It is always in the first violent period of its existence.

At the edges of the plateau, the built vegetation becomes more delicate and more alarming. Bridges extend into open air and stop before reaching anything, their tips flowering into wide fans that catch the wind. Door-like openings form in the sides of towers, but behind them are only chambers of light and wet tissue. Balconies grow from cliffs and then turn their railings inward, closing themselves like leaves. Some paths lead to distant districts; others lead into the ground, where they become root channels and continue without any human scale.

There are no tools, no machines, no abandoned objects from an earlier people. Every function has been absorbed into biology. Walls filter water. Roofs regulate heat. Foundations feed themselves. Channels close when pressure becomes dangerous. The landscape manufactures shelter, drainage, elevation, and ornament in one gesture. Its total construction is not the triumph of civilization over nature; it is a nature that has adopted the behavior of a city without ever becoming civilized.

The plateau is young enough to be dangerous. Its forms have not settled into mutual restraint. Growth collides with growth. Water cuts through fresh walls; walls rise to redirect it; roots undermine the walls and replace them with arches. The whole country is an unfinished arrangement perpetually repairing, multiplying, and undoing itself.

From a distance, under the ordinary sun, it resembles a green highland after a storm.

Up close, it is clear that the storm is not passing.

It is the method by which the place exists.
