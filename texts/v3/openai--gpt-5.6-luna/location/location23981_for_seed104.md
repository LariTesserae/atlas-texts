The water begins above the mountains.

There is no shoreline, no visible surface, no direction that remains reliably “up.” A continental mass rises through a warm, dim sea, its highest ridges suspended so far above the drowned lowlands that they possess their own weather. They are not islands. They are the uppermost folds of an ancient world beneath an ocean several kilometers deep, and their summits form a broken archipelago of stone, root, and green darkness.

The ridges are broad and almost level, worn down by ages of water moving in every direction. Their edges do not fall so much as dissolve. Terraces descend in enormous steps, each one softened by mosses, hanging forests, and curtains of pale vegetation that drift in the currents. Beyond them, the land continues downward into blue-black distances where the old valleys have become trenches and the old plains lie under sediment, root nets, and the slow accumulation of shells.

Warm water circulates everywhere. It rises from deep fissures as cloudy amber plumes and falls again in long, invisible rivers. At the highest elevations, where the pressure is less and the water is saturated with gas, broad bubbles gather beneath overhanging shelves of rock. Some persist for centuries. Others collapse soundlessly, sending a slow concussion through the stone. In these elevated hollows, the water is almost clear; in the basins, it thickens with suspended mineral dust and green spores until distance becomes a gradient of shadow.

The light is dim but not absent. It enters from a surface too distant to see, filtered through miles of ocean and layers of living growth. There is no sunbeam, only a general blue-green radiance, brighter above the ridges and nearly extinguished in the old valleys. At times, the illumination shifts sideways, as if the sky itself were flowing. At times a region remains lit long after the water above it has darkened. Shadows gather under roots and inside cracks, but they do not always agree with the objects casting them.

Vegetation is everywhere, though it is not arranged like a forest in any familiar sense. Dense fronds rise from the rock and flatten beneath the current into vast horizontal lawns. Vine-thickets knot themselves between cliffs, their leaves translucent and full of slow-moving silver veins. Bulbous growths cling to the underside of terraces, sending roots upward into the stone and flowers downward into the dark. Some trees have no trunks: they are columns of woven root, broad at the base and narrowing as they rise, their crowns spread across the underside of the sea like submerged clouds.

The oldest plants have become terrain. A single mat of dark green tissue can cover a valley wider than a city, its surface ridged into hills and channels by centuries of growth. Whole groves lie on their sides, still alive, their branches trailing along the ground and rooting again. Pale reeds grow in enormous curtains from cliffs, each blade as thick as a rope, brushing together in currents too slow to be seen. Their combined movement resembles breathing. It takes hours for one breath to pass through a forest.

There are few animals.

Now and then, something vast and nearly transparent crosses the dimness above a ridge. Its passage is marked only by a temporary clearing in the vegetation and a pressure wave that bends every frond in the same direction. In the deep valleys, small pale things nest in the roots, but they do not call, scatter, or show themselves. Their presence is inferred from the precise disappearance of leaves, from tiny arrangements of shells, from passages that close behind them as though the living plants have swallowed their tracks.

Most of the movement belongs to the landscape itself. Spores drift in slow galaxies. Mineral blooms unfold across the rock like flowers and harden into terraces. Warm currents lift forests from their foundations and set them down again. Entire slopes creep imperceptibly toward the lower basins, held together by roots older than any surviving memory. The change is constant, but so slow that it has the quality of stillness.

The stone is ancient beyond ruin. It has been polished by water until its surfaces are dark, soft-looking, and almost organic. In places it has been dissolved into arches, bowls, and impossible narrow bridges. Erosion has not destroyed the mountains so much as brought them to a final, contemplative form. Every sharp edge has gone. Every fracture has widened into a habitat. The oldest cliffs are honeycombed with chambers where water circulates through warm mineral mud, and where the walls sometimes pulse faintly in response to distant storms.

Scattered across the upper terraces are remnants of building, though it is difficult to say whether they were built for living, worship, storage, or simply left behind by a species that no longer fits anywhere in the world.

A low wall follows the curve of one ridge for several kilometers, its stones fitted without visible mortar and now half-absorbed by roots. A tower leans beneath a shelf of rock, its upper rooms filled with curtains of vegetation. Doorways open onto empty water, some only a few meters high, others large enough for a forest to pass through. In one place, a flight of stairs descends into the open sea and continues farther than the eye can follow. In another, small stone houses stand clustered around a dry-looking square, though everything is submerged and the square is carpeted with living moss.

These structures bear no tools, inscriptions, or metal. Their forms seem less like engineering than a set of agreements with gravity and water. A wall curves away from a current that does not exist. A roof shelters a hollow where the water is perfectly still. A bridge ends halfway across a chasm, yet its continuation can be felt as a change in pressure beneath the skin. The remains are sparse, but each one has become unusually important to its surroundings. Plants grow around them in concentric patterns. Currents divide and rejoin. The ruins function as islands in a place without land.

The water is quiet.

There is no roar, even where entire mountainsides collapse into the abyss. Sound travels poorly through the dense growth and warm sediment. The most common noises are small: the soft ticking of bubbles under leaves, the distant clicking of stone settling, the faint rasp of fronds crossing one another. Occasionally a deep tone passes through the landscape. It is not loud. It is felt in the teeth, in the ribs, in the roots of the plants. When it occurs, every living thing pauses, and for a few moments the currents appear to stop.

Then the world resumes in slightly altered form.

A valley may open where there was only a slope. A ruin may have moved a short distance uphill. A familiar ridge may now be visible from both sides. Currents sometimes flow toward their source. Spores fall upward in narrow columns. Water collects in suspended sheets between two cliffs, holding itself there as a smooth vertical lake before slowly spilling sideways into the dark.

At the highest elevations, the water occasionally thins into a region of wavering vapor. There the vegetation hangs in humid air, roots exposed, leaves beaded with droplets that never fall. A person—or anything shaped like one—could stand briefly on the upper surfaces of stone, surrounded by a warm fog and the dim pressure of the ocean above. But the boundary is unstable. A breath may become a school of tiny lights. A footprint may fill with a view of an entirely different coast. Looking upward, one might see not the surface of the sea but the underside of a forest, its roots descending from the sky.

The landscape has no center. It is too vast for a single climate, though all of it is warm; too old for a clear beginning; too densely alive to be called empty despite the scarcity of creatures. Weather forms inside it as pressure, current, bloom, collapse, and darkness. Storms are not fronts crossing the land but migrations of water through the stone. A rain may begin inside a cavern and fall toward the ceiling. A season may last a thousand years in one basin and pass in a single afternoon on the next ridge.

Everything here is submerged, including time.

The world has been worn nearly smooth, but it has not become passive. It grows, settles, dissolves, and remembers through shape. The last remnants of civilization have been taken into the living fabric. The fauna has diminished to traces. The plants have inherited the scale of geography. Far above, the ocean presses down; far below, the drowned continents sleep beneath forests and sediment.

And through the dim blue water, across terraces older than any surviving name, the quiet currents continue to remake the world.
