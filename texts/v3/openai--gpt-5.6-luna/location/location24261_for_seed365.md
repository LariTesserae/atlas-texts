The settlement occupies a shelf of black stone high above the cloudline, though “above” is not quite accurate. The clouds do not lie below it so much as gather around its edges, threading through the gullies and dripping from the overhangs in slow, silver curtains. The shelf is small enough to cross in a few hundred paces. Beyond it, the land falls away abruptly on three sides into a young landscape of raw escarpments, split rock, and pale gravel still settling from some recent upheaval.

Nothing here has been worn smooth. The stone is sharp and newly broken, its surfaces marked by long glassy planes and seams of green mineral. Rainwater collects in the cuts, but rarely in quantity. There are shallow pools beneath ledges, cups in the rock no wider than a hand, and narrow black channels that run for an hour after a cloud passes before vanishing into cracks. Condensation is more dependable than rain. At dawn, every exposed surface gathers beads of water; by midmorning, the plants have taken most of it.

The vegetation is unexpectedly abundant.

Mosses spread in thick emerald islands across the stone, but there is no continuous carpet. Each island ends suddenly at a dry patch of grit or a bright fracture, leaving open spaces between the living things. Ferns grow from vertical walls, their fronds long and translucent, trembling though there is no wind. Small trees emerge from pockets of soil caught in the rock: crooked, pale-barked things with dark blue leaves clustered only at their tips. Their roots descend openly over the cliff face, gripping the stone like many-jointed fingers.

The largest plants are vine-pillars. They rise from the settlement’s cisterns and abandoned foundations, braided trunks no thicker than a person’s wrist climbing toward the dim sky. Every few days they produce a new loop, a green arch that hardens overnight. The inhabitants cut these arches into beams, ladders, and bridges. If left alone, they continue growing until they bend back toward the ground and root themselves, making a living enclosure. Some of the oldest houses are contained inside such loops, their walls still visible beneath curtains of leaves.

The air is cold, wet, and very clear when the clouds draw back. Light arrives weakly, filtered through a constant upper haze, and seems to gather only on certain surfaces: the wet backs of leaves, the teeth of fresh stone, the pale faces of animals. Shadows are deep but not black. They have a blue-green cast, as if the ground remembers being underwater.

The settlement itself is arranged with careful economy. Low walls of stacked rock mark paths and working yards. Roofs are stretched from woven reed, hide, and the broad waxy leaves of the cliff plants. There are no high towers and no straight roads; straight lines are too easily taken by the weather. Instead, the paths turn repeatedly around cracks, pools, root masses, and places where the rock has begun to move. Wooden stakes support narrow platforms above the dampest ground. Bone hooks hang beneath the eaves. Clay vessels sit everywhere, collecting condensation.

At the center is a reservoir sunk into the stone. It is not large, and its water is dark with leaf-shadow. A ring of polished stones surrounds it, each marked with shallow cuts. No one drinks from the reservoir directly. Water is drawn through braided filters of grass and fungus, then divided into small bowls. The rules of the place begin here.

Water that has stood beneath open sky must not be carried uphill. A pool touched by a white animal belongs to the white animals. No fire may be lit after the second cloud-bell. No one may kill a creature that is looking directly at them. These are not merely customs. They describe the limits of survival, and perhaps the limits of what the world permits.

The people move quietly. Their tools are stone-edged, wooden, fiber-bound, and bone-tipped. There are digging sticks, scraping knives, weighted nets, slings, and small saws made from serrated shell. Metal is absent except for a few dull fragments worn smooth and treasured as charms. Construction is communal and precise: every wall has a drainage gap, every roof a living gutter, every ladder a place where it can be dropped quickly if something climbs after you.

Something is always climbing.

Fauna saturates the margins of the settlement, though not in the usual noisy abundance of a rich valley. The animals are scattered through the open spaces, one here, another far across the shelf, a cluster motionless beneath a ledge. Their numbers become apparent only when the eye adjusts.

A six-legged grazer stands between two houses, narrow as a sapling, feeding on moss with a mouth that opens sideways. It has no visible ears. Along its spine, small green shoots tremble in the condensation. A dozen tiny birds perch on it, but none of them sing. When the animal moves, the birds move with it, hopping from ridge to ridge in perfect silence.

In a dry courtyard, pale rodents with long folded limbs emerge from holes, cross the open ground, and disappear into other holes without disturbing the dust. They travel in straight lines, never looking toward the people. Under the platforms, something broad and soft-bodied waits in the shadows. Only its many reflective eyes are visible, arranged in a crescent along the floor.

The cliffs are inhabited too. Long-winged creatures cling upside down beneath the overhangs, their bodies wrapped in gray membranes like wet cloth. They open and close their wings one at a time to circulate air through the colony. Farther down, where the stone drops into cloud, dark shapes pass silently between the mist. They may be gliding animals, or pieces of the cliff breaking loose, or both.

The most unsettling creatures are the still ones.

They resemble seated figures draped in moss, and from a distance they are often mistaken for old statues. They gather at the edge of paths, beside the reservoir, under the houses. Some have antlers branching from their shoulders. Some have faces that are only smooth, damp planes. They do not move while watched. When everyone inside a house has gone to sleep, they change places. In the morning, the inhabitants count them—not because they expect a particular number, but because an increase means that one of the boundary stones has been crossed.

The animals do not make much sound. The settlement is quiet enough that a person can hear water forming on a leaf. There is the soft clicking of claws on rock, the whisper of fronds rubbing together, the faint patter of condensation falling from roof edges. Occasionally, somewhere beyond the shelf, a deep vibration travels through the stone. It is felt first in the teeth. The people stop whatever they are doing and wait. The vibration may be the mountain settling. It may be a herd moving underground. It may be the landscape remembering a shape it no longer has.

Wind is rare, but when it comes it arrives in sudden vertical breaths. Clouds lift from the gullies and pour over the settlement’s edge. Loose leaves rise, hang motionless, then fall in another direction. The animals respond before the people do. Every creature turns toward the same empty patch of sky.

Weather here is not a backdrop. It is a series of events with consequences. A brief rain can awaken orange threads in the rock that spread across a courtyard overnight. A clear morning can cause a whole wall of moss to detach and slide downhill, exposing hundreds of pale eggs. Frost seals the smaller pools and makes the stone ring like pottery underfoot. When sunlight reaches the shelf for more than an hour, the plants unfurl, the insects emerge, and the quiet becomes crowded with minute movements.

The place is young in every sense. Fresh cliffs crumble at their edges. New channels cut through old paths. Plants colonize the wounds of the earth, and animals follow the plants, and people build where the animals have not yet claimed the ground. Nothing has settled into a final arrangement. Houses are repaired around roots that were not there the previous season. Boundaries shift. A trail can become a nest, a nest can become a pool, and a pool can become a doorway into the stone.

At dusk, the dim light turns the settlement almost colorless. The green plants darken to black. The wet rock reflects a dull violet sky. Animals emerge into the open spaces—not rushing, not hunting in any visible way, but assembling. They stand apart from one another in the yards and along the paths, leaving broad intervals of empty stone between their bodies.

Then, one by one, they lower their heads toward the ground.

Beneath the shelf, something answers.

The sound is too low to hear, yet every vessel trembles on its ledge. The reservoir develops rings. The roots tighten against the walls. For a moment, the entire small settlement seems to breathe: houses, plants, animals, people, and raw stone together.

Then the motion passes, and the high, damp, crowded silence returns.
