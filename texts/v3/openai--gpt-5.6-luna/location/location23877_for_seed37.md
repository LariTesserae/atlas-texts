The place is smaller than a room, though it does not remain the same size when you look away.

It lies beneath a thin skin of stone: a shallow cavity folded into the earth like a pocket in a garment. There is no entrance large enough for a person. Perhaps there was once a crack, or perhaps the hollow simply began inside the rock, assembling itself around a single bead of water. Its walls are raw and unfinished. Pale mineral ridges push outward in interrupted lines, as if the stone is still deciding whether to become cave, shell, root, or bone. Nothing here has been worn smooth by age. The surfaces are sharp, provisional, and faintly warm in places, cool in others.

The light is the first impossibility.

It is bright enough to cast shadows, but there is no source. The entire cavity shines with a clean, diffuse radiance, white at first glance and faintly blue when seen against the darker stone. It seems to come from the spaces between things—from the gaps beneath pebbles, from the air itself, from the unformed seams in the walls. When a small animal crosses the floor, its shadow falls in two directions at once. When water drips, each suspended droplet briefly contains a tiny illuminated landscape: a second chamber, or a sky, or the underside of a leaf that has never grown here.

The floor is a shallow basin of gray grit and translucent fragments. Some pieces are stone. Some are hardened droplets of mineral. Some appear to be bits of eggshell, though no creature in the hollow lays eggs like that. There are small pools scattered between the ridges, never joined, never fully still. Moisture gathers from condensation along the ceiling and falls at irregular intervals. The drops do not always descend. A few hang in the air for several seconds before remembering gravity. Others strike the floor and leap upward as beads of pale vapor, leaving behind the faint smell of rain on dust.

The air is cool and close, but not stale. It circulates through passages too narrow to exist, entering from one wall and departing through another. A breath of it may carry the scent of wet limestone; the next smells of green stems, animal fur, or distant snow. The temperature shifts locally. One patch of ground is cold enough to numb a fingertip. A handspan away, the air has the soft chill of a shaded streambank.

Vegetation is sparse, though the few living things are difficult to classify.

A thread of dark green growth lies across the floor, no thicker than a vein. It has no leaves, but every few minutes it raises a translucent frond, unfolds it, and absorbs the light. After a while the frond sinks back into the stone, leaving no mark. In one corner, pale rootlets emerge from the wall and descend toward a pool, stopping just above the surface. They tremble whenever something moves nearby. There are three small moss-islands on the ceiling, each growing upside down around a bead of water. Their roots point upward into the rock. Their soft surfaces occasionally release a single seed, but the seed falls sideways and disappears into a seam before it can land.

The fauna is abundant despite the emptiness. They are scattered everywhere, but never crowded: a pale six-legged thing under one ledge, a cluster of wingless insects around another pool, a long-bodied animal curled in a crack, dozens of tiny movements at the edge of perception.

Most creatures are nearly transparent. Their organs show as faint strokes of pink, gold, or blue, and their bodies take on the color of whatever lies behind them. Some resemble familiar cave animals, except that they possess too many joints or move according to unfamiliar rules. A beetle-sized creature walks in a straight line until it reaches an obstacle, then continues walking through the obstacle without disturbing it. Small blind moths drift in the bright air, beating their wings silently. They gather not around the brightest places but around the shadows, where they feed on darkness in delicate, invisible bites.

There is a predator here, though it may not be an animal. A narrow black shape slides between the stones, appearing only where no one is looking directly. The smaller creatures respond to it immediately: they freeze, flatten, or vanish. When it passes, a clean lane remains in the grit, but no body or footprint can be found. Once, the shape seems to pause beside a pool. The water rises toward it. Then the whole cavity is briefly reflected in the pool—not as it is, but as a much larger underground wilderness filled with trees, running water, and moving stars.

The sounds are slight. Condensation taps the stone. Tiny claws make dry, precise clicks. Something far within the walls gives a low pulse, spaced so widely that it might be tectonic movement or the heartbeat of the place. The sound has no echo. It seems to enter the ear directly, bypassing the air.

There are traces of construction, but no clear builder. A few flat stones have been arranged in a rough crescent beside the largest pool. Their surfaces bear shallow parallel marks, too regular to be natural and too simple to be writing. One has been set upright against the wall, though nothing supports it. Another has partially dissolved into a root. The arrangement changes between visits—or between glances. Sometimes it resembles a shrine. Sometimes a trap. Sometimes the beginning of a little house.

Nothing made of metal or worked wood is present. No device could function here for long; even a dropped object would become uncertain. A straight edge would soften. A knot would untie itself. A spoken name might attach to the wrong thing.

The laws of the hollow are not broken all at once. They loosen gradually.

Distances depend on attention. The opposite wall is two steps away until you try to reach it, when it becomes a long pale corridor. A pool can be covered by a palm, yet contain a horizon. A creature may be visible from the corner of the eye and absent when faced. The ceiling is solid until a drop falls upward into it, at which point the stone briefly behaves like a surface of water.

Time is similarly local. A patch of moss can grow, flower, and return to bare rock during a single blink. Elsewhere, one drip remains suspended so long that the creatures begin to build their lives around it. Their trails divide around the hanging bead. A small animal sleeps beneath it. Another has laid eggs in its shadow.

The cavity is active, but quietly so. Stone continues to form around empty spaces. Water gathers, withdraws, and gathers again. Roots search through solid mineral. Animals hatch from no visible eggs and disappear into cracks that were not there before. The bright air folds and unfolds. Every process seems to be happening for the first time, and yet some of the marks on the walls suggest the place has been trying to become itself for an immeasurable age.

It is not a cave in the usual sense. It is the beginning of one—or the memory of one, held inside a stone too small to contain it.
