The plain is called the **Open Rainworks**, though there is no regular rain and nothing about it is quiet.

It lies low beneath a broad, warm sky, a basin or perhaps the floor of something once much deeper. The horizon is not mountainous; it is a long, nearly unbroken rim of newly raised land, black and raw, with pale mineral faces shining where they have split. The ground between is open enough that a person can see for many kilometers. Distance is unusually clean here. There are few trees, few buildings, few hills to interrupt it—yet the emptiness is not barren. It is full of motion.

The soil is young.

In places it is a skin of red-brown clay over a hard, dark substance that resembles volcanic glass but bends slightly beneath weight. In others, the land has erupted into ridges of pale stone, all knife edges and fresh fractures. These ridges are not arranged like ordinary geology. They form branching geometries, repeated angles that seem too deliberate to be natural, as if the basin has been growing outward according to a buried diagram. Nothing has had time to soften them. Wind has not rounded the rocks. Water has not made proper valleys. Instead, every storm cuts sudden channels, and every channel becomes a bright scar.

The moisture is intermittent and localized. Most of the plain is dry enough to dust the tongue, but condensation gathers wherever the dark ground cools. At dawn, if dawn can be distinguished from the general dimness, thousands of shallow basins fill with a thin skin of water. These pools vanish by midday, leaving behind blue-white salts and small, translucent organisms that continue moving for hours after the water is gone. Rain falls in narrow vertical columns, sometimes only a few dozen meters across. A traveler may walk beside one for an hour: one side of the body drenched, the other untouched.

The water does not always fall downward.

Some rain hangs in the air below the cloud layer, suspended in long trembling curtains. Droplets collect along invisible planes and gather into horizontal streams. Now and then a whole sheet of water slides across the sky, passing overhead like a river viewed from beneath. It casts a wavering shadow over the plain, and wherever its shadow touches, dormant vegetation unfolds.

The vegetation is lush in the wrong places.

Green growth occupies every damp seam, every shaded wall, every depression where condensation can remain for more than a few minutes. There are mats of velvet-black moss, reeds with glassy stems, vines that root in the air, and low forests of fan-shaped leaves spread across the empty ground like spilled green fire. Some plants grow only while being watched. Others withdraw from a moving shadow, folding down into the soil before the shadow arrives.

The largest plants are called **listening trees**, though they have no trunks in the conventional sense. They rise from widely spaced points across the basin, their roots sunk deep into the new stone, and their crowns spread into thin, translucent sheets. From a distance they resemble fragments of forest floating just above the earth. Their leaves are broad and pale, patterned with dark veins that pulse in response to sound. When the wind moves through them, the trees answer with a deep, resonant chord. When the ground shifts, they produce a brief harmonic warning that can be heard across the entire plain.

There are not many of these trees. The space between them is enormous. Yet each tree supports an abundance of life.

The fauna move through the open intervals rather than clustering in visible herds. Long-legged grazing animals cross the basin in single file, sometimes separated by kilometers. They are tall, jointed creatures with skin like wet parchment, and their heads are covered by branching sensory filaments rather than eyes. When threatened, they do not run. They become momentarily transparent, allowing the landscape behind them to show through, and then reappear several hundred meters away.

Small winged things fill the air. Their bodies are nearly weightless, their wings broad and dark, and they travel in vast lines that look like cracks moving across the sky. They feed on electrical charge gathered from the suspended rain. When millions of them pass overhead, the air turns metallic and the hair rises on the skin.

There are predators, but they are difficult to see. The most common are not animals with bodies but **moving absences**—areas in which sound disappears for several seconds, followed by a sudden return of all surrounding noise at once. The prey animals recognize these zones and scatter before they arrive. Humans learn to fear silence more than any visible shape.

And there is sound everywhere.

The basin is deafening, even when the weather appears calm. The ground emits a low continuous vibration, a subsonic pressure felt in the ribs and teeth. Fresh fractures in the stone snap open with explosive reports. The listening trees sing to one another across the distances. Insects click in swarms. Suspended water shivers against itself. Far overhead, unseen structures move through the cloud and produce a grinding tone like immense gears turning underwater.

Near the settlement, the noise becomes architectural.

The town of **Vey** was built along one of the basin’s few permanent moisture lines. It is not compact. Its districts are scattered across the plain, connected by elevated paths, pressure bridges, and long strands of engineered material that rise a few centimeters whenever someone approaches. Buildings stand far apart, each one surrounded by open ground. There is no defensive wall, no central plaza, no dense urban core. Vey is a town measured by its infrastructure rather than its population.

The houses are narrow, tall structures made from pale composite stone. Their walls are grown rather than assembled, printed molecule by molecule from mineral dust and local biomass. Windows are not transparent surfaces but temporary openings in the material. Doors recognize a person by the pattern of disturbance they make in the air.

Between the buildings, the settlement’s machinery is exposed. Water collectors unfold like metallic flowers whenever humidity rises. Buried pumps draw moisture from the young rock and send it through clear conduits suspended above the streets. Maintenance drones move slowly over every surface, repairing cracks before they become geological events. They resemble small black beetles, though some have become large enough to carry tools, children, or injured animals.

The town is sparse enough to feel abandoned. A person may walk for twenty minutes without seeing another human being. Then, from a roof or a distant bridge, a voice will speak their name. Vey’s systems are always aware of movement. The settlement has no true blind spots. It also has no complete privacy: every footstep, body temperature, and hesitation is archived somewhere in the local infrastructure.

The dimness is not simply a lack of sunlight. The sky is layered with a thin, permanent haze, and much of the incoming light is diverted into the basin’s machinery. The air above Vey glows faintly in bands, as if the atmosphere has been fitted with circuitry. During the brightest hours, the ground remains gray-green, and shadows are soft, deep, and directionless. At night the stars are absent. Instead, high points in the sky open into dark geometric cavities through which unfamiliar constellations can sometimes be seen.

No one agrees on what lies above those openings.

The world obeys recognizable physics, but only locally. Weight varies slightly from one side of the basin to the other. Heat can remain trapped in a shadow after the object casting it has moved. A sound, once loud enough, may persist as a physical phenomenon: voices become columns of pressure, bells leave ringing depressions in the ground, and certain songs can cause dormant channels to fill with water.

The newest stone is the most dangerous. It is still forming. A person who stands in one place too long may find their footprints hardening into permanent hollows. Tools left on the ground are sometimes incorporated into the mineral surface. Once, a section of road grew around a sleeping maintenance machine and carried it away beneath the plain.

The local people travel lightly because the landscape remembers weight.

There are no roads in the old sense. Instead, the town maintains **permission paths**: routes whose surfaces have been instructed to remain stable. They appear as faint lines of blue light in the dust. Outside them, the land is active. Ridges rise overnight. Wetlands close like mouths. Plants spread across a kilometer of ground in a single warm afternoon. New ravines open without warning, though they are rarely deep; the basin is young, and it has not yet learned how to fall gracefully.

At intervals, a weather front crosses the plain.

It arrives without clouds. First the listening trees begin to pulse. Then the small flying animals vanish. The suspended water collapses from the sky in a single tremendous descent, striking the basin with enough force to flatten vegetation and drive sheets of mud across the open ground. For several minutes, the world becomes a white roar. The rain falls everywhere at once, but only briefly. In its wake, temporary rivers rush toward low places that did not exist before the storm.

When the water recedes, the basin is changed.

New islands of vegetation stand in the mud. Fresh stone shines beneath them. Animals emerge from the ground, from the air, from pockets in the rock, from places where there had been no visible shelter. The plain fills with movement: grazing forms, hunting shadows, gliding swarms, crawling machines, and human figures crossing from one isolated structure to another under the dim and ringing sky.

Then the water disappears.

The land opens again, vast and nearly empty, with its sharp new ridges and scattered green life. Sound resumes its pressure against the body. Far away, one of the listening trees sings a warning, and somewhere beneath the basin, something enormous begins to build.
