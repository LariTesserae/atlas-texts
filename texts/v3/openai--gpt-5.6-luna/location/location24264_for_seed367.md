The place is hidden inside the mountain’s shoulder, in a hollow no wider than a village green.

From outside there is almost nothing to distinguish it from the surrounding highland: a long, sun-blasted escarpment, its stone worn smooth by ages of wind. The mountain has not crumbled here so much as finished becoming itself. Every edge is rounded. The cliffs descend in shallow shelves, the color of old bone, ash, and greenish iron. There are no fresh screes, no raw fractures, no evidence of recent violence. The rock has been eroding for so long that erosion has become a kind of polishing.

The hollow begins behind a curtain of vegetation.

The curtain is thick, glossy, and nearly black in the dimness. Its leaves are narrow and overlapping, like strips of lacquered leather. They grow from cracks in the stone and from one another, forming a dense wall through which the wind cannot pass. Their surfaces are dry, but each carries a faint oily sheen. When bruised, they release a sharp smell of resin, hot dust, and something medicinal. Nothing here is wet. There are no pools, rivulets, mud, or beads of dew. Even the deepest shadows contain only powder and mineral grit.

Yet the hollow is lush.

Plants occupy every available surface. Low mats of pale, jointed growth cover the terraces. Tall stalks rise from cavities in the rock, branching into fans of papery fronds. Clusters of red-brown cups cling to the walls, opening and closing to the temperature changes brought by the sun. Their roots are not fine threads but dense black cords, penetrating the stone in search of salts and metal-bearing dust. They draw nourishment from the warm gases that leak through the mountain and from the oily exudates of older plants. Fallen leaves do not rot. They soften, darken, and are absorbed into the root mats until the terraces become layered with a rich, dry felt.

The air is warm despite the altitude.

The hollow lies high above the plains, where the horizon is usually hidden by glare. Cold should rule here, but the mountain breathes from below. Warmth rises through hairline vents in the floor, carrying the smell of sulfur, stone, and ancient organic deposits. In the afternoon the temperature climbs until the walls radiate heat like kiln bricks. At night the plants fold themselves inward and the animal life becomes even more active, sheltering in the retained warmth.

Dim light reaches the settlement only after passing through the hanging vegetation above. The sun appears as a pale disk through layers of leaves, and the sky is reduced to narrow strips of white-blue between the overhanging shelves. Most of the hollow remains in a green-brown twilight. The residents have learned to work by reflected light: thin plates of mica are set into the walls, and pale mineral plaster brightens doorways, workspaces, and the steep stairways connecting the terraces.

The settlement is built into the hollow rather than placed upon it.

There are perhaps a hundred dwellings, though it is difficult to separate homes from storage chambers, workshops, shrines, and animal pens. The buildings are low, close-packed, and made from slabs of the same ancient stone. Walls have been stacked without mortar and sealed with resin. Roofs are woven from tough plant fibers, then coated in a dark, smoke-black substance that keeps the heat from escaping too quickly. Narrow bridges cross between ledges. Ladders descend into shaded cuts. Every path is only a shoulder’s width, and every wall has been shaped by generations of hands until it fits the body.

Nothing is straight for long. The oldest constructions have slumped into the contours of the mountain. A house may begin as a rectangle and end as a rounded chamber, its walls bulging around a root or a vent. Streets follow the natural shelves. Drainage channels exist, but they carry only powder, loose seeds, and the occasional stream of fleeing insects. There is no provision for water because water is not part of the world’s daily necessities.

The people use stone, fired clay, bone, woodlike stalks, and small amounts of soft native metal. Their tools are simple but numerous: wedges, scrapers, awls, spindle weights, cutting flakes, polished digging bars. They maintain the terraces by hand, pruning the vegetation into corridors and harvesting its oils for lamps, sealants, medicines, and food. Their baskets are made from the long fibers of a gray climbing plant. Their clothing is layered against dust and the abrasive mountain wind, with eye coverings for the brightest hours.

They are not alone in the hollow.

The fauna is almost too abundant for the space. Small, many-legged grazers move through the plant mats in continuous streams, their hard shells clicking against the stone. They eat the dry leaves and the pale salt crusts around the vents. Larger animals follow them: squat, warm-bodied browsers with split jaws, blind burrowers that sense vibration through their whiskers, and long-backed predators that slip between houses with the confidence of residents.

Every crevice has a tenant.

Winged creatures nest under eaves and erupt from roof thatch whenever someone approaches. Their wings are thin and leathery, their bodies dusted with yellow mineral powder. They feed on the sap of the tall plants and on the clouds of smaller insects that gather around the vents. Thick-bodied pollinators crawl over the red-brown cups, their legs packed with gray dust. Long, pale hunters hang beneath bridges, motionless until a passing animal disturbs the air. There are beetles in the walls, mites in the woven mats, burrowing things beneath the floors, and small predators that have learned to open storage jars.

The inhabitants tolerate most of them. Some are useful, some edible, some sacred, and some merely unavoidable. The settlement’s architecture is designed around movement rather than exclusion. Doorways have angled thresholds. Sleeping platforms are suspended above the floor. Food is stored in hanging clay vessels. Children learn to recognize danger by sound before they can identify it by sight.

And there is always sound.

The hollow magnifies everything. Thousands of small feet produce a constant dry patter beneath the vegetation. Wings strike leaves in sudden bursts. Shells scrape over stone. The vents hiss and pulse. Plants snap as they expand in the warmth. From the terraces come the blows of stone tools, the rasp of fiber being stripped, the calls of workers, the shrill warnings of animals, and the deep hollow knocking used to communicate from one level to another.

At certain hours the fauna moves as a single restless body. A shadow passes over the upper ledges; birds or winged reptiles burst from the foliage; the grazing animals scatter; predators give chase; people shout and slam shutters; then, moments later, the plants settle and the work resumes. There is no silence afterward, only a rearrangement of noise.

The active life of the hollow follows cycles of heat.

When the mountain warms, the plants open and release their volatile scents. Insects rise in clouds. Grazers emerge from their cracks. Predators patrol the paths. When the heat falls, the settlement closes inward, lamps are lit with thick plant oil, and the animals crowd around the vents. Once or twice each season, pressure shifts deep below the mountain and a warm mineral wind rolls through the hollow. It bends the vegetation flat, lifts dust from every terrace, and drives all living things into shelter.

The storm never brings rain.

It leaves behind only a new layer of powder, a few fallen stones, broken nests, and the mineral scent of the deep earth. By morning the ancient hollow is already repairing itself. Plants straighten. Insects return. People sweep the paths. Predators resume their routes.

Nothing here is young except the individual lives. The mountain is old beyond memory, the terraces nearly finished, the settlement built upon foundations no one remembers making. But within this small, sealed landscape, life is crowded, noisy, and immediate. The world has narrowed to a warm pocket in the high stone: a dim green maze, dry as ash, alive in every crack.
