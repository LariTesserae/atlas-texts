At the highest shoulder of the mountain there is a hollow no larger than a village green, though nothing village-like has ever managed to fill it.

It is a basin cut into the summit rock, a shallow, enclosed place on the edge of a tremendous drop. Three sides rise as dark walls of weathered stone; the fourth breaks open abruptly, and beyond it the world falls away in shelves of cloud. Peaks appear and disappear at eye level, their upper faces pale with old snow. On clear days, the basin seems to hang above the weather. On wet days, the clouds enter it and remain, trailing through the rocks like torn wool.

The rock is gray-green and hard, marked with long mineral bands that shine after rain. It has not been freshly broken in centuries. Its edges are rounded, its cracks widened into permanent seams, its surfaces polished by wind, ice, and the passage of small bodies. Water comes from those seams rather than from any visible spring. It gathers in dark beads, joins itself in thin trembling lines, and runs toward the basin’s lip. The flow is never great, but it is constant enough to keep the stone slick and the hollows black.

There is one pool, scarcely wider than a person lying down. It occupies a depression at the center of the hollow and is fed by a weeping wall. The water is cold enough to numb the fingers immediately. Beneath its clear surface, pale threads of root and tiny silver larvae move among the gravel. When the pool overflows, it does so in a narrow spillway cut into the rock, and the water drops out of sight. Its falling can be heard for a long time afterward, striking unseen ledges far below.

Vegetation has claimed only the places where water and grit collect. Moss cushions the north-facing cracks. Small alpine grasses grow in mats, their blades dark and thick, lying flat against the weather. There are low plants with red-veined leaves, tiny yellow flowers, and pale lichen that resembles frost even in summer. At the back of the basin, where a shelf of stone catches blown soil, there is a dense patch of dwarf shrubs no higher than the knee. Their branches are knotted together by years of wind. In the brief warm season they bear sour blue fruit, and the fruit is always gone by morning.

Most of the basin remains bare. The vegetation does not spread evenly; it stops sharply at the boundaries of water, shelter, and soil. A few yards may hold a miniature wetland, and beyond it there is only stone, open air, and the long view downward. The emptiness is not peaceful. It makes every living thing conspicuous.

Animals arrive in numbers, but they do not occupy the place thickly. They cross it, feed, nest, and vanish into the surrounding heights. Black-winged birds wheel through the hollow in sudden flocks, their wings flashing silver underneath as they turn. They nest in inaccessible holes above the basin and spend much of the day screaming at one another across the wind. Small cliff mammals emerge from cracks at dusk: long-eared, thick-furred creatures that stand upright to listen before darting among the stones. Their droppings enrich the moss beds, and their trails have become narrow dark grooves in the lichen.

There are insects everywhere when the sun reaches the floor—blue-bodied flies hovering above the pool, moths clinging to the lee side of rocks, and swarms of minute winged things rising from the grass in glittering columns. They disappear almost instantly when the cloud closes in. Higher up, large mountain goats pick their way along ledges that seem impossible to hold them. Their hooves knock loose pebbles, and the pebbles descend for several seconds before their impacts begin: a scattered, accelerating clatter from the cliffs below.

The dominant sound is the wind.

It enters from the open side of the basin and is compressed between the walls, becoming a deep, steady rush. At certain angles it whistles through cracks in the rock. At others it produces a booming note that seems to come from beneath the ground. Rain adds a thousand smaller sounds—ticking on stone, pattering in the grass, hissing through the shrubs. The birds cut across everything with harsh cries. Water falls out of sight. Hooves strike ledges. Nothing is truly quiet, though there are occasional moments when the wind pauses and all the separate noises stand exposed: the pool trembling, insects clicking in the moss, a bird beating its wings somewhere beyond the cloud.

The air is cool even under ordinary daylight. Sunlight reaches the basin cleanly when it reaches it at all, bright enough to sharpen every edge, but the warmth is quickly taken by the stone. At night, frost forms along the watercourses and vanishes into vapor when the first light touches it. Weather changes quickly. A clear floor can become a white room in minutes. The clouds do not merely pass over the basin; they eddy within it, revealing and concealing the same few yards of ground until the place seems to shift around itself.

There are signs that people have used the hollow, though no permanent settlement could survive here. A waist-high wall of stones follows the safest side of the basin, not enough to defend anything, only enough to break the wind. Near it stands a shelter made from overlapping slabs, roofed with hide and weighted with bones. The hide has gone stiff and gray. Inside are a fire-blackened hollow, a chipped stone scraper, and several lengths of cord made from twisted plant fiber. Someone has tied feathers to the cord and hung them above the pool. They tremble constantly, even when the air feels still.

At the basin’s highest point is a cairn, carefully maintained. Its stones are flat, green-veined, and unlike the local rock. They may have been carried from a lower valley, one at a time, over many generations. Nothing is carved into them. The only mark of intention is their arrangement: a narrow column leaning slightly toward the open drop.

The place has a simple rule, understood by the animals and by anyone who has stayed there long enough: nothing remains where it is placed unless it is sheltered from the wind. Loose objects travel outward. Feathers, seeds, scraps of hide, ash, even the dead insects caught in the grass are lifted and carried toward the precipice. The cairn persists because its stones are too heavy. The shrubs persist because their roots are braided through the cracks. The people’s shelter persists only because it is continually repaired.

Everything else is in motion—water beneath stone, cloud through the basin, birds across the void, goats along the ledges, and weather wearing down a landscape that has already reached its settled form. It is not a young or ruined place. It is an old, narrow place balanced at the limit of what can live there, loud with the effort of continuing.
