The high country begins where the clouds lose interest in the valleys below.

It is not a range of peaks so much as an immense, lifted country: a continent-sized table of dark stone, folded and tilted in long, shallow ridges. The ridges rise through mist and disappear into it, then return miles away, green-black and shining. Nothing here has the sharpness of fresh upheaval. The cliffs are old, rounded by weather, their faces furred with moss and pale mineral growth. Water comes out of them everywhere—not in dramatic falls, but in innumerable dark seams, threads, curtains, and damp breaths. The whole plateau seems to be slowly weeping.

The rock is mostly basalt, slate, and a heavy iron-colored stone that stains every pool orange at its edges. Underfoot, the ground is resilient rather than solid. There are mats of sedge, sponge moss, low ferns, and root-woven grasses so dense that the soil cannot be seen except where something has passed through it. In hollows, water lies just beneath the surface. A bootprint fills from below. A hand pressed into the moss comes away cold and dripping.

The vegetation is not tall in the ordinary sense. The cold and elevation keep the trees low and wind-shaped, but they are everywhere. Groves of black-barked conifers huddle in the lee of ridges, their branches hung with green ropes of lichen. Broad-leafed plants grow in layered shelves along the ravines, each leaf thick, waxy, and beaded with condensation. There are flowering plants, though their colors are subdued: blue-gray bells, white stars, bruised purple cups that open only during the briefest thinning of the cloud. Their roots interlock beneath the surface in a continuous living fabric.

At a distance, the plateau appears forested. Up close, it is packed almost beyond passage. There is no clean margin between bog and woodland, stone and meadow, stream and root-bank. Every space is occupied by something: a fern unfurling through another fern, beetles under bark, pale worms in the waterlogged soil, small mammals moving through tunnels beneath the moss. Even the air seems full. Spores drift in it. Midges hover over the pools in slow, compact clouds. Condensation gathers on the larger leaves and falls in regular drops, making a rain that never quite becomes rain.

The atmosphere is cool enough that breath is visible in the early hours, though there is no true morning boundary here. The light remains broadly normal—gray daylight beneath the cloud cover, silver when the cloud thins, a brief and startling gold when the sun reaches the plateau—but the sun rarely seems to touch the ground. Clouds form low in the valleys and climb the slopes in long, deliberate masses. They collect around the ridges, divide, and pass through the vegetation like another kind of tide.

Weather has its own local habits. A clear patch over one basin may persist for days while rain falls on the next basin over. Mist gathers in the depressions and drains downhill at walking speed. On certain afternoons, the entire landscape begins to sweat. Rocks shine. Tree trunks darken. Water beads on the backs of animals and runs in narrow channels through the grasses. Then, without thunder or wind, the moisture withdraws into the soil, leaving the upper surfaces dry while the roots below continue to drink.

There are streams, but they do not dominate the place. They are too numerous to become singular. Water emerges from the ground in sheets and disappears again under mats of vegetation, reappearing lower down as black, fast-moving channels. Some streams run warm where they cross buried seams of mineral heat. Around them grow stands of enormous reedlike plants, their hollow stems resonant with low vibrations. When the wind moves through them, they do not rustle so much as hum.

This is where the industrial character of the landscape becomes apparent.

There are no factories, roads, towers, fences, foundations, or recognizable artifacts. No thing has been built. Yet the plateau operates at an industrial scale. Beneath the living surface, pressure and water move through the stone in organized networks. Mineral-rich fluids are forced upward through vents and fissures, cooled, filtered, and drawn down again through the root systems. The mosses trap metal particles. Fungi break them down. Certain dark reeds concentrate them in their tissues, and when those reeds die, their deposits form thin metallic layers in the peat. Whole valleys are slowly processing the mountain.

In places, the ground gives off a deep mechanical throb—not loud enough to be called sound at first, more a pressure in the ribs. It comes from below. A buried chamber fills with water; a sealed pocket of heat expands; a plug of stone shifts by the width of a finger. The event travels through the plateau as a sequence of distant knocks. Pools tremble. Birds lift their heads. Nothing breaks. The system has been doing this for longer than the present forms of life have existed, and its movements are measured in centuries.

Some plants seem adapted to this hidden circulation. Their root clusters form dense radial arrays around warm seams, as though planted according to an invisible engineering plan. Tall, jointed stalks lean toward one another over ravines and pass water from crown to crown. In the deepest wetlands, pale bulbous growths pulse faintly under translucent skin, taking in the mineral fluids and releasing them in measured trickles. Their function is unclear, but the surrounding ecology depends on them. Where they grow, the water is clean enough for drinking. Where they fail, the bog becomes metallic and empty.

The fauna is abundant but restrained. Herds of six-legged grazers move through the high meadows, their bodies compact and furred against the cold. They feed on the tough upper grasses, leaving the deeper plants untouched. Their hooves are broad and flexible, spreading over the moss rather than breaking it. Smaller animals follow them: long-bodied scavengers with shining black faces, red-jointed burrowers, and flocks of quiet, winged things that pick seeds from their coats.

Predators exist, but the density of the vegetation keeps them close. A hunter may be only a few yards away and remain invisible behind a curtain of fern. There are cats or catlike animals here, though they are less often seen than inferred: a depression in the moss, a sudden silence among the small birds, the pale remains of a grazer arranged beneath a low tree. The largest predator is a slow-moving, bear-sized amphibious animal that lives along the warm streams. It has no roar. When disturbed, it releases a low resonant note that travels through the water and can be felt in the ankles.

Sound does not carry far. Moss, mist, wet soil, and dense foliage absorb it almost immediately. The plateau is not silent, but its sounds are close and soft: droplets striking leaves; the tiny tearing of roots; the wet click of insects under bark; distant water moving through stone. Herd animals make brief throat-notes to one another. Birds call only when they must, and their calls seem to come from everywhere at once.

At times, one hears something that resembles machinery. A rhythmic clacking from beneath a ridge. A long metallic groan from a valley whose walls are entirely natural. A pulse in the reeds that repeats every several minutes. These sounds never resolve into a visible source. They are the audible edges of processes too large and too old to have an operator.

The plateau has no wilderness in the sense of emptiness. It is full to the margins, layered and continuously active. Rain enters the moss, the moss enters the soil, the soil enters the roots, the roots enter the grazing animals, the animals enter the scavengers, and everything eventually returns to the mineral water. Erosion has not ceased; it has become patient. Ridges are lowered grain by grain, but their sediment is caught almost immediately by vegetation. Channels change course without abandoning their valleys. Cliffs soften and become slopes, slopes become bogs, bogs become forest, and the forest climbs back over the stone.

The rules of the place are not hostile, but they are strict.

Nothing stays dry for long. Nothing remains exposed. Open ground is claimed within a season. A fallen tree becomes a bridge for roots, then a garden for fungi, then part of the soil. Fire rarely spreads; the wetness closes around it. Metal rusts quickly, though some plants preserve it in their tissues. Noise is muffled. Distance is deceptive in the mist. A ridge that looks nearby may take a day to reach, while a valley can seem to approach on its own as the cloud shifts.

And always, below the green surface, the old pressure continues: water descending, heat rising, stone settling, minerals being separated and recombined. The landscape does not merely contain an ecosystem. It behaves like one vast, quiet machine—unbuilt, self-repairing, and so densely alive that there is nowhere left for anything to stand apart.
