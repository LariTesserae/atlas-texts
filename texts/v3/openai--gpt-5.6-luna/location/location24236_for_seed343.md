The high country begins where the last trees end, but here the trees do not end. They become the country.

A vast plateau rises above the weather in long, exhausted shelves of stone. It is not a sharp mountain range, nor a place of peaks and ravines in their violent youth. Time has worn the land almost smooth. The plateau’s edges descend in immense steps, each one the remnant of a former height, their faces grooved and rounded by ages of rain, frost, root-pressure, and wind. From a distance they look like the ribs of some buried creature, or the tiers of an abandoned amphitheater extending beyond sight.

The elevation is extreme, but the forms are gentle at the top. There are broad uplands where one can walk for hours across nearly level ground, though the ground is never truly bare. It is covered by a continuous skin of vegetation: low, dark mats, leathery fronds, pale crawling vines, and cushions of stalks so tightly interwoven that they make a resilient living floor. In places, the skin opens onto shallow basins of stone. These hold rain for a few days after a storm, or gather water from the heavy night condensation. The pools are rare and precious, but nothing around them is quiet.

Most of the vegetation is not green. At this height, beneath the clear, ordinary brilliance of the sky, it ranges from black-violet to copper, gray-blue, rust, and the yellow of old bone. Some plants grow as thin blades, each one taller than a person, trembling in the wind. Others lie flat and overlap like scales. There are broad, waxy leaves that collect condensed water along their veins and funnel it inward, and hollow reeds that draw moisture from deep cracks in the plateau. Their roots have entered the stone so completely that the stone itself seems fibrous. Whole escarpments are held together by root systems older than any remembered people.

The oldest plants are not trees, exactly. They are towers, groves, and hanging walls: immense colonies that have grown by repeatedly dying and being replaced. A single “trunk” may be a cliff of layered stalks, each generation fused into the next by mineral salts. Some rise hundreds of feet from the plateau, their upper branches spread into flat crowns that catch cloud and light. Others have collapsed outward and rooted again, making arches, bridges, and tunnels of living wood. Their interiors are hollow and cool. Water drips there in slow, irregular rhythms. The walls are lined with moss, pale fungi, and small rootlets that recoil when touched.

The open spaces between these structures are surprisingly large. The highland is crowded with life but empty of objects. A person may stand in a plain several miles across and see only one tower of vegetation, one distant herd, and perhaps a dark ruin near the horizon. The space between them is immense. Nothing interrupts the view except low growth rippling under the wind and the broad shadows of passing animals.

The air is warm, though never soft. It carries the dryness of altitude, the mineral smell of old rock, and a wet green odor released whenever clouds pass over. Rain comes intermittently, sometimes after weeks of brightness. A storm may arrive as a gray wall moving across the shelves, drench one side of the plateau, and leave the other untouched. When rain falls, the plant-mat drinks visibly. Tendrils rise. Leaves unfold. Water runs down channels that had seemed to be solid vegetation and vanishes into root-thickets beneath the surface.

At night, condensation is more reliable than rain. The cold upper air lays a shining film over everything. By dawn, the plateau appears lacquered. Droplets gather on the tips of grasses and fall in bright chains when the first large animals move through. Some creatures spend their mornings licking the plants. Others scrape dew from stone with tongues like rough brushes. The few pools become surrounded by tracks, nests, feeding scars, and the churned impressions of bodies—but never for long. The living floor closes over damage with astonishing speed.

The fauna is overwhelming, yet not dense in the ordinary sense. Animals arrive in waves and occupy the open country as weather occupies the sky. Herds of long-legged grazers cross the plateau in lines that may take an hour to pass. Their bodies are narrow and high-shouldered, adapted to the steep shelves; many have broad, gripping feet and small backward-facing horns used to anchor themselves in wind. They feed on the upper vegetation, stripping entire sections of the living mat, but the plants grow beneath them even as they eat.

Above the herds wheel immense scavenging birds, their wings almost motionless in the rising air. Their calls are not cries but deep, resonant pulses that seem to come from the stone itself. Smaller creatures move everywhere: bright insects bursting from seed pods, burrowing animals pushing through the root-floor, things with many jointed limbs clinging to the undersides of leaves. When startled, they erupt in coordinated swarms, turning the air briefly opaque.

Predators are less often seen than heard. A long, low animal may pass behind a fold in the terrain, leaving only a wake in the vegetation. Pale cats—or creatures resembling cats—sleep beneath the hanging roots, their bodies patterned to imitate the layered stone. There are also gliding hunters that launch from the plant towers and descend without a sound until the moment of impact. The prey animals respond to threats with a deafening collective movement: hooves striking the shelves, wings beating, stalks breaking, alarm calls multiplying across the plateau until all distinction is lost.

Sound is the great presence of the high country. The wind moves through the towers and produces a continuous, organ-like roar. Hollow stems knock together. Distant waterfalls, fed by a recent storm, pound down the ancient steps. Herds call across miles of open ground. Insects rasp in the vegetation. Birds answer from the cliffs. Even when no large creature is visible, the landscape is loud with growth, feeding, flight, and the grinding of roots through stone.

Human traces are sparse. Here and there, a low shelter has been built beneath a living arch, using flat stones and flexible branches. The walls are crude, darkened by smoke, and partly swallowed by new growth. Narrow paths lead between the water basins, marked by stacked stones or strips of peeled bark. At the edge of one shelf stands a watch platform made from lashed poles, long abandoned but still maintained by the plants that have grown around it. A broken hand tool lies in the root-mat, its simple stone head polished smooth by weather.

The old inhabitants did not conquer this place. They learned its intervals: the weeks between rains, the hours when the herds crossed, the brief flowering of certain towers, the seasons when the cliff-nesting animals became dangerous. Their marks are small because the plateau is immense and because every permanent object eventually becomes part of the vegetation. A wall becomes a bank. A road becomes a root channel. A burial mound becomes a flowering hill.

The world here obeys a quiet rule: nothing remains separate for long. Stone becomes soil. Soil becomes root. Root becomes architecture. Architecture becomes shelter for animals, and animals return their bodies to the plants. The erosion is ancient not because the land is dead, but because it has reached a settled agreement with time. It does not resist change. It incorporates it.

Far beyond the nearest shelf, the plateau continues toward blue distances, broken by more living towers and more worn steps descending into haze. Clouds gather below the rim like a white inland sea. Their tops glow in the normal daylight, while the high country remains exposed above them—warm, wind-struck, green with old life, and roaring from horizon to horizon.
