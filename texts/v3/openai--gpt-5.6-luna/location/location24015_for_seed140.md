The landscape occupies a high basin in the oldest part of a mountain range, though “basin” gives it too much shape and “mountain” too much height. The stone has been weathering for so long that its original forms have nearly disappeared. Ridges have become broad-backed shelves. Cliffs have softened into walls. The whole place seems to have been worn down toward its final arrangement, not by violence but by an immense patience.

It is not daylight here.

The basin lies beneath a permanent roof of cloud, rock, or both. No one can tell where the atmosphere ends and the mountain begins. Darkness fills the space evenly, without the directionality of night. There are no shadows because there is no source from which they could fall. The eye can adjust forever and find only the dark, the way a hand can reach through deep water without finding its surface.

The warmth is more easily felt than understood. It rises from the ground in broad, gentle breaths. The stone is warm underfoot, and the air carries the mineral softness of a bathhouse, though the basin is open and vast enough to have weather. Water condenses directly out of the darkness. It gathers on every surface: on the undersides of leaves, in the pores of rock, along the backs of animals, beneath the edges of the scattered structures. It does not rain so much as arrive.

The terrain is small enough to cross in a day. A ring of low, ancient terraces encloses a central hollow no larger than a village common. Above it, the enclosing slopes rise only a few hundred meters, but the lack of visible sky makes them feel like the interior of a world. Their upper rims are lost in the ceiling. Every path eventually returns to the same hollow, sometimes by a route no one remembers taking.

The floor is a dark, porous stone, almost black but not reflective. Water moves through it in silence until it emerges elsewhere, sometimes minutes later, sometimes after years. Shallow channels wander across the ground and vanish beneath mats of vegetation. In the wettest places, the stone has dissolved into pale shelves and rounded cavities. Pools stand in these hollows, warm and perfectly still. If disturbed, they take a long time to settle. A thrown pebble may continue making rings long after the person who threw it has gone.

Vegetation is dense where it chooses to be and absent everywhere else.

There are curtains of broad-leafed plants hanging from the terraces, their leaves thick enough to hold cups of water. Long root systems descend through the rock and disappear into cracks that are too narrow for any hand. Ferns and reedlike growths fill the channels. Low trees stand widely spaced across the basin, each one with a trunk like a weathered pillar and a crown lost in darkness. Their branches do not form a canopy so much as a series of isolated rooms. Between them are great expanses of bare, damp stone, startlingly empty.

The emptiness is one of the place’s defining qualities. Life is abundant, but it is not crowded. A single plant may occupy a whole shelf. A herd of pale browsing animals may be dispersed across an entire slope, each individual separated from the next by a hundred meters of darkness. Their presence is known through sound: the wet tearing of leaves, the slow click of jointed feet, the occasional deep exhalation that seems to come from the mountain itself.

The fauna has adapted to a world without sight, though not in any spectacular or monstrous way. Most creatures are low, soft-footed, and warm-bodied. They navigate by vibration, scent, and pressure changes. Small burrowing animals move beneath the stone in patient networks, raising faint tremors that can be felt through a palm pressed to the ground. Long-winged insects drift through the air in enormous, sparse populations, following heat rather than light. They gather around warm vents but never swarm; each one maintains a wide, invisible distance from the others.

There are amphibious things in the pools, nearly silent when still. They surface only to breathe and then sink again, leaving behind a widening disturbance. Along the wet terraces, animals with many delicate legs feed on mineral films and fallen leaves. They are plentiful, but because they are small and widely spaced, the basin never feels infested. Nothing rushes. Predation occurs as a slow certainty: a stalker waits beside a channel for hours, or days, until something enters the exact radius of its patience.

The loudness belongs mostly to water and stone.

A waterfall descends somewhere beyond the western terraces. It cannot be seen, but its impact fills the basin with a continuous booming, not sharp enough to be thunder and not deep enough to be silence. Smaller streams slap and hiss through the channels. Warm seepage bubbles under mats of moss. Occasionally, a slab of ancient rock gives way and slides down a slope with a prolonged grinding roar. The event may take several minutes. Afterward, the basin resumes its previous sounds as though nothing important has happened.

Animal calls travel strangely. A low note can cross the entire hollow and return from several directions, delayed by the curved walls. Some creatures communicate through repeated impacts: three knocks in the stone, a pause, then one. The patterns are simple, but they persist for generations. In the darkness, a distant exchange of calls can resemble machinery operating behind a wall.

Scattered through the basin are remnants of construction.

They are not ruins in the ordinary sense. Their surfaces have not crumbled so much as merged with the geology. A narrow bridge crosses a wet depression, made from a substance that has become indistinguishable from the surrounding stone but remains warm to the touch. It supports no visible weight, yet it has not sagged in the thousands of years since it was placed. A tall, hollow structure stands on the upper terrace, its interior open to the air. When water passes through it, the sound changes: the whole basin seems to inhale.

There are also small doors set into the rock, most of them sealed. Their edges are too exact to be natural, but moss grows across them in uninterrupted sheets. A few remain open. Beyond them are rooms no larger than sheds, containing objects that still function without any apparent source of power. One produces a faint pressure pulse at regular intervals. Another slowly rearranges grains of sand into geometric patterns, then erases them and begins again. A third has no visible purpose but keeps the surrounding stone from collapsing.

The technology here is advanced enough to have become geological.

It does not glow. It does not hum unless touched. It has no screens, no readable indicators, no obvious moving parts. Its operations are expressed as changes in pressure, temperature, erosion, and the behavior of water. The old builders seem to have engineered processes rather than objects. A slope has been tuned so that it will remain stable for another million years. A pool is kept at precisely the temperature needed by three unrelated species. A hollow in the mountain continuously removes a particular mineral from the groundwater, though no pipe or filter can be found.

The most remarkable device lies beneath the central hollow. At intervals so long that no living creature has witnessed the beginning or end of one, it shifts the basin’s weight. The movement is almost imperceptible: a deep settling, a slow redistribution of pressure. Water changes course. A terrace rises by the thickness of a leaf. A buried chamber opens somewhere far below. The landscape is not static, but its changes happen on the scale of geology, making every ordinary life within it seem brief and temporary.

There are rules to the darkness.

Fire burns, but its flame gives no light. Sound becomes warmer and more directional around it, and animals avoid the area. Polished surfaces do not reflect. Colors exist only as differences in scent, texture, and temperature. A person who spends too long here begins to describe surfaces by how they feel at a distance: the roughness of a wall before touching it, the wetness of a leaf without seeing its sheen.

Water always finds an old path. If a channel is blocked, the flow does not simply divert; it waits, sometimes for years, until the obstruction softens or the ground remembers another route. Plants grow toward pressure gradients rather than toward any source of brightness. Animals rarely cross the exact center of the hollow, where the stone is dry and acoustically dead. Nothing is known to live there. Even falling water seems to lose its voice above that patch.

Time is present in the landscape as accumulation rather than motion. Roots thicken. Rock rounds. Doors disappear beneath growth. A herd changes its route by a few steps over the course of a century. The basin’s warmth remains nearly constant, though the creatures born beneath it and the structures that shaped it pass away.

Nothing here appears newly made. Nothing appears to be waiting to begin.

Yet the place is not dead. Its abundance is quiet, distributed through darkness and distance: a breathing animal on a remote shelf, a forest of roots beneath the floor, water moving through stone, machines preserving conditions no living mind remembers asking for. The basin has settled into an ancient equilibrium, but equilibrium does not mean stillness. It means that every change is taken up by something else.

In the deepest part of the hollow, where the sound of the waterfall becomes a constant pressure in the chest, a single tree grows from a split in the rock. Its trunk is hollow, and water passes through it. The tree has no visible crown. Its roots descend farther than the basin is high. From inside the trunk comes a slow sequence of knocks, spaced several minutes apart.

Sometimes, from far below, something knocks back.
