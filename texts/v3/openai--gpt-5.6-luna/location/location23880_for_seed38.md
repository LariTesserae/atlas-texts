The high country begins where the ordinary forests end, though there is no clean boundary between them. The trees simply become larger, older, and more structural until the forest is no longer something that grows upon the land. It is the land.

A warm mist lies over the plateau almost every morning. It gathers in the hollows, threads between trunks, and beads along the undersides of enormous leaves. The air is never dry. Water lies just beneath the surface everywhere: in black pools under root-bridges, in slow channels hidden beneath mats of moss, in the soft mineral soil where a footstep releases a cold seep. Rain comes often, but gently, as if the sky has forgotten how to fall. Much of it is caught before reaching the ground. It collects in leaf-cups, hanging gardens, folded bark, and the translucent bladders of climbing plants, then descends by degrees in silver threads.

The plateau is high enough to have its own weather. Below its rim, cloud seas press against the escarpments and sometimes spill over them in long white cataracts. Above, the sky is usually a pale, muted blue, veiled by high vapor and the vast breathing of the vegetation. The light arrives dimly, filtered through layers of foliage and suspended moisture. Even at midday, distances are green-gray and uncertain. The sun can be felt more often than seen: a broad warmth in the wet air, a quiet brightening behind the leaves.

The land has been wearing itself down for so long that sharpness has nearly vanished. Ridges are immense, rounded backs. Cliffs have become sloping walls of stone, their original faces hidden beneath centuries of curtains, roots, and dripping growth. Ancient channels have been polished into shallow troughs. Mountains do not stand here so much as rest, flattened and patient beneath the living cover.

Stone appears only where the plants permit it. It is dark, damp, and fine-grained, often banded with rusty mineral stains. In exposed places it has a skin of pale lichen that resembles old ash. Elsewhere, entire shelves of rock have been swallowed by roots. The roots do not merely grip the stone; they enter its cracks, thicken inside them, and gradually replace the mountain’s interior with fibrous wood. A fallen trunk can remain standing for centuries because a dozen other organisms continue to inhabit and support it. A bridge of roots may be broader than a village and so old that its wood has become indistinguishable from stone.

There are few open spaces. The largest are not clearings but broad, low basins where the vegetation has settled into a different form: reed plains, fern marshes, fields of leaf-sized floating plants, or expanses of soft green plates overlapping like scales. These plains can extend beyond the visible horizon. Their surfaces move almost imperceptibly in the wind, a slow collective breathing. From a distance, they seem to be grassland. Up close, they are layers upon layers of life, the lower leaves dark with water, the upper ones warm and waxy, each supporting smaller stems, tendrils, flowers, and pools.

The dominant trees are not vertical in the usual sense. They rise in clustered columns, then spread into immense horizontal shelves. Their crowns meet far overhead, forming a continuous second terrain. Some trunks are hollow and broad enough to contain ponds. Others have split into arches, galleries, and shaded corridors, creating a forest architecture without any builder. A tree may support a hanging meadow, a series of pools, and a colony of pale flowering vines before its first branch is reached.

The vegetation is nearly total, but not uniform. There are zones of warm, red-veined leaves around mineral seepages; zones of blue-black fronds where water moves beneath the soil; zones of pale, almost colorless growth in the deepest shade. Flowers are sparse but enormous, opening over months rather than days. Their petals hold rainwater in shallow bowls. When they close, the water remains inside them, and small animals live there until the next flowering.

In places, plants have taken on the qualities of weather. A slope may be covered in tall, flexible stalks that lean toward one another and maintain a permanent fog beneath their crowns. A wall of climbing leaves may release a warm exhalation at dusk. Vast mats of moss slowly contract when the air cools, pushing hidden water toward lower ground. The movement is too slow to watch directly, but after a season a path will have narrowed, a pool will have shifted, a whole bank will have folded inward.

The ecology is crowded without being frantic. Small lives occupy nearly every surface: shelled things moving through the wet litter, long-bodied grazers suspended beneath leaves, insects with soft wings that never seem to beat, and minute pale animals that cluster around the warm places in the roots. They are abundant, but their abundance is subdued. There are no great swarms, no constant alarm. Predation and feeding happen in silence or near silence, under leaves, within flowers, between layers of moss.

Larger animals travel through the vegetation as though through deep water. Their backs appear and disappear among the green. Some are long-legged and delicate, stepping from root to root without disturbing the pools. Others are low and broad, covered in a coat of living moss that flowers during the wet season. There are slow browsers whose mouths are hidden among facial folds, and tall, many-jointed creatures that remain motionless for hours while birds and epiphytes settle on them. Their calls are infrequent: a hollow note from far across the basin, a low pulse felt in the ribs more than heard, a series of soft clicks beneath the canopy.

The quiet is not emptiness. It is saturation. Water ticks from leaf to leaf. Fibers creak as trees bear their immense weight. Hidden channels murmur under the ground. Occasionally a whole stand of tall growth leans together with a sound like distant surf. Most other sounds are softened by the vegetation before they travel. Even thunder arrives as a delayed pressure, a broad trembling under the feet.

There are traces of construction, though it is unclear whether they were made by people or by a species that regarded growth as a form of building. A few narrow causeways cross the marshes, formed from interwoven roots that have thickened into smooth, pale ridges. They lead nowhere obvious. Small chambers open in the sides of old trees, their entrances rimmed with flat stones. There are terraces on some slopes, but the terraces have been absorbed by vines and now hold pools, fern beds, and colonies of insects. A freestanding wall may appear in the forest, made from stones fitted without mortar. Its upper courses have fallen away, while its lower courses are embedded in living wood.

Nothing built is maintained in the ordinary sense. The structures are not ruins so much as agreements between old works and newer growth. A stair descends into a ravine and becomes a root system halfway down. A roof survives as a sheet of broad leaves grown into the shape of shingles. A doorway opens into a chamber that is warm, wet, and entirely occupied by flowering plants. Whatever civilization once marked this plateau has been reduced to patterns the ecosystem continues to repeat.

Time behaves with unusual patience here. The forest advances, but not as a visible invasion. It thickens by the width of a hand over a lifetime. Pools migrate through the roots. Whole hills settle by fractions. A tree dies standing and remains part of the canopy for centuries, nourishing the next arrangement of life. The oldest things are not the tallest. They are the horizontal ones: mats, shelves, buried root networks, and low ridges of growth that have accumulated across the plateau until they possess the scale of geology.

There is a faint strangeness in the way the place holds its boundaries. Water sometimes runs uphill along a root for several yards before descending. Leaves turn toward sounds that occurred hours earlier. A patch of flowers opens only when someone enters its shade, though it may take a long time to complete the opening. Certain pools reflect not the canopy above them, but a deeper layer of forest that cannot be found nearby. Animals will drink from these pools without hesitation.

The plateau seems to remember slowly. A footprint in the mud may remain for years, gradually filling with moss. A fallen branch may continue to cast a shadow after it has dissolved, because the plants beneath it retain its shape and refuse to grow there. Paths are not worn by use so much as persuaded into existence. If enough creatures pass the same way over enough centuries, the roots part, the ground firms, and the canopy bends overhead.

At the far edges, the high land declines toward the cloud sea. The forest thins only slightly, becoming a fringe of immense grasses and hanging roots. Water pours from the plateau in countless small threads, each one disappearing into vapor before reaching the depths below. Beyond the rim, there are occasional glimpses of lower mountains, but they appear submerged, blue and indistinct, as if the world beneath this place belongs to another age.

The whole landscape has the feeling of having reached a final form without becoming still. It is not young, not ruined, and not exactly alive in the simple sense. It is an ancient balance of growth, moisture, stone, and slow movement. Nothing hurries. Nothing is truly separate. The weather grows from the forest; the forest grows from the water; the water emerges from the old bones of the land.

Under the dim canopy, the plateau extends for days in every direction, warm and dripping, crowded with quiet lives. Its oldest paths are being closed by leaves. Its oldest buildings are becoming roots. Its mountains are sinking gently into green.
