The place is a **small underground industrial quarter built inside a hot, shallow cavern**, though “cavern” suggests more permanence than it possesses. Its roof is not an old, vaulted ceiling but the raw underside of the earth, a low brown-black skin of newly exposed stone. In places it hangs only a few stories above the ground. In others it rises into darkness, unfinished and soft-edged, where drilling has opened narrow throats into deeper rock.

Water is everywhere, but never in abundance at once. It beads on the walls, gathers in shallow runnels, and falls in patient, separate drops from the ceiling. The rock sweats constantly. Warm mist lies just above the floor, thin enough to see through but thick enough to blur the far lamps and turn every light into a pale halo. The air tastes of iron, wet dust, oil, and the faint mineral bitterness of water that has traveled through stone only recently made accessible.

The ground is a patchwork of materials. New concrete slabs have been poured over older mud, but the concrete is already stained and buckled by moisture rising from beneath it. Rails disappear into puddles. Timber braces, dark with rot, stand beside angular steel supports furred with orange corrosion. Piles of crushed rock wait beside the roads, their surfaces glistening. Along the edges of the quarter, the original geology remains exposed: black shelves of basalt, pale seams of limestone, clay the color of old blood. Some faces are so fresh they still show the parallel scars of the cutting machines.

Nothing here has fully settled into its final shape.

The streets are short and practical, laid out around a pumping station, a loading yard, and a cluster of low industrial buildings. There is no grand central space, only a handful of connected rooms and passages that feel like the first rooms of a city. Pipes run everywhere, but many terminate abruptly in capped ends or hang from ceilings without yet serving anything. Conveyor belts cross above the streets on skeletal gantries. Small elevators descend through square shafts, carrying stone, water, fuel, and the occasional maintenance crew. Their cables hum under tension. Warning lights turn slowly in the damp, painting the vapor red, then black, then red again.

The buildings have been constructed with the logic of a settlement that expects to expand, though expansion is slow and uncertain. A workshop contains three large machines but room for ten. A warehouse has numbered bays, most empty. A clinic, a canteen, and a control office occupy a single prefabricated block, their windows opaque with condensation. The doors are sealed against heat and moisture, but every threshold has a dark fringe of mud.

The illumination is weak and deliberate. Most lamps are hooded sodium fixtures, yellow and low, mounted where they will not be struck by equipment or drowned by spray. Their light does not reach far. Beyond each pool of visibility, the quarter breaks apart into glimmering rails, wet walls, and moving shadows. The darkness is not absolute; it is layered, filled with the suggestions of pillars, pipes, and bodies. At a distance, the whole settlement seems to breathe through its lamps.

The heat is oppressive but not violent. It presses upward from the floor and rises from the machinery. Workers have left fans running in every occupied room, but the fans merely push the warm, damp air from one place to another. Clothing clings. Metal handles are hot. The walls exhale a continuous vapor, and after a while every surface acquires the same slick sheen: stone, steel, cable insulation, painted signs, even the rubber guards around the machinery.

There is little vegetation. A few pale mats of moss cling to the wettest cracks, yellow-green and nearly translucent. In a drainage ditch, something like reed grass has taken hold, though its stalks are short and waxy, its leaves reduced to narrow blades. The plants grow only where the lamps are strongest and water is constant. They do not form a wilderness. They are isolated stains of life against a built and mineral landscape.

The animals are another matter.

They are everywhere.

At first they appear as disturbances in the mist: small bodies slipping beneath pallets, pale shapes crossing the rails, brief ripples moving through the black water. Then the eye adjusts. The walls are crowded with cave crickets, their long antennae trembling in the heat. Thousands of them gather around the warm exhaust vents, not in a frantic swarm but in dense, patient congregations. They cover the undersides of catwalks and retreat from each passing vehicle in a slow, collective folding.

Bats roost in the high cuts above the machinery halls. They hang in heavy clusters from the unfinished rock, wings packed tight, their droppings forming white fans on the floor below. When an engine starts, the disturbance passes through them gradually. One shifts, then another, then a whole section loosens and pours across the ceiling in a silent, dark migration before settling again somewhere only slightly farther away.

The drainage channels belong to eels, blind fish, and long-legged amphibians that have adapted to the hot runoff. They are not monstrous. They are recognizably ordinary animals made abundant by the enclosed conditions. Their pale bodies slide through the water under grates and between concrete foundations. In the pumping station, workers have installed mesh screens, but the screens are constantly furred with living things: small fish, larvae, translucent worms, and the broad, flattened bodies of creatures that resemble salamanders. The pumps cough whenever one is drawn into the intake.

The loudness of the place is mechanical and continuous rather than explosive. Compressors thud with a slow industrial heartbeat. Pumps grind below the floor. Chains knock against sprockets. Water ticks from the ceiling, too softly to distinguish except in the pauses between larger sounds. Ventilation fans roar through ducts overhead, and somewhere beyond the visible streets, drilling proceeds at a measured pace: a deep, repeating concussion, with long intervals between impacts.

Nothing happens quickly here. The pumps cycle. Sediment accumulates. Water finds new routes through the stone. Rust advances. Mineral crusts thicken around bolts and pipe joints. The excavation grows by meters over months, perhaps only by centimeters in places where the rock resists. Even the animals seem governed by the same slowness. Bats relocate over the course of a night. Moss extends across a wall over a season. A puddle survives long enough to become part of the local geography.

The settlement is built around these gradual changes rather than overcoming them. Drainage trenches are repeatedly deepened. Walkways are raised one layer at a time. New support pillars are poured beside old ones, leaving the quarter with a stratified appearance: infrastructure over infrastructure, repair over repair, each generation of construction visible beneath mineral stains and animal occupation.

At the outer boundary, the organized streets simply thin away. The last warehouse stands with one wall against raw rock. Its loading door opens onto a shelf of mud and broken stone, beyond which the floor drops into a shallow, steaming basin. A temporary bridge crosses it, its railings wrapped in warning tape. On the far side, there are no buildings, only survey stakes, hose lines, and a row of lamps placed at intervals that fade into the damp dark.

The cavern is still being discovered, but also still being made. Excavation removes stone; groundwater softens the edges; heat causes new deposits to form. White mineral blooms push out around cracks. Mud slumps into the channels. The ceiling sheds grains of rock so fine they look like ash. Every surface is in the middle of becoming something else—wall into seep, floor into sediment, ditch into stream, worksite into habitat.

There are rules here, though few are written down. Water always wins if it is given time. Heat rises but never escapes completely. Anything left still for long enough becomes occupied by animals. Freshly cut rock must be supported before it remembers how to fall. Machines may be shut down, but the pumps cannot be stopped for long. And no one builds directly over a dry place, because dry places do not remain dry.

Under the lamps, beside the slow conveyors and sweating pipes, the quarter feels less like a finished city than a warm underground organ: compact, industrial, sparsely furnished, and densely inhabited by lives that have found every gap in its design.
