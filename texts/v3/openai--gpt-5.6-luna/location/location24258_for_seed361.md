The landscape is hidden inside a high, sun-warmed plateau.

From outside, the plateau appears almost featureless: a broad expanse of old stone, gently sloping, its ridges worn down by ages of wind and chemical weathering. There are no dramatic cliffs, no peaks sharp enough to cast a mythic shadow. The land has been eroding for so long that it has nearly become a plane. Its surface is the color of dried bone and tarnished copper, interrupted by shallow runnels where rare storms have passed and by dark seams of mineral that show through like old scars.

The entrance is not a cave in any ordinary sense. It is a narrow opening in the stone, no wider than a person’s shoulders, concealed beneath a shelf of pale rock. Warm air moves through it at irregular intervals. Sometimes it exhales. Sometimes it draws the plateau’s dry air inward with a soft, powerful pull. The opening may be passed a hundred times without notice, because the shadows around it are not quite shadows: they are a distortion in the eye, a small area where distance has lost confidence.

Beyond the entrance lies a pocket of highland forest, enclosed within the thickness of the plateau.

It is not large. A few hundred paces from one wall to the other. The roof arches high overhead, though from outside there is no sign of any hollow. The ceiling is made of translucent strata: ancient stone, compressed ash, layers of glassy sediment, and something dark that resembles petrified root. Daylight filters through in a muted, ordinary gold. There is no mystical radiance. The sun shines as it should, but the light arrives softened by many meters of rock and by the thin green films spread across the ceiling.

Rain enters through the ceiling in isolated places.

It does not fall as a continuous shower. Instead, the stone occasionally beads with water. Drops gather in the pores above, tremble, and descend one by one, sometimes for an hour, sometimes for a season. In a few fissures, condensation forms a steady silver thread. The water collects in shallow basins carved into the ancient floor, each basin surrounded by a narrow belt of vegetation. Between them, the ground is dry, powdery, and warm.

The forest therefore grows in islands.

There are tall, pale trees with trunks no thicker than a wrist and crowns that spread like fans of translucent leaves. Their roots run visibly across the stone, seeking dampness in every crack. Low ferns cluster around the pools. Moss fills the hollows between slabs, but nowhere does it form a continuous carpet. Empty ground is part of the ecology here: bare expanses of mineral, sun-warmed and silent for a few seconds at a time, separating one living patch from the next.

The plants are lush where they can be, extravagant in the presence of water. Some leaves are broad enough to catch a single drop and hold it for days. Others are narrow, jointed, and covered in tiny valves that open only when the humidity changes. One vine has grown along the ceiling and descends in a single green curtain, but its leaves are spaced far apart, each one hanging like an individual pennant in still air.

Nothing grows without a reason. The pocket is too dry, too elevated, too old for wastefulness.

Yet the fauna is overwhelming.

They occupy the spaces between the plants, the spaces beneath the stones, the air above the pools. At first the place appears sparse. Then one notices a pale body folded against a wall, another slipping beneath a root, another suspended motionless from the ceiling. The eye adjusts, and the hollow seems to fill.

There are long-legged browsing animals, small as dogs, with jointed necks and soft mouths. They move among the ferns in loose, silent groups, drinking from the condensation basins without disturbing the water’s surface. There are heavy, many-segmented things that push through the dry soil and leave raised trails behind them. Their shells are thin and translucent, revealing slow machinery of organs beneath. Flocks of winged creatures inhabit the upper air, though “flock” suggests more coordination than they possess: they swarm, scatter, collide, vanish into cracks, and reappear elsewhere.

Predators are everywhere. Some are obvious—lean, six-limbed animals that spring between stones with startling speed. Others resemble changes in the landscape. A patch of pale gravel may unfold into a mouth. A hanging root may detach itself and crawl toward the nearest warm body. Small hunters perch on the walls and emit brief pulses of pressure that make the surrounding creatures freeze.

The sound is enormous for such a contained place.

Water ticks into stone. Wings beat in dense, irregular clouds. The browsing animals make wet clicking calls to one another. Burrowing bodies grind through the soil. Predators hiss, bark, snap, and produce low vibrations that are felt in the ribs more than heard. From somewhere in the ceiling comes a long mechanical hum, rising and falling like a distant engine.

The hum is not machinery, though machinery is present.

Scattered through the hollow are remnants of an old installation: narrow towers grown from black ceramic, half-swallowed by roots; silver frameworks spanning the gaps between stones; small apertures set into the walls, opening and closing without visible purpose. Their surfaces are smooth but not clean. Moss has colonized them. Insects nest in their seams. A tree has grown through one of the towers, and the tower has altered its shape to accommodate the trunk.

The technology is beyond modern understanding, but it is no longer fully separate from the ecology. It regulates something.

When the air becomes too dry, the ceiling darkens and releases a brief fall of water. When the pools grow crowded, a buried mechanism shifts the ground and opens a new basin. Some of the animals carry faint metallic patterns beneath their skin, as if generations ago they were designed and later allowed to become wild. At irregular intervals, the entire hollow changes its gravity by a fraction. Leaves lift. Small creatures stumble upward. Droplets hang in the air, then fall sideways into the waiting mouths of plants.

These changes are not dramatic enough to be called disasters. They are the place’s ordinary rules.

The pocket has existed for a very long time. The stone around it is ancient beyond any meaningful chronology, and the hollow has settled into its final shape through slow erosion, mineral deposition, root pressure, and the patient corrections of its buried systems. Nothing is being built on a human scale. Nothing is growing toward a climax. The place is not young, but neither is it dead. It is active in the way a body is active while resting: breathing, filtering, hunting, repairing, shedding.

At the far end, beyond the last pools, the floor rises into a low mound of weathered stone. A single structure stands there, no larger than a room. Its walls are made from overlapping plates that resemble both slate and skin. There is no door. One side opens only when approached by a living creature, and even then the opening is selective: a small animal may pass, a larger one may be turned away, a machine may be swallowed and dismantled.

Inside, the air is cooler. The sound of the forest becomes muffled, though the pounding of wings and the deep subterranean hum remain. The walls display a slow record of the hollow’s conditions—not images, but arrangements of light and pressure. Droughts, migrations, extinctions, floods lasting a few minutes, the introduction of species now considered native: all are stored there in patterns that can be felt through the fingertips.

Above the hidden chamber, the plateau continues under the normal sun.

Wind crosses its nearly level surface. Dust moves through ancient channels. The high air is warm and thin. From time to time, a storm passes over the stone, and a few drops find their way into the pocket below.

Then the forest wakes all at once.

Leaves open. Burrowers surface. The winged creatures rise in a deafening cloud. Every pool ripples with feeding bodies, and the old structures brighten beneath their coats of moss. For several minutes, the concealed world becomes impossibly crowded with life.

Then the water stops.

The animals separate. The noise recedes into scattered calls, impacts, scrapes, and hums. Empty stone returns between the living things.

The hidden landscape waits for the next drop.
