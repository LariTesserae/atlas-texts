The landscape lies low and broad beneath a permanently bruised sky, a coastal industrial basin where the land has almost forgotten how to rise.

It is not quite a marsh, though water is everywhere. Shallow black water occupies the spaces between roads, loading yards, tanks, and long concrete buildings. It gathers in old rail cuts and beneath pipe bridges. It stands in the wheel ruts of service lanes, beads along retaining walls, and seeps from the seams of the pavement. After rain, which comes often and heavily, the whole district seems to sweat. Concrete sweats. Rusted steel sweats. The undersides of bridges drip steadily into the channels below. At low tide, mud the color of ash appears beneath the water, smooth and slick except where thousands of small tracks score it.

There is no green.

The basin was once cleared, paved, dredged, burned, and salted so thoroughly that almost nothing rooted there. The few remaining weeds die in cracks before they become recognizable as plants. No reeds edge the water. No grass softens the embankments. No trees break the long, low horizon. The soil, where exposed, is not soil in any generous sense, but compacted gray silt mixed with cinders, powdered brick, salt crust, and flakes of industrial paint. Here and there, mats of dark microbial film spread across the wet ground, too thin and colorless to be called vegetation. They wrinkle as the water recedes and shine like oil in the dim light.

The city around it is extensive but strangely empty. It is a settlement built for moving materials rather than housing people: warehouses without windows, processing halls, pump stations, settling ponds, substations, cranes, conveyors, and ranked storage tanks. The buildings are widely spaced, with long distances of open pavement between them. Their scale is industrial, but their presence is intermittent. A refinery wall may run for half a mile and then end at a flooded lot. A row of worker housing stands beyond a fence, most of its windows dark, each block separated from the next by drainage channels and vacant service roads.

The construction is old enough to have acquired a settled form. Nothing looks newly placed. The concrete has taken on a permanent gray-brown dampness. Expansion joints have widened. Steel gantries carry several generations of cable and pipe, some active, some blind and capped. The oldest structures have been reinforced instead of replaced, their original shapes buried beneath buttresses, braces, patched walls, and additions made for machinery that has since vanished. Erosion has not destroyed the district so much as incorporated it. Water has made its own routes through the designed ones.

Long embankments divide the basin into sectors. They are crowned with roads, but the roads are seldom used. Their surfaces are bleached and blistered by heat, then darkened by rain. Drainage grates vanish beneath sediment. Culverts carry a constant brown flow toward the tidal flats. In places, the pavement has sagged into the marsh so gradually that one can see the old geometry continuing underwater: a submerged intersection, a line of parking bays, the foundations of a demolished warehouse.

The temperature is oppressive at ground level. Even under cloud, heat rises from the black water and the broad concrete surfaces. The air is wet enough to cling to the skin. It carries the mineral smell of salt mud, hot metal, stagnant channels, diesel residue, and the faint sourness of animal bodies. Rain does not cool the district for long. It strikes the roofs in a brief gray sheet, fills every depression, and then leaves behind a thicker, warmer vapor.

The light remains dim throughout the day. Low cloud presses down from the sea, and the industrial haze turns the sun into a pale disc that can be looked at without pain. Under bridges and between buildings, the air is almost blue. Lamps are mounted everywhere—along catwalks, at gates, beside pumps—but many are unlit, and those that work cast weak amber islands across the wet ground. At night the basin becomes a collection of distant glows reflected in black water, with enormous portions of it left unillumined.

For all the apparent emptiness, the place belongs to animals.

They are not hidden. They are present in masses, occupying the water, the drains, the mud, the roofs, and the hollow interiors of the machinery. Eels knot themselves in the warm discharge channels. Small fish crowd the shallow basins until the water seems to ripple even when there is no wind. Crabs move over the mud in broad, lateral tides, sheltering beneath pallets and rusted sheets. Catfish gather below the outflow pipes, their whiskers trembling in the current. In the deeper settling ponds, dark backs surface and disappear.

The water channels are thick with frogs, though their bodies are difficult to distinguish from the wet debris until they move. They cling to tank walls and cluster around drain mouths. Snails cover the submerged concrete in dull shells. At the edges of the paved yards, insects gather in such numbers that whole vertical faces of walls seem to vibrate: midges, mosquitoes, flies, beetles, and small moths drawn to the weak lamps and the warmth leaking from machinery. They rise in clouds when a door opens or a vehicle passes, then settle again with astonishing speed.

Birds dominate the upper levels. Gulls stand on cranes and warehouse roofs, but there are also cormorants, herons, terns, and black-winged birds that nest in the abandoned pipework. They fill the rafters of the largest sheds. Their droppings whiten the tanks and walkways. At certain hours, hundreds lift from one pond together, cross the basin, and settle on another structure, a moving discoloration against the cloud. Yet they are not constantly calling. The abundance is often nearly silent: a mass of bodies shifting in the dark, wings folding, claws tapping on metal.

The quiet is industrial rather than natural. There is always some low process underway—the distant churn of a pump, the muted throb of a generator, the hiss of pressure escaping from a valve—but these sounds are widely separated. Between them lie long stretches of rain, dripping water, and wind moving through empty frames. A crane cable knocks once against a mast and then stops. Somewhere behind a wall, a motor starts, labors, and shuts down. The noise never builds into a continuous roar. The district is too large, and too sparsely occupied, for that.

At intervals, the silence is broken by animal movement. A thousand small bodies enter the water at once. Fish strike the surface in a settling pond. Crabs click over a sheet of metal. Birds erupt from the roof of a warehouse, circle once, and return. Frogs call from the drainage ditches in a low, uneven chorus that resembles machinery heard from far away. The sound fades as quickly as it comes.

The active processes here are slow but relentless. Tidewater moves through sluice gates. Rain fills the basin, pumps empty it, and seepage fills it again. Heat drives vapor from the ponds. Salt crystallizes on the lower walls, dissolves, and returns. Rust advances under paint. Sediment rises in the channels. Fish breed in warm effluent; birds follow them; scavengers follow the birds. The city continues to sort, drain, cool, lift, and discharge, though much of its original purpose has been reduced to maintenance.

A few workers still cross the district in white helmets and rubber boots, traveling by elevated walkways where the ground has become unreliable. Their vehicles move slowly, tires cutting through shallow water. They inspect gauges, clear screens, open floodgates, and replace lamps. The rules of the place are practical and old: do not enter a closed yard, do not trust a dry-looking surface, do not stand beneath a discharge pipe, do not leave food exposed, and never wade after dark. The water is not mysterious. It is simply hot, deep in places, chemically harsh, and full of things that feed on whatever enters it.

Beyond the last warehouses, the industrial grid dissolves into tidal flats. There the horizon opens fully. Low seawalls and causeways extend across the wet land, their straight lines slowly losing their authority. The flats are a broad gray-brown field under the dim sky, scored by channels that gleam whenever the cloud brightens. Far off, more tanks and chimneys stand in rows, softened by vapor. Closer in, the only elevations are spoil heaps, bridge crossings, and the occasional stranded barge settling deeper into the mud.

Nothing in the landscape feels newly catastrophic. It has been this way for a long time. The water has found its levels. The buildings have acquired their stains. The animals have learned the schedules of pumps and tides. Industry and wetland have not merged into anything strange; they simply occupy the same exhausted ground, each continuing according to its ordinary rules.

The result is a hot, dim, weathered city with almost no plant life and far too many animals—a place that appears empty until one looks into the drains, under the walkways, across the roofs, and down into the dark moving water.
