The landscape is a broad, low basin under an unchanging white-gold sun.

Nothing in it is natural—not the ground, not the hills, not even the mud. The basin was made by hand, though the hands that made it belonged to people no longer present. Its floor is paved in vast plates of pale fired clay, fitted together with seams as straight as writing. Between the plates, narrow channels carry warm water toward the horizon. The channels are lined with glazed brick, blue in the shade and silver in the sun, and every one of them runs at the same depth, with the same slow current, as if the entire country were a single shallow instrument designed to play one note.

The paving has cracked, but the cracks do not widen. They are sharp, fresh-looking fissures, black beneath their rims, as though the ground had been broken moments ago and then instructed to remain broken. Some have filled with clear water. Some contain beds of moss, reeds, or thick flowering vines. A few descend farther than they should: one can kneel at the edge and see the underside of the world, a second basin hanging below the first, its own roads and gardens upside down in the darkness.

The air is hot and wet. Heat rises from the clay in visible shimmers, while a fine condensation gathers on every surface—on walls, leaves, statues, ropes, and the smooth backs of insects. Rain comes constantly, but only as a mist so fine that it is first noticed as brightness around the sun. Droplets cling to the air in suspended veils. They gather along the edges of roofs and arches, swelling into perfectly round beads that remain hanging indefinitely. If one falls, it falls upward until it touches the underside of a cloud.

The basin extends for several miles, open enough that the horizon is always present. There are no forests, no wild mountains, no natural ridges. Instead, low constructions rise at generous intervals: stepped platforms, reservoir towers, long roofless halls, stairways that lead nowhere, and isolated walls built from cream-colored blocks. Their arrangement suggests a town spread thinly across a plain, or the remains of a machine too large to understand. Each structure is made of simple materials—sun-baked brick, woven reed, timber darkened by oil, plaster, rope, fired tile—but the totality is immense.

The people who built it worked without metal. Pegs, wedges, stone blades, bone needles, wooden levers, and braided cords are still visible in the old work. The larger stones were moved by ramps and rollers. Some walls bear the shallow impressions of fingers. A few doorways are no wider than a person’s shoulders, but their thresholds are worn smooth by use. There are no hinges. Doors are slabs that have been taught to remember being open.

Vegetation fills the spaces the builders allowed it to fill.

Reeds grow in long, deliberate ranks beside the channels, each stalk the same height and bending in the same direction, even when there is no wind. Broad-leafed plants climb the walls in green curtains. Their leaves are waxy and almost luminous, veined with pale blue. Vines cross courtyards and vanish through solid masonry, emerging from the other side with their flowers already open. In the gardens, fruit hangs heavily from low trees trained along frames of wood. The fruit is warm when picked and contains clear water, a hard seed, and occasionally a small carved object: a bead, a button, a miniature key, a tooth.

Nothing grows directly from the earth. Every root enters a pot, a crack, a trough, a woven basket, or a pocket of soil sealed into the construction. Even the great trees stand in masonry wells. Their roots do not spread outward; they descend into chambers beneath the paving, where they are fed by a network of clay pipes. If a tree is cut down, its roots remain green and continue flowering in the dark.

The ecology is abundant but strangely uncompetitive. Plants do not choke one another. A vine will stop at the edge of another vine’s shadow. Moss grows around a footprint but never over it. Fallen leaves remain bright and intact on the ground, neither rotting nor blowing away, until an insect carries them to one of the composting rooms built beneath the terraces.

The insects are plentiful. Large transparent dragonflies patrol the channels, their wings beating without sound. Beetles with white shells emerge from wall cracks at noon and arrange themselves in rows along the paving seams. Small yellow moths gather beneath every overhang, though there are no lamps. Their bodies give off a dim warmth that can be felt on the skin. Here and there, winged things resembling birds move through the air, but they have no feathers. They are made of folded leaves, thin skin, and jointed sticks, and when they land they collapse into harmless bundles. By morning they are standing again.

Other animals occupy the gardens: long-legged waterfowl with blank, porcelain faces; squat amphibians that carry their own shallow ponds on their backs; pale goats tethered to posts that do not cast shadows. They are abundant, but each species keeps to its assigned region. A flock will not cross a particular line in the pavement. A grazing animal may stare at a fruit tree for years without trying to eat from it. Hunger exists here as a rule of behavior, not as a pressure.

The sounds of the basin are few.

Water whispers through the channels. Reeds rub together, but the noise is soft and distant, like cloth being folded in another room. There is an occasional hollow knock from beneath the paving, regular enough to suggest a machine, though no mechanism can be found. Voices do not carry. Someone may call from one end of a courtyard and be heard clearly at the other, but the words vanish a few paces beyond the boundary, leaving the surrounding air untouched.

There is no wind. Smoke rises in straight columns. Hanging ropes remain perfectly still. The trees do not sway, yet their leaves are always wet.

The sun has not moved since the place was built. It shines from high overhead, brilliant and almost colorless. Shadows are short, sharp, and unreliable. A wall may cast a shadow toward the north in one courtyard and toward the south in the next. Some shadows contain detail that the objects themselves do not: a staircase appears in the shadow of a blank wall; a person’s shadow may kneel while the person stands. Nothing changes when this is noticed.

At the center of the basin stands a wide, shallow reservoir surrounded by hundreds of steps. The water is clear enough to show the tiled bottom, but the bottom is not always beneath the surface. Depending on where one stands, it may appear a few inches down, or miles below, with the entire constructed basin reflected beneath it. The surface never ripples except when someone touches it. Then the disturbance travels outward in a perfect circle and stops at the reservoir’s edge.

A stairway descends into the water. The steps remain dry. At the bottom is a doorway, and through the doorway one can see the same reservoir from another angle. Sometimes a person enters and returns immediately. Sometimes they emerge years older, carrying a green branch. Sometimes they come out before they have gone in.

The local rules are firm, though they do not explain themselves.

A thing that is made may be repaired by placing its pieces near one another and waiting. Broken pots draw themselves together overnight. Collapsed walls rise grain by grain. A spilled basket of seeds returns to the basket if left unattended. Natural objects—fallen branches, shed skins, dead insects—cannot be repaired, but they also do not decay.

Water remembers the shape of any vessel that has held it. Pour it onto the ground and it gathers into that shape: a cup-shaped puddle, a bowl-shaped pool, a narrow bottle of water standing upright on the paving. Fire burns only what has been prepared for it. It will consume oil, rope, dried reeds, and certain marked woods, but it will not cross a line scratched into clay. Metal, where it exists at all, is soft and dull, used mostly for bells and needles. It cannot be sharpened.

The structures do not age. Their surfaces may become wet, stained, green with moss, or covered in flowers, but the bricks do not crumble. A newly built wall and a thousand-year-old wall have the same crisp edges. The only erosion occurs in the mind: after spending long enough in the basin, one begins to forget whether a stairway was always there, whether a reservoir tower has moved, whether one has passed the same doorway once or many times.

The place is not abandoned. It is maintained by absence.

Every morning, footprints appear along the channels, though no one is seen making them. Freshly tied knots show up in old ropes. Roofs are swept clean. Gardens are pruned with careful, invisible cuts. On some platforms, meals are laid out: flatbread, fruit, bowls of warm water, and little heaps of salt. The food does not spoil. By evening, it is gone.

Across the open basin, widely spaced figures sometimes stand in the sunlight. They may be gardeners, statues, travelers, or people waiting for instructions. They wear simple woven garments and carry hand tools. They do not speak. If approached, they turn their heads with great attention, as though recognizing someone from a dream, then point toward the nearest constructed entrance.

Beyond the entrance is always another part of the same landscape: another hot, wet plain of tiled ground; another sparse town of reservoirs and green walls; another sun fixed in the same white sky.

And somewhere, beneath all the paving, water continues to move through the clay pipes at the same patient speed, feeding the roots, filling the cisterns, and holding the whole immense made-world in its quiet, permanent equilibrium.
