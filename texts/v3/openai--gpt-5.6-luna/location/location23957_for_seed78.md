The forest begins above the weather.

It occupies an immense highland, a continent of ridges and suspended basins where the air is thin, the cold persistent, and the ground newly made. The mountains are not worn down into gentleness. Their faces stand in raw planes of black stone, blue-green shale, and pale mineral crust, as though the whole region rose from the earth only yesterday and has not yet learned how to soften its edges. Ravines split the upland in long, straight wounds. Some are miles deep. Others end abruptly in walls, their bottoms hidden beneath cloud or darkness.

There is almost no ordinary soil.

Instead, the landscape is held together by plants.

They form mats across the rock, thick as roofing felt and dark as wet leather. They gather in terraces along the cliffs, their roots gripping fresh stone in braided cords. They fill the cracks with pale, swollen stems. They climb, bridge, knot, and suspend themselves over empty space. In places the vegetation is not growing on the mountain so much as replacing it: a living crust spread over ledges and hollows, a continuous skin of mosses, lichens, thorn-vines, bulbous reeds, and low black fronds.

The plant life is nearly total, but it is not dense in the usual sense. There are enormous intervals between one growth and the next—wind-cut corridors, open shelves of stone, vertical distances too broad for any vine to cross. A single colony may cover a valley, yet within that valley one can stand for hours without seeing another tree, another person, or even a second obvious species. The vegetation is distributed in great isolated architectures: a tower of stalks on one ridge, a hanging garden beneath an overhang, a plain of close-cropped silver leaves on the far side of a chasm. Between them is exposed rock and a great deal of air.

The largest plants are not trees, though they resemble the beginnings of them. They rise in thin, jointed columns hundreds of feet high, branching only near their tips. Their trunks are hollow and translucent, layered with green-black tissue. In the darkness they show a dim interior pallor, not enough to illuminate anything, only enough to reveal their presence as vertical shadows within shadows. During the brief periods when the sky clears, their upper branches unfurl broad sails that tremble in the high wind. When storms come, the sails fold shut, and the whole forest seems to bow.

Some plants grow downward.

From the undersides of cliffs hang enormous root curtains, each root separated from the next by dozens of yards. They descend toward the cloud layers without reaching them, pale strands turning slowly in the air. Water gathers along their surfaces as condensation, beads into heavy globes, and falls. The drops strike the rocks far below with the sound of thrown gravel. In calm weather, these falling beads are the only rivers in the landscape.

Water appears suddenly and locally. A rain squall may cross a ridge in minutes, darkening one basin while the next remains dry. Mist collects in hollows and disappears by noon. Condensation gathers on the plant towers, runs through their hollow trunks, and is stored in bulbous reservoirs beneath the living crust. Here and there, a basin holds a black pool, cold enough to steam faintly when the air shifts. Around each pool the vegetation becomes almost lush, rising in thick layered shelves, then thinning sharply a few hundred yards away.

Nothing flows for long. Water drops, gathers, sinks, freezes, or is taken into the plants.

The cold is not merely a condition of the air; it shapes everything. At night, which occupies most of the day, the exposed rock shines with frost. The plant mats contract with small dry clicks. Suspended roots harden into rigid cords. Pools skin over in clear ice, but beneath the ice the plant colonies continue to pulse and circulate, their slow internal movements marked by faint tremors in the surface.

The darkness is deep. Cloud cover, high cliffs, and the density of the atmosphere below the plateau make the lower sky almost permanently black. Even when the upper sky is clear, starlight arrives weakly through layers of drifting vapor. The horizon rarely exists as a line. It is a succession of silhouettes: ridges, stalk forests, hanging roots, and distant shelves fading into one another.

There is a low greenish glow in some valleys, produced by the plants themselves. It rises from lichen fields and from the inner walls of the great hollow stems. It is too weak to cast shadows, but the eye adjusts to it, and then the world becomes visible in fragments: a slick stone, the underside of a leaf, the pale ribs of a feeding animal, the slow movement of something large on a far ledge.

The silence of the open spaces is deceptive.

The highland is loud.

Wind passes through the plant structures with the force of water through a canyon. The hollow stalks boom and whistle, each one tuned to a different depth of sound. When a storm front crosses the plateau, the entire forest becomes an organ: long moans from the root curtains, sharp fluting from the folded sails, booming pulses from the buried stems. The rocks answer by cracking in the cold. Ice breaks on distant pools. Falling water rattles down cliff faces.

There are also sudden biological noises—bursts of clicking, shrill calls, the tearing of leaves, the heavy impact of bodies landing on stone. Sound travels enormous distances in the thin, cold air. A disturbance on one ridge can be heard across a basin long after the creature responsible has vanished.

The fauna is abundant, though scattered like the vegetation.

Many animals live in the plant structures rather than upon the ground. Small pale climbers move along the undersides of leaves, gripping with hooked feet and feeding on the translucent tissue. They resemble strips of torn bark until they move. Long-winged things nest inside the hollow stalks, emerging at dusk in flocks that circle the columns without making a sound. Their wings are membranous and dark, their bodies warm enough to leave brief trails of vapor.

Larger grazers travel from colony to colony across the open shelves. They have broad, low bodies and multiple jointed legs designed for distributing weight over the living crust. Their mouths are ringed with hard cutting plates. When a herd feeds, the sound carries for miles: a wet ripping, followed by the creak and collapse of severed stems. The animals do not stay long in one place. Their presence is followed by sudden green regrowth, as if the plants require the damage.

Predators wait in the gaps.

Some hang beneath ledges, wrapped in mats of dead vegetation. Some lie flat against the black stone, visible only when they unfold. There are narrow-bodied hunters that leap between the plant towers, crossing distances no animal should be able to cross, and enormous burrowing forms that move under the living crust in slow waves. When one passes, the ground rises and sinks behind it. Herds scatter. Root curtains shiver. Birds pour out of the stalks in dark spirals.

The rules of the ecosystem are not entirely familiar. Plants respond to injury with movement. A severed vine may recoil toward its colony. A trampled mat may close over the footprint before the next frost. Some groves turn their leaves toward approaching bodies even in complete darkness. In the largest basins, the plant architecture seems to coordinate across miles, opening pathways before storms, sealing pools from animals, or producing great synchronized releases of spores that drift through the black air like slow snow.

The spores are not always harmless. They settle on the skin as a fine silver powder and begin to warm. Travelers cover their faces with woven masks, though the masks are primitive and often patched with plant membrane. A person who breathes too much of the powder may dream while awake, or lose the sense of distance, or become convinced that the nearest cliff is gradually approaching.

There are people here, but only in small numbers.

Their settlements are scattered along the reliable condensation zones, usually beneath overhangs or inside the hollow roots of dead plant towers. A shelter may consist of stacked stones, woven stalks, and a skin stretched over a frame of living branches. Smoke is rare; fuel is scarce and the wind steals it. Fires are kept in shallow stone basins and fed with dried root fiber, burning blue and briefly.

The inhabitants use hand tools of stone, bone, hard plant resin, and the sharpened mineral nodules that form around the pools. They cut channels into certain stems to collect water. They harvest the broad leaves for coverings and the tough inner fibers for rope. They mark safe paths with bundles of white fungus tied to exposed stakes. A few old bridges cross the narrowest ravines, made from braided roots trained over years into load-bearing cables. Most have sagged or snapped. The gaps they once crossed are now empty, with lengths of dead rope hanging far below.

There are no roads in the highland. There are only routes known by weather, plant behavior, and sound.

A traveler may move for days without encountering a dwelling, then find a small stone platform on a ridge, its surface polished by generations of feet. Nearby stands a single carved post, wrapped in dried leaves. The carving might depict a predator, a storm, or a human face stretched into a long vertical shape. No one agrees. In some places, little towers of stacked rock rise beside the paths. They are not monuments. They are warnings: of unstable crust, nesting animals, sudden water, or plants that remember being touched.

The youngest cliffs are the most dangerous. Their surfaces shed plates without warning. Whole terraces break away and fall into the cloud-filled chasms, producing impacts that arrive as delayed thunder. When a cliff collapses, the exposed mineral face is immediately colonized. Within days, dark threads spread over it. Within a season, pale roots begin to descend. Within a few years, the fall is concealed beneath a new living skin, though the scar remains sharper than everything around it.

The land is still rising in places.

Long ridges tilt by imperceptible degrees. Pools migrate uphill. The great plant colonies adjust their roots, pulling themselves across the stone. At certain times, the whole plateau vibrates with deep subterranean movement. The sound is felt more than heard, a pressure in the teeth and ribs. Animals become restless. Stalk forests lean in different directions. Water appears in new cracks.

Then the weather changes.

A vast front arrives from beyond the visible ranges, bringing snow, freezing rain, and winds strong enough to strip the upper growth from the towers. The open spaces fill with white motion. The cliffs vanish. The plants bend until their stems lie nearly horizontal, and the animals disappear into their shelters, burrows, and sealed root chambers.

For several days the highland is nothing but darkness, wind, and the drumming of ice.

When the storm passes, the place has shifted. New pools shine in the hollows. Broken stalks litter the ledges. Herds emerge thin and frantic. Green growth appears on fresh stone. From far away comes the booming call of a predator, answered by another across the basin, then another farther still.

The plants slowly raise themselves.

The forest above the weather resumes its breathing.
