The high country is dark even at what passes for noon.

It lies above the cloud deck, a vast interior plateau lifted so long ago that its edges have forgotten how to fall. The land is not sharply mountainous. It is broad, level in the manner of something immense and old, broken by shallow basins, tilted shelves, and distant ridges worn down to their structural bones. From one horizon to the next, the same black-brown stone continues: iron-rich rock, weathered into plates and broad polygonal slabs, with pale mineral seams showing where ancient pressure opened the ground and let it cool again.

There is no sun visible here. Above the plateau hangs a ceiling of dense, high atmosphere—smoke-colored cloud, perhaps, or an ocean of dust suspended at impossible altitude. It absorbs the day and gives back only a dim, diffuse glow, never brighter than the inside of a closed eye. At night the darkness becomes complete, but the land does not become empty. Thin blue-white glimmers travel under the surface. Warm fissures breathe in slow pulses. In the far distance, entire ridgelines occasionally brighten from within, not with flame but with a dull red pressure, as though the plateau were a sleeping engine whose workings reached all the way to the horizon.

The warmth comes from below.

The air is mild and heavy against the skin, carrying the mineral smell of wet stone, hot metal, and something faintly organic. Moisture is scarce in the open, but it is never entirely absent. Condensation gathers wherever the rock cools: beneath overhangs, in the lee of ridges, along shaded cracks. During certain weather cycles, vapor descends from the dark overhead and settles in silver films over the plain. It beads on stone, runs a short distance, and vanishes into porous ground. Rain does not fall so much as arrive in brief, localized events—a black curtain miles away, a sudden hiss over one basin, a few minutes of hard droplets striking the rock before the clouds withdraw.

Those places become centers of life.

The plateau is mostly bare, but not barren. Low mats of dark vegetation cling to the dampest seams, more like mineral stains than plants. Their surfaces are leathery and ridged, designed to catch condensation. They spread slowly around warm vents and die back at the edges, leaving rings of brittle brown growth. Here and there stand thin, jointed stalks the color of old copper, each no taller than a person. They lean toward the faintest warmth rather than toward light, and when the ground pulses beneath them they close their segmented leaves with a dry, synchronized click.

Between these sparse growths the land is occupied by animals.

They are everywhere, though rarely gathered in obvious herds. Small pale bodies move along the stone in the half-dark: many-legged grazers that scrape the mineral mats with black mouthparts; long, low scavengers that flatten themselves against the warm ground; quick, membranous things that leap from crack to crack and disappear before the eye has adjusted. Their abundance reveals itself in traces—fine tracks crossing open plains, shed plates caught in fissures, distant movement in the haze, a constant turning-over of the upper stone.

The larger creatures are more difficult to see. They travel enormous distances between condensation fields and thermal basins, following routes established over generations. Some are tall and narrow, balanced on several jointed limbs, their bodies held high above the ground to escape the heat. Others are broad, plated animals that crawl with deliberate patience, carrying entire dark gardens of symbiotic growth along their backs. Their hides are matte and absorb the little available light. When they stop, they become outcrops. When they move, the outcrop comes apart.

Predators are present, but the emptiness gives them no need for speed. A hunter may remain motionless for hours beside a mineral seam, its body cooling until it matches the surrounding stone. Then it unfolds. A row of sensory fronds opens along its neck. It listens to the vibrations of distant movement through the rock and begins walking—not quickly, but with the confidence of something that has learned the scale of the world.

The sound of this place is subdued. There is no continual wind, only broad atmospheric shifts that pass over the plateau like a hand over fabric. At long intervals, a pressure front moves through and makes every loose plate of stone tremble. The sound is almost below hearing: a vast metallic murmur, followed by the soft ticking of rocks settling back into place.

Closer sounds are sharper. Condensation dripping into a hollow. The click of closing leaves. The dry rasp of a grazer crossing a slab. The faint pumping breath of an animal hidden in a crack. Sometimes, in a basin, dozens of small creatures move at once and produce a sound like static beneath a floor.

The industrial character of the landscape is not made by machines. It is inherent in the geology.

The plateau contains natural systems operating at a scale and regularity that resemble refineries, turbines, and automated plants. Heat rises through networks of vertical shafts. Mineral fluids circulate beneath the crust, separating, depositing, and recombining elements in broad subterranean chambers. Vents open and close according to pressure. In some regions, the ground is ribbed with parallel channels where hot brine has flowed for thousands of years, leaving behind terraces of glassy salts. In others, slow-moving fields of black dust pour from unseen fissures and gather in fans around the bases of the ridges.

There are no pipes, but the land has pipes. No generators, but the land generates. No towers, but vapor columns rise miles high in the darkness, feeding weather systems that travel across the continent and return altered. The mechanisms are exposed only in fragments: a spinning eddy of warm grit; a crack that exhales at exact intervals; a basin whose surface rises and falls with the deep pulse of the mantle.

Nothing has been built here. Nothing bears a tool mark, a road, a wall, or a discarded fragment of manufacture. Yet the place feels engineered because every process is efficient, repeated, and consequential. A failure in one distant thermal field can shift the weather for hundreds of miles. A newly opened fissure can create a temporary oasis, and creatures begin arriving within days. An old vent closing can erase an entire food web over a season.

The erosion is mature rather than violent. The stone has been worn, not destroyed. Its surfaces are rounded at the edges, its fractures filled with sediment and mineral growth. Basins have reached their natural depths. Ridges have settled into long, stable forms. Even the ravines seem less like wounds than channels accepted by the land. When rare floods cross them, they do not carve new paths; they return to routes remembered by the terrain.

Distance behaves strangely on the plateau. The air is clear enough to show the first contours of far-off ridges, but the intervening space is so immense that objects lose scale. A moving animal may remain a speck for an hour. A cloudbank can take a day to cross the horizon. Storms have their own geography. They form over warm depressions, travel in broad rotating systems, shed moisture in isolated zones, and vanish into the dark at the edge of sight. No single observer can hold the whole weather pattern in mind.

There is a rule to the darkness: anything that produces its own light becomes visible from very far away.

A warm fissure glowing under stone can be seen across a basin. A swarm of winged animals, their bodies faintly luminescent, appears as a slow constellation moving over the plain. Some predators exploit this, remaining entirely dark while they wait near the brighter feeding grounds. Other creatures have abandoned sight altogether. They read heat, vibration, chemical gradients, and the minute electrical disturbances caused by living bodies.

Another rule governs the water. It never pools for long in the open. Moisture either sinks, evaporates, or is taken. A fresh film on the rock draws insects within minutes, grazers soon after, and hunters after them. The briefest rain can reorganize the population of a whole region. In this way, the sparse water makes the abundant fauna possible: life is scattered most of the time, but whenever the land sweats, the emptiness folds inward.

The high plateau remains quiet because its vastness absorbs disturbance. A struggle, a migration, the collapse of a vent—each event may be intense at close range, yet vanish into the surrounding miles. There are no settlements to interrupt the cycles, no artificial lights to soften the dark, no structures to provide shelter or impose direction. The creatures move according to pressure, warmth, moisture, hunger, and inherited routes.

Above them, the obscuring atmosphere turns slowly.

Below them, the old industrial earth continues its work.
