The water is not above the landscape. It is the landscape.

You descend through a cold blackness that thickens rather than deepens, until the distinction between sea and air has become meaningless. There is no visible surface, no moonlit skin of water far overhead, no rising direction except the slow pressure in the ears. The basin lies beneath an ancient drowned continent, though its original contours have almost entirely worn away. Ages of current have planed the stone into long, low shelves and broad depressions, all buried beneath silt the color of old bone.

The bottom is nearly flat, but not empty. It extends farther than the eye can follow, a dark plain crowded with forms: shale plates standing edgewise in the mud, pale chimneys, low mounds of mineral crust, forests of dead stalks, and thousands upon thousands of narrow vents where the sediment exhales. Nothing stands alone. Every ridge is pressed against another ridge. Every hollow is occupied by drifting ash, bacterial fleece, or a turbulent coil of water.

The water moves constantly.

Far off, perhaps beyond the visible region, some enormous current turns over. The motion arrives as a shudder through the plain. Silt lifts in curtains. Loose stones skitter across the sediment, not rolling so much as being carried a few inches, abandoned, and carried again. Tall mineral needles bend beneath the pressure. The darkness fills with suspended grains, and the whole basin becomes a storm without weather: a blizzard moving sideways through the drowned world.

Then the current reverses.

The silt settles for a few moments, revealing the true density of the floor. It is packed with the remnants of lives too old to have names. Shells have been ground into powder and fused with mud. Blackened mats of ancient growth lie beneath newer layers, each one a thin, compressed season. Ribs and plates appear here and there, but they are not the remains of recently dead animals. They are geological. Some have been mineralized until they are indistinguishable from the rock; others have been worn smooth by water into curved, ivory-colored bars. The age of the place is not displayed in ruins. It is present in the absence of anything sharp or unfinished.

Vegetation is sparse, though there are things that resemble plants.

In the sediment stand short clusters of translucent fronds, each no taller than a person’s hand. They are gray-green where the darkness permits color, and they grow in dense patches around the warmest cracks. Their surfaces are furred with minute living films. When disturbed, they fold into themselves and remain closed for hours, like fists. Elsewhere, threadlike growths cling to mineral chimneys, forming damp veils that pulse faintly as water passes through them.

There are no broad forests, no waving meadows. Light is too scarce, the water too cold, the currents too violent. Life has been reduced to adherence, filtration, burrowing, and waiting.

Fauna exists in intervals.

A pale, many-jointed shape may emerge from beneath a ledge, pause with its sensory filaments spread, and vanish when the water changes. Small blind scavengers gather around a newly exposed seam in the mud, their bodies nearly transparent, their movements quick and purposeless-looking. Once in a great while, a larger animal passes beyond the range of sight. Its presence is known only by the way the silt rises in a long wake, or by a hollow pressure that moves through the body like distant thunder. Nothing announces itself. Nothing calls. The animals have no need to make themselves known in a world where sound travels farther than vision.

Even the rare movement is muffled.

The prevailing sound is a low, continuous pressure: not quite silence, not quite a note. It is the vibration of water against stone, the distant grinding of submerged shelves, the soft hiss of sediment pouring down a slope too gentle to see. At times, pebbles tick together. A vent snaps. Something small scratches in the mud. These sounds do not break the quiet so much as reveal how deep it is.

There is no artificial light. The darkness has several degrees, though none can be called bright. Close to the mineral vents, a dim blue-white radiance leaks from films of living organisms and from the mouths of certain cracks. It does not illuminate so much as mark the edges of things. A low ridge appears as a darker interruption in the glow. A drifting cloud of silt crosses the faint light and becomes a blacker cloud within the blackness.

The vents are the most active parts of the plain.

They rise from the old seabed in crowded fields, some no higher than a knee, others forming sprawling, collapsed towers. Their fluids are warmer than the surrounding water but not warm enough to comfort. They carry metals, salts, and the chemical breath of the buried earth. Around them, the sparse growth becomes frantic. Fronds crowd the cracks. Films spread over stone in iridescent sheets. Tiny organisms swarm, disappear, and return according to the rhythm of the flow.

The vents do not burn. They bloom.

A plume rises from one opening, curls sideways, meets another plume, and collapses into a dense cloud that races along the floor. Mineral particles precipitate from it as it cools, building new crust over old crust. The landscape is ancient, but it is not still. It is constantly adding itself to itself, layer by layer, in a slow violence that has no beginning visible to the inhabitants and no conclusion anyone could recognize.

Now and then, a whole section of the basin gives way.

The collapse is almost silent at first. A seam opens beneath the mud. One shelf tilts. A forest of mineral stalks leans as one. Then the floor falls inward, and a pressure wave pushes across the plain. The darkness fills with stone. Old sediment rises from below, carrying the sealed remains of older worlds. For a time, the water becomes opaque. Everything is reduced to touch: the impact of grains against skin, the drag of the current, the tremor of distant rock.

When the cloud clears, the place has become slightly different. A trench has appeared. A vent has been buried. A new slope, almost imperceptible, sends silt sliding forever toward a hollow. The basin does not erode toward disappearance. It erodes toward its final shape, and the final shape keeps moving farther away.

No tool or structure has been made here. The only straight lines are fractures. The only repeated forms are those produced by chemistry, pressure, and growth. If anything intelligent has ever entered this depth, it has left no mark that remains separate from the seabed. A scraped stone would be buried. A footprint would fill. A shelter would become sediment.

The world’s rules are simple and absolute: cold slows everything but change; darkness conceals distance but not motion; water occupies every space; pressure makes all bodies temporary. The oldest things are not the tallest or strongest. They are the layers beneath the layers, the flattened reefs, the compressed mats, the mineralized hollows that have survived because nothing distinguishes them from the ground.

Across the open basin, the currents continue to collide.

They fold around shelves, plunge into cracks, and rise again in long, invisible spirals. Silt moves in vast sheets. The sparse growth bends in unison. Somewhere nearby, a blind creature is feeding. Somewhere far away, a section of the ancient floor is beginning to fail.

And beneath it all, the drowned earth remains crowded, cold, and restless—an immense plain with no horizon, packed so tightly with sediment, mineral, and buried time that there is almost no room left for anything new.
