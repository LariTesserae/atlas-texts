The settlement occupies a shallow basin where the ground never quite dries.

It is not a wetland, exactly. Most of the basin is firm: dark aggregate, old concrete, brick, and a thin skin of soil held together by roots. Water arrives in brief, uncertain events. A rainstorm passes over the district and leaves behind shallow black pools in the machinery yards. Condensation gathers beneath the roofs at night and falls, drop by drop, from iron beams. Narrow channels in the pavement carry these offerings toward a low reservoir at the center of the settlement, though some disappear into cracks before reaching it.

Everything is green, but not in the manner of a flourishing forest. The plants have taken possession of the place as a material.

Moss forms the insulation on factory walls. Ferns rise from drainage gutters in orderly rows. Flat mats of liverwort spread over the roofs, thick enough to soften footsteps. Vines enter the joints of brickwork and slowly widen them, their stems becoming cables, their leaves overlapping into awnings. There are no broad groves and no impenetrable jungle. The vegetation is sparse in the horizontal sense—bare stretches of concrete and open corridors remain between its colonies—but every vertical surface supports something living. A rusted tank bears a green hide. A retaining wall has a fringe of pale roots hanging from its upper edge. The old street signs are furred over until only their silhouettes remain legible.

The district is small enough to cross in less than an hour. Its boundaries are clear: a low embankment on one side, a belt of reed-filled drainage channels on another, and beyond them flat, open land disappearing beneath low cloud. Within those boundaries stand the remnants of an industrial town, though the town has become quiet and scattered.

There are perhaps a dozen occupied structures. They cluster around the reservoir and the old processing hall, leaving long gaps between them. Houses stand separated by vacant lots where grasses and medicinal weeds grow waist-high. A tram line runs nowhere, its rails emerging from the plant cover and vanishing beneath it after three streets. Utility poles still carry insulated cables, but the cables sag between supports and are thickly wrapped in climbing stems. At intervals, a transformer clicks and gives off a faint warmth, enough to attract a patch of silver moss.

The largest building is a former plant-processing hall, low and windowed, with a roof supported by green-painted steel trusses. Inside, the air is cool and humid. Rows of machinery remain in place: belt drives, sorting drums, pressure vessels, feed hoppers. Most have been adapted rather than removed. Roots pass through their housings. A conveyor belt moves at a barely perceptible speed, carrying fallen leaves from one end of the hall to the other. The leaves are deposited into a grinder, where they become damp, dark pulp. Pumps send the pulp through pipes into growing beds beyond the building. The factory still produces something, though no one speaks of it as production. It makes soil, insulation, fuel, medicines, and new plant matter from the remains of the old vegetation.

The machines operate in cycles. At dawn, valves open with a series of soft knocks. Fans turn beneath the roof, stirring the hanging fronds. A generator coughs once, catches, and settles into a low vibration. By midday the condensers begin to drip. In the evening, excess water is released through sluices into the reservoir, where it spreads in thin reflective sheets over the roots of the reeds.

The sound never becomes loud. It is the sound of a place working below the threshold of attention: the pulse of a pump, the intermittent hiss of a pressure line, the wet slap of a leaf against corrugated metal, the ticking contraction of cooling pipes. Wind moves through the planted corridors with a dry whisper despite the dampness, because most of the leaves are narrow and stiff. Rain, when it comes, is immediately divided among thousands of surfaces. It taps on metal roofs, patters on leaf layers, and sinks through the green architecture in stages.

The air is cool enough that breath sometimes shows in the early hours. It smells of wet iron, bruised stems, mineral mud, and the faint sweet odor of fermentation. Low cloud often settles over the basin, but the light beneath it is ordinary daylight: gray, even, and sufficient. Nothing glows. Nothing casts dramatic shadows. The place has the subdued visibility of an overcast afternoon that has lasted for years.

The old streets have been reduced to passages between living structures. Some are paved with cracked blocks and lined with drainage grooves. Others are roofed by trellises of trained vines, making green tunnels whose floor remains bare from constant use. The residents maintain these paths with shears, hooked poles, and small electric cutters. They do not clear the plants away. They trim them into channels, walls, screens, and load-bearing forms.

A warehouse has become a communal hall. Its doors are sealed with layered root mats, leaving a narrow central entrance. A former apartment block stands at the basin’s edge, its lower rooms abandoned to shade plants and its upper rooms connected by external walkways. The walkways are made of old industrial grating overlaid with woven stems that harden as they grow. They flex slightly underfoot but have supported generations of weight.

Every structure is repaired in two directions at once. Metal is welded where it has failed, and roots are guided around the weld. Brick is replaced when necessary, but the replacement is often planted rather than fired. Walls are allowed to settle. Roofs are weighted with soil beds so that they do not lift in the occasional storms. The settlement’s oldest buildings have ceased to be distinguishable from the engineered plants that hold them together.

Water is carefully apportioned. The reservoir is shallow and lined with concrete, though its interior has been colonized by a floating web of roots. It is divided by low weirs into clear and dark sections. One receives runoff. Another supports algae used in the processing hall. A third is reserved for drinking and is covered by a mesh of broad leaves that collect condensation at night. The leaves are harvested in the morning and shaken over ceramic basins.

Nothing here grows rapidly enough to feel wild. Growth is continuous, deliberate, and difficult to see from one moment to the next. A root may occupy a pipe over the course of a season. A wall may become visibly softer over several years. The settlement’s established forms—its eroded concrete, tilted rails, greened machinery, and low roofs—have settled into an equilibrium that is neither ruin nor preservation.

There are few animals. Small insects appear around the processing hall when its vents open, then vanish into the foliage. Thin-legged water striders gather in the reservoir’s quiet corners. Once in a while, a dark bird crosses the basin and lands on a chimney, but it does not remain. The plants dominate the food web so completely that animal life seems incidental, permitted rather than necessary. The people too are quiet and widely spaced, emerging from their buildings to tend valves, cut back corridors, inspect roots, or collect the morning’s condensed water.

The main activity occurs in places no one is watching. Underground, old foundations channel moisture toward planted chambers. Fungal threads move through the packed soil beneath the streets. In the factory, belts turn and grinders work. Along the embankment, reeds release seed in pale bursts whenever the wind shifts. Each process is modest, but there are many of them, and they overlap without hurry.

At the edge of town, a line of abandoned streetlamps leans over the drainage channels. Their bulbs no longer work. Moss has filled their housings, and narrow plants emerge from the glass. Beyond them lies the open basin: low ground, scattered pools, exposed stone, and the gray horizon. Behind, the settlement appears less like a city than like a machine that has been left in a garden and slowly taught to root.

When rain begins, the people close their doors and let the infrastructure receive it. Gutters fill. Leaves bow under the weight. The reservoir darkens. Pumps start one by one, and somewhere within the processing hall a belt advances with a soft, steady groan.

The whole place breathes through its plants, its vents, its seams, and its old industrial lungs.
