The landscape is called the **High Garden Below**.

It occupies the summit of a mountain that rises impossibly far beneath the sea. No one can see the mountain itself. Its stone has been covered for ages by a continuous growth of pale vegetation, and the water above is so deep that the surface is only a rumor: a pressure, a darkness, a remote change in the character of the dark. Here, at the mountain’s highest reaches, the ocean is not empty space around the land. It is the atmosphere. It moves through everything.

The summit is broad enough to be a region rather than a peak. From any one place, the next place is visible only in fragments: a terrace beyond a curtain of fronds, a bridge-ridge disappearing into black water, a white slope descending toward a basin full of slow-moving bodies. The distances are open and immense. There is no horizon, only the gradual loss of things into green-black obscurity.

Nothing here is naturally bare.

Every surface has been made by plants, though not in the ordinary sense of roots growing into soil. The vegetation is the soil, the stone, the masonry, and the weathering. Over unimaginable time, the first growths fused themselves into foundations. Their dead tissues mineralized without collapsing. Their living descendants continued the work, feeding on the old layers, laying down new ribs, plates, vaults, and stairways. The result is a world entirely constructed by growth.

There are long avenues of bundled stalks, each one thick as a tower and ribbed like the inside of a whale. Their surfaces are cool, hard, and faintly porous, with a texture between bone and wet bark. There are plazas grown from flattened mats of root-fiber, their edges raised into low parapets. There are archways made from the interlocked stems of ancient kelps, no longer flexible, their green-black tissues turned to a substance like polished slate. The architecture is not designed in a human way. It does not prefer symmetry, entrance, shelter, or view. It has the patient logic of a forest that has been growing toward pressure.

Some walls curve inward for miles before joining another wall. Some staircases rise toward nothing, ending at a suspended garden where the water is thick with tiny drifting animals. Some chambers are so large that their ceilings cannot be seen, only guessed at by the distant pale undersides of leaves.

The mountain’s high terraces are covered in vegetation that reaches upward and outward through the water. There are forests of ribbon-plants, their leaves hundreds of meters long, hanging vertically in the current like curtains in a windless hall. They are not blown back and forth. The water presses through them in a constant direction, and they hold their positions with the stillness of things that have learned the exact shape of the force acting on them.

Other plants form enormous globes, bladders, and bell-shaped chambers. Their skins are translucent and clouded, lit from within by a dim bluish phosphorescence. They pulse, but not with any visible rhythm. Their surfaces contract and relax so slowly that a single movement takes centuries. To an observer, they seem nearly motionless. Inside them, water circulates through forests of fine tendrils, and small animals nest among the filaments.

There are meadows of branching organisms that resemble frost, coral, antlers, and dead trees all at once. Their tips carry clusters of dark flowers. When something passes near them, the flowers open—not rapidly, but all together, as if the entire meadow has noticed. A cloud of minute creatures then emerges, feeding on the mucus and spores released into the water. The cloud disperses. The flowers close. The meadow returns to its long equilibrium.

The water is cool enough that every surface is cold to the touch, but not hostile. It carries no ordinary sunlight. Above, below, and between the terraces, the darkness is nearly complete. The plants provide the only illumination: faint internal glows, pale veins, suspended beads of green fire, and broad leaves whose undersides shine like moonlit ice. These lights do not brighten the world so much as prevent it from becoming entirely absent. They reveal a column of architecture, the curve of a creature’s flank, the pale edge of a bridge—and leave the rest to the imagination.

At intervals, the built vegetation rises into monumental forms. One is a forest of columns gathered around an empty central shaft. The columns are gray-green, each covered with horizontal scars from old growth. Their crowns vanish upward. The shaft descends into darkness and is filled with a slow, vertical river. Water pours upward through it as well as downward, with no visible source or destination. The surrounding plants have grown around this contradiction and made it part of the landscape. Bridges cross the shaft at many levels, though few of them lead anywhere that can be understood.

Another district consists of enormous terraces, each one covered in a different kind of plant mass. The first terrace is soft and fibrous, like a field of moss. The next is armored with overlapping plates. The next is a dense tangle of hollow reeds. Farther out, the terraces become smooth and pale, shaped like the shells of extinct things. At their outer edges the construction falls away into a sheer drop, and the mountain continues below as a descending city of vegetation: balconies, ribs, tunnels, and hanging groves fading into the lower ocean.

The place is sparse despite its abundance. There is life everywhere, but it is distributed across vast distances. A single tree-form may occupy an entire basin. A single animal may cross an open terrace for hours before reaching another living presence. Between the great growths are wide chambers of dark water, empty except for drifting particles and the occasional slow movement of something large.

The fauna are numerous, but most are quiet in their bodies and enormous in their patience.

Long, translucent animals hang beneath the plant-canopies, their bodies held rigid by internal chambers. Their mouths open and close without haste, filtering the water. Flat creatures with many jointed fins rest against the walls, nearly indistinguishable from fallen leaves until they begin to move. Pale eel-like forms live inside the architectural tissues, emerging through narrow pores to graze on the films of algae and mineral that coat the older structures.

There are herds of small black animals that move in precise, scattered formations. They have no apparent leader. They travel along the avenues and across the terraces, leaving faint trails of disturbed sediment. When they pass a living wall, portions of the wall open for them, and then seal again.

Larger animals inhabit the open water beyond the terraces. Their calls arrive before their bodies. A broad-headed grazer with hundreds of delicate fins may take an entire day to cross the visible distance. Behind it follows a cloud of smaller scavengers. Some creatures are too large to be seen whole: a pale underside glides over the upper dark, or a series of eyes appears between the leaves, each eye the size of a doorway. Nothing attacks. Predation exists, but it is slow, ritualized, almost architectural. A hunter settles over its prey and grows around it. A colony of filter-feeders closes like a flower. The consumed body becomes nourishment for the structure, and eventually structure becomes habitat again.

The sound is immense.

Water moves through the plant-built world with the steady thunder of a submerged storm. Narrow passages amplify it into a deep, continuous roar. Wide chambers turn it into a pressure felt through the bones. The great ribbon-leaves knock against one another far away, producing muffled impacts like doors closing in an abandoned city. Hollow stems moan when currents pass through them. Some of the oldest chambers resonate with the calls of animals, stretching each note until it becomes a long metallic vibration.

There are no moments of true silence. Even the apparently empty spaces hum. The architecture conducts sound over great distances, so that a movement made in one district may be heard as a faint tremor on another terrace. The calls of the fauna overlap with the noise of the water, and together they form something like weather: not music, because there is no pattern intended, but a stable field of pressure and tone.

This stability is one of the governing rules of the High Garden Below.

The currents do not change. There are no tides, no storms, no seasons, no warming or cooling beyond the narrow permanent cold. Water enters every chamber at the same speed and exits by routes that may have been established before the present plants existed. The animals migrate, but their migrations are loops. The plants grow, but their growth is immediately balanced by decay. A new branch rises as an old branch becomes a pillar. A chamber fills with roots while another opens nearby. Nothing advances. Nothing truly disappears.

Age here is not visible as ruin. It is visible as completion.

The oldest structures are not crumbling. They have become too fully integrated to be called structures at all. Their surfaces are layered with the remains of earlier forms: fossil leaves, shell fragments, mineral veins, compacted nests, and the traces of organisms that no longer exist. New life grows over them, but never completely conceals them. The whole landscape is a cross-section of itself, with every age exposed in terraces and walls.

There are places where the living architecture has stopped growing altogether. These are called the Still Rooms. Their walls are smooth, dark, and covered with pale markings made by extinct root systems. Water passes through them without disturbing a grain. Animals enter and remain there for long periods, facing the same direction. Nothing can be heard within except the distant sound of the surrounding currents, reduced to a soft vibration.

No tools exist here. No machines, lamps, roads, or manufactured objects interrupt the world. Yet almost nothing is untouched. The plants have made every boundary, and the animals have made every path by repetition. The distinction between natural and built has vanished. A bridge is a stem that learned to carry weight. A tower is a colony that learned to stand under pressure. A wall is a root mass that outlived the mountain surface it first covered.

At the highest point of the region is a vast, open basin surrounded by low terraces. The basin is filled with dark water and scattered with pale growths that resemble drowned trees. Above it hangs a ceiling of leaves, so high and thick that it resembles a second ocean suspended upside down. From the center of the basin rises a single column, white at its base and green-black toward the top. It reaches into the darkness without bending.

The animals gather there in great numbers, but they do not crowd one another. They occupy different depths: small bodies near the floor, long bodies among the middle plants, massive silhouettes above. Their presence is abundant and yet widely spaced. From a distance, they look like stars arranged in a pattern too slow to interpret.

Nothing happens at the basin.

Or rather, everything that happens has happened before and will happen again. The plants release their spores. The animals pass through the water. The currents roar through the old architecture. A translucent shape turns in the dark. A leaf opens. A chamber fills and empties. The high garden remains submerged, cool, ancient, and fully alive—a constructed wilderness at the summit of a buried world, holding its final form beneath an ocean that never changes.
