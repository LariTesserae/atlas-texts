The place is a high notch in a mountain, no larger than a village square, enclosed on three sides by cliffs and open on the fourth to an immense white sky. It is so high that the surrounding peaks do not feel like mountains anymore. They are broken shoulders of dark stone rising through a glare of cloud, while the notch itself hangs above the weather.

The floor is not level. It consists of shelves and shallow basins cut into a pale, hard mineral that may once have been ordinary rock but has been altered by centuries of freezing moisture. Its surface is gray-white, faintly translucent in places, and crowded with ridges no higher than a hand. Between these ridges lie narrow seams of black stone. Water gathers only there: thin films under ledges, beads in hollows, small lenses sealed beneath clear crusts of ice.

Nothing grows. There are no grasses in the cracks, no moss on the shaded walls, no lichen softening the stone. The absence is complete enough to seem deliberate. Every surface is mineral, ice, ash, or the dull remains of human work.

The cliff walls are densely crowded with formations. Short blades of frost stand shoulder to shoulder in the deeper recesses, though they do not melt even when the sun reaches them. They are not ice exactly. Some are glassy and blue; others are chalk-white, or faintly amber beneath the surface. They thicken during the damp hours, drawing moisture from the air, and slowly collapse when the air dries. The fallen pieces are incorporated into the floor, so that the whole notch seems to be making itself from its own debris.

At intervals, a bead of water appears high on the cliff. It trembles there, bright as a small lens, then travels downward along a predetermined groove. The groove may be natural, but it has the regularity of a channel cut with a tool. The bead joins others in a basin, where it remains for a day or a season, depending on the weather. Eventually the water vanishes into a hairline crack with a sound too faint to be called a trickle.

The air is cold and brilliantly clear. Sunlight strikes the upper ridges with a hard, almost metallic brightness. Shadows are blue and sharply bounded. Looking outward is difficult: the sky is a pale, depthless field, and the clouds below reflect so much light that the notch appears to float over a second, inverted world.

There is no warmth anywhere except the brief warmth of direct sun on dark rock. At night the mineral shelves contract with small, widely spaced clicks. During the day, when condensation begins, the clicks reverse and become minute pops. The cliff is always moving, but almost too slowly to witness. A ridge leans. A crust separates. A seam opens and closes.

The sounds are sparse. Wind passes over the notch rather than through it, producing a long, hollow note at the entrance. Within the walls, water makes the occasional soft tap. Frost needles break in clusters with a brittle whisper. Sometimes a deep vibration travels up through the floor, not an earthquake and not quite a groan, as though some enormous pressure were shifting far below.

The density of the place comes from surfaces rather than living things. Every ledge is layered with frost, salt, stone splinters, old water marks, and narrow channels. The formations press close together, leaving only a few walkable paths. A person moving through them must turn sideways and place each step carefully. The notch is small, but it feels full—packed with mineral growth, trapped weather, and the accumulated evidence of long, repetitive change.

Scattered among the formations are signs of habitation. A low shelter leans against the southern wall, built from stacked slabs and roofed with overlapping plates of dark stone. Its walls are held together by rawhide thongs gone stiff with age, and by wedges of ice that have been deliberately fitted into gaps. There are no fires inside; there is nothing to burn, and the air is too thin for one to last. Instead, shallow reflective bowls have been set along the wall to collect condensation. Some are made from hammered copper, greened almost black. Others are carved directly into the rock.

A narrow ladder of pegged stone leads to a higher shelf. The pegs are worn smooth by hands and boots, though the people who placed them are absent. Beside the ladder stands a frame of bone-colored mineral rods, tied together with cord. It might have been a marker, a drying rack, or an instrument for measuring the growth of the frost. Strips of cloth hang from it, frozen stiff and faded nearly white. When the wind catches them, they tremble without flapping.

The inhabitants, if any still come here, do not leave much behind. There are no bones, nests, tracks, droppings, or signs of animals. The only footprints are human, and even those are temporary. A passage of weather fills them with pale dust or seals them under clear ice. Tools lie in a shallow recess: stone hammers, a wedge of dark metal, a chisel with a wrapped handle, and several flat trays scored by repeated use. Their purpose is not obvious. They may have been used to break mineral crusts, harvest water, or cut the translucent formations into plates.

The rules of the notch are simple but strict. Moisture comes from above and from within the walls, never from a visible stream. It appears in pulses, usually when clouds pass below and the air pressure changes. Anything left uncovered becomes frosted. Anything warmed by the sun becomes wet, but only briefly; the water is pulled away into the stone before it can run freely.

Light behaves strangely at the edges of the formations. The translucent blades gather the sun and release it in dim, delayed gleams. A wall may continue to shine for several minutes after it has entered shadow. In the evening, the notch is full of low blue radiance, not enough to illuminate a face but enough to reveal the clustered shapes around it.

Nothing in the place hunts, feeds, reproduces, or dies in any familiar sense. Yet the notch has a cycle that resembles an ecology. Moisture arrives. Mineral structures thicken. Pressure builds in the shelves. Formations fracture, slide, and are reabsorbed into the floor. The old channels fill, new channels open, and the bright crusts migrate toward the places where the air is wettest. The process is active, continuous, and indifferent.

At the center of the notch is a basin no wider than a doorway. Its bottom is perfectly smooth and dark. Once every few days, or perhaps once every few years, a film of water covers it. The film is so thin that the rock remains visible beneath it. When sunlight reaches the basin, the water shivers without wind and arranges itself into branching lines. These lines persist for a few breaths, then vanish.

There is nothing monumental about the place despite its altitude. It is not a range, a desert, or a continent. It is one enclosed chamber in the mountain, a small working hollow above the clouds. But the brightness, the cold, and the closeness of the mineral growths give it the solemnity of a cathedral. Its ceiling is the open sky. Its pillars are cliffs. Its only congregation is the silent, recurring weather.
