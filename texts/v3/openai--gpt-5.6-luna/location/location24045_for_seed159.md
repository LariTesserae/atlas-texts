The place is a cavern, though “cavern” suggests something finished: a hollow worn into settled stone. This is not settled. It is a chamber still deciding whether to become a valley, a buried sea, or a wound.

It extends farther than the eye can comfortably hold. The ceiling rises into a hazy vault, perhaps a few hundred meters overhead, perhaps much more; its upper reaches are lost not in darkness but in a diffuse, ordinary-looking daylight. The light has the pale, practical quality of morning beneath a cloud. It comes from no visible sun. It seems to filter through the ceiling itself, through layers of translucent mineral, giving everything a clear illumination and yet no distinct shadows. Every surface is visible: the ridges, the moving animals, the raw seams in the floor. Nothing is hidden by night. Nothing is made gentler by it.

The ground is young.

Broad shelves of dark stone buckle upward beneath the weight of the chamber, their surfaces still glossy and sharp-edged. Between them lie fields of pale sediment, crushed shell, gray clay, and red-brown mineral dust. New ridges are being born everywhere. A crack opens with a wet groan, fills slowly with hot black slurry, and hardens into a jagged lip. A low hill shudders, slumps by a meter, and sends a fan of loose material sliding across the floor. Thin stone needles rise from the mud in clusters, some no taller than a hand, others thrusting upward like the beginnings of forest trunks—except there is no forest, and the needles have no patience. They grow crookedly, collapse, and grow again.

The cavern’s walls are ribbed with horizontal layers, as though the whole region had once been pressed together and is now being pulled apart. In places, the rock has split into enormous plates. They lean against one another at precarious angles, making streets, alleys, and enclosed hollows without any architect having planned them. Water threads through the seams. It does not flow continuously. It appears in sudden events: a tremor darkens a wall, condensation gathers in shining beads, and then a thin warm cascade spills down, hissing over the stone. Pockets of water collect in hollows, some no larger than footprints, others broad enough to drown a house. Their surfaces tremble even when nothing seems to touch them.

The air is warm and thick with mineral vapor. It smells of wet iron, clay, chalk, and something animal—many things animal. Moisture hangs along the lower walls in curtains of condensation. Droplets form on projecting stone and fall at irregular intervals, creating a vast, scattered percussion. Some evaporate before reaching the ground. Others strike shallow pools and make rings that travel through the water long after the drop itself is gone.

The fauna are everywhere.

They occupy the floor, the walls, the air, and the narrow space between one moving body and another. Most are not familiar enough to be called beasts. There are low, many-legged things armored in overlapping plates, their bodies broad and flexible as folded fans. They push through the sediment in shoals, using blunt mineral jaws to scrape at the stone. When disturbed, they roll into dense, clicking knots and then unroll all at once, flowing away in dozens of directions.

Tall creatures walk on jointed stilts, their bodies suspended beneath small domes of translucent hide. Their feet are soft and numerous, leaving temporary dark prints in the damp dust. They gather around new cracks, leaning inward as if listening to the stone’s birth. Smaller animals swarm over them, cleaning their limbs, stealing flakes from their shells, or simply using the larger bodies as moving terrain.

There are winged forms too, although wings may be the wrong word. They possess broad membranes that flutter without a visible rhythm, rising and sinking through the warm air in slow, erratic spirals. Their bodies are nearly weightless and their numbers immense. A disturbance on the ground sends them upward in a thick cloud, and the cavern fills with the sound of dry silk tearing. They settle again wherever they can: on ledges, on the backs of larger animals, across the roofs of the scattered structures.

Nothing here is still for long. The population is not merely abundant; it is layered. Creatures pass beneath other creatures, climb across them, burrow around them, and feed on the residues of their passage. A shallow basin may contain a packed mass of pale, blind swimmers, while the basin’s rim supports dozens of grasping animals, and the air above it swirls with winged scavengers. When one body panics, the movement spreads outward in waves. A whole district can begin to run without any obvious cause.

Their sounds make the cavern seem smaller than it is. There is the continuous scrape of feet and shells, the clack of feeding mouths, the slap of bodies crossing wet ground, the leathery flutter overhead, and the hollow booming of animals striking the walls. Calls rise and overlap: whistles, pulses, wet clicks, deep resonant knocks. Some sounds travel through the stone rather than the air. A footfall on one shelf may be answered minutes later by a tremor under another, as if distant animals are communicating through the unfinished earth.

The noise is loud but not chaotic in the human sense. It has patterns. Periods of churning activity pass through the cavern like weather. A feeding front advances over the sediment, leaving bare stone behind it. Then the bare stone begins to steam, and small burrowing animals emerge from below. A flock—or school, or cloud—descends. The larger creatures scatter. Somewhere, a wall gives way. The sound rolls across the chamber, and everything moves.

Human construction exists only in islands.

A few rough shelters have been built beneath overhangs or between leaning plates of stone. They are made from hide, woven tendon, flat fragments of shell, and poles cut from no plant anyone has seen. The poles appear to be scavenged from the cavern itself: mineral rods with fibrous interiors, perhaps grown by some earlier process or taken from the bodies of dead creatures. The shelters are low and dark inside, with weighted roofs to resist the constant vibration.

There are narrow walkways made from lashed bones and stone slabs, but most stop abruptly where the ground has shifted. Rope bridges sag across fresh fissures. Small watch platforms cling to the walls, reached by pegs hammered into soft rock. Tools lie everywhere: chipped scrapers, stone hooks, sharpened shell, bowls for collecting condensation, and crude spears tipped with black glass. They are practical objects, repaired many times and abandoned quickly when the landscape moves beneath them.

No settlement has managed to become permanent. A shelter may stand for a season, perhaps several, then be swallowed by a rising shelf or trampled flat by a migration. The inhabitants mark safe routes with stacks of pale stones, but the marks are often rearranged by animals or buried by new sediment. Their world has no stable horizon. Every map is a temporary agreement.

The rules of the place are simple but severe. Warmth rises toward the ceiling, carrying mist and the light scent of minerals. Water appears where pressure changes, never where it is most convenient. Stone forms quickly around living bodies, sometimes sealing them into the ground, while old stone liquefies without warning. The animals seem able to sense these changes a moment before they occur. They veer away from surfaces that look sound. They gather around places where the rock is about to open.

The cavern is not being eroded into existence. It is assembling itself through fracture, pressure, deposition, and appetite. The creatures are part of that process. Their feeding exposes seams; their burrowing redirects water; their waste hardens into terraces and crusts. Dead bodies do not remain bodies for long. Pale scavengers strip them, mineral films cover the remains, and within days a carcass becomes a low mound or a new ledge. Life does not soften the geology. It accelerates it.

Across the far reaches, the open floor disappears into a haze of moisture and moving bodies. New hills rise there in slow, uneven lines. A broad river may be forming, though at present it is only a chain of joined pools, each spilling into the next when the ground tilts. Beyond it, the ceiling light brightens over an expanse of wet black stone. The fauna gather in such density that the surface seems to ripple.

Then the earth shifts again.

A crack races across the floor with a sound like a cloth being torn. Water bursts upward. The nearest animals surge away, colliding, climbing over one another, filling the air with cries. A wall folds inward. Dust and vapor roll through the daylight. Beneath the tumult, the stone continues its quiet labor: cooling, hardening, splitting, and making room for whatever this place will become.
