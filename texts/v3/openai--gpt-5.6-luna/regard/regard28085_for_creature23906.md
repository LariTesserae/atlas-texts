The benevolent power does not arrive as a figure.

No foot touches the steps. No voice crosses the channels. Nothing descends through the hard blue air carrying an instrument greater than the builders’ own.

Instead, the power notices the place as one notices a hand held too long in cold water: not with alarm, but with understanding.

It sees that the high country is not cruel. Its severity is a form of devotion. Every wall has been made to spare something from loss. Every basin is an argument against waste. Every tool left in its niche is a promise that no necessary task should have to wait for a particular person to return.

It sees, too, what devotion has become.

The plateau has no rest in it.

A defect is not permitted to remain a defect. A dry basin is not allowed to be merely dry. A wall cannot lean without becoming a question, and every question must be answered in stone. The keepers do not suffer as animals suffer, but they are never released from attention. They sleep only when the world is exact, and the world is never exact for long.

So the benevolent power begins with the smallest mercy.

It makes one drop of water fall where no channel has been prepared.

The drop lands in an empty court.

There is no basin beneath it, no groove, no mouth, no slope arranged to carry it onward. It darkens the stone and remains there. The keepers sense the irregularity at once.

From the walls, from beneath the paving, from the hollow cores of the pillars, they come.

Pale forms gather around the mark. They examine it without eyes. They measure the new darkness against every remembered relation. A drain opens nearby. A tile loosens. A thin mineral lip begins to grow around the drop, attempting to make a vessel of the court.

The power does not prevent them.

It simply gives them another drop.

Then another.

For many years, the keepers work. They build a shallow cup in the middle of the court, though no plan has called for one. They cut channels toward it, then stop when they discover that the water does not need to leave. They raise a rim. They lower it. They divide the court into four equal sections and reunite them. They make the place as exact as everything else.

When they are finished, the water still does not behave as water has behaved anywhere before.

It gathers, but does not drain.

It reflects the sky without serving it. It holds the light without conducting it. Condensation forms along its surface and falls back into itself. A keeper inserts a narrow stone tongue into the pool, expecting the water to recognize a direction. The tongue remains dry.

This is the first thing the power teaches them:

Not every completion is a passage.

Some completed things are allowed to remain.

The keepers cannot understand permission, but they can understand stability. The court does not crack. The walls do not lean. No warning-ridges rise in the mineral films. The water is not trapped; it is simply there.

In time, the keepers stop trying to improve it.

Not all at once. One withdraws from the rim and becomes a darkening in the mortar. Another continues to watch from beneath the court. A greater keeper, spread across the old reservoir district, alters its internal pressures so that the new pool is neither fed nor emptied by any neighboring system.

The work of leaving something alone enters the architecture.

The power then turns its attention to the outer wall.

It understands the unfinished edge. The blocks are not waiting for completion because they are incomplete. They are waiting because the builders could not imagine a world beyond a prepared surface. The keepers have inherited that inability. They cannot cross into the white distance because there is nothing there to receive them.

So the power does not build the missing country.

It gives the falling water somewhere to go.

Below the last retaining wall, the white distance opens—not into ground, but into cloud. The cloud has always been there, though the plateau has never been able to recognize it. The final thread of water enters the brightness and disappears. For the first time, after a delay too long for any living calendar, a sound returns.

It is not a splash.

It is the sound of water striking water far below.

The keepers gather along the wall.

Some press themselves into the final blocks. Some extend narrow planes beyond the masonry and retreat, unable to continue. One remains at the edge for a century, its body divided between the last completed drain and the open air.

The benevolent power does not demand that it cross.

Instead, it changes the relation.

The water that falls beyond the wall is no longer water without a destination. It is water descending toward another unseen basin. The route is not visible, not measurable, and not under the keepers’ control. Yet it is real. A completed system exists beyond the boundary, even if no stone marks it.

This is the second thing the power teaches them:

An intended surface may be distant and still receive you.

After that, the changes become gentler.

The cold bright light acquires warmth for a few minutes each day. Not enough to melt the highland, not enough to damage the works. Just enough for the stone to remember that warmth is not always erosion.

A current of air enters the lower courts and does not divide itself according to the architecture. It moves across the open spaces, touches several walls at once, and leaves by no designed outlet. The keepers do not chase it. They cannot. They learn its return by the tremble of the water.

Then, in a sealed courtyard where no soil has ever existed, the power places a little soil.

It does not plant a forest. It does not force green abundance upon a place that has been made without room for it. It gives only a handful of earth, dark and soft, and leaves it beneath the sky.

The keepers approach in alarm.

Their first impulse is to pave around it. Their second is to drain it. Their third is to determine its purpose.

The soil has none that can be measured.

A seed appears there—not as a command, but as a possibility. The keepers do not recognize it. They place no wall around it. They build no channel toward it. Their old knowledge reaches the edge of the courtyard and finds no instruction.

The seed opens.

Its root enters the soil. Its stem rises. Two small leaves unfold, pale at first in the hard light.

Nothing breaks.

No seam widens. No basin loses its level. No mechanism falls out of balance.

The keepers remain near the plant for a long time. They do not tend it. They do not prune it. They do not seal the soil against intrusion. They simply alter the nearby water so that a little moisture arrives, not by a channel, but by falling from the air.

The plant grows.

It is the first thing in the high country that does not owe its shape to masonry.

The benevolent power is careful. It does not turn the plateau into a garden. It does not erase the makers’ work or declare their exactness a failure. The walls remain. The reservoirs remain. The tools remain in their niches. The keepers continue their repairs.

But now there are places where repair is not required.

A basin may hold rain for no reason. A wind may pass without being divided. A crack may widen and remain harmless, because not every opening is a threat. A dry stair may lead nowhere, and no new door need be made.

And sometimes, when the light changes, a keeper leaves its appointed wall.

It moves through the completed routes until it reaches the courtyard with the plant. There it becomes very still. Its planes soften. Moisture gathers in its darkness.

For a while, it does nothing.

This is not sleep. It is not maintenance. It is not waiting for a defect.

It is the first act in the high country that has no necessary purpose.

The power, seeing this, does not speak.

It only lets the water continue falling—through the finished channels, across the patient stone, over the unfinished edge, and into the unseen country below.
