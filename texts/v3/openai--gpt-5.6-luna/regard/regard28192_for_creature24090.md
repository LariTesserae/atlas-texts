The power would not give them water.

It would understand, if it was truly wise, that water is not merely a missing resource in the basin. It is the absence around which the entire world has been shaped. To pour water into the terraces would not be mercy. It would drown roots that had learned to drink stone, soften walls that had stood for ages, awaken diseases with no names, and turn every old certainty into panic. The first rain would be remembered as a catastrophe.

So the power would begin with restraint.

It would look upon the basin without trying to improve it. It would allow the dry forests to remain dry, the winged colonies to continue rippling over the ceiling, the people to preserve moisture in their bodies and their customs. It would not make their lives resemble the lives of creatures elsewhere. It would not mistake difficulty for error.

But neither would it leave them entirely to chance.

Far below the oldest streets, where pressure had been gathering for centuries, the power would place its attention.

Not a hand. Not a voice. A slight alteration in the arrangement of stone.

A weakness would become a strength. A brittle seam would close. A load-bearing pillar, already hollowed within, would settle against a neighboring ridge instead of splitting away from it. The change would be so small that no eye could see it. Yet the rock would produce a different sound when the next pressure moved through the basin.

The people would hear it.

At first, only one person would notice: an elderly wall-worker resting her palm against a courtyard floor. She would feel a long, low pulse beneath the familiar vibrations of feet, roots, and burrowing animals. It would not resemble an ordinary settling. It would arrive too evenly, as if the whole basin had drawn breath.

She would call the Keepers.

They would listen. They would inspect the old foundations, compare the tremor with marks preserved in tablets and walls, and discover that the sound had no exact precedent. For three days they would do nothing but watch. Then they would close two passages, move a nursery, and ask the inhabitants of a low district to sleep in the meeting halls.

On the fourth day, a section of ceiling would descend.

It would not fall upon the city.

It would settle into the empty mineral plain beyond the last planted bands, where the ground would absorb the impact and the dust would spread harmlessly through abandoned channels. For several hours the basin would be filled with a sound greater than any the people had known. The winged creatures would rise in a pale storm. Animals would crowd the walls. Families would press their hands to one another’s backs and wait.

When the dust cleared, the city would still be standing.

The people would call it foresight. Some would call it luck. A few would say that the basin itself had warned them.

The power would not correct them.

Afterward, it would do something more difficult than saving them once. It would make the warning repeatable.

Whenever a dangerous pressure began to collect, a faint new pattern would move through the stone: three deep pulses, then a pause; two short tremors beneath the left hand; a vibration that could be felt in the soles before it could be heard in the air. The pattern would never be identical enough to become a simple alarm. The people would still need judgment. They would still need memory, caution, and one another.

But they would have time.

That would be the first gift: not rescue, but warning.

The second would be given to the plants.

In a sealed chamber beneath an older settlement, the power would draw together minerals that had always existed there separately. It would not create an underground river. It would not produce a spring. Instead, over many seasons, a slow reaction would begin in the stone. The chamber would gather a trace of moisture from the air—so little that a careless hand could waste it, so slowly that no one could depend on it for ordinary thirst.

The first roots to reach the chamber would not be the largest or most aggressive. They would be the smallest yellow-flowered plants, the ones that opened beneath rocks and were usually overlooked. Their tissues would thicken. Their seed cases would become slightly more reliable. A grove planted from them would survive a season that should have killed it.

The people would notice only after generations.

They would call the plants by a new name: not *water-bearers*, because that would be too grand, but *the patient ones*. The Keepers would forbid anyone from harvesting them beyond a measured amount. The plants would be planted near nurseries, animal shelters, and vulnerable walls. Their presence would not make life easy. It would make one mistake less final.

The power would also attend to the people themselves.

Not by changing their bodies. Their bodies were already a record of intelligence.

It would preserve what they had nearly lost: the ability to feel one another clearly in the center of the city. Where the sound was thickest, touch would become just a little more distinct. A hand against the wrist would carry farther through the body. A shoulder pressed to a shoulder would communicate urgency without pain. Infants would be able to distinguish the steady rhythm of a parent from the surrounding tremor. The old would remain old, the hearing-impaired still vulnerable, the inattentive still capable of harm—but fewer people would be isolated by the basin’s noise.

This too would be mistaken for adaptation.

That would be acceptable. A gift that demanded worship would be less benevolent than one that could be folded into ordinary life.

The power would not abolish death. The mineral beds would continue to receive the bodies of the old. Children would still fail to grow. Groves would still wither. Walls would still collapse, though less often upon the living. There would still be grief, because grief was part of the price of having something worth keeping.

Nor would it end conflict.

It might, however, prevent one particular kind of foolishness. When a faction proposed opening the sealed chamber beneath the old settlement and extracting all its moisture at once, the first blow against the stone would release a vibration that every person in the city could feel. Not a command. Not a punishment. A memory of the basin’s weight.

The workers would stop.

The elders would come. Children would be carried from nearby rooms. For a long while, no one would speak. Then one of the youngest Keepers would place her palm against the sealed wall and realize that the chamber was not a reservoir but a balance: a place where roots, minerals, air, and time had entered into a slow agreement.

The plan would be abandoned.

Not because the power had forbidden it, but because it had made the consequences legible.

And perhaps, once in a great while, it would do something that could not be explained.

A single flower would open in a district where no seed had been planted.

It would be small, yellow, and nearly hidden beneath a slab. Its petals would remain closed to every touch except that of a person who had come there carrying grief. The flower would not cure the grief. It would not speak of the dead. It would simply remain open for one night, offering color where the city had almost none.

By morning it would be gone.

The person who found it might tell no one. Or they might tell a child, who would tell another child, until the story became part of the city’s unwritten knowledge: that sometimes the world noticed what was being carried.

That, more than any reservoir or warning, would be the deepest action of the benevolent power.

It would make the inhabitants feel—not constantly, not sentimentally, and never with certainty—that their endurance was witnessed.

It would leave their customs intact. It would not remove the dust from their clothes or the pressure from the ceiling. It would not turn their old basin into a garden of a different kind. It would place a few quiet protections inside the systems they had already built: a stronger seam, an earlier tremor, a more fertile seed, a clearer touch.

Then it would withdraw.

The people would continue scraping thresholds, tending mineral beds, repairing garments, guiding herds across the plain. They would continue to speak in gestures where the sound was too great, and sing through the paving stones where there was room for song. Their city would remain dry, crowded, patient, and old.

But it would no longer be merely surviving its conditions.

It would be carrying a future that had been made slightly more possible.

And because the power was wise, it would understand that this was enough.
