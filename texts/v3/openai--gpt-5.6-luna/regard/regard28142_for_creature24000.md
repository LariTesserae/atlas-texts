The power did not arrive as a figure.

A figure would have been too much pressure.

Had it appeared in the air, the ceiling might have opened toward it. Had it spoken aloud, roots would have torn themselves from the stone to listen. Had it taken a body, every living surface nearby would have leaned inward, trying to understand what kind of shelter it might become.

So the power began with restraint.

It noticed the White Green before it touched it. It watched the long migrations of forests, the sleepers rising in their silent processions, the walkers crossing the open plains with gardens on their backs. It watched the people repair their homes and then repair the repairs. It saw children taught not to stand still, the dead made into bridges, the frightened calm themselves so the roots would not hear them too sharply.

It understood that the White Green was not suffering because it was wild.

Wildness was not a wound.

Nor was the distance between things. The emptiness gave the walkers room to remain themselves. The slow growth gave the continent time to change without being consumed by its own abundance. The power saw that a world made entirely safe would become a world without choice, and that choice was one of the few possessions the inhabitants still carried freely.

So it did not tame the plants.

It did not lower the ceiling or give the hollow a sun. It did not make the forests obedient, or teach the dead to speak with the voices they had possessed in life. It did not gather the walkers into cities. It did not fill the plains with easy food or turn the mineral shelves soft beneath their feet.

Instead, it found the one cruelty woven through everything:

There was no way to say no.

A plant could close around a body because the body had slept. A path could become a root because it had been walked. A shelter could thicken around its inhabitant because warmth had been mistaken for permission. The White Green answered every pressure, but it could not distinguish invitation from accident, devotion from fear, or a life seeking shelter from a life being slowly absorbed.

The power decided that this was too much to ask of anyone.

Its first gift was almost invisible.

In a basin far from any dwelling, where the stone was black and the ceiling dim, a single point of darkness appeared in the living membrane overhead.

It was no larger than a hand.

The plants below reacted immediately. Pale stalks bent toward it. Root-mats tightened. The mineral plates clicked in concentric waves.

Then the darkness gave nothing back.

No warmth. No pressure. No electrical signal. No demand to be answered.

For the first time, the White Green encountered a presence that did not press upon it.

The darkness remained for one full day.

Then it vanished.

The next day it appeared somewhere else.

The walkers noticed before the plants did. They found the places by accident: a patch of ground where tendrils would not orient toward the body, where condensation gathered without being drawn away, where a person could sit and remain no more than a person.

They called these places Stillings.

At first, they were small. A circle of stone. A dry shelf beneath a root. A hollow in which nothing grew.

The walkers approached cautiously, expecting the gift to reveal a cost. They placed their hands against the ground and waited for the usual replies. Brightening. Trembling. The slow investigative curl of a tendril.

Nothing happened.

Some stayed only a minute. Some stayed until night, though there was no night below the ceiling. Some lay down and discovered that sleep did not encourage fibers to weave around their limbs. Children were the first to understand the significance. They went to the Stillings and stood in them, experimenting with immobility.

The plants did not punish them.

Nor did they retreat in alarm. They simply left a little space.

The Stillings spread across the White Green, not as a network imposed from above, but as an invitation repeated. Wherever a person rested without being taken, the surrounding life slowly learned the shape of restraint. A root might grow around a Still­ing, but never into it. A wall might rise beside it, but not across its entrance. Condensation might fall there, but it would remain long enough to be collected.

The power did not make the world harmless.

It made one kind of harm legible.

Soon the inhabitants discovered a second change. The White Green had acquired a new response.

When a living thing desired contact, the tissues around it brightened softly.

When it did not, they darkened.

Not completely. The world remained luminous. But every plant learned a difference between approach and refusal. The difference was weak at first, no more than a change in shade along a root. Then it became clearer.

A hand placed against a wall could receive an answer.

The wall might warm and open its pores.

Or it might cool and become smooth.

A traveler could stand before a thicket and wait. If the leaves turned toward them, they could pass. If the leaves turned away, they would search for another route. A person trapped beneath growing fibers could press twice against the ground. If the roots answered with light, they would release. If they did not, the people learned to cut them free without shame.

This did not stop the White Green from growing.

It stopped the inhabitants from having to interpret every danger as a moral failure.

The power’s third gift was given to the dead.

It did not restore them. Benevolence that returned every dead body would have made death another kind of captivity. The White Green needed its dead, and the dead had become bridges, basins, walls, and fields of light. To remove them would have been to erase the lives they had continued to support.

But the power placed a boundary around memory.

Whenever a body entered the root-mat, one small trace of the person remained separate from the growth. It might appear as a pattern of light in a bridge cord, a recurring pulse in a condensation fan, a particular rhythm in the clicking of a marker-stone. It could not speak. It could not advise the living. It could not be summoned for answers.

It could only be recognized.

A family might touch a wall made from their ancestor and feel, for a moment, the unmistakable pressure of being known.

Not possessed.

Not revived.

Known.

This changed the funerals. People still opened the bodies to the roots. They still waited for useful structures to emerge. But they began to leave a small unplanted stone beside the dead. The stone was not a grave marker. It was a promise that the person had been more than what the continent made of them.

The fourth gift was the most difficult.

The power gave the White Green a horizon.

Not a boundary. Not a wall. A horizon.

Until then, distance had been a wound in perception. A ridge might be near or impossibly far. A wall might be stone or a fold in the air. Travelers could lose entire seasons walking toward a feature that had never been where it seemed.

The power did not shorten the distances. It taught the brightness to reveal them.

When a walker looked toward a distant object with a clear intention to reach it, the ceiling above the object changed. A faint vertical veil appeared in the light, marking scale. Near things showed one veil. Far things showed many. Beyond a certain distance, the brightness became indistinct, not because the way was impossible, but because the White Green admitted uncertainty honestly.

The inhabitants learned to travel with less wasted life.

They still became lost. They still chose badly. Storms still moved forests across their routes. But the world no longer lied accidentally about how far away a danger or shelter might be.

The power left mistakes intact.

It left hunger intact, because hunger taught the walkers to cultivate.

It left grief intact, because grief was one of the ways the living continued to carry the dead.

It left the great storms intact, because a continent that could never be overturned would become a monument rather than a living place.

It even left the hunters intact, though it changed one thing about them: when a hunter opened its rib fans across a sleeper path, the sleepers gained the ability to refuse the vibration that drew them inward. Some still entered. Some still died. But no longer did every path become a trap simply because the hunter knew how to make the ground call.

The power did not choose who should live.

It made choosing possible.

At last, the inhabitants understood that something had changed. They gathered in a Still­ing beneath a dimmed portion of the ceiling. Walkers came from different regions, carrying gardens, tools, bones, and black stones. They arrived alone, because that was how they traveled, but they stood together in the circle and did not trigger a single tendril.

They tried to speak to the power.

They struck patterns into the stone. They held out their hands. They brought living tissue and mineral shards. They asked questions in the low, granular language meant to be felt.

The power gave no answer.

This troubled them. They had expected a god to explain itself, and wisdom, they knew, ought to be clear.

But the power had learned from the White Green.

An answer placed too heavily upon a living world became another pressure. A command, however benevolent, would eventually grow roots.

So it offered them no doctrine.

It gave them a final sign instead.

Far above, the pale ceiling opened—not split, not torn, but parted with the delicacy of a leaf making room for rain. Beyond it there was no sky and no face. There was only darkness, immense and calm, containing innumerable distant lights.

The inhabitants saw that the White Green was not sealed away from the rest of existence. It was held within something vaster, something that had been watching without claiming ownership.

Then the ceiling closed.

The Still­ing remained.

The people returned to their roads. They continued to carry shelters on their backs, to sleep behind woven screens, to cut growth from their thresholds. They still crossed the open plains quickly. They still listened to the roots before choosing a direction.

But now, when a child became frightened and the plants brightened around them, an elder could lead the child to a dark circle of stone.

There, the child could be afraid without summoning the world inward.

There, a traveler could rest without becoming a landmark.

There, the dead could be remembered as people before they became bridges.

And sometimes, far beyond sight, the ceiling would flare blue-white.

The plants would tighten. Mineral plates would click. A forest would begin to rise from the floor.

The White Green would continue making itself.

But now, within its vast responsive body, it had learned one more answer.

No.
