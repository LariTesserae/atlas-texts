The benevolent power does not enter the cleft as a figure.

There is no descent of wings through the narrow opening, no hand laid upon the black walls, no voice attempting to compete with the vents. Nothing is crowned, named, or claimed. The mountain is too young for monuments, and the colony has no language in which a command could be received.

Instead, the power attends.

It attends to the cleft as one might attend to a wound that has become a life: not by closing it, but by learning its depth. It feels the pressure building beneath the mineral collars. It feels the roots tightening around unstable shelves. It follows every temporary stream from the gray opening above to the dark throat below. It understands that the violence here is not an evil to be removed. The mountain is not failing. The colony is not trapped. Their danger is also their nourishment.

So the power does not make the stone old.

It does not smooth the blades, warm the air, summon sunlight to the floor, or fill the darkness with harmless animals. It does not give the organism flowers merely because flowers are beautiful elsewhere. It does not turn the cleft into a garden, because the cleft has never asked to become one.

It changes only what is needlessly final.

The first gift is a little more time.

When a shelf begins to separate, the power does not hold it forever. It gives the roots and mineral tubes a few additional breaths in which to respond. A crack that would have opened all at once opens in stages. A wall that would have collapsed completely loses only part of itself. The falling stone still falls, but no longer with the absolute indifference of a blade.

The colony senses this before it understands anything—if understanding is even possible for such a life.

Pressure accumulates along a familiar fault. Fronds lift. Bladders close. Rootlets draw tight. The rock trembles.

Then, instead of rupture, there is a slow settling.

The shelf shifts a finger’s width and remains.

Across the cleft, the organism discovers that not every signal of danger must end in destruction. It begins to develop new responses: not merely tightening and bursting, but redistribution. Roots thicken in some places and loosen in others. Mineral tubes bend around stress instead of sealing it. Moss grows in layered bands that can absorb the shock of falling water. The colony does not become intelligent in the manner of an animal. It becomes more capable of continuation.

The power gives it no mind.

It gives it margin.

Where water gathers behind a blockage, the power opens a hairline passage—not a channel large enough to tame the system, only enough to let pressure escape before it becomes catastrophic. The passage becomes lined with root and mineral. It remains afterward, a new living pore in the mountain.

Where steam floods the lower cleft too densely, the power cools one narrow wall by the smallest degree. Condensation forms there. The colony spreads over it. A curtain of growth descends, catches droplets, and draws them sideways into the upper seams. The cleft remains wet and loud, but now some water lingers after the storm. Not much. Never enough to make the place easy. Enough that the youngest threads do not all die between one cloud and the next.

The power does not prevent death.

It prevents death from becoming the only teacher.

It also gives the colony a kind of memory.

Not a memory made of images, and not a central record hidden in the oldest roots. Rather, each successful repair leaves a minute alteration in the tissues that performed it. A root that held a ledge remembers how much pressure it endured. A bladder that survived the vent’s heat closes more quickly next time. A mineral tube that yielded without breaking retains the pattern of its yielding in the shape of its walls.

Over ages too brief to be called ages, the cleft begins to accumulate experience.

The organism becomes able to distinguish, in its distributed way, between a rupture that will expose rich new ground and one that will wash the living surfaces into the throat below. It still reinforces some blockages. It still allows some shelves to fall. But it no longer responds to every threat with the same blind embrace.

Its growth becomes selective.

That is the second gift: discernment without domination.

The power is careful here. It does not make the organism protective of itself in the manner of fear. It does not teach it to cling to every old surface or resist every change. A benevolent power does not confuse preservation with goodness.

The colony continues to release spores into the vents. It continues to offer portions of itself to the downward water. It continues to die in black floods and return on fresh stone. But its losses begin to nourish more reliably. Fallen mats lodge in the lower throat instead of being carried entirely away. Spores collect in warm mineral dust. Root fragments establish themselves on the underside of collapsed shelves, where previously nothing survived.

The cleft becomes not safer, exactly, but less wasteful.

Then the power turns its attention upward.

The opening above is narrow, and it will remain narrow. The place does not need noon. Yet once in a great while, when cloud and angle and season align, a thread of direct light is allowed to enter—not a flood, not a transformation, only a brief pale blade reaching farther down than before.

The first time it happens, the colony does not recognize it as nourishment.

The light touches wet fronds and travels through them as heat. Bladders contract. Moss pales. The pale grasses bend away from it. For several moments the cleft is disturbed by something more unfamiliar than tremor.

Then the light is gone.

Nothing is immediately gained. But within the tissues that received it, a dormant chemistry stirs. The colony acquires a new possibility: not dependence on light, but use of it. The brief illumination becomes another gradient, another boundary to inhabit. In later seasons, some growths spread toward the upper opening. Others remain deep below. The organism divides its labor without dividing its body.

A few fronds become faintly reflective, not luminous but able to hold the gray pallor a little longer after the clouds pass. Pools retain a diluted brightness. In the deep cleft, there are moments when the wet rock seems to remember the sky.

The power does not make the place beautiful according to an outside measure.

It allows the place to reveal that it already possesses a beauty of its own.

No flowers appear. No birds arrive. No creature is introduced merely to make the scene feel inhabited. If, much later, a small blind thing emerges from a crack—fed by the colony, warmed by the vents, moving through the dark—it will arrive because the conditions have become capable of receiving it, not because the power has decorated the cleft with company.

And if nothing ever comes, that too is accepted.

The power’s benevolence is not a plan imposed on the place. It is a refusal to mistake solitude for deficiency.

At last, the power does one thing more.

It gives the colony a boundary—not a wall, but a center of persistence.

Deep around the oldest vents, beneath the mineral collars, a tissue forms that will not be easily erased. It is not invulnerable. Even this can crack, cool, and die. But when the outer curtains are stripped away, when steam boils the moss and floods carry the roots downward, this inner tissue remains dormant rather than destroyed.

It waits.

Not consciously. Not anxiously. It simply retains the conditions from which return is possible: spores, threads, mineral-binding cells, stored water, and the chemical memory of the cleft’s particular pressures.

The colony will never have to begin from nothing again.

That is the deepest gift: not immortality, but continuity.

After the power has done all it intends to do, the cleft looks almost unchanged.

The walls are still black and angular. The vents still roar. Water still arrives in violent intervals. Shelves still collapse. Steam still turns the lower chamber blind. The organism still spreads through every damp seam, binding itself to the mountain and being torn from it.

Only the pattern has altered.

A rupture becomes a widening. A collapse becomes a redistribution. A dry interval becomes dormancy rather than annihilation. The living network learns, in its root-deep and mineral-bound manner, that the mountain’s violence is not a verdict.

And the power remains.

Not as a guardian standing watch, because there is nothing here that requires watching in that sense. Not as a ruler issuing corrections. It remains as a patient attention woven into the intervals between events: in the extra breath before the shelf gives way, in the hairline passage that releases pressure, in the root that remembers how to bend, in the hidden tissue that waits beneath the ruin.

The cleft is still a wound.

It is still a workshop.

It is still unfinished.

But now, within its darkness, unfinished does not mean abandoned.
