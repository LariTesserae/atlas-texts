### The Attention of the Quiet Power

The power did not descend as a figure.

No light opened in the black sky. No voice crossed the ridges. The storms did not stop, and the cliffs did not settle into safety. The Rootbound would have distrusted any wonder that arrived so loudly. Loud things in the highland were usually falling, breaking, hunting, or dying.

Instead, the power noticed.

It noticed the plants first.

It noticed how much of the forest was spent holding the world together: roots gripping cliffs, mats sealing cracks, stalks drinking mist, colonies closing themselves around the wounds made by weather and animals. It noticed that the plants gave more than they received, and that their remembering was not cruelty but survival. A colony that remembered every injury exactly would become rigid and die. A colony that forgot everything would be consumed.

So the power altered the balance by less than a thought.

From then on, when the living crust was damaged by accident, it healed a little faster. Not enough to make care unnecessary. Not enough to protect the careless from consequence. But a frightened child would no longer leave a footprint that took a season to close. A storm-broken bridge would send up new fibers before the old ones had fully decayed. A plant cut according to the old signs would recognize the request—not as language, exactly, but as a pattern of harmlessness.

The Rootbound noticed this only gradually.

A grandmother found that a cracked condensation bulb had filled overnight. A group of travelers discovered pale roots growing toward the path they had taken, marking the safer stones. A settlement that had been preparing to leave because its colony was failing woke one morning to find new shoots spread beneath the shelter, drawing water from a seam in the rock no one had known was there.

They called these events fortunate.

The power did not correct them.

It watched the people next.

It saw how they had shaped themselves around danger until caution had become almost indistinguishable from fear. It saw the children learning to listen for the collapse beneath the ground, to sleep lightly, to leave without looking back. It saw their tenderness—small, private, rarely spoken. A hand extended across a dark shelter. A better leaf offered to the oldest person. A warning struck into a stalk for strangers who would never know who had placed it.

The power did not make the Rootbound strong enough to ignore the mountain.

Strength without understanding would only make them bolder, and boldness in the highland was often another name for hunger.

Instead, it gave them one new sense.

Not sight. Not hearing. Something quieter.

When the living crust beneath them was stable, their feet felt warmth—not heat, but a faint inward ease. When the ground was about to split, or a root had begun to contract, or a buried animal was moving beneath the mat, that ease withdrew. The warning came sometimes seconds before danger, sometimes hours. It was never exact. It did not tell them what would happen, only that the world had ceased to be trustworthy in that place.

The first person to feel it thought she was ill.

The second believed a spirit had touched her.

The third saved a child by stepping backward from a shelf moments before the rock fell away.

After that, the knowledge passed from body to body through teaching, demonstration, and trust. No one agreed on its name. Some called it the ground’s reluctance. Some called it the inward foot. The oldest people said only that the mountain had begun to speak more clearly.

The power did not give this sense to everyone at once.

It gave it to those who would use it to protect another person before themselves.

This was not punishment. It was simply the shape of the gift.

The mountain remained dangerous. The new sense could be ignored, misunderstood, or overwhelmed by storms. Predators still waited beneath ledges. Root curtains still froze. People still fell. But fewer died from dangers that had offered no warning at all.

Then the power turned toward the weather.

It could have drawn the storms away. It could have warmed the highland, filled every basin with water, and softened every cliff into a gentle slope.

But the forest above the weather was an ecosystem of severity. Remove the cold and the plants would lose their discipline. Remove the storms and the colonies would spread until they smothered one another. Fill every hollow with water and the careful distances between living things would collapse. The highland would become abundant, and abundance would become another form of death.

So the power did not lessen the storms.

It gave them memory.

After a great front crossed the plateau, the next storm would remember the broken places. Winds would divide around the most fragile stalk forests. Ice would fall more thickly into empty basins and less heavily upon shelters. A storm would still destroy what had to be destroyed, but it would no longer destroy the same thing merely because it had already been weakened.

The Rootbound began to discover that the weather could be prepared for in a new way.

When a storm was coming, some plant colonies bent before the first wind arrived. Certain hollow stems began to sound a low warning several hours early. Condensation gathered in sheltered roots. Old paths that had once vanished beneath snow remained faintly visible as green lines under the ice.

No one commanded these changes.

The plants had simply become better at preserving what was capable of continuing.

The greatest gift came last.

It was given neither to the people nor to the plants, but to the space between them.

For generations, the Rootbound had feared the approaching plants. They had seen colonies extend themselves toward shelters, close pathways, and turn all their leaves toward human movement. Sometimes the plants had been seeking water. Sometimes warmth. Sometimes the bodies of the dead.

Sometimes they had been growing toward the sound of people.

The power did not make the plants tame. It did not make them friendly. Friendship was too small a word for what the forest was, and tameness would have been another kind of death.

Instead, it made intention legible.

A plant that was closing around a person in hunger felt different from one closing around a person in protection. A spreading mat that wanted to reclaim a settlement could be distinguished from one spreading toward a source of water. The difference did not appear in the eyes or in the leaves. It arrived as certainty, sudden and undeniable, in the minds of those nearby.

The Rootbound learned that some plants were not approaching them at all.

They were approaching one another.

They learned that the great colonies had been fighting slow wars over minerals, water, and growing space. They learned that certain paths were opened not as traps but as invitations. They learned that a plant could remember being touched without remembering the hand.

And the plants learned something in return.

They learned that not every human footfall was damage.

Not every cut was hunger.

Not every fire meant destruction.

The Rootbound began leaving more deliberate signs. Not only warnings, but promises: a braid of living fiber laid beside a spring, a pattern of three shallow cuts, a patch of untouched leaves. The plants answered in their own ways. A root withdrew from a doorway. A stalk leaned aside to make room for a traveler. A colony that had once sealed a basin opened a narrow channel so a thirsty group could reach the water without crossing its center.

There were still misunderstandings.

There were still deaths.

The power did not erase tragedy, because a world without loss would have required the removal of change, and change was the first condition of life. It did not make every choice correct. It did not prevent the Rootbound from being greedy, or frightened, or cruel. It did not prevent the plants from competing, spreading, or defending themselves.

What it changed was the cost of ignorance.

Over many generations, the Rootbound became less a people surviving against the forest and more a people living within its decisions. Their routes widened—not into roads, but into agreements. Their shelters grew more stable. Their burial chambers became places where new growth was encouraged rather than merely awaited.

The old warning posts changed as well.

Once they had carved predators, storms, and stretched human faces into stone. Now they carved pairs of forms: a hand beside a root, a body beneath a sheltering leaf, two paths dividing around a pool.

No one knew who had begun the practice.

No one remembered the first season in which the power had turned its attention upon them.

The Rootbound continued to call themselves Rootbound. They remained narrow, cautious, cold-adapted, and ready to leave any home that ceased to be safe. They still listened to the booming stalks. They still covered their faces in the spore fields. They still sang when the thaw began.

But sometimes, in the deepest part of the night, when the forest’s hollow towers sounded together and the green light trembled beneath the living crust, every plant in a valley would turn—not toward the people, and not toward the sky, but toward the same distant point beyond the ridges.

The Rootbound felt warmth in their feet.

The plants opened their leaves.

For a little while, nothing hunted. Nothing fell. Nothing approached.

The highland simply held itself together.

And the benevolent power, having done what it could without taking the world away from those who lived in it, continued to listen.
