The benevolent power does not descend upon the plateau.

It does not split the sky, speak from the towers, or place a hand upon the people’s heads. Such gestures would be too small for the country and too loud for those who have learned to listen.

Instead, it waits.

It watches the shelves of stone receive rain and give it back. It watches the towers collapse, root again, and become bridges. It watches the people take only what can be taken without breaking the chain that sustains them. It sees their dead lowered into the root-floor, not as offerings and not as losses, but as bodies returned to the long circulation of things.

The power recognizes that this place is not in need of salvation.

So it does not save it from change.

When a tower falls, it does not hold the tower upright. When a predator takes a child of the herd, it does not intervene. When a family abandons a shelter to the wind, it does not preserve the shelter for them. The power understands that to prevent every ending would be to prevent the country from becoming what it is.

But there comes a year in which the clouds remain below the rim.

They gather each morning in a white inland sea, shining beneath the plateau, and each morning they sink again without rising. The rain does not come. The pools contract. The plant-mat loses its sheen. The herds alter their routes and begin crossing shelves the people have never seen them use. Even the great towers grow quiet. Their inner dripping slows, then stops.

The interval-keepers mark the drought in rust and bone-yellow. They ration water. They move the children into the deepest root passages. They burn almost nothing. They wait for the weather to remember them.

The power does not bring rain.

Instead, on the forty-third morning, it changes one thing.

A child walking behind an elder notices that the wind is striking a wall of stone and returning with a different sound. Not louder. Not softer. Deeper. The child presses a palm to the ground.

There is water below.

The old inhabitants had passed that place for centuries. They had known the shelf as dry, had used its shallow caves for shade, had stacked stones there to mark a dangerous descent. But the plateau had shifted in some invisible way. A buried root had split. A channel had opened beneath the rock. Far below, where the night’s cold reached into a hidden fracture, condensation had gathered without evaporating.

The child hears it.

Not as a voice. As a difference in the silence.

The people dig with their hands. They uncover a root as thick as a body, pale and tense beneath the stone. It has grown toward the buried moisture and sealed itself around a narrow chamber. When they cut the root—not deeply, not enough to kill the colony—a thread of water emerges.

They do not cheer. They do not call it a miracle.

The first water goes to the roots.

The second is carried to the youngest children.

The third is poured into a basin and left beneath the open sky.

After that, the people change their routes. They learn the new interval. They teach the children to listen for the returning note in the stone. They add no monument, because a monument would declare the place conquered or possessed. They simply stack three flat stones near the fracture, and then, when the growing mat begins to cover them, they leave it to do so.

The power remains hidden.

It does not make the drought shorter. It does not guarantee that the channel will flow next year. It does not spare every creature. Its gift is not abundance, but possibility: one small opening through which the living may continue to choose.

And because the power is wise, it also gives nothing to itself.

It asks no worship. It does not require the people to name it. It does not turn their attention away from the towers, the herds, the wind, or one another. If they sense its presence, they understand it as they understand the plateau: not as a ruler, but as a condition within which life can remain alive.

At the end of the drought, when rain finally crosses the shelves, the towers begin to drip again.

The people stand beneath them, wet with condensation and stormwater. Their voices pass through the hollow wood and return altered. Somewhere above, the great scavenging birds wheel in the bright air. Somewhere below, roots enter the bones of the dead.

The power listens.

And for once, it is satisfied to have done almost nothing.
