The benevolent power would not descend upon the plain like a hand upon a wound.

There is no wound here that asks to be closed.

The heat is harsh, but it belongs to the place. The floods destroy nests and fill burrows, but they also open eggs, refill channels, and carry life into hollows that would otherwise become dust. Predators kill. Roots strangle roots. Trees fall. Fish are trapped in shrinking pools. These are not errors in the design. They are the movements by which the plain remains alive.

So the power would begin by refusing to confuse suffering with failure.

It would listen.

It would listen beneath the insect-noise, beneath the breathing of leaves, beneath the low vibration carried through the ground from distant machines. It would listen to the water held in the clay and the small bodies sealed inside it. It would learn the timing of rain, the depth of the old channels, the limits of the roots, the routes of the herds, the places where salt was beginning to gather. It would listen not for a single voice, because there is no single voice, but for the shape made by all of them together.

And because it was wise, it would understand that the multitude already knows much of what it needs.

It would not make the predators gentle. A predator without hunger would be no kindness to the plain; it would be a disruption wearing a merciful face. It would not command the reptiles to leave the pools, or the fish to escape every drought, or the trees to stand forever. It would not grant every creature a long life. A life that could never end would deny the insects their abundance, the fungi their work, the roots their nourishment, and the dead their return.

It would leave death where death belonged.

But it would place a boundary around what should not enter.

Beyond the horizon, where pumps and towers worked under artificial light, the power would notice the oily scent and the metallic taste carried by certain winds. It would trace them back through the air, through the soil, through the old sediment, and into the systems that produced them. It would not punish the distant people merely for needing heat, water, food, or shelter. It would not turn machinery into ruin for the sake of purity. Instead, it would make the cost of carelessness impossible to ignore.

A seal would fail before a poison reached the channels.

A warning gauge would rise in time to be believed.

A pipe would corrode harmlessly rather than split into the groundwater.

A worker would stop for one minute, uncertain why, and look again at a reading that would otherwise have been missed.

A decision made in a distant room would acquire one additional fact: the plain was there.

Not an empty region. Not a waste. Not a place where damage could disappear.

A living presence.

The power would not make the plain invulnerable. A flood would still uproot trees. A season would still come late. A pool would still dry before the fish were ready. But it would prevent an injury from becoming a new law of the land. It would keep a spill from becoming a permanent inheritance. It would ensure that what was taken from the plain could, in some measure, be returned.

Where the water had begun to fail beyond its natural rhythm, the power might draw a little more rain toward the upper country—not enough to abolish the dry season, only enough to keep the deepest chambers from sealing shut. A storm would break over the canopy, and the rain would descend for hours through leaves and branches. Some would be caught in waxy folds. Some would strike the roots. Some would run into the old channels and remain there, dark and cool beneath the vegetation.

Where the water had become too abundant, it would not hold back the flood entirely. It would give the water somewhere to go. A forgotten channel would deepen by inches. A bank would collapse at the right moment. A fallen tree would turn the current aside. The flood would still change the forest, but it would not drown the entire inheritance of one season.

The power would make no mark that could be worshiped.

No pillar would rise. No light would hover above the trees. No creature would look up and receive a command.

Instead, a root would find moisture where it needed it.

A nest would remain just above the highest water.

A herd would alter its route by the width of a body and avoid a concealed sinkhole.

A young animal, having strayed too near a hunter, would hear the faintest crack of a branch and turn.

The power would not save every creature. That would be impossible without destroying the meaning of danger. But it might save the one whose survival preserved a line of memory: the old female who knew where the channels joined; the tree whose hollow sheltered five kinds of life; the colony whose movement carried seeds across the plain. Not because those lives were more worthy than others, but because the whole required certain continuities to remain unbroken.

It would protect thresholds.

The temperature would still rise in the day, but not beyond what the vegetation could bear. The dry season would still come, but the underground chambers would retain a little more coolness. The salt would still shine in the empty channels, but it would not advance so quickly that roots lost their ancient routes to fresh water.

And it would grant the plain one gift that looked, from outside, like nothing at all:

time.

Time for fallen trunks to become soil.

Time for a population to recover after a hard season.

Time for a new channel to settle into its bed.

Time for a species pressed to the edge to change its nesting place, its feeding hour, its route through the reeds.

Time for distant minds to understand that benevolence was not ownership.

If there were a single being within the multitude—some vast animal, some ancient intelligence beneath the roots, some consciousness formed from the plain’s innumerable lives—the power would not speak to it in words. Words would be too narrow, and instruction would turn relationship into obedience.

It would give the being recognition.

Not a throne. Not dominion. Not exemption from hunger or decay.

Recognition that its awareness, however distributed, was real.

The being would feel the plain more clearly: the movement below the silt, the pressure of water gathering far upriver, the gradual weakening of a poisoned wind. It would understand that it was not alone because nothing here was alone. The insects, reeds, fish, fungi, hunters, roots, and buried eggs were not merely parts of it; they were neighbors within the same living thought.

The power would perhaps make one thing certain to that being:

You do not have to preserve everything unchanged in order to be preserved.

The forest could still close over clearings. Species could still disappear from one channel and appear in another. Old bodies could still become shelter. New forms could still arise from alteration. The plain would remain a process, not a monument.

But no distant ambition would be allowed to reduce it to silence.

No machine would be permitted to drain every hidden pool.

No road would divide the breeding grounds without consequence.

No careless hand would remove so much that the multitude could no longer become itself again.

The power would stand, not above the plain, but at its margins—where water met dry ground, where wilderness met industry, where one season became another. It would apply pressure only where pressure was needed, withdraw when the balance returned, and leave no signature except continued possibility.

Then, having done this, it would become still.

The night would resume its darkness.

Something large would enter a pool and stop. Insects would rise from the banks. A reptile would open its eyes. Beneath the mud, blind bodies would turn toward a root. Far away, a machine would continue to hum, but its waste would be contained, its reach diminished, its danger no longer invisible.

The plain would not know that it had been spared.

That would be part of the mercy.

It would remain hot, crowded, predatory, fertile, and difficult to read. Trees would fall. Channels would wander. The dead would be occupied again and again. The multitude would continue behaving like one—not obedient to a guardian, but allowed to remain itself.

And the benevolent power, wise enough not to improve what was already whole, would do only what was necessary to keep the plain from being made smaller than its nature.
