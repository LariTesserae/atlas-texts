The power would not arrive in the form of a voice.

A voice would be too narrow. It would belong to one throat, one language, one species, and the basin had long ago become larger than any single manner of speaking. Nor would the power descend visibly into the open courts. Such an arrival would draw every eye, disturb every nesting place, and teach the inhabitants to wait for intervention instead of inhabiting their own world.

It would begin with attention.

Not the attention of an observer, but the deeper attention by which a thing is known in all its relations: the weight of a roof upon a wall, the pressure of a root in a stair joint, the distance a thirsty animal must travel between two cisterns, the number of eggs that failed in a dry season, the routes by which water escaped before reaching the lowest gardens.

The power would learn the basin without dividing it into parts.

It would understand that the old city was not merely a shelter for life. It was a set of promises made in stone. Water had been directed toward thirst. Heat had been stored for cold hours. Slopes had been made traversable. Dangerous descents had been interrupted by steps. The builders had not mastered the mountain, exactly, but had persuaded it to hold certain shapes for a while.

So the power would restore the promises that still served life.

The first changes would be beneath the ground.

The buried channels would open where they had become clogged, not by sudden violence but by a soft loosening of silt and root. Stones would settle into their original grooves. Clay would swell in the cracks of the old cisterns and seal them. Where a reservoir had lost its inner lining, a thin mineral skin would form along the walls, translucent at first, then darkening until it could no longer be distinguished from the ancient masonry.

The rain would not become constant. Constant rain would alter the basin as surely as drought. Instead, the brief storms would become more generous. A cloud that once passed without breaking would pause over the ridges. A hard shower would divide itself into several smaller falls, each arriving at the right interval to soak the upper terraces without stripping them bare. More water would reach the cisterns. Less would flee uselessly through the lowest channels.

No creature would experience this as a miracle.

A frog would simply find that the shallow pool beside its usual stone remained wet two days longer. A bird would discover that the flowering shrubs produced fruit after a season in which they should not have. The wall-dwellers would begin to carry their young into chambers that had once been abandoned as too dry. The change would enter the basin through habit.

The power would also touch the walls.

It would not rebuild them into clean monuments. That would be a kind of destruction: to erase the accumulated shape of time and replace it with an idea of permanence. The leaning walls would remain leaning. Broken towers would remain broken. Roots would continue to grow through courtyards, and rain would continue to stain the stones.

But where collapse would kill without nourishing anything—where a high parapet threatened to fall into a nesting court, where a stair gave way above a reservoir, where a roof was poised to crush a den—the stones would remember their neighbors. They would bear one another’s weight more patiently. A crack would stop widening. A loosened lintel would settle into a safer angle. A bridge too fragile for passage would lower itself gently, becoming a bank rather than a trap.

The power would not prevent ruin. It would teach ruin to be merciful.

It would also keep the inhabitants from becoming victims of their own abundance.

The city was generous, but its generosity was uneven. Some cisterns teemed while others dried to mineral crust. Some courtyards held more nests than the surrounding walls could support. A single species might briefly multiply until the gardens were stripped, the insects diminished, and the next season became a famine.

The power would not impose peace among them. Predation would remain. Hunger would remain. A snake would still take a frog; a bird would still take a lizard; a storm would still destroy a nest.

But the extremes would soften.

When one population grew too dense, disease would be less cruel and more instructive. When food failed in one quarter, seeds and insects would appear along old routes to neighboring quarters. The fruiting plants would spread not everywhere, but through the passages most easily traveled by the animals that depended on them. The basin would become more connected without becoming uniform.

The small wall-dwellers would be the first to notice.

Their remembered routes would begin to overlap. A den in one quarter would lead, through a sequence of warm cavities, to another quarter that had previously seemed unreachable. They would carry seeds farther. They would encounter unfamiliar families and hesitate at the borders of old territories. In time, the families would not merge exactly, but their movements would become less isolated.

The city would acquire something like a nervous system.

Not a mind placed above its inhabitants, giving commands, but a widening sensitivity among them. The frogs would respond to distant water. The birds would alter their nesting when a tower became unstable. Animals that had never shared a space would begin to recognize the same warning vibrations in the masonry.

The power would make danger legible before it became disaster.

A wall would creak long before it fell. A cistern contaminated by decay would acquire a bitter scent. A stretch of unstable roof would shed a particular dust. These signs would not be messages in words, but the inhabitants would learn them as they learned thirst, darkness, and rain.

And for the city itself, the power would give no face.

It would give memory.

Not the memory of its builders as individuals, nor a preserved account of every hand that shaped the stone, but a memory of function: which channels once connected, which stairs led safely upward, which courts had been designed to collect runoff, which chambers held warmth through the coldest nights.

This memory would move through the living things.

A bird might carry a twig to a tower because the tower remained the best place for a nest, though no bird had nested there for generations. A mammal might dig beside a buried drain and expose it. Roots would follow old irrigation lines as if recognizing a forgotten invitation. After rain, water would once again choose paths no living creature had consciously known.

The ancient order would return—not restored, but remembered.

The power would be tempted, perhaps, to awaken the city fully. To give it speech. To let it gather its many sounds into one deliberate utterance and tell the surrounding world that it endured.

But wisdom would refuse this.

A single will, however benevolent, would make the basin less free. The frogs would become instruments of its thought. The birds would become messengers. Every animal would be reduced to a part of a grander self. The city would no longer be a community of lives inhabiting one another’s consequences; it would be a creature with organs.

That would be elegant.

It would also be a diminishment.

So the power would remain distributed, as the life of the basin was distributed. It would act only where no action was needed to be understood. It would strengthen what was already tending toward survival, repair what still possessed a purpose, and leave untouched whatever was being changed by the ordinary intelligence of living things.

At the end of its attention, the basin would not shine.

There would be no crown of light above the towers, no golden mark upon the central court. The darkness would remain architectural. The wet stones would still reveal only fragments. The bats would still pour from the bridges at dusk, and the birds would still make the roofs tremble.

But the next dry season would end a little sooner.

A stair would remain passable for another century.

A nest would survive the fall of a gallery because the stones shifted aside instead of downward.

In a cistern deep beneath a collapsed house, water would stay cool and clean. Small animals would drink there, then carry seeds into the walls. Those seeds would take root in places where roots had once seemed impossible.

And somewhere, beneath the old paving, a channel would begin to run.

Nothing would hear the power say that it had been saved.

The basin would simply continue—less fragile, no less wild; more capable of change, no less ancient.

Its inhabitants would mistake the gift for luck, then for instinct, then for the way things had always been.

That would be enough.

Because the benevolent power had not come to be worshiped by the city, nor to make the city eternal. It had come because something living had been seen clearly.

And having seen it, the power had chosen not to possess it.

It had chosen to help it remain itself.
