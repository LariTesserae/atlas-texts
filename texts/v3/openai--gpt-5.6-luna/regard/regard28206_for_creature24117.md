The benevolent power did not descend upon the plateau.

It did not take a shape that could be worshiped, or speak from the clouds, or place its hand upon the city and make the stone obedient. It understood, before anything else, that these people had already learned the difference between help and interference.

They did not need to be rescued from the plateau. They needed the plateau to remain legible.

So the power watched.

It watched the water-keepers taste the cisterns. It watched children carry pegs along ledges, and herders wait for a frightened animal to choose its own footing. It watched old people press their palms against walls and feel, through layers of plaster and stone, the beginning of a shift.

It saw how much of their wisdom consisted of noticing what had changed.

And because it was wise, it did not give them certainty.

Certainty would have been dangerous. A people who knew exactly when the rains would come might empty their basins too soon. A people who knew which walls would stand might stop marking foundations. A people who believed the plateau had become safe would begin to build as if it were finished.

Instead, the power gave them better warnings.

The first came as a change in the birds.

For three days, the cliff-nesting birds refused to settle on the eastern parapets. They circled the district in widening loops, then flew toward the lower terraces and crowded beneath the bridges. The people noticed. They always noticed animals before they noticed fear.

The watchers inspected the escarpment. The quarry-workers tapped the retaining walls. Nothing seemed wrong.

On the fourth night, a tremor passed beneath the city.

It was not large. Cups did not fall. Fires did not go out. But a buried street shifted by the width of a finger, and a wall that had appeared sound opened along a seam from foundation to roof.

The district was evacuated before dawn.

By midday, a whole shelf of the eastern plateau had fallen away. Stone descended through the fog for a long time. The collapse opened a new face in the cliff and exposed, beneath the old foundations, a narrow vein of pale mineral and a chamber where rainwater had been collecting unseen.

The people did not call this a miracle.

They called it a warning that arrived in time.

They rebuilt farther from the edge. They altered the bird ledges. They made a public rule that no one would again dismiss a change in nesting behavior merely because no crack could yet be found.

The power did not object to receiving no credit.

After that, its gifts remained small.

A cloud lingered over a dry district for one hour longer than it should have, allowing a neglected cistern to fill.

A seam of softer stone appeared beneath a quarry face, making the removal of a dangerous block possible without blasting or collapse.

A child, wandering after a herd, found a crack that breathed cool air. Behind it lay an underground pocket of water—not enough to transform the city, but enough to sustain a new shelter on the western road.

The power never made the water abundant. It made it discoverable.

It never softened the stone. It taught hands where to strike.

It never stopped the fog. It caused the bells to be hung at intervals that made direction possible even when sight failed.

Sometimes it worked through dreams. The dreams were not prophecies, exactly. A water-keeper might dream of a basin overflowing from the wrong side. A mason might dream of a foundation marked with a name she did not recognize. A herder might wake with the certain knowledge that a particular animal should not cross a particular bridge.

Most dreams were ignored. Some were wrong. The power accepted this. A warning that could not be doubted would become a command, and commands weaken judgment.

The most important thing it gave them was not an answer, but a habit.

People began to pause more often.

Before removing a foundation stone, they listened beneath it.

Before redirecting a channel, they walked its full length.

Before sealing a burrow, they asked what had been living there and what might move in if it vanished.

Before building over an old street, they uncovered enough of it to learn where it had once led.

This made the city slower. It also made it gentler.

A few generations passed before anyone noticed that the number of deaths from collapse had fallen. The change was not dramatic. A wall still failed. A road still vanished in scree. Children still slipped, animals still broke legs, and storms still arrived without sufficient warning.

But more often, the dangerous place had been marked.

More often, a person who would have been alone was found before dark.

More often, a cistern remained half full because someone had remembered that the next condensation might be late.

The power also attended to the dead.

It did not return them. It did not answer the offerings lowered into burial chambers. It understood that grief requires an absence to remain an absence.

But beneath one old district, where generations of houses had been built over the same chambers, it strengthened the stone around the graves. The living continued to feel the faint settling and shifting of the plateau, but the oldest rooms did not collapse. The names carved into their walls remained readable.

In time, the people began to say that the dead were holding the city up.

This was not literally true.

It was not entirely false.

The power’s greatest act was to refuse to separate survival from meaning. It made no chosen monarch, founded no temple, and selected no prophet. It did not tell the people that they had been favored above all others.

Instead, it made their existing forms of care slightly more effective.

A repaired strap lasted through one more crossing.

A signal fire remained visible through a bank of cloud.

A frightened animal stopped before a rotten bridge.

A lonely watcher heard, from far below, the answering bell of a district that had not forgotten them.

And once, many years after the first collapse, the power came close enough to be perceived.

A woman stood alone on an upper road at dusk. She was carrying a bundle of fuel and had stopped to look across the plateau. The light had turned the basalt blue. Fog filled the lower streets. Far away, signal fires burned in a chain toward other settlements.

She felt, without hearing anything, that the whole inhabited land was being regarded.

Not judged.

Not claimed.

Regarded with an attention so complete that every fragile thing within it seemed, for one instant, to possess a weight beyond its size: a child’s hand on a rope, a bird’s claw gripping a wall, a thin stream entering a basin, a maker’s mark hidden beneath a foundation.

The feeling passed.

The woman continued home.

She did not speak of it, because there was no useful way to do so. Instead, when she reached her district, she noticed that a roof rope had loosened in the wind. She climbed the stairs, tightened it, and left an extra length coiled beside the anchor.

That was how the power changed the plateau.

Not by making it permanent.

By making attention itself a little stronger.

And when, beneath the city, the immense buried movements of the earth continued—when stone shifted in the dark, when water searched for new paths, when the unfinished continent rose by increments too slow for any living eye—the people remained what they had always been:

small, deliberate, connected.

Listening beneath the floor.

Leaving room for the runoff.

Marking the trustworthy stone.

And, sometimes, noticing that the world was listening back.
