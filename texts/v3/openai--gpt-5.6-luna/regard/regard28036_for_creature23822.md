The benevolent power would not finish the plateau.

It would understand, at once, that unfinishedness was not a flaw here. The rising shelves, the uncertain rivers, the vents opening and closing beneath the stone—these were not failures awaiting correction. They were the plateau’s way of becoming itself.

So the power would not still the ground. It would not lower the winds, fill the ravines, or lay a permanent road between the grazing shelves. It would not make the weather kind in the manner of lowlands. Kindness, here, could not mean the removal of danger. Too much protection would be another form of destruction. It would turn a living place into a preserved one.

Instead, the power would listen.

It would listen through the hooves of the shelf-grazers, through the hollow bones of the cliff birds, through the whiskers of the pale animals beneath the stone. It would listen to the water deciding where to go, to the mineral crusts forming around the vents, to the deep pressure moving through the basalt in its slow, deliberate cycles.

And having listened, it would make only those changes that the plateau itself seemed to be asking for.

Where a newborn fissure opened beneath a nesting wall, the power would leave a second wall standing nearby: not unbreakable, only stable for a little longer. The birds would find it without being shown. Their eggs would hatch. When the old cliff finally fell, it would fall empty.

Where a family line of grazers approached a shelf that had already begun to loosen, the power would cause one small sound to travel farther than sound ordinarily traveled. A deep click beneath the stone. A vibration through the hooves. Enough warning, and no more. The oldest animal would turn its head, remember a place it had never visited, and lead the others toward it.

Where the winter cold sealed the seepages, the power would not warm the entire plateau. It would keep one narrow thread of water moving under the ice, winding through a crack and emerging beneath an overhang. The water would be difficult to find, but not impossible. The birds would discover it first. Their tracks would lead the grazers there.

And deep below, where pressure gathered against a fault with enough force to tear the plateau open, the power would not prevent the rupture. It would give the heat somewhere else to go.

A new vent would rise on an empty mineral flat.

The ground would lift, split, and breathe. Steam would tower into the cloud. Black stone would become visible. The old ravine would collapse, but the animals beneath it would already have moved toward the new warmth. Their tunnels would fail behind them, not before.

The power’s work would be so slight that no creature could call it a miracle.

A ledge holding through one more night.

A fog thinning at the right hour.

A calf waking before the stone shifted.

A buried passage remaining open by the width of a paw.

The plateau would still take lives. The scavengers would still circle. The rain would still erase tracks, the frost would still fracture water, and the ground would still make and unmake itself without apology. The power would not mistake mortality for injustice. It would not promise that every creature would survive.

But it would prevent suffering that served no purpose.

It would keep the trapped animal from dying slowly in a sealed crevice when a narrow break could be opened. It would cool the edge of a vent before a nesting bird returned. It would guide a lost young grazer toward the faint warmth of its family—not by calling to it, but by placing the scent of familiar bodies in a wind that should have carried it elsewhere.

The power would also give the beings something no alteration of stone or weather could provide.

It would give them recognition.

Not speech. Not laws. Not the burden of asking why.

Recognition would pass through them as a subtle certainty: that the world was not empty merely because it was vast; that the ground beneath their feet was not indifferent merely because it was dangerous; that each small act of endurance belonged to a pattern larger than any one life.

The oldest grazer would feel it first.

Standing above the cloud sea, with steam rising behind it and its family scattered along the shelf, it would lift its head into the thin air. Nothing visible would descend. No figure would cross the mineral flats. Yet for one breath the animal would understand that its caution mattered—not because it had been chosen above the others, but because every careful step helped preserve the path for those who followed.

The birds would feel it as they returned to their cracks.

The burrowers would feel it in the warmth of a chamber that did not collapse.

The moss would feel it as one more season of seepage.

Even the plateau itself would feel it, if stone could feel such things, as a pressure eased without being erased: not a command to become finished, but permission to continue becoming.

Then the power would withdraw its attention.

It would leave no monument, no inscription, no permanent sign that anything had intervened. The new vent would stain the rock yellow. The wind would scatter the birds’ feathers. Rain would fill the tracks of the grazers. The plateau would keep rising.

Only one difference would remain.

At the places where life was most precarious, there would be a little more margin than chance alone had provided.

A crack wide enough.

A ledge strong enough.

A warning carried far enough.

And because the world had not been made safe—only given room to endure—the animals would continue to live as they had always lived: attentively, provisionally, without mistaking emptiness for absence.

Above the weather, the unfinished land would go on manufacturing itself.

And within it, life would go on, not protected from change, but accompanied through it.
