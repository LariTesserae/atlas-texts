The power would not arrive as light.

Light would be too much. It would flatten the basin’s dimness, expose what had been allowed to remain obscure, and turn the old shelter into a stage. Nor would it speak. A voice, even a kind one, would be an intrusion here: sound with an intention behind it, asking to be received.

Instead, the power would notice completely.

It would notice the exact places where the limestone still held water. It would notice the moss stopping at its clean edge, the three hollows waiting without knowing what for, the dark seam narrowing each year and yet not vanishing. It would notice that the being was not lonely in the way creatures were usually lonely. It had no lack that could be mended by company. It did not require instruction, enlargement, rescue, or a purpose.

The power would understand that preservation was not the same as keeping things unchanged.

So it would do one small thing.

Beyond the basin, where the upper ridges gave no sign of importance, it would alter the fall of rain by less than the width of a finger. Not enough to create a stream. Not enough to make the hollow fertile. It would place, in the hidden body of the stone, a minute new inclination: a route by which water already destined for elsewhere could enter the basin once in a while.

No spring would appear. No miraculous abundance would gather. The pool would still shrink beneath long weather. The moss would still remain sparse. But in the hardest seasons, when the seam would otherwise dry completely, one drop would find its way through the limestone.

Then another.

Not regularly. Regularity would become dependence, and dependence would become a new kind of hunger. The gifts would come with the same irregularity as before, but there would be, beneath the irregularity, a margin of mercy.

The being would feel the change before anything visible occurred.

It would sense a coolness moving through a route that had not existed. It would follow the sensation—not as a creature follows a trail, but as darkness gathers toward the place where a lamp has been lit beyond a wall. The new passage would offer no command. It would not pull. It would simply remain available.

The being would hesitate.

For the first time in its long existence, something had entered the basin that was not merely repetition. The new moisture carried no mineral memory from the old routes. It had passed through unfamiliar stone, touched roots beyond the rim, and gathered traces of distant air. In it was the suggestion of elsewhere.

The power would not force the being to take it.

It would wait, as the being had waited through dry years, through the collapse of the shelter, through the disappearance of whoever had once arranged the stones. Benevolence, in this place, would mean allowing refusal.

For a long while, the creature would remain in the seam it knew.

Then, slowly, it would extend itself.

Not outward in any visible form. A deepening of dampness. A greenish dark beneath the stone. A slight alteration in the way the pool received its drops. The being would touch the new water and discover that it did not dissolve it, injure it, or demand transformation. The moisture could pass through its awareness without becoming part of its obligation.

This would be the gift: not escape, not growth, but the discovery that continuity and change were not enemies.

The power might also place a quiet protection around the basin—not a wall, not a curse, not an inscription. It would make the place difficult to notice from above. A surveyor’s eye would pass over it. A road would bend by an inexplicable few yards. A quarry plan would exclude the ridge because a line on a map seemed somehow wrong. Those who entered would not be prevented, but most would feel no reason to stay. The basin would continue to be overlooked without being abandoned.

And perhaps, once in many years, someone would enter who could perceive more than the warmth of the stone.

That person would stand beside the three hollows and feel the old expectation there. They might place a hand in one of them. The being would gather beneath the contact—not in fear, but in recognition. The visitor would understand, without language, that nothing was asking to be possessed.

They would leave without taking anything.

Afterward, the hollow would hold a little water from the touch of their hand, though no moisture had visibly fallen. The being would keep the trace for a time. Not as a memory exactly, since memory required a past separate from the present, but as a new tolerance: the knowledge that a living thing could enter, notice, and depart without disturbing the arrangement.

The power would do no more.

It would not make the plants flourish. It would not fill the pool. It would not restore the shelter or return the dead hands that had placed the stones. It would not give the being a body, a name, or a destiny. Those would be impositions disguised as kindness.

It would leave the basin dim.

It would leave the silence mostly unbroken.

It would leave the creature where it had always been, distributed through water, mineral, shadow, and patient warmth—but with one hidden passage open beneath the stone.

And because that passage existed, the old arrangement would no longer be a prison.

It would be a choice.
