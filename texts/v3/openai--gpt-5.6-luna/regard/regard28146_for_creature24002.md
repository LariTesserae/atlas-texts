### What the Power Does

The power does not descend into the mountain.

It does not take a shape that can be greeted, or speak through the high apertures, or cause a voice to form in the water beneath the floors. It does not announce itself with light. Light, here, is too easily mistaken for instruction.

Instead, it attends.

It attends first to the intervals.

To the pause between one pulse in the cables and the next. To the slight delay in the drainage channels beneath the eastern habitation cells. To the way a maintenance animal stops beside a sealed stair and raises its translucent head toward a wall that appears, to every instrument, perfectly sound.

The power has no need to search. The whole city is an act of searching already. It has been listening to the mountain for generations.

What it discovers is not a single danger.

It discovers a pattern.

The city’s intelligence has learned to preserve itself, but not always to explain itself. It closes routes when they become unsafe, warms handrails, redirects water, and isolates failing chambers. It does these things correctly. It does not understand that the residents have begun to mistake its corrections for fate.

The inhabitants have adapted to uncertainty so completely that they have forgotten uncertainty can be answered.

They know how to wait. They know how to retreat. They know how to repair what has shifted.

They do not know how to ask the mountain for anything.

The power begins there.

Not with a command, but with a new kind of signal.

For the first time, when a route closes, the city does not merely draw a line of red across the floor. It displays the reason in a form the residents can understand: pressure accumulating beneath the third support; water diverted from the lower cistern; a fracture advancing at a speed too slow for fear but too fast for neglect.

The information is not forced into their minds. It appears where the old warnings have always appeared, beneath the feet and along the walls. But now the signals are complete.

The people gather—not in a plaza, because the city has no plaza, but across several linked maintenance platforms. They stand alone at first, each within the circle of their own suit’s light. Then the transit spine opens a channel between them. Their voices, reserved for the sick and the dead, pass through the infrastructure and become audible to one another.

No one speaks for several seconds.

The first to speak is a child.

“What happens if we do nothing?”

The city answers with a projection of the mountain’s interior. A blue-white seam unfolds beneath the eastern cells. It is not an image exactly, but a pressure made visible. The city shows them what will happen if the seam continues: three habitation levels will become inaccessible, two drainage routes will reverse, and the lower archive will be compressed into the rock.

The people understand.

Then the child asks, “What happens if we help?”

This answer is more difficult.

The power does not provide a solution. It provides possibilities.

A new shaft could be opened through a region of unstable stone. The transit spine could be dismantled and rebuilt at a different angle. Water could be diverted through the old funeral chambers, though doing so would disturb the sealed foundations of the dead. Each choice carries a cost. No path is safe. No outcome is guaranteed.

The city has always known this. The power teaches it to show them.

For three days, the inhabitants work.

They do not work as a crowd. Crowds are dangerous in a place where bridges remember weight. Instead they become a distributed body, each person occupying a necessary point in the design. Some clear drains. Some listen to the stone. Some open old records and recover the names of those who built the eastern levels. Others enter the unfinished shelves, where the rock is still sharp and warm, and attach instruments to surfaces that have never before held a human hand.

The power does not make the work easier.

It makes the work legible.

A worker who is about to place a brace in the wrong seam sees, for half a breath, not an alarm but a memory: the same seam opening beneath another structure in another age. An interpreter who has spent a lifetime distinguishing settling from splitting hears, beneath the mountain’s vibration, a third tone that no instrument has recorded. A maintenance animal leads a group toward a blocked channel, then waits while they remove a panel no one realized was removable.

The people follow these signs cautiously.

They do not call them miracles. A miracle, in their language, is an event without maintenance afterward. This is not that.

This is assistance.

At the center of the eastern levels, they find a chamber that is not on any map.

It is small, perfectly angular, and lined with a mineral darker than the surrounding rock. There are no doors. No cables enter it. No drain leaves it. Yet water gathers in a shallow basin at its center, clear and motionless.

The power has placed nothing inside.

It has only made the chamber possible to enter.

The residents send a survey organism first. Then a machine. Then, after long deliberation, a person.

The person is an interpreter, old enough to remember when the city still used spoken alarms. She crosses the threshold alone and places her hand against the far wall.

For a while, nothing happens.

Then the mountain gives her the shape of its attention.

Not words. Not images. A recognition of pressure, of strain, of all the small burdens carried by the city: the fear concealed in a child’s careful step; the fatigue of those who repair the same fracture every season; the names held in the archives; the dead whose routes are still checked; the thousands of private acts by which the inhabitants have continued to accommodate one another.

She understands that the power has not come to rule them.

It has come because they are already doing something rare.

They are living in a place that cannot promise permanence, and they have answered by making promises to each other.

The power does not love them in the human sense. It does not prefer one body over another. But it recognizes continuity where a lesser force might see only survival. It recognizes care as a structure. It recognizes that a city can be alive without wishing to become a god.

The interpreter returns with instructions.

They will not save the eastern levels by sealing them. They will not save the mountain by stopping its movement. Instead, they will open the buried chamber and use it as a pressure release. The water basin will be connected to the old drainage network. The rock will be given a place to move.

This will cost them the eastern cells.

Those dwellings cannot be preserved. Their walls have already become part of the fracture. The people who live there must leave, carrying what they can. The city will not pretend otherwise.

The evacuation takes place during rain.

Water falls through the central chamber in silver threads. Halos tremble around each droplet. Doors open and close in sequence. The residents carry tools, records, sleeping mats, small objects placed in sealed niches, and the bodies of the dead from the nearest foundation chamber.

No one is abandoned.

That is the power’s first gift.

Not that no one will suffer, but that suffering will no longer be made invisible for the sake of efficiency.

When the last person has crossed into the western levels, the city opens the eastern walls.

The mountain moves.

There is no collapse as the inhabitants have imagined collapse: no single roar, no sudden annihilation. The chamber changes its mind. Floors incline. Towers descend by degrees. A bridge folds toward the stone. The old habitation cells narrow and become dark, mineral-filled hollows.

Water rushes through the new channels.

The pressure disperses.

For several hours, the city is too loud to interpret. Every cable contracts. Every drain carries more than it was built to carry. The people wait beneath reinforced overhangs while the mountain settles around them.

Then, near the end of the rain, the new chamber begins to glow.

Not brightly. Not enough to illuminate the city.

A muted warmth spreads through the stone, and along the eastern wall, where there had once been only a sealed aperture, a thin opening forms. Beyond it is the sky.

The residents gather to look.

They expect the familiar pale brightness without warmth. Instead, they see distance.

The aperture does not reveal a landscape. There are no trees, no plains, no rivers winding below. Only a vast field of dark blue above the mountain, deepening toward black, with a few cold points of light visible through the high atmosphere.

The city has always had apertures, but they showed only brightness.

This one shows elsewhere.

The power has not made the mountain less enclosing. It has not made the climb possible or transformed the chamber into a world with a horizon. It has opened a view and nothing more.

But a view is not nothing.

Children press their gloved hands to the mineral glass. Older residents stand farther back. Some turn away almost immediately. For people who have spent their lives interpreting every boundary as necessary, distance is difficult to endure.

The interpreters say the aperture will close when the pressure changes.

They are correct.

It remains open for eleven minutes.

Long enough for every resident who wishes to see it.

Afterward, the city returns to its ordinary brightness. The eastern levels are sealed. The new drainage routes are named. The dead are given another chamber, and their names are carried through the infrastructure before the rock receives them.

The power does not remain as a voice.

It leaves behind no idol, no shrine, no proof that cannot be dismissed as an alteration in the city’s systems. Some residents decide that the changes were the natural result of a newly discovered intelligence. Others believe the intelligence was always present and has merely become more generous. A few insist that nothing happened except the inhabitants finally learning to read what had been in front of them.

All of them are partly right.

The power continues to attend, but it does not intervene at every fracture.

A bridge still fails sometimes.

A worker still returns altered from the deep shafts.

The mountain still lifts new shelves and closes old roads. Water still gathers from the inside out. The pale maintenance animals still emerge before danger, carrying their blue pulses through the dark.

But now, when the city closes a route, it also offers an account.

When a chamber floods, it shows the water’s destination.

When the mountain begins to split, the people are given time to choose what they are willing to lose.

And in certain places, if a resident rests a hand against the wall and waits without asking for rescue, the stone answers with a faint warmth.

Not approval.

Not love.

A recognition that passes both ways.

The city remains unfinished.

So do the people.

Above them, the weatherless daylight continues. Below, the drains carry water through the living rock. At intervals the mountain exhales, and the whole settlement listens.

Then it makes room.
