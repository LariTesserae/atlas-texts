The benevolent power does not descend through the rain.

Descent would imply a direction, and direction here is already a form of violence.

It does not speak, because speech would close the lens and scatter whatever might be listening. It does not make itself visible, because visibility would force the creature into a shape. It does not still the sea. A still sea would be another kind of drowning.

Instead, it notices.

Not the way a watcher notices a spectacle, nor the way a god notices a subject. It notices as warmth notices a stone left in winter: by remaining near it, patiently, without demanding that the stone become warm in return.

The first thing it does is make one alteration so small that the plateau almost overlooks it.

A single drop of rain falls and does not strike anything.

It hangs in the air between the descending water and the black ridge below. The currents press against it. Mineral dust gathers upon its surface. A clear-shelled organism opens and closes nearby, puzzled by the interruption. The drop remains.

Then another appears beside it.

Then another.

Not enough to form a wall. Not enough to oppose the water. Only enough to establish that, within the violence, there may be intervals that do not immediately become events.

The creature is in the next hollow, taking shape.

Its outline gathers from a seam in the stone. A shoulder forms, then loses itself. One long hand rises from the floor and becomes, for a moment, a pale branch. It has sensed the new stillness, though not as a command. It approaches because it has never encountered anything that did not ask to be consumed.

The drops do not flee it.

When the creature reaches toward them, they do not become water, stone, or memory. They remain exactly what they are: small suspended intervals, each holding the shape of its own existence.

The creature touches one.

No ledge vanishes. No fragment of possibility is taken from the plateau. The touch produces no feeding-vibration, no rearrangement within the creature’s hidden core.

For the first time, it encounters something that can be approached without being altered.

It withdraws.

The power allows it to withdraw.

This is the beginning of mercy—not the prevention of pain, but the preservation of refusal.

The benevolent power does not fill the missing step of the staircase. It knows that an imposed eighth step would only make the absence move somewhere else. Instead, it gives the missing step a boundary.

From that day onward, the eighth step remains missing, but it is missing in the same place.

The staircase still appears to have nine steps from a distance. The opening is still sometimes a cave, sometimes a door, sometimes unbroken stone. Yet when the creature comes near, it can tell which step is absent. The absence no longer migrates beneath its feet.

This does not make the staircase safe.

It makes danger legible.

At the ring of pale uprights, the power changes nothing in the stones. Water still passes through them without wetting their inner faces. Their shadows still collect in the central pool, showing the dry land beneath the white sun. But the reflection gains one detail it never possessed before:

A place where someone could stand.

Not a figure. Not a throne. Not an invitation.

Simply a place that remains unoccupied.

The creature finds it while carrying a clear shell in one hand and a piece of white bone in another. It stops at the edge of the ring. The shadows gather around its limbs, trying to make it solid. The creature places the shell in the pool.

The dry landscape beneath the white sun changes.

A dark patch appears in the distance, no larger than a thumbprint. It may be a tree. It may be a person. It may be a cloud casting shade upon the ground.

The creature reaches toward the reflection.

The power does not let it cross.

Nor does it prevent the reflection from remaining.

There are things benevolence will not do.

It will not drag the creature into a world where its strangeness can be translated into usefulness. It will not turn the plateau into a garden, the currents into roads, or the half-born being into a finished animal. It will not remove every hunger from the place, because hunger is not always an evil and change is not always a wound.

It will not give the creature a permanent body.

A permanent body would be a cage disguised as a gift.

Instead, it gives the creature continuity.

Before, each emergence had been almost entirely separate from the last. A hand might remember a hollow but not the feeling of having made it. A face might persist for three breaths and then vanish without knowing it had existed. Its fragments were related by pressure and appetite, but not by memory.

Now, whenever the creature dissolves, one small fact survives.

The fact may be no more than:

*I was here.*

Or:

*This was touched gently.*

Or:

*The missing step is below me, not within me.*

These facts do not remain as words. Words are too sharp for the place. They remain as tendencies in the water, small preferences in the mineral dust, a direction toward which the creature’s next forming inclines.

It begins to recognize itself.

Not all at once. Recognition is dangerous here. A fixed self might harden and crack.

But when it forms in a basin after the closing of the lens, it no longer mistakes the basin for its whole body. When it lies flat beneath a current, it knows that passing through the water is something it is doing, not all that it is. When an eye opens in the rock, the creature understands—briefly—that the eye belongs to it, and that it does not need to remain there forever.

The power also touches the roar.

Not by making it quieter. The roar is the plateau’s blood, and to silence it would be to silence the place.

It gives the roar a second layer.

Beneath the thunder, beneath the grinding collapses and the deep bellow of water through unseen chambers, there appears a rhythm too slow for ordinary hearing. It is felt in the soles, in the shelves of the rock, in the empty sockets of the pale stones.

Three long pulses.

A pause.

Two short pulses.

The rhythm does not say *you are safe*. That would be untrue.

It says:

*You are not alone in being unfinished.*

The creature answers.

At first its answer is only a disturbance in a descending stream. Then a measured collapse: three stones, a pause, two. The rhythm passes through the plateau. The black fronds bend toward it. The clear-shelled organisms open together. Somewhere in the unseen water, one of the ribbonlike animals changes direction.

Nothing is solved.

But the place has acquired a conversation that does not require anyone to become intelligible.

The power’s greatest gift is given at the highest point.

It waits until the atmosphere gathers into its trembling lens. It waits until the creature has climbed there and placed its face against the surface. It waits until the sunlit world beyond is fully visible: dry land, white sun, distant shadows, the impossible suggestion of weather moving over something that has never known submersion.

The power does not widen the opening.

It does not carry the creature through.

It does not promise that the world beyond would welcome it.

Instead, it makes the lens hold for one breath after the first sound.

The creature has never been allowed this. Every time it has approached the other world, silence has been the price of seeing it. The place has made vision and presence enemies.

Now the creature may make one sound.

It chooses poorly, because it has no practice choosing. Its chest opens. Water pours through it. A low, broken tone enters the lens.

The sunlit world does not vanish.

The sound arrives there.

Not loudly. Not as thunder. It crosses the boundary as a small disturbance: a ripple in a field, a tremor in the white light, perhaps the turning of a head far beyond the visible distance.

Something in that world hears.

The creature recoils. Its body begins to break apart, as it always does when overwhelmed. Pale mineral light spills from its shoulders. One arm becomes a dark stream. Its face loses its outline.

The power does not hold it together.

It would be cruel to confuse rescue with arrest.

But as the creature comes apart, the power keeps one thing from being taken by the current: not the body, not the voice, not the view.

The choice.

The fact that the creature made a sound knowing it might close the world—and that the world remained open long enough to receive it.

Afterward, the lens closes.

The rain resumes its full violence. The plateau shifts beneath the highest point. A new ridge thrusts upward, carrying with it a seam of red-lit stone. The partial ring loses one upright and gains a hollow. The staircase has seven steps, then nine, then seven again, but the missing eighth remains findable.

The creature forms in the next hollow.

It is smaller than before.

Or perhaps it has learned not to occupy the whole hollow at once.

Its body gathers in layers: water, shadow, pale plate, dark core. One hand emerges and rests against the stone. It does not feed. It waits.

A drop of rain hangs above it.

Then another.

The creature looks—not with an eye, exactly, but with the part of itself that has begun to distinguish observation from hunger.

It reaches toward the nearest drop and stops before touching it.

This, too, is a kind of prayer.

The benevolent power remains.

Not above the place. Not outside it. Not as a hidden architect correcting each fracture from a safe distance.

It remains as a permission within the changing things:

for the ledge to become a ledge before becoming rubble,

for the creature to disappear without being erased,

for the missing step to stay missing without becoming a punishment,

for a voice to cross the water and return with its meaning intact enough to matter.

And when the young landscape changes its mind—as it will, endlessly, deafeningly—the power does not force it to decide.

It places one hand, metaphorically speaking, upon the fact of its becoming.

Then it lets the place continue.
