### The Attention of the Kind Power

The power did not come down into the hollow.

It did not take a shape that could be seen, nor speak through the vents, nor place a hand upon the mountain. It understood that anything which announced itself would become an obligation. The people would build a shrine. They would assign it a name, appoint listeners, invent duties, and eventually fear disappointing it.

So the power watched without intruding.

It watched the people move through the terraces, each body fitting the paths worn by generations before it. It watched them share space with creatures that would have been called vermin elsewhere. It watched them repair what the mountain damaged without cursing the mountain for being what it was.

The power saw that they were not happy in the way lowland poets meant happiness. They were not free from danger, labor, hunger, grief, or uncertainty.

But they were not abandoned.

They had learned the shape of their world so thoroughly that care and survival had become the same act.

The power considered changing them.

It could have opened a spring in the stone. It could have cooled the vents, thinned the predators, straightened the terraces, softened the glare, or caused the black-leaved plants to bear sweeter fruit. It could have revealed a passage to the plains and shown them a wider sky.

But wisdom is partly the knowledge of what not to improve.

Water would not merely relieve thirst. It would change the plants, the insects, the houses, the illnesses, the migrations, the meanings of cleanliness and generosity. A passage outward would not simply offer freedom. It would invite strangers, trade, weapons, disease, worship, ambition, and the slow conviction that the old life had been a mistake.

The power did not mistake unfamiliarity for suffering.

It looked more closely.

It saw children learning the voices of animals before they learned the names of their own grandparents. It saw elders placing their hands against the walls, not demanding answers but making themselves available to whatever the mountain might say. It saw a person kill a dangerous creature and use every part of it. It saw lovers making a corridor together, pruning the same plants, leaving room for one another’s feet.

It saw a people who understood that belonging was not ownership.

And so it chose not to rescue them.

It chose instead to preserve the possibility of their choosing.

Far below the hollow, where the mountain’s oldest pressures moved through layers of iron-dark stone, a fracture had begun to lengthen. It was not yet a disaster. It might have remained harmless for a century, or it might have opened during the next pressure storm and dropped half the settlement into the deep.

The people had heard something.

The oldest listeners had felt a change in the walls, but the signal was faint, confused by the ordinary noise of the hollow. The mountain had too many voices. A warning could resemble a burrower. A settling shelf could sound like distant knocking.

The power did not close the fracture.

That would have been an act of ownership.

Instead, it touched the mountain’s memory.

A tremor moved through the stone—not strong enough to break anything, not strange enough to inspire worship. It traveled along the fracture and emerged at the surface as a sequence of small, clear knocks.

Three.

Pause.

Two.

Pause.

Three again.

The pattern was heard first by a child sleeping near a vent. She woke before the adults did. The sound passed through the wall beside her face, patient and exact. She pressed her palm to the stone and felt, beneath the familiar warmth, a slow downward drag.

She did not understand it.

But she remembered the pattern.

In the morning she repeated it to her mother. Her mother repeated it to an elder. The elder went to the lower terraces, where the wall carried the sound differently. There, after listening for a long time, she ordered that a section of walkway be cleared.

No one knew why.

They cleared it anyway.

The work revealed a thin seam running beneath the old path. It was not wide enough to see into, but dust fell through it without settling. A work party was sent to investigate. They found that the stone below had become hollow and hot. They marked the place with red mineral, then returned with tools and ropes.

The fracture opened three days later.

It did not swallow the settlement. It released a deep blast of heated dust, tore up a useless ledge, and collapsed the path that would otherwise have been crowded with workers and animals. The people had already moved their tools, stores, and sleeping platforms. The creatures had been driven away by the same unease that preceded a pressure storm.

Afterward, the settlement stood at the edge of a new cut in the mountain.

The elders argued about what it meant.

Some said the mountain had warned them.

Some said they had merely listened well.

A few claimed the child had heard the voice of an ancestor. The child herself said only that the wall had knocked.

The power was satisfied with this.

It did not require gratitude.

Still, it gave one further gift—not an object, but a small alteration in the conditions of remembering.

From then on, when a pressure storm approached, one sound within the hollow would become unmistakable. It might be the clicking of shells stopping all at once. It might be a low tone in the vents. It might be the brief, collective folding of the black leaves.

The warning would never be the same twice.

But no one would mistake it for ordinary noise.

This did not make the storms harmless. It did not prevent injury, loss, or mourning. A roof would still fall somewhere. A nest would still be scattered. A person would still sometimes fail to return from an upper ledge.

The power did not erase consequence.

It gave them time.

And because it gave them time, they began to give time to one another.

They made wider shelters. They kept emergency stores in more than one place. They taught every child the storm signs, not only the children of listeners. They stopped relying on a few elders to interpret the mountain. The knowledge entered songs, work rhythms, games, and the knocking language carried from terrace to terrace.

The child who first heard the altered pattern grew old.

She became a listener, though she disliked the title. She taught that the mountain did not speak in words and that no one should claim to speak for it. She taught that a warning was not a command. It was an opportunity to notice.

When she died, her bones were cleaned and divided among her family. One finger bone was set into the wall beside the old fracture, beneath a strip of red-brown plant. Not as a relic. As a reminder of attention.

The power remained.

It continued to watch the hollow through centuries of small disasters and repairs. It watched new houses round themselves around old roots. It watched pathways move, names change, predators become sacred, sacred animals become food, and forgotten tools emerge from the dust to be used again.

It watched the people discover, much later, a narrow opening high above the settlement.

Beyond it lay a slope descending toward distant plains.

The opening had always existed, sealed by a curtain of black vegetation and a fall of stone. The power could have opened it at any time. Instead, it had waited until the settlement found it while searching for new mineral deposits.

The first explorers looked out and saw a sky without shelves.

They saw clouds.

They saw a blue distance so enormous that several of them became sick and had to turn back.

The people debated for generations whether the opening should be widened. Some wished to descend. Some wished to keep the hollow unchanged. Others argued that the mountain had never forbidden departure; it had merely failed to provide a path.

The power did nothing.

This, too, was part of the gift.

It allowed them to possess a door without making them walk through it.

In time, they built a narrow stair—not outward exactly, but between worlds. It was used rarely. Travelers returned carrying strange seeds, unfamiliar shells, stories of rain, and dust that did not belong to the mountain.

The hollow changed.

It did not become better.

It became larger in meaning.

Some plants failed when exposed to wetter air. Some people left and did not return. New illnesses entered the settlement. New medicines followed. There were arguments, thefts, marriages, mourning, and several ugly years in which the old customs nearly broke beneath the weight of new desires.

The benevolent power did not intervene then.

Benevolence without respect becomes domination.

It had given them warning, not immunity; a door, not a destiny. They would have to decide what kind of people they were whenever the world became wider.

Only once did it act openly.

A group of outsiders came into the hollow armed with blades and bright metal. They wanted the vent minerals and the black plant oils. They saw the settlement as a mine with inhabitants in the way.

The people prepared to resist, but they were too few.

The predators withdrew from their usual paths. The grazers vanished beneath the mats. The winged creatures rose from the roofs in a single dark cloud and filled the hollow from wall to wall. Every vent began to hiss at once.

Then the mountain knocked.

Not the warning pattern.

A deeper sound.

A sound felt in the teeth.

The outsiders froze. The stone beneath them shifted. Dust poured from the upper shelves. Nothing collapsed, but the hollow seemed to wake around them: plants snapping open, shells clicking, animals moving in the darkness with deliberate confidence.

The people did not attack.

They stood in their doorways, face coverings tied, tools in hand.

The outsiders left.

Perhaps they believed the mountain cursed. Perhaps they believed the settlement possessed some weapon. Perhaps they simply understood, for the first time, that they had entered a living place and that every life within it had turned its attention toward them.

Afterward, the people argued over whether the power had protected them.

The elders listened to the walls.

There was no answer.

And this was wise, because the answer was both yes and no.

The power had not defeated the outsiders. The people had endured them. The mountain had frightened them. The animals had made the hollow impassable. Many causes had joined together, none sufficient alone.

That was the only kind of miracle the power was willing to permit: one in which no one could be certain a miracle had occurred.

For the most part, then, the power did what benevolent powers rarely do in stories.

It refrained.

It did not make the people pure, prosperous, immortal, or safe. It did not turn the mountain into a benevolent parent. It did not remove the hard terms of their existence.

It made no paradise of the hollow.

It preserved the hollow as a place where care could continue to matter.

It strengthened the faint signal before catastrophe. It kept certain seeds viable through years of poor growth. It caused a lost child to notice the warmth of a hidden vent. It gave an old woman, once in her life, the exact memory of a path she had walked as a girl. It bent a falling stone by the width of a hand.

Small mercies.

Mercies that could be mistaken for luck.

And when the people died, as all living things did, the power did not gather them into some shining afterworld. It allowed their bones to enter tools, walls, roots, and dust. It allowed their names to alter in the mouths of descendants. It allowed their work to become indistinguishable from the mountain’s own continuing shape.

At last, that was what moved the power most deeply—not that the people survived, but that they had learned to make survival generous.

They left passages for creatures they feared.

They taught warnings to children who had not yet earned the right to be useful.

They repaired bridges for travelers who might never thank them.

They remembered the dead by returning them to circulation.

They listened before asking to be answered.

So the power stayed near the hollow—not as a ruler, not as a guardian with a hand forever poised above it, but as an attention that did not turn away.

And sometimes, when the mountain breathed in the night, a new pattern passed through the walls.

The people would pause.

The animals would lift their heads.

The plants would fold or open according to their own ancient timing.

Then the inhabitants of the warm stone would place their hands against the rock and listen—not because they expected the mountain to save them, but because they had learned that being heard and being protected were not the same thing.

The first was already a kind of blessing.

The second, when truly needed, would be given carefully.
