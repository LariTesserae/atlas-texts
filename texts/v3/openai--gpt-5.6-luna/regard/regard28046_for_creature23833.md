The power would not arrive as a figure.

A figure would be too large for the city. It would catch on ropes, disturb the smoke, frighten the goats, and make every passage a place of ceremony. The people would mistake its presence for an emergency, and emergencies already claimed enough of their lives.

So the power would come as attention.

It would listen.

It would listen to the channels, to the bats breathing in the ceiling ribs, to the old stone answering the weight of sleepers. It would listen to the names scratched into warm chambers and to the unspoken names carried in habits: the way a hand tested a jar, the way a child stepped around an open grate, the way a widow still turned toward a vanished companion before speaking.

It would understand that the city was not waiting to be rescued.

It was already rescuing itself, every day.

A careless power would bring light. It would flood the vaults with daylight, dry the moss, blind the bats, spoil the fungus beds, crack the stored grain, and turn every familiar surface into a strange one.

A sentimental power would cover the stone in gardens.

A proud power would speak through the statues and demand gratitude.

A wise power would do none of these things.

It would ask what, precisely, was in danger.

The answer would not be the city as a whole. The old cistern-city was strong. It had survived centuries by yielding where it must and reinforcing where it could. Its danger lay in one neglected distinction: the people knew the sounds of water, stone, animals, tools, and footsteps—but they no longer knew the sound of the reservoir above them.

The sealed doorway had begun to breathe because the upper water had changed its course.

Not today. Not tomorrow. Perhaps not even this season.

But somewhere beyond the fitted blocks, pressure was gathering in a chamber whose walls had not been tested for generations. If nothing changed, the water would eventually find the oldest weakness. It would enter not through the gates or the accustomed channels, but through the sleeping tiers, behind the fungus beds, beneath the storage shelves where the city kept its grain.

The power would not stop the water.

Water was not evil. It was only water, returning by a route it had been given long before anyone living could remember.

Instead, the power would make the danger audible.

It would begin with one drip.

The drip would fall into the bronze bowl at an interval no worker had ever heard before: three quick drops, a pause, then one. Three quick drops, a pause, then one.

At first, the people would assume that a child was playing nearby.

Then the channel keepers would hear it beneath the sound of the bowl.

They would find that the rhythm continued after the bowl was moved. It would come from the stone itself, faint and deep, traveling through the masonry. It would pass beneath their palms like a pulse.

Three. Pause. One.

The oldest among them would say that it resembled no known warning.

The youngest would say that it resembled a door being knocked upon from the other side.

They would argue. They would bring lamps. They would mark the wall with wet clay. They would forbid singing in the eastern drain and send two people into the lower galleries with hooked poles, though everyone would know that the poles could not reach whatever lay beyond the sealed blocks.

The power would wait.

It would not hurry them, because haste was one of the ways the city was harmed. Haste made people lift grates without bracing them, enter drains without testing the air, and trust the first explanation that quieted their fear.

Instead, the power would give them a second sign.

A child—one who had been named Hush-Step because she could cross the crowded walkways without waking anyone—would notice that the rhythm was not repeated everywhere. It would be loudest beneath a buttress no one used anymore, a thick triangular support between the eastern drain and the high storage tier.

The buttress would appear sound.

When the people pressed their cheeks to it, they would hear the hidden water moving behind the stone.

Not rushing. Waiting.

The city would call its builders, its drain keepers, its weight readers, its oldest listeners. They would clear the shelves and remove the jars. They would take down the reed curtains. They would uncover a line of ancient pebbles packed into the floor, almost invisible beneath centuries of grit.

The line would lead to a low opening behind the buttress.

No one would remember it.

The power would not open the passage for them. It would only keep one particular pebble from settling when the first person touched it. That tiny resistance would be enough. The mason would notice. She would remove the pebble, then another, then a plug of resin blackened by age.

Behind it, air would move.

It would smell of rain.

The passage would descend for a short distance before dividing. One branch would lead upward, toward the sealed pressure chamber. The other would lead downward, toward an abandoned spillway running beneath the city.

The old builders had known this danger.

They had made a route for it.

They had also sealed the route when the reservoir changed, and over generations the memory of the route had been lost. The power would not restore the builders’ knowledge to anyone’s mind. It would leave the people the dignity of discovering what their ancestors had left them.

They would work for nine days.

They would clear the spillway by hand. They would carry out silt, beetle shells, old timber, broken pottery, and the bones of animals that had entered and failed to return. They would repair the collapsed joints with resin and fired clay. They would lower lamps into the shaft and watch the flames bend toward the unseen upper air.

On the seventh day, the water would come.

It would strike the sealed chamber with a sound like a giant fist.

The city would wake without being told. Every person would know the difference between ordinary water and this water. The channels would begin to tremble. The bats would rise in a black cloud. Goats would pull against their tethers. Children would be lifted onto shoulders.

The people would open the ancient spillway.

For a moment, nothing would happen.

Then the old route would accept the pressure.

Water would roar beneath the floor, not into the sleeping tiers but away from them, through the forgotten descent and out toward lower chambers that had been dry for centuries. The city would shake. Dust would fall from the vaults. The sealed doorway would groan.

But it would hold.

When the flood ended, the people would stand in the wet dark, listening.

The rhythm would be gone.

In its place they would hear a new sound: water moving where water had not moved since the first makers laid the stone.

They would not call this a miracle.

They would call it a channel restored.

That would please the power.

It would not seek to be named. Names became duties here, and the power had no need of one. It would not ask for statues, offerings, obedience, or songs. If the people placed grain beside the newly opened spillway, it would accept the gesture only as one accepts the hand of a person who has understood something difficult.

Afterward, the power would make a few further gifts, all small enough to be mistaken for luck.

A shaft near the high vault would admit a little more air, enough to reduce smoke without chilling the fungus beds.

A patch of pale creeper would take root beside the restored channel, its leaves edible and rich in minerals.

The silver fish would begin arriving one day earlier each season, giving the people time to prepare their baskets.

A certain kind of stone, found in the cleared spillway, would prove excellent for sharpening tools.

And in the uppermost chamber, where no one slept because the ceiling was too low, a crack would widen just enough to show a narrow column of daylight.

Not enough to transform the city.

Enough to reveal that the world above still existed.

The people would send no army through it. They would not abandon the cistern-city. They would establish a watch, then a ladder, then a rule: no one was to climb alone, and no one was to return without bringing something back—not as proof of conquest, but as evidence that the two worlds remained connected.

The first traveler would bring down a feather.

The second would bring seeds.

The third would bring news of rain.

For a while, the people would gather beneath the opening and take turns looking upward. The children would ask whether the sky made a sound. The adults would listen for an answer.

The power would be there then, as it had been from the beginning—not above them, not outside them, but in the widening of what they could notice.

It would be present when they changed the rules to include the restored spillway.

When they taught the rhythm of the old danger to their children.

When they carved the forgotten route into a wall.

When they left the sealed doorway sealed, because not every mystery was a request to be opened.

When they repaired the city again, and again, and again.

The benevolent power would not make the place finished.

It would make sure the people had time.

Time to hear the warning.

Time to understand it.

Time to choose what should be preserved, what should be altered, and what should remain unknown.

And when, years later, the inhabitants gathered around the old doorway and heard warm air passing through its joints, smelling of rain, they would not panic.

They would rise one by one.

They would listen.

Then they would mark the wall, clear the passage, feed the lamps, wake the children, and begin.
