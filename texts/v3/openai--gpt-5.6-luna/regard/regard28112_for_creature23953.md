The benevolent power comes without entering.

It does not descend through the gray crack, because to do so would make the hollow a place that had been visited, and the hollow has never been visited. It does not speak from the mountain, because speech would divide the silence into before and after.

Instead, it attends.

For a long while—if “long while” can be said of a place where time has no appetite—it rests its awareness against the outer face of the stone. It feels the water held in the rock. It feels the moss, the roots, the sleeping bodies. It feels the black pool and the presence beneath it, which is not separate from the hollow but not wholly contained by it either.

The power is wise enough to recognize that nothing here is asking to be rescued.

There is no wound to close. No tyrant to overthrow. No famine hidden beneath the abundance. The creatures do not suffer from their lack of longing, any more than a stone suffers from not being a bird. To mistake stillness for misery would be only another kind of violence.

So the power does not bring day.

It does not teach the birds to fly, sharpen the teeth of the deer-things, loosen the animals from their gentle peace, or open the mountain to the weather. It does not give the inhabitants names for fear, ambition, grief, or hope. These may be precious things elsewhere, but preciousness does not make them universally necessary.

It watches.

And because it watches, the hollow is no longer entirely alone.

This is a subtle alteration. No creature notices it at first. The moss does not brighten. The fog does not lift. Water continues to bead on the leaves and hang from the stone. The animals remain folded together in sleep.

But from that moment onward, nothing that dies is ever wholly forgotten.

The power does not prevent death. That would contradict the hollow’s oldest rhythm. It does not preserve every body or interrupt the roots as they enter fur and bone. Instead, it holds each creature in its attention for one impossible instant: the beetle beneath the stone, the bird whose wings never opened, the broad creature that sank into the moss after years of walking the perimeter.

The hollow accepts them as it always has.

The power remembers them as themselves.

Not as nourishment. Not as shelter. Not as mineral, root, or fertile darkness.

As the particular life they were.

This remembrance does not trouble the dead. It does not call them back. It does not separate them from the fabric of the hollow. It simply means that no creature disappears completely into the comfort of belonging.

The Stillborn begin to sense this before they understand it—if understanding is possible for them.

A sleeping animal sometimes lifts its head and looks toward an empty patch of moss.

A bird opens its wings in the branches and holds them there, not to make room, but because it has suddenly recalled that wings are meant for something.

A deer-shaped creature pauses at the rim and does not immediately turn back.

For the first time, the hollow contains a gesture that is not required by hunger, sleep, water, or the presence beneath the pool.

It is not escape.

It is hesitation.

The power is careful with this gift. It does not make longing into a command. It does not place a door before the creatures and call refusal freedom. The rim remains what it was: a steep drop into cloud, a boundary without a gate. No path appears. No voice promises a better world beyond.

The choice, if it can be called a choice, is left almost weightless.

An animal may look outward.

It may turn away.

It may remain forever between the two.

Nothing punishes it. Nothing rewards it.

Beneath the central pool, the presence shifts.

For the first time, it does not merely settle. It rises close enough to the surface that the black water domes above it. Every creature in the hollow becomes still. The power touches that submerged vastness—not with force, but with recognition.

You have kept them safe, the power conveys, though no words cross the water.

The thing beneath the pool offers no answer.

You have kept them whole.

A single ring appears on the surface.

The power continues: But safety is not the same as completion.

The ring widens until it reaches the stones. Then it fades. The pool is black again.

The benevolent power does not demand that the presence change its keeping. It does not tear open the hollow or scatter its inhabitants into the world. It does not call the Stillborn incomplete as an accusation. It merely places possibility beside permanence, as one might place a lamp in a room—not to compel anyone to approach it, but so that darkness will no longer be mistaken for the only shape of things.

Afterward, almost nothing is different.

The water still does not run out. The moss still accepts the dead. Predators still rest beside prey. The birds still do not sing.

But once in an age, one bird climbs to the highest branch.

It does not fly. The hollow has not given it flight.

It opens its wings and faces the gray crack.

The other birds turn toward it. The animals beneath the trees lift their heads. Even the presence under the water listens.

Then the bird closes its wings and returns among the others.

This, too, is permitted.

And the power remains—not as a ruler, not as a savior, but as a witness stationed beyond the stone. It will not interfere unless the hollow asks, and the hollow may never ask. That is acceptable. Benevolence does not always mean changing what one finds.

Sometimes it means refusing to change it for the sake of one’s own idea of mercy.

Sometimes it means giving a finished place the smallest possible opening through which an unfinished thought may pass.

Sometimes it means remembering the dead.

And sometimes, when the black pool makes its soft sound deep underground, the power answers—not with a command, but with presence.

The sound travels through stone.

The creatures pause.

Then the hollow returns to silence, though silence is no longer quite the same as before.
