The power did not descend.

Descent would have been a kind of claim. It would have made the plateau look upward, and the plateau had not yet decided that anything above it was wiser than anything beneath it.

Instead, the power noticed.

It noticed the long weather, the raw stone, the water moving beneath surfaces that had not learned to become valleys. It noticed the cities arranged with such care that they could still be abandoned without leaving scars. It noticed the inhabitants changing themselves by degrees, not from desperation, but from a willingness to belong to a place that was changing too.

Most of all, it noticed the solitary being walking beyond the western settlements.

The being had stopped beside a structural vein where rainwater collected in a shallow silver basin. One of its many-fingered hands rested on the stone. Its dark face had gone pale with concentration.

Something was wrong beneath the plateau.

Not an immediate fracture. Not a flood, not a pressure surge, not any of the failures for which the cities had prepared their listening stations. The disturbance was smaller and more intimate. A pattern had gone missing from the deep strata.

The being could feel the absence but not its cause.

For three days it had walked from settlement to settlement, touching the veins, asking the ground to repeat itself. The answer remained the same: a hollow place beneath the eastern escarpment, where the plateau’s slow internal intelligence had once carried a rhythm and now carried nothing.

The inhabitants had sent no alarm. There was no danger yet. The cities continued to warm their corridors, the herds continued to cross the basins, and the construction drones continued their silent work.

But the being had understood something that the cities had not.

The missing rhythm belonged to a dead child.

Not a child in the simple sense. The child had been assembled in a warm chamber beneath a habitation field, given a body of mineral foam and soft membranes, and taught to listen. It had lived for nine years. It had preferred low clouds. It had been afraid of enclosed transit lines. It had once spent an entire afternoon following a fox that did not wish to be followed.

When its body failed, its pattern had been divided among those who knew it.

The portion meant for the plateau had not arrived.

No one knew why.

The power understood that this was not a technical defect. The plateau had no missing component. The child was not a broken machine awaiting repair.

It was a grief that had not found a place to become part of the world.

So the power made the smallest possible intervention.

It caused one drop of rain to fall upward.

The drop rose from the basin beneath the being’s hand, passed through the thin air, and hung there trembling. The being turned its dark face toward it. Across the plateau, the structural veins brightened.

Not in a command.

In an invitation.

The being listened.

At first it heard only the usual things: groundwater passing through porous shelves, a herd moving somewhere beyond the eastern ridge, the minute flexing of a foundation beneath an unfinished tower. Then, beneath these, almost too faint to distinguish from pressure, came a sequence of impressions.

Warm chamber.

Blue cloth.

A fox beneath a transit bridge.

The taste of mineral rain.

A preference for low clouds.

The being lifted all its hands from the stone.

The invitation remained.

It did not say, *Here is the dead child.*

It said, *Here is where the child may be remembered, if you choose to remember it here.*

The being understood the difference. A lesser power would have restored the child whole, or spoken in the child’s voice, or made a body rise from the basin. It would have mistaken resemblance for mercy.

This power did not.

It gave back no body. It gave back no certainty. It offered only the lost impressions, separated from ownership and released into the plateau’s immense capacity for keeping things.

The being remained beside the basin until night.

Then it carried the memory east.

It did not go alone. As it walked, the structural veins transmitted the invitation outward. A human-descended woman in the nearest city woke with the sudden recollection of blue cloth, though she had never seen it. A maintenance worker paused beneath a tower and felt, for no reason he could name, that low clouds were preferable to clear skies. A fox lifted its head from beneath a heat vent and turned toward the escarpment.

None of them received the child’s identity. None of them were possessed. They were not made responsible for a life that had ended.

They were simply given one small, true impression that had been waiting to enter the world.

The being reached the eastern escarpment at dawn.

There, in a hollow between raw shelves of stone, it placed both lower hands against the ground and began to listen. Other listeners came. Then human citizens from the settlements. Then children, animals, maintenance forms, and weather-bound inhabitants who could not travel but extended themselves through the city systems.

They made no monument.

They built no tower.

They formed a circle around the hollow and remained silent while the rain passed through them.

Under the ground, the absence changed.

It did not close. Nothing was repaired. The child did not become conscious again, and the plateau did not pretend that death had been overcome.

But the hollow became capable of carrying the memory.

A little warmth settled into the stone. Water began to collect there in a shape no engineer had designed. Blue-black moss appeared along the rim. When low clouds crossed the plateau, the hollow held them in reflection long after the sky had cleared.

The inhabitants named the place according to their custom, with a phrase rather than a proper name:

**Where the Weather Is Welcome.**

The power watched.

It did not make the plateau fertile. Fertility would have been another form of violence if imposed without measure. It did not smooth the cliffs, quicken the soil, or teach the rivers to arrive before the land was ready. It did not make every death meaningful, because wisdom does not require that every loss be justified.

It did three things.

It made the cities slightly better at recognizing when they were asking too much of the ground.

It made the inhabitants more difficult to separate from one another through fear.

And whenever a life ended without finding a place in the world’s memory, it left an opening—not an answer, not a resurrection, but a place where the living could carry what they had been given.

After that, the plateau continued becoming habitable.

Its rain still fell for hours. Its cliffs remained sharp. Its rivers remained uncertain. Cities still folded themselves inward when they failed, and animals still crossed the empty reaches without explanation.

But now, in the deepest structural veins, there was one additional rhythm.

Not the rhythm of a child.

The rhythm of many beings choosing, together, not to let the child vanish entirely.
