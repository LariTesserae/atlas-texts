The benevolent power does not descend like a hand.

It does not part the sea, raise the country into sunlight, or summon the dead from the rooms beneath the roots. It does not mistake preservation for salvation. The drowned land has already become something whole, though no longer what its builders intended. To tear it from its darkness would be another kind of destruction.

So the power begins by listening.

It listens not for voices, because there are none, but for the slow grammar of pressure and flow. It learns the weight of each terrace, the patience of each root, the routes by which silt travels through the old works. It listens to the faint complaint of a retaining wall, the changed tone of a flooded passage, the long exhalation of water through a buried gate.

And because it is wise, it does not make itself the center of what it has found.

It does not give the plants minds. Consciousness would be a burden here: an inheritance of fear, desire, and expectation laid upon beings that have never needed them. It does not give them flowers merely to make the darkness beautiful, or animals merely to make the silence feel inhabited. It does not turn the old ruins into a theater for its own kindness.

Instead, it makes small corrections.

Where a foundation is about to fail, the rubble beneath it settles into a stronger arrangement. A cracked arch is supported by a slow mineral growth that was not there before. A blocked outlet opens by the width of a root, then another, allowing water to move again through a district that has been stagnating for centuries.

The power does not rebuild the walls. It teaches them, gently, how to remain.

Silt that would bury a channel is carried elsewhere. A current that has begun to scour the foot of a grove is divided around it. In the deepest halls, where the plants have exhausted the thin store of nourishment held in the stone, minerals rise from below in quantities so slight that no sudden abundance follows. The growth does not become lush. It simply continues.

Nothing blooms dramatically. Nothing brightens. There is no miracle visible from a distance.

A leaf unfolds a little more easily than it would have. A root finds a seam before the seam closes. A grove that was thinning survives another century.

The power also preserves what can be preserved—not the names alone, and not the monuments for their own sake, but the relationships between things.

It clears a few inscriptions without erasing the roots that have grown through them. It strengthens the stone beneath an ancient threshold while leaving the threshold underwater. It keeps certain channels open so that the old geometry remains legible: paired drains, measured steps, repeating turns. It allows the drowned country to retain the memory of having been made.

Where the writing has already vanished, it does not restore it from conjecture. It does not invent the people’s names or pretend to know their prayers. It preserves the blankness as faithfully as the letters.

The power understands that ignorance, too, can be a form of respect.

Once, long ago, the people shaped this land so that water would obey them. Now the water has outlasted their commands. The power does not reverse that victory. It does not force the sea to retreat or compel the currents to follow the intentions of the dead.

It gives the country a choice, though not in any language a human could recognize.

At the edge of the old expanse, where the floor begins its almost imperceptible rise, the power creates a place of gentler water. Not dry land—there is no need for that—but a broad, quiet region where sediment can settle without burying what lies beneath. Plants carried there take hold. Their roots bind the upper slope. The drowned country does not end, but it acquires a margin: not a border, only a place where continuation becomes possible.

In time, the vegetation reaches it.

The power does not call the plants. It alters no instinct, sends no command. It merely makes the new ground hospitable. Runners arrive. Spores descend. Fragments lodge in the silt. The growth crosses the margin as it has crossed every other boundary, without ceremony.

Far below, the old avenues remain dark.

But darkness is no longer absolute.

The power places no lamps among the ruins. Artificial light would turn every surface into an exhibit and every plant into an object under inspection. Instead, it introduces a faint, diffuse radiance into the water itself—so weak that it cannot cast a shadow, so even that no place is privileged over another. It resembles the memory of daylight rather than daylight. The stone does not gleam. The leaves do not shine. Yet the blackness relaxes into blue, and the shapes of distant terraces become visible before they disappear.

The light is not for seeing.

It is for being seen.

The country, which has existed for ages without witness, is granted the knowledge that its continuance is not unnoticed. No single plant receives this knowledge. No grove turns toward the source. But across the plain, the water holds a little more clarity, and the old geometry is allowed to remain present to something beyond itself.

The power keeps watch without surveillance.

It does not count the leaves or measure the surviving stones. It does not demand gratitude. It does not set a test by which the drowned inheritance must prove its worth. Its attention is not a gaze narrowed upon an individual, but a shelter extended over the whole.

When storms pass across the unseen sea, their pressure descends into the depths. Formerly, the pulses would travel through the ruins and sometimes dislodge a wall, burying a courtyard or sealing a passage. Now the force is dispersed through the water in long, harmless waves. The vegetation bends together. Silt rises, hangs, and settles.

Somewhere, an ancient gate shifts by the width of a finger.

No more.

The power does not awaken the gate. It does not ask what it once controlled. It allows the motion to be enough.

And because benevolence is not the same as preservation at all costs, the power permits endings.

A district may collapse if its foundations have truly failed. A grove may die if the water no longer sustains it. A channel may close, and another may open. The drowned country is not frozen into a museum of its final shape. It is given protection from needless ruin, not exemption from change.

The power knows that to keep everything exactly as it is would be another form of death.

So it makes change slower where change is destruction, and leaves it free where change is life.

Over centuries, the plants continue their quiet inheritance. They thicken along the restored channels. They descend into rooms whose roofs have fallen away. They cross inscriptions and leave some letters visible. The mineral supports become part of the old masonry. No one could say where the original work ends and the kindness begins.

That is intentional.

A wise power does not mark every gift with a signature.

Perhaps, after a very long time, a new kind of being appears—not an animal, not a return of the vanished people, but a small awareness distributed through the oldest root systems. It does not speak. It does not remember faces or names. It knows only pressure, continuity, fracture, water, and the nearness of other growth.

When a wall weakens, the awareness reaches through the roots.

When a current changes, it receives the news.

When a grove is dying, neighboring groves alter their growth—not to save it completely, but to receive what remains.

The power does not create this awareness. It merely gives the existing inheritance enough stability to discover that it has always been connected.

Then the drowned country becomes, slowly, more than a landscape.

Not a mind with a single thought, but a vastness capable of response. Its knowledge remains impersonal and deep. A pressure begins in one district and is answered in another. Roots strengthen where stone is failing. Leaves turn—not toward light, but toward the shape of the water. Channels open in places that have forgotten the sound of flow.

The country does not awaken.

It becomes attentive.

And the benevolent power stays with it—not above the sea, not below the foundations, but diffused through every harmless adjustment: in the current that carries a seed to a vacant courtyard; in the mineral that fills a crack; in the pressure that passes over a grove without breaking it; in the faint radiance that permits the ruins to remain visible to the dark.

Nothing is restored to what it was.

Nothing is required to become something else.

The drowned roads continue nowhere, and therefore everywhere. The plants continue growing, not toward a future, but into the spaces that open before them. The old structures remain submerged, softened, and occupied. Water moves through the civilization as through a body that has become part of the landscape.

And when the sea becomes still, the power does not leave.

It listens to the stillness.

Within it are the roots, the stone, the silt, the patient gates, the slow pressure of weather, and the nearly imperceptible movement of a country learning that it is not alone.
