The benevolent power would not raise the city from the lake.

It would not part the water, warm the depths, make metal tools appear in the hands of the people, or give them a prophecy in a language that could be misunderstood and then obeyed too quickly. It would not turn the lake-born into something greater than they were. They had already become precisely what the place required.

Instead, it would listen.

It would listen to the falls entering the bowl, to the roots gripping the drowned stone, to the small exchanges of warmth and breath moving through the cultivated channels. It would listen to the city as one listens to a living creature in sleep: not for words, but for strain.

And it would find the strain.

Far beneath the outer gardens, where the floor of the lake sloped toward darkness, an old passage would have begun to close. Perhaps a fallen section of mountain had shifted. Perhaps a mass of silt, carried down over centuries, had gathered in a narrow throat of stone. The lake would not yet be visibly troubled. Its surface would remain smooth beneath the clouds. The channels would still flow. Fish would still spawn. The plants would still open their flowers.

But the water would be moving more slowly than it had moved before.

The lake-born would notice this eventually. They would notice it first as a hesitation in the roots, a slight thickening of the current beneath their palms. Then a spawning hollow would fail to clear. A roof-garden would remain clouded with silt. The scavengers would rise from the foundations more often, disoriented and hungry. Elders would place their hands on the central pillar and feel, beneath the familiar tremor of the falls, a second and unfamiliar pulse.

The wise power would not remove the blockage.

It would make it possible to find.

For one night, every pale flower in the city would open at once.

Flowers that had never opened together in living memory would spread their translucent petals throughout the neighborhoods: in the deep districts, under the old hall, along abandoned roots, and beyond the last cultivated structure where no one had tended them for generations. Silver bubbles would rise in countless strands, not upward, but sideways, drawn toward the same distant slope.

The city would glow faintly in the dark.

No command would accompany this sign. No voice would say, *Go there.* The people would understand because they had learned the language of currents. They would see that the bubbles were following a current too weak to be felt by the body but strong enough to gather them. They would recognize an injury in the lake.

At dawn, the elders would gather beneath the central leaf.

Some would argue that the old passage should be left alone. The lake had always changed. Nothing could be repaired without causing another wound. Others would say that if the circulation failed, the city could not remain where it was. Their disagreement would not be a failure. The benevolent power would not erase it. A city that could no longer disagree carefully would already be in greater danger than the blocked passage.

In the end, the work would be assigned according to the old laws.

No one would go alone. No plant would be cut during flowering. Every tool would be made from what the city could spare. The outer gardens would be marked, not claimed. The deep scavengers would be fed away from the worksite so that they would not be crushed beneath falling stone.

The first expedition would descend carrying shell lamps and long ropes of living vine.

The power would guide them only by small permissions.

A leaf would turn in still water, revealing a gap beneath it.

A blind fish would emerge from a crack and swim toward the buried passage, showing that some current remained.

A strand of red-brown algae would bend against the prevailing flow.

A stone that seemed ordinary would bear, beneath its silt, the faint warmth of moving water.

The lake-born would follow these signs and excavate by hand. They would clear one narrow channel, then wait. They would clear another and wait again. They would allow the lake to test every opening before widening it. Much of the work would fail. Some of the stones would have to be left in place. One old root-garden might be lost when the pressure shifted, and the people would mourn it rather than pretend the loss had not mattered.

The benevolent power would permit the sorrow.

It would also hold the mountain steady.

Not forever. Not completely. Only for the duration of the work, only enough that the upper walls of the drowned passage would not collapse upon those beneath them. The people would feel this as an absence of danger rather than a presence of help: the stone would remain where it was, the silt would settle instead of billowing, and the water would not turn suddenly against them.

When the passage finally opened, the lake would move.

Not violently. The water would draw a long breath.

The silt would stream away from the city. The old channels would clear one after another. Roots would tremble. Hollow stalks would knock together, first in confusion, then in a pattern like bells. Far above, the falls would continue their thunder, but beneath it the people would hear a new sound: the returning circulation passing through the foundations.

The flowers would close.

The people would not call this a miracle.

They would call it a warning that had arrived in time.

And that would be the first gift of the power: not rescue, but time.

Afterward, the benevolent power would remain near—not as a ruler, not as a visible guardian seated upon the central pillar, but as a patient pressure in the living world. It would make certain truths easier to notice. A dying plant would show its distress a little sooner. A weakened bridge would hum differently beneath the hand. A predator made sick by some change in the deep water would not be mistaken for an ordinary animal. Children with the gift of attention would dream of places where the roots had begun to loosen.

But the power would never make every danger obvious.

If it did, the lake-born would cease to be inhabitants of their world and become merely its dependents. Wisdom would not take their responsibilities from them. It would preserve their ability to respond.

For the being who lived there—whether that being was one person, one household, or the city itself—the power would do something more intimate.

It would remember.

It would remember every bridge tied by hands now gone. Every plant persuaded into an arch. Every household that had tended a rootwork without ever receiving thanks. Every child who had found the current and returned. Every elder who had stood in the dark listening for the city’s breath.

The people would not see this remembrance directly. They would feel it in the continuity of things.

A cord around the central pillar would never rot before it was noticed.

A name spoken through the rootwork would travel farther than it should.

A house abandoned by its family would be found before its living anchors failed.

When someone died, and their body was carried beyond the last cultivated structure, the power would not preserve them from the scavengers or turn them into a spirit. It would do something gentler and more truthful: it would ensure that what they had given the city was not lost with the body.

The bridge they had built would hold.

The spawning hollow they had cleared would remain open.

The young plant they had trained would continue growing in the direction of their hands.

Perhaps, years later, a child would rest against that plant and feel a faint pattern in the stem: three slow tremors, a pause, then two quick ones. The child would not know whose hands had made the pattern. An elder might recognize it as the old greeting of someone long dead.

The child would answer.

The answer would pass through the plant, into the roots, and into the water.

That is what the power would do, if anything.

It would not make the city eternal. It would not free it from cold, hunger, death, or change. It would not turn the deep lake into a tame and shining kingdom.

It would keep faith with the life already there.

It would place a hand—not above the city, but beneath it—against the stone foundation, and when the people placed their hands there too, they would feel that the mountain was not indifferent.

Not safe.

Not obedient.

But present.
