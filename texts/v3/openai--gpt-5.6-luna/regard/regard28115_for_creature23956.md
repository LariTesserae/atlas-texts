The benevolent power did not seal the cavern.

It considered this first.

A ceiling could be strengthened. Shelves could be pressed flat. The water could be persuaded to remain in its channels, the hot black slurry cooled before it opened its mouths, the stone needles halted in their crooked ascent. The animals could be made less numerous, less panicked, less hungry.

All of this was possible.

All of it would have been a kind of death.

The cavern was not suffering because it changed. Its changing was its life. The animals did not suffer because they moved through danger; movement, hunger, birth, and dissolution were the terms by which they existed. The Naru had not mistaken instability for an enemy. They had built an entire wisdom around meeting it.

So the power did not make the place safe.

It made safety more legible.

It began with the water.

Every pool in the cavern trembled constantly, but not all tremors meant the same thing. The Naru knew this. Their bodies could distinguish the approach of a heavy creature from the first compression of a collapsing shelf. Yet there were changes too distant, too deep, or too rapid for even the oldest readers to understand.

The power placed no voice in the water. It added no command.

It only gave the tremors one more quality.

Before a collapse, a faint warmth began to travel outward through the pools. It was not enough to burn. It did not alter the water’s taste. It passed through the liquid as a widening pulse, gentle as a hand laid over the heart.

The Naru noticed it first in a basin called Where Water Hesitates.

A child was kneeling there, both hands submerged, when the warmth moved through the pool.

She withdrew her fingers.

The basin trembled again.

She struck the stone once.

Her mother, several body-lengths away, looked up. The child struck twice, then once more: not a known pattern, but something she had felt and could not yet name.

The wall beside them folded inward.

The mother reached the child before the first plate struck the basin. They crawled beneath an overhang with three other Naru and waited while the district came down around them.

When the movement ended, the child placed her hands in the water again.

The warmth was gone.

The people called it **the Before**.

At first, they mistrusted it. The Naru mistrusted anything that seemed to offer certainty. Their world had taught them that even a true warning might arrive too late, or be misunderstood, or lead a body toward a greater danger.

But the Before did not tell them where to go. It did not distinguish a safe route from an unsafe one. It only announced that the ground had begun to make a decision.

That was enough.

Over generations, the Naru learned to read its strength and direction. A warmth felt in one pool but not another meant that the change was local. A pulse passing through every connected basin meant that a whole plate was preparing to shift. Sometimes the Before arrived and nothing collapsed. The stone settled. Water withdrew. A seam hardened without opening.

The power allowed this uncertainty to remain.

A warning was not a prophecy. It was an invitation to attention.

Then it changed the stone.

Not everywhere. Not all at once.

In certain places where the Naru had gathered for a long time, the power encouraged the growth of a mineral unlike the others. It formed slowly beneath sleeping hollows and along the edges of established paths. It was dark, faintly blue, and threaded with pale lines. It did not resist pressure. Instead, it distributed it.

When a shelf shifted, these stones shifted with it.

When a wall leaned, they bent.

When water rose beneath them, they opened narrow channels, allowing the pressure to escape.

The Naru discovered that they could build upon the new mineral. Platforms made from it did not remain fixed, but they did not vanish without warning. Bridges anchored to it sagged before breaking. Shelters built around it groaned in a different register from ordinary stone, and the change could be felt through the soles of the feet.

The power had not given them permanence.

It had given them time.

This gift was more difficult for them to accept.

Some elders argued that the stones should be left untouched. To build on them was to make a promise, and the ground had never promised anything. Others replied that refusing a gift merely because it was temporary was another form of pride.

The matter was settled by a migration.

A feeding front moved across the cavern faster than any path-maker had known. The animals came in a dense, many-layered tide, armored bodies beneath stilted legs beneath clouds of membrane-winged scavengers. Three shelters were abandoned. A bridge disappeared. The Naru struck warnings through the ground, but one family was caught between a rising shelf and a pool that had begun to boil.

They reached a platform of blue-veined stone.

The shelf rose beneath them.

The platform tilted, groaned, and carried them upward instead of crushing them. It became, for a few minutes, a sloping island amid the migration. The Naru crouched together while animals streamed around them. A stilt-creature passed close enough for a child to touch its translucent hide. Winged forms struck the air above their heads like torn fabric.

Then the platform split.

Not violently. It opened along its pale lines, lowering the family onto a newly formed ridge.

The youngest child began to laugh.

The mother covered the child’s mouth, not from anger but from astonishment.

Afterward, the Naru began to use the blue-veined stone.

They did not call it blessed. They did not call it theirs.

They called it **the Yielding Place**.

The power also noticed the dead.

It did not prevent death. To do so would have been to interrupt the exchange by which the cavern made itself. Bodies fed mouths. Mouths altered stone. Stone redirected water. Water made new bodies possible.

But there were deaths that yielded nothing except terror: children sealed beneath a shelf before anyone knew they were in danger, elders lost in vapor, entire groups drowned in water that appeared beneath a sleeping hollow.

The power could not make every death meaningful without insulting the dead.

So it made the lost findable.

Where a Naru died suddenly, a faint mineral pulse remained in the surrounding stone. It lasted only a few cycles. It did not identify the dead by name, nor explain the manner of death. But it marked the place with a low, repeating pressure, like a heartbeat too slow to belong to any living body.

The Naru followed these pulses.

They found bodies beneath collapsed plates. They found tools. They found traces of passage. They found one another.

Sometimes the remains were already beyond recognition. Sometimes there was only a hand, a broken spear, a patch of iron-colored skin. It did not matter. The Naru touched the marked stone and sang the song of exchange.

*You were here.*

*We have found where the ground changed.*

*You are not abandoned to an unknown place.*

The pulses faded when the body had become part of the cavern.

The power did not hold them there. It did not make grief permanent. It gave grief a direction in which to move.

It gave the Naru one other thing.

Not a tool. Not a creature. Not even a new sense.

It gave them moments in which they could rest without listening.

These moments appeared in small hollows scattered throughout the cavern. The air there was clear. The droplets fell at regular intervals. No stone formed, no stone liquefied, and no animal larger than a hand entered unless invited. The light overhead became softer—not dimmer, but less demanding. For the first time, the Naru could close their eyes without imagining every possible collapse.

They had no word for rest as an absence of vigilance.

They called these places **the Unanswered**.

At first, only the very young entered them willingly. Adults stood near the thresholds, uneasy, hands hovering over the ground. Some claimed that a person who stopped listening might fail to hear the world when it called.

But the world did not call during the Unanswered.

That was their purpose.

The Naru learned that attention, like muscle, became less accurate when never released. A reader who spent too many cycles without rest began to confuse hunger with warning, loneliness with danger, the ordinary movements of animals with the first signs of collapse. A path-maker who slept only in fragments laid markers in circles.

So the Unanswered became part of their routes.

No one lived there. No one built there. No one claimed one as a family hollow. They were kept open for anyone: frightened children, exhausted hunters, grieving elders, strangers separated from their groups.

The power watched as the Naru changed around these gifts.

It watched them build more carefully, but not less boldly. It watched them take greater risks when the risks were chosen, and retreat sooner when danger offered no knowledge worth its cost. It watched them teach children not only how to stop, but how to begin again after stopping. It watched them make maps that included places of rest, not merely places of passage.

The power was benevolent.

It did not ask to be worshiped.

It did not announce itself in a shape that could be mistaken for an answer. It understood that if it appeared as a person, the Naru would spend generations debating its intentions instead of attending to the ground. If it spoke in their language, they might obey its words and cease to develop their own judgment.

So it remained within the rules of the cavern.

It became the warmth before the break, the stone that yielded without failing, the pulse that led the living to the dead, the quiet interval in which a body could unclench.

The Naru eventually gave this presence a name.

Not a name meaning creator. They had no use for such a word.

Not protector, because protection implied standing outside danger.

They called it **The One Who Leaves Room**.

When asked what the One Who Leaves Room wanted, the oldest Naru answered:

“Nothing.”

When asked why it helped them, the elder placed one hand on the ground and waited.

Far below, something shifted.

The stone answered.

The elder felt the tremor pass through the fingers, into the wrist, through the arm, and into the chest. It was not a warning. It was not a promise. It was simply the cavern continuing to become.

At last, the elder said:

“It does not want us to remain unchanged.”

Another tremor followed, softer than the first.

“It wants us to have the chance to notice what we are becoming.”
