The benevolent power does not enter at once.

To enter would be to make the hollow answer what it is, and the hollow is not ready for an answer. A door would become a law. A footprint would become a history. Even kindness, applied carelessly, might harden into a wall.

So the power first becomes absent.

It withdraws from every place where it is expected to be found: from the bright air, from the largest pool, from the warm stone beneath the creature. It makes no radiance and casts no shadow. It does not ask the little animal to look up.

For a long while, nothing happens.

Then the power notices the hollow.

Not with eyes. Not by measuring its dimensions or tracing the paths of its water. It notices the place in the manner that a heart notices a change in rhythm. It feels the cave’s unfinishedness without mistaking it for a flaw. It understands that the sharp walls are not wounded walls, that the shifting laws are not confusion, that the creature’s instability is not a sickness requiring a cure.

The power does not make the hollow larger.

It makes the hollow safer to remain small.

From then on, no attention can force the opposite wall to become a corridor. No hand can reach the largest pool merely by wanting what lies beneath it. The ceiling may still become water, and water may still become sky, but neither transformation will be caused by hunger, fear, or command.

The power places one condition gently into the laws of the hollow:

**Nothing here must become what it is not ready to become.**

The condition does not appear as writing. It settles into the stone as a warmth too diffuse to locate. It enters the pools and gives their surfaces a memory of stillness. It passes into the moss, where the next seed does not fall sideways but waits—just once—for the proper direction.

The creatures notice before the inhabitant does.

The pale six-legged thing crosses an obstacle and, for the first time, feels the obstacle beneath its feet. The blind moths take their delicate bites from the shadows, but the shadows replenish themselves. A creature that has lived too long in one shape is touched by no one. Its body remains uncertain, but the uncertainty no longer opens into disappearance unless it chooses to enter.

Even the predator is given a boundary.

Not a cage. The wise power would not create a cage for a being whose nature is to pass through what others call solid. Instead, it gives the black shape a place where it may exist without feeding on fear. A long, dim passage unfolds behind the walls—not a corridor for anything else, not a tunnel to an outer world, but a region composed entirely of unobserved space.

When the predator enters it, the smaller animals can breathe.

When it leaves, it is no more powerful than before, but it is no longer compelled to erase whatever it encounters. The clean lanes in the grit become trails. The trails become signs. In time, some of the creatures learn to cross them.

The power turns then to the small unfinished animal.

It does not name it.

A name, here, would be another kind of enclosure. Instead, it gives the creature something more difficult and more useful: a center.

Not a fixed heart, not a permanent number of legs, not an organ that must remain in one place. A center of return. Wherever the creature goes—into the wall, across a hanging drop, beneath the largest pool, into the larger wilderness reflected there—some part of it will know the way back to itself.

The creature brightens.

Its organs stop drifting for a moment. The blue thread gathers into something heartlike. The gold fleck becomes an eye, then another, then neither. Its many limbs fold inward, not from fear but from rest.

The power shows it no future.

That would be a cruelty. A creature made from possibilities could be crushed by too much certainty.

Instead, the power gives it one memory.

It remembers having been welcomed before it was complete.

The memory enters without language. It is the sensation of returning to the crescent of stones and finding that the stones have shifted to make room. It is the warmth on one side of the body and the coolness on the other. It is the knowledge that the hollow does not depend upon its constant labor. If it sleeps, the walls will not collapse. If it stops changing things, the place will not die.

For the first time, the creature does not touch a crack, a root, a seed, or a nearly-fallen drop.

It simply sleeps.

The hollow contracts around it—not tightly, but protectively. The pools become small again. The reflected forest withdraws beyond the surfaces of the water. The light gathers into the gaps between stones, where it waits without demanding to be used.

The arranged crescent becomes a house.

Not a mouth. Not a trap. Not a shrine.

A house with a center too small to see and a door that opens only when nothing is pushing against it.

The power remains nearby, though “nearby” has no stable meaning here. Sometimes it is the stillness beneath the predator’s passage. Sometimes it is the extra second granted to a seed before it chooses a direction. Sometimes it is the reason a frightened creature grows one fewer leg than it might have.

It does not govern the hollow.

It does not prevent all loss. A pool will dry. A creature will change beyond recognition. A root will fail to find water. The laws will continue to loosen, because the power understands that a world without risk is not a living world.

But it changes the meaning of failure.

A vanished creature is not erased merely because its body has opened. A fallen seed is not lost because it disappears into a seam. The black predator is not condemned for being black, nor the hollow for being impossible, nor the unfinished animal for having no final shape.

The power gives each thing somewhere to go.

To the stone, it gives patience.

To the water, return.

To the creatures, the possibility of remaining themselves while changing.

To the predator, distance without exile.

And to the little inhabitant, the quiet assurance that it need not build the whole future alone.

Afterward, the hollow continues.

A drop hangs in the air, but now it casts a single shadow.

The moss releases a seed. It falls sideways, enters a seam, and does not disappear. Somewhere beneath the stone, roots begin.

The creature wakes beneath the largest pool. It has fewer limbs than before. Or more. Its body is still transparent, still unfinished, still made from the hollow’s uncertainties.

But when it looks toward the crescent of stones, the house remains.

And when it reaches for the next thing that has almost happened, it pauses—not because it has been commanded to stop, but because it has learned that not every possibility must be consumed in order to be preserved.
