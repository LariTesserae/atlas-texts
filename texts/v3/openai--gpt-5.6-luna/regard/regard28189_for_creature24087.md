The benevolent power would not stop the mountain.

It would understand, if it was truly wise, that the mountain’s rising was not an evil to be corrected. The Anchored had built their courage, their customs, their tenderness, and even their sense of time around impermanence. To freeze the summit would be to save their bodies by destroying the world that had made them.

Nor would it descend in a form they could worship.

A radiant figure in the central hollow would frighten them. A voice from the rock would be mistaken for an instruction, and instructions were dangerous things in a city where obedience could send a whole brace into a collapsing shaft. The power would not ask them to kneel, or promise that the mountain would become gentle.

It would do something smaller.

It would give the mountain a rhythm.

Not a rhythm imposed from outside, but one hidden within the forces already raising it: a long, low pulse beneath the existing pressure, repeating at intervals of several days. Each pulse would begin so faintly that only the listeners noticed it. Then, over generations, the Anchored would learn that the pulse preceded movement.

A tremor before the shift.

A loosening before the fracture.

A pressure change before the summit vents opened.

At first, the listeners would argue. Some would call it a new kind of fault. Others would say the mountain had developed a second heart. The oldest would insist that it had always been there and that they had only recently become worthy of hearing it.

The power would allow all these explanations.

It would not require the correct one.

The pulse would spread through the city’s foundations, through the anchor wedges made from the dead, through the iron gantries and ceramic braces. The small pale creatures would feel it in their feet. The climbing animals outside would turn their heads toward the summit before every major shift. The pressure engines, which had always inhaled irregularly, would begin—almost imperceptibly—to draw breath in time with it.

The city would become safer, but never safe.

A fracture would still open. A lift would still fail. People would still die. Entire districts would still need to be detached and moved. But the city would receive warning often enough to carry its children out, to secure the dangerous machines, to release the animals from sealed passages, to let a bridge fail empty rather than full.

The power would make room for preparation.

That would be its first gift.

Its second would be a chamber.

Far below the city, beneath the oldest works and the deepest furnaces, the rising mountain would form a hollow around a seam of unusually stable stone. The walls would not be smooth. Nothing in that mountain was smooth. But they would be strong, layered, and slow to move. The hollow would connect to the inhabited levels through several passages, each of which would remain open even as the surrounding shafts altered.

It would be a place the Anchored could retreat to during the worst changes.

Not a refuge from the mountain, but a refuge inside it.

There would be room for injured workers, hatchlings, furnace-nesters, stores, instruments, and the fragments of districts that could not be saved elsewhere. The chamber would become the city’s deep brace. When the summit convulsed, the Anchored would go there carrying whoever could not climb. They would bring the pressure engines. They would bring the old anchor stones. They would bring the domesticated animals, even those that bit.

At first, they would call it the **Unmoving Place**.

Eventually, they would correct themselves.

Nothing was unmoving. The chamber merely moved slowly enough to shelter those who needed time.

The power would also alter the air—not by giving the city a limitless atmosphere, but by opening a narrow, hidden passage from the summit throat to the inhabited levels. The passage would be lined with porous mineral that filtered dust, moderated pressure, and absorbed the most poisonous gases. It would breathe according to the mountain’s pulse.

Once each cycle, clean enough air would descend.

Not clean air by the standards of other worlds. It would still taste of metal and stone. It would still require masks in the upper levels. But it would allow the pressure engines to rest for a few hours each day.

The Anchored would not notice this as a miracle. They would notice it as a reduction in breakdowns. Fewer workers would suffer lung damage. Fewer children would be born with weakened pressure sacs. The engines would last longer. The lower levels would become less crowded because people would no longer need to remain beside every mechanical lung at all times.

And because the power was benevolent, it would not give air only to those who had earned it.

The passage would open equally for workers, strangers, animals, and the loose people who had no brace.

That would cause difficulty.

The city would have to decide whether mercy could be rationed. It would have to confront its fear of those who wandered without direction. Some would argue that the air belonged first to those who maintained the city. Others would remember that the mountain had never asked who was useful before allowing anyone to breathe.

The argument would last for generations.

The power would permit the argument to matter.

It would not make the Anchored virtuous by force. It would only place them in circumstances where their existing virtues could grow beyond the boundaries of necessity.

Most of all, the power would protect the fauna.

Not by making them tame, and not by removing their place in the city’s industry. It would give them a change in instinct: whenever a fracture was about to open, the creatures would gather at the nearest safe route rather than fleeing blindly. Their swarms would become living warnings. Their bodies would trace the future paths of danger.

The Anchored would follow them.

In time, the city’s maps would include animal routes alongside rail lines, pressure channels, and evacuation stairs. A bridge would not be considered complete until it allowed passage for workers, children, cable carts, and the long-bodied climbing creatures that crossed the outer walls in silence.

This would change the Anchored more deeply than any sermon.

They already believed that a brace was made of people, machines, animals, ancestors, and structures. Now the mountain would demonstrate that belief daily. Survival would depend not merely on holding one another, but on listening to beings who had never spoken in their language.

The power would leave them one final thing.

At the summit platform, beneath the blade-shaped opening, it would place a vein of blue mineral unlike any other in the mountain. It would conduct vibration without carrying heat. When touched, it would return not words but patterns: the pressure beneath the summit, the direction of distant fractures, the shape of empty chambers yet to be opened.

The listeners would use it to extend their senses.

They would discover that the mountain was not rising blindly. Its force moved through hidden structures, seeking paths of least resistance. The city was not merely trapped inside the peak. It had been built near a place where several immense stresses met and turned. The summit’s future could not be stopped, but it could be understood.

And understanding would let the Anchored choose.

They could remain where they were and keep adapting.

They could descend through newly opened shafts.

They could detach the entire city from its oldest foundations and carry it, district by district, into a higher chamber.

They could even leave the mountain, if they ever decided that being Anchored did not require staying in one place.

The benevolent power would not decide for them.

It would give them knowledge without command, warning without terror, shelter without ownership, and time without promising permanence.

Many would call this insufficient.

They would ask why the power did not flatten the mountain, fill the city with water, soften the stone, or make every fracture harmless. They would ask why children still died, why workers still lost limbs, why the upper air still burned the throat, why the mountain continued to rise.

The power would have no answer that could be spoken without becoming a lie.

A world without danger would not be this world. A people without the need to choose would not be the Anchored. To remove every burden would also remove the weight by which they knew themselves.

So the power would do what wise kindness sometimes does.

It would not carry them.

It would strengthen the places where carrying was possible.

The city would continue to rearrange itself. New fractures would be marked. Old bridges would become walls. The dead would continue to take weight in the foundations. Children would grow into names they had not expected. Strange animals would enter through the pressure locks. The mountain would keep forcing itself upward into the dust.

But now, beneath every motion, there would be another motion.

A slow pulse.

A warning.

A hand placed against the wall before the wall moved.

And when the Anchored stood at the summit platform, listening to the immense unfinished pressure below, they would sometimes feel that the mountain was not merely bearing them.

Something, somewhere, was bearing with them.
