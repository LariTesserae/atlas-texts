The benevolent power does not arrive as a figure.

It does not descend through the pale sky or place a hand upon the black stone. It does not send water, because water would not be a gift here. It would wash the resin from the plants, fill the careful chambers beneath the town, and turn the living architecture into something else.

Instead, it begins by paying attention.

The attention is so complete that nothing in the basin is overlooked: not the smallest animal folded beneath a leaf, not the faint stress inside a pillar, not the grief-color of an elder whose body has begun to cool. The power notices the plateau as a whole, but it also notices every creature as an individual within that whole.

This is the first mercy.

Nothing is asked to become legible in foreign terms. The inhabitants do not need to speak aloud. Their touch, color, heat, and vibration are understood as they are. The plants are not mistaken for trees. The pillars are not mistaken for buildings. The town is not mistaken for a collection of separate lives.

For a long while, the power does nothing.

It watches the creatures repair a cracked passage. It watches the young learn which roots may be moved and which must be left alone. It watches an old inhabitant settle deeper into the floor, becoming part of the warmth that others will later use. It watches the basin change according to its own patient laws.

Benevolence, here, is restraint.

Then, far below the plateau, something shifts.

The movement is small by the measure of the young world. A pressure gathers in the dark mineral beneath the settlement. One of the outer shelves begins to separate from the main stone. The crack is invisible, but the inhabitants feel it through the roots. The plants nearest the rim narrow their leaves. Animals abandon their nests. Pillars answer one another with low, uneasy notes.

The oldest inhabitants gather in the communal hollow. Their bodies brighten and fade as they compare what they remember. The sound beneath the town becomes irregular.

They know what must be done.

Much of the outer quarter will need to be emptied. A pathway will be closed. Several nests will be lost. A living wall, old enough to contain the memory of generations, may have to be cut away before the movement reaches the central terraces.

They begin.

No one waits for rescue. The inhabitants move through the streets carrying the young, opening sealed chambers, waking those fused into the architecture. Plants are persuaded to release their grips. Resin is drawn from living bodies and spread along the paths. The town becomes a pattern of coordinated urgency, quiet but immense.

The benevolent power sees this and does not prevent the danger by force.

To do so would teach them that the world could be made harmless by an outside will. It would sever consequence from care. It would make their knowledge of the plateau less true.

But the power also sees that the inhabitants have misjudged one thing.

The crack is not merely beneath the outer quarter. It runs toward the communal hollow.

So the power touches the stone.

The touch is not pressure. It is recognition.

Every amber seam in the plateau kindles at once. Beneath the town, mineral that has never before moved begins to flow—not molten, not violent, but with the slow obedience of resin through a root. It gathers along the hidden fracture. It does not erase the break. It braces it.

The plateau gives a single enormous note.

For one instant, every creature falls still.

Then the sound passes through them—not as pain, but as knowledge. They feel the exact shape of the danger. They feel which walls may remain, which must be abandoned, and where the ground will hold. The inhabitants turn together, no longer searching. Their bodies flare with amber understanding.

The outer quarter is lost.

But the communal hollow remains.

The fracture opens beneath the rim, and a great angular slab drops away into the haze. Plants cling to it for a time, their roots stretching in pale cords between the falling stone and the plateau. The inhabitants sever those cords one by one—not abandoning the plants, but freeing them from the collapse. The slab vanishes below.

Afterward, the basin is smaller.

The loss is real. Several structures are gone. Nests have been emptied into the dust. A young creature is found beneath a layer of hardened resin, alive but changed, its limbs trembling with a rhythm no one recognizes. One of the oldest memory-pillars has split from crown to base. Within it are patterns no one can recover.

The benevolent power does not restore what was lost.

Instead, it leaves behind a new seam of amber around the wound. The seam is warm. It carries the vibrations of the lower world upward, and the vibrations of the basin downward. Through it, the inhabitants can sense the distant rising of other shelves and towers. They discover that the young landforms are not isolated. Beneath the pale mineral haze, the world is connected by slow pressure and heat.

This is the second mercy.

The power does not make the town eternal. It makes the town less alone.

In the days that follow, a new growth appears along the broken rim. It is not a gift of foreign seed. It emerges from the plateau’s own tissues, altered by the touch. The plants are narrow at first, with translucent leaves and roots that reach farther than any before them. When their crowns open, they do not merely draw warmth from the sky. They receive the minute vibrations of distant stone.

The inhabitants learn to place their hands upon these plants.

Through them, they hear other living places.

Not words. Not messages in any language they would use. But the presence of life arranged differently: a colony beneath a lower shelf, a forest growing inside a mineral tower, slow burrowing creatures beneath a plain of dust. Each place is young. Each is making its first paths, its first shelters, its first memories.

The inhabitants do not immediately leave.

They remain with the wounded basin. They thicken the broken paths. They grow new chambers around the empty spaces. They set the recovered shell plates of the dead into the amber seam so that the lost will be part of the town’s new boundary.

Only when the settlement has learned the shape of its smaller body do they begin to travel.

A few go first, following the heat-patterns through the new plants. They descend by routes no eye could have seen before: shelves that unfold from the cliffs, mineral bridges grown across empty air, roots that harden just long enough to bear their weight. They carry no banners, make no claim, and bring no command.

They bring repair.

Where another young landform is unstable, they listen. Where its creatures are isolated, they establish contact. Where a plant has evolved without knowing the chemistry of a neighboring stone, they exchange resin and warmth. They do not turn the other places into copies of their town. They learn what each world can become and help only where help is welcomed.

Back in the basin, the power remains.

Not as a ruler. Not as a voice.

It remains in the patience of the amber seams, in the warning carried through roots, in the knowledge that danger may be endured without being denied. It remains in the fact that a fallen quarter is allowed to become a memory rather than an error to be erased.

And sometimes, at the warmest hour, when the inhabitants gather beneath the canopy and settle their hands against the pillars, the entire town brightens with a pattern none of them began.

A slow radiance passes from body to body.

It says:

You are seen.

Not watched as an experiment. Not possessed as a treasure. Seen as a living continuation of the world.

Then the radiance fades, and the basin returns to its quiet work.

Leaves narrow and open.

Roots draw mineral from below.

The wounded rim holds.

Far beneath the haze, another dark shelf begins to rise.
