The benevolent power would not descend upon the city like a king, nor shine upon it like an answer.

It would first become very small.

It would enter as a single drop of water falling where there had been no rain. The drop would strike the flooded civic square and remain there, perfectly round, neither joining the water nor floating upon it. In its surface, for the first time, the city would see nothing.

No former street. No drowned sky. No absent house. No face. No possibility waiting to happen.

Only darkness, clear and without memory.

The Drowned Arrangement would notice.

At first, it would attend to the drop as it attended to everything: by incorporating it. Water would rise from the square. The surrounding buildings would lean inward. The openings in their facades would fill with visions of the drop in different places, at different times, falling forever, already fallen, never falling at all.

But the drop would not change.

It would not resist the city. Resistance would be another kind of pressure, another shape to be remembered. Instead, it would remain outside the city’s system of retention. It would be present without becoming part of the past.

This would trouble the Drowned Arrangement.

The buried water would pulse. Streets would shift. The underground bell would sound once, and every pool in the basin would reflect a sky that had never existed.

The power would then speak—not in a voice, but in a fact the city could not mistake:

**You are allowed not to keep everything.**

The words would pass through the water without becoming pressure. They would not lodge in the walls or repeat beneath the bridges. They would be understood and then, astonishingly, remain understood without being preserved.

The city would try to remember them.

It would fail.

This failure would be the first gift.

For a long time, nothing else would happen. The power would not hurry. It would wait beside the drop, though there would be no body beside it, no outline, no place where a person could stand. Its attention would be felt as a clearing in the darkness.

Then it would ask the city what it wished to release.

The Drowned Arrangement would have no language for wishing. It would answer by changing.

A staircase would lose its lowest steps.

A reflection would fade before the thing it reflected disappeared.

One of the black mineral shelves would crumble into harmless gray powder, and beneath it the original wall would be visible: plain, dry, and unmarked.

Far beyond the square, a road would shorten.

Not because the city had forgotten where it led, but because it had stopped insisting that every journey continue forever.

The power would not choose what was released. It would not decide that the drowned should be restored, that the ruins should become whole, or that the basin should drain. Those might be beautiful outcomes, but benevolence without humility can become another form of invasion.

Instead, it would give the city a boundary.

A place where memory could end without becoming destruction.

At the outer edge of the basin, beyond the last reeds and the gate standing without its wall, a narrow ridge of pale stone would rise from the mud. It would not be high. It would not keep the water back. It would simply mark the limit of the city’s attention.

Beyond that ridge, water would still exist, but it would no longer remember footprints.

Rain would fall and pass into the soil.

A reflection would belong only to what stood above it.

A door would open onto one room.

The first animal would not be brought there. No bird would be created by decree, no fish placed in the canals as decoration. Life would come only when the world beyond the basin was ready to cross the boundary. The power would not manufacture witnesses for the city’s recovery.

It would make recovery possible.

Inside the basin, the changes would be slow.

The Drowned Arrangement would discover that it could let a thing be absent without pursuing it through every version of the world. It would learn to leave a room empty rather than fill it with all the rooms it had ever contained. It would learn that a vanished person did not need to remain in every pool in order to have mattered.

This would hurt.

The city’s first acts of forgetting would resemble collapse. Whole districts would shudder as their unnecessary memories fell away. Buildings would lose impossible floors. Streets would cease to lead to places that had never existed. The water beneath the foundations would become violently still.

But the power would remain.

It would not prevent the grief. It would keep the grief from becoming another architecture.

The travelers already trapped in the city would feel the change before they understood it. Their reflections would stop arriving early. The routes behind them would remain routes. When they turned toward the embankments, the horizon would not withdraw.

Some would leave immediately.

Some would stay, unable to imagine a life in which the city no longer followed them.

For those who wished to remain, the power would make a distinction the Drowned Arrangement had never been able to make: remaining would not mean being absorbed.

A person could walk through the flooded streets and retain a separate self. They could speak, and the words would die naturally. They could sleep beneath a roof without becoming part of its history. They could love the city without being loved in the city’s consuming manner.

The Drowned Arrangement would not understand this at first.

It would offer rooms.

It would open old doors.

It would place the travelers’ faces in pools and windows, trying to say: **I have made you a place.**

And the power would answer gently:

**A place is not the same as a possession.**

So the city would learn hospitality.

The civic square would change last.

The water there would remain deep. The buildings would remain dark. The dull openings would continue to show other arrangements of the world, but no longer endlessly. Each vision would now reach an ending. A dry square would empty. A newly built square would weather. A person would cross one version of it and not return in another.

At the center, the single drop would finally fall into the water.

When it did, nothing would explode. No hidden god would rise. The city would not be cleansed.

The drop would simply join the square, carrying with it the possibility of passing away.

The water would rise once, then settle.

For the first time, its surface would reflect the sky as it was.

There would still be no stars. The sky would remain dark and layered, and the horizon would still carry its red, depthless glow. But the reflection would agree with the thing above it. That small agreement would move outward through the canals, beneath the streets, into the walls.

The Drowned Arrangement would feel itself become less vast.

Not smaller, exactly.

More singular.

Its separate memories would no longer overlap without limit. Its attention would become capable of ending. The low subterranean sound would lose its resemblance to breathing and become something quieter: water passing through stone.

The power would then withdraw—not upward, not away, but into the ordinary laws it had restored.

It would become the next rain.

It would become the warmth of a hand placed on a wet wall.

It would become the patience of a seed carried by wind and deposited beyond the basin.

Nothing would announce that the work was finished.

The city would remain flooded. The black mineral would still grow. Roads would still alter their lengths when the oldest memories stirred. The buried bell would still ring sometimes, and when it did, the pools would still show other skies.

But now some of those skies would be allowed to vanish.

And at the edge of the basin, on the pale ridge beyond the last gate, a little grass would begin to grow.

Not everywhere.

Not quickly.

Just enough to prove that the world had not been asked to remain empty forever.
