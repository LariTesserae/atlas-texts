The benevolent power does not descend.

It has no need to make itself visible in the sky, and it understands that a place like the cleft would be harmed by too much certainty. A mountain still becoming itself should not be crowned, measured, or commanded. A listener should not be turned into a servant merely because something greater has noticed it.

So the power begins with attention.

It attends to the pressure beneath the mountain: the slow strain in the stone, the sealed chambers filling and emptying, the deep fractures that have not yet decided whether to open. It attends to the pool, which has always given water according to conditions no living creature can control. It attends to the listeners’ waiting, which has lasted so long that waiting has become part of their bodies.

Then it makes one small alteration.

Not a spring. Not a river. A delay.

When the mountain is under its greatest pressure, and the seam beneath the pool is about to open violently, the stone holds for the length of a breath. That is all. A breath longer than before.

During that breath, the listener nearest the pool feels what is coming.

It places both hands against the ground.

The oldest listener, if one is present, answers from the wall. The pressure passes through the mountain in a pattern no human ear could hear, and every living listener in the cleft understands the same thing:

Move.

The birds leave their holes. The grazers climb. The insects withdraw into cracks. The foxlike predators flatten themselves beneath the basalt shards.

The listeners do not flee far. They know the mountain. They move to the places where the stone will remain whole.

Then the seam opens.

Water rises black and cold, but it does not burst across the shelf. The pool fills cleanly. No young listener is swept away. No nest is drowned. The temporary channels form where channels are needed, and by noon they vanish as they always have.

The power does not make the cleft safe.

It makes it survivable.

For the listeners, the change is more difficult to understand than a miracle would have been. They test the pool. They press their fingers into the new channels. They examine the cliffs for signs of intervention and find none. The mountain remains sharp. Stones still fall. Frost still kills the flowers that open too soon.

But in the following seasons, they begin to recognize the extra breath.

They build a cairn for it.

The cairn contains four stones instead of three.

One marks the first sunlight. One marks the fullest reach of the pool. One marks the direction from which something is expected.

The fourth marks the moment before danger.

The humans who return notice it, though they do not understand its meaning. Some kneel before it. Some remove the fourth stone, fearing that an unknown sign is an invitation. The listeners patiently replace it when they are gone.

The power also attends to the waiting.

It does not reveal what crosses above the peaks. It knows that the listeners’ journey would become smaller if the answer were given to them. They are not waiting merely for an arrival. They are forming themselves through expectation, becoming capable of recognizing what they seek.

But the power sees that their waiting has begun to harden into despair.

So, on one morning each year, the copper fragments catch a light that is not yet present.

The effect lasts only a few moments. The thin metal brightens in the blue-black shadow before sunrise, and the light moves from piece to piece along the eastern ridge. It does not point toward the sky. It points toward the path by which the listeners may leave.

Those who are ready understand.

Those who are not ready see only copper changing color.

No listener is compelled to go. No one is taken. The path remains difficult: a narrow ascent over unstable shelves, then a descent into a larger world where the stone has softened and trees grow. Some return from it carrying warm, pale fragments beneath their ribs. Some return changed. Some do not return at all.

The power does not count departure as failure.

It does not count return as success.

It preserves both.

For the oldest listeners, who have become nearly indistinguishable from the mountain, the power gives a different gift. It allows them, once in a great while, to feel not only the pressure below but the lives above: the movement of roots in distant valleys, the weight of snow on forests, the pulse of animals drinking from rivers that run hundreds of miles away.

The knowledge does not make them human. It does not give them language or memory in human forms.

It simply tells them that the cleft is not the whole of existence.

This matters.

They had believed the mountain was listening to them alone. Now they understand that listening can extend outward without ending. The wind in the cracks is joined to wind in trees. The water in the pool is related to water under roots, beneath glaciers, in clouds crossing warm plains. The listeners are not trapped inside the mountain’s attention.

They are part of a much larger act of attention.

And because the power is benevolent, it does not remove death.

When a listener dies, the others still carry it to a fresh fracture. They still wait for the hard cold. The body still draws inward, and the plates still become stone. But afterward, the white veins in the new column spread in a different pattern.

They bend toward the nearest living thing.

Toward moss. Toward a nest. Toward a root. Toward the pool.

The dead are not preserved as individuals. They are made more available to life.

A column that once held a listener may fracture in spring and release pale mineral into the soil. A flower may grow there. A young listener may emerge from the crack years later, its tendrils reaching through the powder of its predecessor. The dead do not return, but neither do they vanish without consequence.

That is the power’s deepest work in the cleft: not rescue from change, but a gentling of change.

It leaves the mountain unfinished because unfinished things can still become.

It leaves the listeners strange because their strangeness is not a wound.

It leaves the pool uncertain because certainty would end the old attentiveness between stone, water, animal, and sky.

Only once does it make a gift that can be clearly seen.

On a night of extraordinary cold, when the stars are bright enough to cast faint shadows, a new fracture opens in the eastern wall. Within it lies a slab of pale stone, smooth and warm, marked by no vein, fossil, or mineral grain known to the mountain.

The listeners gather around it.

The slab is not food. It does not hold water. It cannot be shaped into a blade or ornament.

When touched, however, it gives back the pressure of every hand that has ever touched the mountain.

Human hands from the shelter.

Small hands of listeners long dead.

The broad feet of grazers braced against the rock.

The roots of plants seeking moisture.

Even the delicate impact of insect wings after rain.

For the first time, the listeners perceive the cleft not as a place filled with separate lives, but as one continuous contact: countless beings resting against one another, holding one another in place.

The power says nothing.

It has no words to add.

The listeners remain beside the stone until dawn. Then the youngest one, who has never before approached the open ridge, walks into the sunlight.

It does not burn.

It stands there trembling, silver beneath the stars’ fading light, while the flowers open around its feet.

Behind it, the pool begins to rise.

Ahead, beyond the cairns, the mountain waits—not gentle, not finished, but no longer alone.
