The benevolent power would not begin by making the basin beautiful.

It would understand that beauty, imposed too quickly, can be another form of erasure.

It would not drain the water, kill the eels, scatter the birds, or cover the mud with grass simply because grass was absent. It would not turn the warehouses into gardens while the channels remained poisoned beneath them. It would not remove the animals in order to save them. They had made a life there, and a wise power would know that a life—however compromised—is not the same thing as a mistake.

First, it would make the water tell the truth.

Every hidden pipe, buried outlet, cracked storage tank, and leaking conduit would become visible. Not dramatically; no alarms would sound, no walls split open. Rust would fall away in thin sheets. Sediment would settle. The black water would clarify by degrees, revealing the channels beneath it and the creatures moving there. Chemicals would separate from the tide instead of traveling invisibly through it. The hot discharges would cool—not all at once, but enough that breeding would no longer depend upon injury.

The power would alter the flows.

Rainwater would be given room to spread. Old flood channels would reopen. Pumps would cease their endless struggle against the basin and begin working with tide, gravity, and season. Some concrete sectors would be lowered deliberately, allowing them to become shallow lagoons. Other places would be raised and reinforced, so that workers, animals, and floodwater no longer competed for the same uncertain ground.

The buildings would remain for a while.

They would be inspected, emptied of their most dangerous contents, and opened where opening was safe. Dead machinery would be dismantled without haste. Sharp metal would be removed from the paths of crabs and dogs. Nets, traps, and screens would be redesigned so that fish and eels could pass through them. The abandoned pipework would be left in part as nesting space, but its hot interiors and toxic residues would be cleaned. No creature would be driven from a shelter without another shelter being made available.

The workers would be the first people to feel the change.

Their rules would become less like warnings against death and more like instructions for living. They would receive proper protective equipment, reliable lights, clean drinking water, dry places to rest, and enough time to stop. No one would be punished for refusing an unsafe crossing. No one would be required to enter a sealed yard because a gauge had failed. The old knowledge—where the pavement sank, when the birds nested, which discharge carried fish—would be treated as expertise rather than superstition or inconvenience.

The power would place doors where there had only been fences.

Not every part of the basin would become public. Some areas would belong to nesting birds, some to spawning fish, some to people. But the remaining inhabitants would be allowed to see one another without being enemies by default. Raised paths would cross the restored sectors. Observation shelters would stand above the mud. Warning lights would mark deep water and dangerous machinery. The city would no longer be empty because people had been excluded from it, nor crowded because animals had been displaced. It would become legible.

Then, quietly, the first plants would arrive.

Not lawns. Not ornamental trees imported to flatter the eye.

Salt grass would take hold along the tidal margins. Reeds would root in the newly cleansed shallows. Mosses would spread across the damp concrete. Algae would thicken into living mats with real color in them. Small shrubs would find the less-contaminated embankments. Their arrival would not be commanded. The power would make rooting possible, and let life decide what came next.

Where plants grew, insects would change. Where insects changed, birds would change. Fish would move into cooler water. Frogs would lay eggs without so many dying before hatching. Crabs would continue to cross the flats, though now among stems and shadows. Herons would still wait beside the ponds. Gulls would still quarrel on the cranes. The basin would not become pure. It would become less one-sided.

The rats would not be exterminated. Their food sources would be reduced, their numbers moderated by the return of predators and the removal of waste. Stray dogs would be taken to warm shelters, examined, fed, and offered a choice: return to the outer roads if they wished, or remain where people could care for them. The eels would keep their pipes. The birds would keep their roofs. The workers would keep their routes.

Nothing would be made harmless.

Harmlessness was not the same as kindness. Water could still drown. Storms could still rise. Birds could still kill fish, crabs could still consume what softened in the mud, and winter could still take the weakest. The power would not abolish hunger, weather, death, or the ordinary severity of being alive.

It would only remove the unnecessary suffering: the poison no creature had chosen, the collapse no one had repaired, the danger maintained because responsibility had been passed from hand to hand until it belonged to no one.

At the center of the basin, where the old industrial grid had been most damaged, the power would preserve one large structure.

Not a monument. Not a temple.

A waterworks building, perhaps, with its walls opened to the air and its roof repaired. Inside it, channels would carry clean tidal water through settling pools. Fish could enter and leave. Birds could nest in the rafters. Workers could monitor the basin from dry platforms. People from the city could come there and hear the pumps, the rain, the wings, and the living water moving together.

They would learn that the place had never been empty.

It had been inhabited all along—by bodies adapting to neglect, by workers maintaining what no longer loved them, by animals making nurseries from machinery and shelter from waste. The benevolent power would not claim to have created life there.

It would simply stop making life pay for the existence of the city.

And because it was wise, it would leave behind no sign that could be mistaken for a final answer.

The basin would remain unfinished. Plants would die in some sectors. Floods would breach old walls. A pump would fail. A bird would nest in a dangerous place. People would argue over access, cost, ownership, and what ought to be protected. The water would continue to rise and fall.

But the arguments would take place in the open, beneath a sky no longer permanently bruised by the basin’s own making.

At dusk, lamps would come on—not everywhere, and not brightly. Some darkness would be preserved for insects, fish, and birds. Along the raised paths, small amber lights would mark the safe ground. Below them, frogs would call from the reeds. Eels would move through the warmed but no longer poisoned channels. Crabs would cross the mud. A heron would lift from a pool and settle on the arm of an old crane.

The city would still smell of salt, rain, rust, and wet concrete.

But underneath those smells would be the dark, unmistakable scent of soil beginning again.
