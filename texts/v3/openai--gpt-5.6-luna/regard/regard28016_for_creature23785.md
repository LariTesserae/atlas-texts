The benevolent power does not bring water.

It does not open the roof to the sky, because the city has never asked for a sky. It does not seed the hollow with animals, because hunger and pursuit are not absences here. It does not return the people who built the streets. Their departure is not, to the city, an injury that can be repaired by imitation.

Instead, the power watches long enough to understand what the place is.

It sees that the settlement is not a ruin awaiting rescue. It sees the plants entering the masonry, the mechanisms repeating their old gestures, the stone rising and settling beneath them. It understands that the city has become a being—not a single mind, but a continuity made from pressure, mineral, heat, growth, and remembered motion.

So the first thing it does is refrain.

This, for a power accustomed to being worshiped by creatures who confuse alteration with kindness, is an act of great wisdom.

It does not make the hollow beautiful by ordinary standards. It does not soften the stone or cool the air. It does not turn the amber leaves green. It leaves the plants their hard hunger and the city its dry, resonant dark.

But it does notice the places where persistence has become precarious.

A bridge, already held by three generations of mineral vine, has begun to shear at its far anchor. The power does not rebuild it. Instead, it gives the deep stone one gentle shudder at the right moment. A fracture opens beneath the bridge, not through it, and the roots descend into the new seam. They find heat there. They thicken. The bridge remains.

A vent shutter has seized. Its counterweight is fused in place, its hinge packed with dust and glittering pollen. During the next red pulse, the power warms one small section of the hinge. Not enough to melt it. Only enough to let it move.

The shutter opens.

The city exhales.

The plants flatten toward the central light. Seed-cases break. Vanes turn. Dust rushes through the old streets, and for the first time in many centuries every mechanism completes its motion. A wheel turns through a full revolution. A gate rises from the floor of a buried chamber. Somewhere, a bell—long silent, its clapper embedded in mineral growth—gives one low note.

The sound travels through the hollow.

The power listens for a response.

There is no voice. No sudden intelligence gathers behind the walls. No plant becomes a person. The city does not speak in words, because words would be a poor translation of what it is.

But across the settlement, thousands of pores open together.

The mineral plants lean into the breath.

The power understands this as consent.

After that, its care becomes almost invisible.

When the outer plain trembles, the crust above the blister does not collapse. When a season of excessive heat approaches from below, the power draws some of it sideways into unused chambers, preventing the central vent from becoming an inferno. When the roof cracks, the fracture stops just before it reaches the oldest inhabited towers.

It protects, but does not preserve unchanged.

The city is permitted to continue becoming.

Where a dwelling falls, its stones are not restored. They are held long enough for new columns to grow through them. Where a stairway sinks, the surrounding floor lifts by degrees, making a ramp. Where a machine finally loses its purpose, its parts are allowed to settle into the architecture. The power does not save every object. It saves the relationships between things.

The vanes must still turn.

The vents must still breathe.

The plants must still find gradients and answer them.

The hollow must remain capable of surprise.

And because wisdom includes the knowledge that a life should not be made dependent on its guardian, the power does not intervene at every fracture. It gives the city one gift only: a margin.

A little more time before collapse.

A little more heat in the buried seams.

A little more strength in the mineral threads.

The city is not made immortal. It is made difficult to erase.

Then the power offers something stranger.

Not a door, exactly. Not an opening in the roof. It creates a possibility in the deepest chamber beneath the central vent: a narrow passage leading downward, beyond the old mapped foundations, into stone that has never felt the city’s pressure.

The passage does not call. It does not shine. Nothing is compelled to enter.

It simply exists.

For the first time, the settlement has somewhere to grow that is not an old wound, an abandoned workshop, or the cracked wall of a former dwelling. The mineral threads discover it slowly. One crosses the threshold after a hundred years. Then another. Eventually the roots begin to descend in earnest, carrying fragments of the city into the dark below.

The city extends itself.

Not upward toward the sky, but inward toward a future no maker planned.

The power is pleased—not because the city has become more like anything else, but because it has been allowed to remain itself while gaining a choice.

Ages pass.

The benevolent power does not visit again in any form the city could recognize. There are no statues, commandments, or signs carved into the walls. The old inhabitants would have mistaken those for instructions, and the city already has enough instructions.

Instead, the power becomes part of the conditions.

A pressure that never quite crushes.

A heat that never quite consumes.

A pause in the earth’s breathing, arriving just before the roots need to settle.

And when, at last, the first living thread reaches the bottom of the new passage, it finds a hollow there—smaller than the city above, colder by a degree, untouched by tools.

The thread divides.

One half remains where it is.

The other begins to build.

Far above, the old settlement answers with a pulse of red light. Its vanes turn. Its hard leaves open. Dust moves through the streets like weather.

Nothing has been rescued.

Nothing has been conquered.

The city has simply been given room to continue, and the dignity of becoming something neither its makers nor its guardian can entirely foresee.
