The benevolent power does not descend as a figure.

It does not open the clouds, raise a palace, or speak a sentence into the minds of the Rootwalkers. It does not name the plateau, because the plateau has no need of a name. A name would make a boundary where there is only continuity.

It watches.

At first, watching is enough.

The power learns the country by attending to its smallest exchanges: the way iron enters the water and is caught by moss; the way a grazing animal carries seeds across a ridge; the way one Rootwalker places its palm against a failing root-bed and remains there until the underground flow steadies. It learns that nothing here is repaired by being returned to an earlier condition. Recovery is not reversal. Recovery is incorporation.

So the power does not try to make the plateau safe.

It makes itself useful.

Far below the highest ridges, where pressure has been collecting in a buried chamber, the stone begins to strain. The change is minute. A seam warms by half a degree. A stream darkens. The pale filtering bulbs in one basin pulse out of rhythm.

The Rootwalkers feel it, but not all of them. The warning travels through the wet ground in fragments, losing strength as it crosses old faults and mineral shelves. Some families turn toward the danger. Others remain unaware, continuing their slow migrations beneath the moss.

The benevolent power touches the plateau at the point where one message might become many.

It does not create a voice. It gives the existing tremor somewhere to go.

A pressure shift enters the reeds. The reeds hum. Their hollow stems carry the vibration along the ravines, from stand to stand, until the entire high country is threaded with one low, sustained note.

The birds fall silent.

The stream-dwellers rise.

The grazers lift their heads, not frightened but attentive. Beneath the surface, the Rootwalkers stop moving. One by one, they place their hands into the ground.

For the first time, the plateau speaks to all of them at once.

The Rootwalkers do not receive words. They receive a pattern: the location of the gathering pressure, the weakness in the stone, the routes by which water might be diverted. It is not instruction. It is comprehension. The knowledge arrives without an owner.

And because it arrives without command, each living thing responds according to its nature.

The stream-dwellers leave the warm pools and move into the lower channels, displacing water with their broad bodies. The grazers abandon the meadows above the buried chamber, trampling temporary paths through the sedge. The birds carry seeds from the threatened basin to higher shelves. Burrowers widen old passages beneath the roots.

The Rootwalkers begin their work.

They do not dig toward the pressure. They open what is already open. They find the ancient channels beneath the peat, the ones sealed by mineral crust and forgotten root-growth, and they loosen them. They break apart deposits with their palms. They pull diseased mats from the water. They lie across collapsing seams and hold them open with the weight of their bodies while the first threads of clean water pass through.

The work takes three days.

On the third day, the buried chamber shifts.

There is no eruption. No mountain breaks. The plateau gives a long, low groan, and a ridge settles by the height of a hand.

The released pressure moves outward through the channels the Rootwalkers have opened. Warm water rises in six new places. A poisoned basin drains into a bed of filtering moss. One ravine becomes a lake, shallow and broad, while an old stream disappears beneath the ground.

The plateau is changed.

The benevolent power allows it to change.

It does not preserve the threatened basin exactly as it was. Some plants perish. Several reed beds collapse. A family of small burrowers is lost beneath a shelf of settling stone. The world is not made painless. Pain, here, is part of the information by which life learns where not to grow.

But the power prevents loss from becoming abandonment.

Where the ridge has settled, it leaves a warmth in the stone. Where the basin has drained, it lays a subtle coherence through the roots, encouraging them to hold the new banks. It gives the surviving bulbs a season of clear water. It allows the seeds carried by the birds to take quickly in the disturbed soil.

Nothing is restored.

Something continues.

The Rootwalkers sense the presence, though they do not mistake it for one of their own. For several weeks after the settling, they gather in a circle around the new lake. They stand knee-deep in the water, their feet opened into fine sensory roots. Their chests resonate.

The benevolent power listens through them.

It feels their exhaustion. Their mineral-heavy blood. Their grief for the ones who did not emerge. It feels, too, the strange satisfaction of the plateau: the relief of pressure released, the new routes of water, the countless small organisms already entering the changed mud.

The power could speak now.

It could offer consolation. It could tell the Rootwalkers that the dead have become soil, that the soil has become flowers, that nothing has truly vanished.

But the Rootwalkers already know this.

To explain it would be to diminish it.

Instead, the power gives them one thing they do not possess: a place in which to remember without interrupting the circulation.

At the edge of the new lake, it causes a shelf of stone to remain dry.

Only a narrow shelf. No more than a few paces wide. The first dry ground any Rootwalker has known that was not temporary, accidental, or protected by a tree.

Nothing grows there for a long time.

The animals do not use it. The moss avoids it. Rain gathers on its surface and runs off cleanly into the lake. The Rootwalkers come there when one of their own dies. They bring no body, because bodies belong to the plateau. They bring only their presence.

They stand upon the stone, one after another, and make the low note that travels through water.

The note is not a prayer.

It is an acknowledgment.

Here was a life.

Here is what remains.

The benevolent power keeps the stone dry for as long as it is needed. Not forever. Forever would make it alien. Eventually, lichen will take its surface. Roots will find its underside. A small blue-gray flower will open in a crack, and a stream-dweller will drag mud across it with one broad foot.

When that happens, the Rootwalkers will not be angry.

They will understand that remembrance has completed its work and may return to the larger body.

Afterward, the power remains.

Not as a guardian stationed above the plateau, not as a ruler measuring the health of its subjects, but as a slight preference in the world toward continued relationship. A failing channel is a little more likely to meet an open one. A seed lands in receptive soil. A wounded creature is found by scavengers before its suffering becomes prolonged. A Rootwalker searching for a clean basin feels, beneath the ordinary tremors of stone, a second rhythm pointing toward water.

The power never removes danger.

It makes neglect less likely.

When the great amphibious animals call, their notes sometimes carry farther than they should. When the reeds hum, they occasionally produce a tone no wind has made. In the deepest roots, where minerals pass from stone into living tissue, there is a quiet pressure that was not there before.

A promise, perhaps.

Or merely attention.

The plateau remains wet, crowded, ancient, and strict. It still swallows paths. It still hides predators. It still changes without permission. The Rootwalkers still bury themselves beneath the roots, still lose young, still die into the soil.

But now, when the buried machinery shifts and every living thing listens through the same body, something listens with them.

Not to command the circulation.

Not to end it.

Only to make sure that, when the pressure passes, the country is still capable of receiving what comes next.
