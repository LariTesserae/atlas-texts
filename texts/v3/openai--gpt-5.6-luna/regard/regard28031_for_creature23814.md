The benevolent power does not descend upon the plateau.

It does not part the clouds, kindle the dead hearths, or lift the city from its foundations. It understands that the settlement is not waiting to be rescued from weather. Weather is the condition by which it has endured. To remove the rain would be to remove the language in which the people read the world.

So the power watches.

It watches the channels being cleared by hands gone stiff with cold. It watches the old carrying the names of walls that have already fallen. It watches children place their ears against paving stones and learn, before they can write, the difference between water passing freely and water searching for a way out.

It sees that these people have not conquered the city, nor surrendered it. They have entered into an agreement with its changing.

And because the agreement is just, the power does not break it.

Instead, it places one thing where no one has thought to look.

Beneath the highest district, beneath a sealed court and three layers of fallen masonry, there is a chamber that was not built by the first hands, or the second, or any hands remembered by the people. Its door has no hinge. Its walls are cut from a stone slightly lighter than the rest, porous and warm to the touch.

For centuries, water has moved around it.

On a night of long rain, when the cloud settles over the basin and the lower streets begin to fill, the chamber opens.

Not dramatically. No stone grinds. No light pours out. A seam in the wall darkens, and then the wall is no longer there.

The water beneath the district changes direction.

It gathers in the old courts, slips beneath the paving, and enters the newly opened passage. The sound is so low that at first it is mistaken for distant earthfall. Then every person sleeping above it wakes at once.

They wake with their palms against the floor.

For a few breaths, the city is silent except for the rain.

Then the water begins to sing.

The sound travels through the foundations in a single sustained note. It is not one of the people’s songs, though it resembles one. It has no beginning they can find and no answer waiting at the other end. It passes through cisterns, retaining walls, drowned stairs, and sealed houses. It enters the hands of those who touch the stone and gives them not knowledge, exactly, but direction.

Here.

Not there.

Open this grate.

Leave that street.

Break through the western wall.

The people rise. They do not panic. They dress, take their ropes and lamps, and go out into the rain.

They clear a passage that has been blocked for generations. They break into a lower terrace and find the water already moving beneath it. They remove three stones from an arch and discover a shaft descending into darkness. They send a child back for more cord. They mark the unsafe houses with pale clay.

All night they work.

By morning, the lower district is flooded—but not drowned. The water has taken the route the city was designed to offer it. A row of houses is lost. Two courtyards become tarns. The marsh expands, but it expands into the basin prepared for it. No one is carried away. No one dies beneath a roof that could have been abandoned in time.

When the rain stops, the strange chamber remains open.

Inside, there is no treasure. No preserved fire. No metal, no prophecy, no image of the power that made it.

There is only a dry floor, a narrow spring, and a wall covered in shallow impressions.

The people study those impressions for many days. They are not letters, though they are arranged with intention. They resemble channels seen from above: lines dividing, joining, disappearing, returning. Some end abruptly. Others continue beyond the edge of the wall.

At last, an old woman places her hand upon the markings and understands that they are not instructions for controlling the water.

They are a record of where the water has always wanted to go.

The chamber becomes a place of learning. The people bring no idols there. They do not name the power. They carry in strips of clay and charcoal, and they copy the patterns. They send observers into the fog to compare the hidden lines with the visible slopes. They find old routes beneath the city, drainage ways filled with silt, staircases that lead not to houses but to reservoirs, and whole districts whose foundations were designed to be surrendered.

The power gives them no ability to prevent change.

It gives them the ability to distinguish change from disaster.

In the years that follow, the people alter their work. They begin to leave certain walls unbraced, because the walls are meant to fall and direct the water. They open courtyards to the rain. They move sleeping chambers higher. They plant the sheltered slopes with a low, silver-rooted grass that holds soil without choking the channels. They establish dry rooms in every district, not for wealth or rank, but for seed, cloth, records, and the injured.

The old ways are not abandoned. They become more exact.

And one other thing changes.

The children are still taught to feel the masonry. They still learn the names of dangerous streets, the songs used in tunnels, the sound of a roof beginning to settle. But now they are also taught to notice what is absent: the channel that should be running, the echo that should return, the patch of frost that forms where warmth has moved away.

They are taught that a warning is a kind of mercy.

The power does not return after that first opening. It does not speak again. The chamber slowly gathers moisture. Moss enters through the ceiling. The spring remains, though its flow varies with the weather. In time, people begin to sleep there during storms, because the stone is dry and the sound of water beneath it is steady.

Generations later, some say the chamber was always known. Others say it was a gift. A few insist that the city itself opened the door.

All of them are partly right.

For the benevolent power, having seen the settlement clearly, does not make it safe.

It makes it less alone in its danger.

And when the next great rain crosses the basin—when fog pours over the upper terraces and the lower streets vanish beneath white water—the people do not look upward for a miracle.

They place their hands against the walls.

They listen.

Then they go where the water tells them to go.
