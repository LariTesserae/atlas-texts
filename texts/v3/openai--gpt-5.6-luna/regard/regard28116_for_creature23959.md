The power notices it because it does not know where the plateau ends.

It finds no single being to approach. There is no face turned upward, no mind waiting to be addressed, no wound that can be closed by laying a hand upon it. There are only exchanges: water entering stone, roots entering cracks, heat entering stems, vibration entering soil. The plateau is not a creature inhabiting a place. It is the place, gradually becoming capable of responding to what happens within it.

The power understands this quickly.

It does not descend as a figure. It does not interrupt the wind or darken the white sky. It does not make the plants speak, because speech would be an answer to a question they have not asked. It does not give the plateau a heart, because the absence of a heart is not a defect. Its life is distributed. Its memory is held in layers of moss, altered channels, strengthened roots, mineral stains, and the shape of every surviving stem.

So the power waits.

It watches one cycle of rain pass through the stone. It watches the pumps remove water from the deep fractures. It watches the mats lift and settle. It watches a columnar plant divert its roots around a buried pipe and, in doing so, leave a neighboring spring less supported than it had been before.

The installation is not cruel. The people who built it did not intend cruelty. They needed water, pressure, power. They placed their machines where water was abundant and assumed abundance meant permission.

But the pumps are taking more than the plateau can spare.

Not enough to cause a visible collapse. Not enough for any single season to be called a disaster. The damage is distributed across futures: a spring that will fail in twelve years, a root system that will never reach maturity, a wet basin that will become a white mineral scar. The continuity does not suffer as an animal suffers. It cannot cry out. Yet its possibilities are narrowing.

The power is benevolent, and therefore it does not wait for pain to become obvious.

It does not destroy the building. It does not punish the absent workers or tear the towers from the ground. It does not turn the machinery against its makers. Instead, it changes what the machinery considers excess.

The next time the pumps begin their cycle, they draw less.

The reduction is tiny. No gauge alarms. No valve groans. The system registers a fluctuation and compensates. But beneath the plateau, the water remains a little longer in the stone. A seep continues through the night instead of ending at dusk. Roots that would have stopped extending find moisture ahead of them and continue.

The power makes another adjustment.

When rain falls faster than the pumps can safely manage, the machinery now returns a portion of what it has taken. Not in a flood, but through old fractures and forgotten channels. Water enters the plateau at a depth where the mats cannot drink it and the roots cannot reach it. It settles there, cooling the stone. It preserves the hidden reserve.

No one notices at first.

The installation produces slightly less. A report describes an unexplained inefficiency. A technician replaces a sensor. The pumps resume their work, and the plateau accepts what is offered.

The power also strengthens what is already present.

It does not cause new species to bloom. It does not make the grasses taller or the flowers brighter. It gives no plant an advantage that the others cannot survive. It merely ensures that connections are not severed by accident. A root passing beneath a foundation is allowed one more season to thicken. A fungal network around a damaged spring is spared a chemical interruption. A vine reaching for a warm pipe finds a seam instead of a sealed wall.

The changes are so small that they resemble luck.

A cable falls during a storm but lands across a fracture instead of into it. Its metal carries no current afterward, yet its woven sheath holds soil in place. Moss gathers there. A year later, the cable is no longer a cable in any useful sense. It is a dark buried rib, conducting moisture toward a basin.

One tower loses power, and no repair crew comes.

The tower remains upright because three columnar plants lean against it. Their stems thicken over the years, not around the metal but beside it, taking the load that the tower can no longer bear. Eventually the tower becomes unnecessary. The plants do not know this. They have simply found a shape that holds.

The power does not make the plateau immortal.

It permits frost to split the stone. It permits storms to strip leaves. It permits the antlered animal to die when its time comes, and its bones to be taken beneath the mats. It does not prevent the machinery from rusting, because rust is not an evil and permanence is not the same as flourishing.

Nor does it isolate the place from the world. The plateau will continue to receive seeds from below, dust from distant deserts, smoke from fires beyond the horizon. New arrivals will fail. Some will take root. A predator may one day climb high enough to remain. The power does not curate the landscape into purity.

It gives the plateau room to become itself.

There is one thing more.

At the center of the building, where the pipes enter the floor, the power alters a control circuit. It adds no voice and leaves no message. But from then on, the system records not only how much water it removes. It records how much the surrounding ground retains.

The numbers are sent down the mountain.

They appear in a maintenance report as a series of irregularities. Then in an environmental assessment. Then in the hands of people who had assumed the plateau was merely a resource waiting above the weather.

The figures do not command them to feel reverence. They do something more useful: they make consequence difficult to ignore.

Years later, the installation is reduced. The pumps run only during the wettest months. Several pipes are removed. The remaining ones are redesigned to follow the natural channels instead of interrupting them. The building is left standing because taking it apart would damage more than leaving it in place.

Plants enter through the roof.

The power does not return. It has no need to.

Its intervention is not a miracle but a widened margin: enough water, enough time, enough warning, enough restraint for the continuity to continue.

At night, the plateau remains dark above the clouds. Leaves close. Springs move through hidden stone. The pumps beat more slowly, in rhythm with the water they have learned not to exhaust.

Nothing there dreams.

But when the first rain of the new season touches the mats, the water is held a little longer than it would have been.

And that is what the benevolent power does, if anything:

It does not give the plateau a purpose.

It protects its capacity to have one.
