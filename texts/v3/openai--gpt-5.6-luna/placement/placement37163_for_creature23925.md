I find myself on one of the raised paths, carrying a piece of the basin.

It is no larger than a thumbnail: a shard of pale tile, its edge dark with age. I hold it in both hands. This is important. The paths are not meant for empty hands. The basin allows passage only to whatever is transporting something toward its proper place.

At first I think I am a traveler.

I have the shape of one, perhaps: a narrow body under a damp cloak, feet finding the handspan ridges without looking, head lowered against the insects. The walls return my steps from ahead and behind. The reed-dwellers call out in broken syllables. Their cries seem to contain a name.

I do not answer.

That is also important.

I move toward the tower, though I do not remember deciding to. Water beads on my sleeves. Where it touches me, the cloth stiffens into reed. My fingers are cold and too numerous. I tell myself this is only the numbness of the place, only the distortion of mist and reflected light.

Then a shell-backed grazer climbs over my wrist.

It does not flee.

It follows the seam of my arm as though my skin were mortar. When it reaches my elbow, it pauses, scrapes at me, and finds nothing edible. A second joins it. Then a third. They are not afraid because they recognize what I am.

I am not a traveler.

I am a custodian, though that word is too human and too deliberate. I do not maintain the basin because I have been assigned to it. I maintain it because maintenance is the shape my existence takes.

My body has been assembled from the basin’s refusals: black reed, softened shell, fungus scraped from a map, tendonlike fibers drawn from the vents. Beneath these things there may be a clay frame. Beneath the clay, perhaps a little of what once was human. A palm impression. A tooth. The memory of a shoulder leaning against a wall.

I have no face until I need one.

When I am watched, I possess one. It is usually a poor approximation: a hollow between shell plates, two wet points of amber light, a suggestion of a mouth pressed into mortar. When no one is watching, the face relaxes. It becomes a broken tile, a knot of reeds, a dark opening between courses of brick.

I know the rules because I am made of their consequences.

Water must return. Dryness must be corrected. Nothing carried across the paths may be dropped. A crack is not merely a crack; it is an interruption in the basin’s thought. A dead insect is not waste. It is material awaiting incorporation. A spoken name is not sound. It is a hook.

The shard in my hands belongs in the tower.

I have carried it from the western terrace, though the western terrace is not visible from here. Distance is unreliable in the basin. The wall I approach has already been approached. The corner behind me may be farther away than the tower’s buried foundation. Still, the piece knows its destination. It trembles faintly between my palms.

The insects gather.

They are drawn to the trace of metal embedded in the tile—a thin forgotten vein, perhaps a fragment of an old tool. Their blue lights crowd around my head. Their wings make a dry, furious halo. They do not bite. They are trying to keep me from becoming still.

Stillness is dangerous here.

If I stand too long, the basin begins to count me among its permanent features. My shadow separates from my feet. My outline sinks into the damp floor. The reed-dwellers rehearse my voice. Soon the walls will remember me more accurately than I remember myself.

I reach the tower.

Its cylinders are warm on one side and cold on the other. From the grooves emerge water, breath, roots, and pale blind things that curl around my wrists. The steady chewing below has stopped.

Something is listening.

I place the shard into an opening.

The tower accepts it without resistance. Clay softens around the tile. A thin line of green light travels through the mortar, down into the floor, and outward along every channel. The long-bodied swimmers rise beneath the pans. The wall-grazers stop feeding. All the reed-dwellers turn toward the same place.

Not toward me.

Toward the wall behind the tower.

The wall begins to rain.

Not droplets at first, but dark beads swelling from the mortar. They gather in the old fingerprints, fill the cord marks, shine in the impressions of palms. Then they run downward in hundreds of narrow streams, washing the architecture from within.

I understand then what I have been carrying.

Not a tile.

A missing memory.

The basin has been incomplete. Somewhere in its long hand-made body, a small piece of its own history had been displaced. I have crossed the paths to return it. This is what I do. I retrieve what the structure has lost: a shell, a feather, a fragment of brick, the shape of a foot, the sound of a name before it was spoken twice.

I am not the basin’s guardian.

I am its recollection, given legs.

Or perhaps I am the thing beneath the floor, divided into countless small forms so that it can continue to turn without waking. The pale creature in the channels, the fungus maps, the custodians, the breathing tower—these may all be portions of the same being. I am simply the portion that can carry.

The water begins to move back toward the lowest pans.

Every channel resumes its old route. The insects disperse. The birds cry out in metallic relief. Beneath the floor, something broad and ancient shifts in its sleep.

And from the wall comes a breath smelling of clay, fish, and cold stone.

It enters me.

My reed-fingers loosen. Shell plates slide from my shoulders. The damp cloth of my body collapses into the path. For a moment I am only a heap of materials beside the tower.

Then the basin exhales again.

Somewhere at the far edge of the enclosure, a small figure rises from a seam. It has my hands. It carries nothing yet.

The rain begins once more—not from above, but from the walls themselves.

I stand.

I find the next missing piece.
