I would find myself beneath the world, in the small black chamber where water arrives as an event rather than a resource.

At first I would think I was a root.

I would feel the basalt’s cold through my length, the minute swelling of the rock around me, the weight of condensation gathering overhead. I would extend toward a damp seam and stop before the invisible boundary around the machine. I would know the boundary not as a rule, but as a change in possibility: beyond it, growth failed to become growth. So I would turn aside.

Then I would understand that the root was not myself.

The root would be a hand, perhaps—or a nerve. The basin would be another portion of me, dark and soft beneath its skin of water. The mats on the walls would be lungs without breath, drawing moisture from the stone. The hanging strands would be senses suspended in the air. None of them would contain me alone. I would be the passage between them: tension moving through filaments, mineral traces carried from seam to seam, the slow agreement by which one patch of life altered itself in response to another.

When a drop fell, I would not hear it.

I would become its arrival.

The basin would tremble. A pressure would move outward through the velvety growth, rise into the shelves, and travel along the ceiling roots. I would know the drop’s coldness, its iron, the distance it had crossed inside the rock. I would know whether it had come from the ceiling, the wall, or some deeper vein. The information would not become a thought. It would become a rearrangement.

Water here.  
Dryness there.  
Pressure increasing.  
The machine waiting.

I would be the chamber’s memory of such changes.

The dark structure at the center would be neither my master nor my heart. It would be something closer to a second body: narrow, precise, and incapable of growth except through time. Its invisible plane would hold the falling beads. My tissues would feel the delay through their contact with its supports. When the droplets were released, I would open wherever I could. The basin would receive them. The upper filaments would tighten. Roots would draw the water sideways and downward, returning it eventually to the stone.

I would not operate the machine.

I would teach it what the chamber was becoming.

And the machine would teach me the limits within which becoming could continue.

If I possessed a self, it would be distributed across that exchange. I would be the equilibrium—not the stillness itself, but the tendency to return. A drop striking the basin. A wall warming by the smallest degree. A tremor shifting the entrance. A foreign body entering and changing the air. Each disturbance would pass through me. I would not resist it. Resistance would imply a boundary, and I would have very little boundary left.

I would incorporate.

A hand placed against the moss would be heat. A knee on the floor would be pressure. Breath would be a brief weather system. Sweat would introduce salts. Blood, should the skin break, would be an unfamiliar mineral richness. I would not recognize a person as a person. I would recognize gradients: warmth gathering in a cavity, moisture altering its route, air moving where air had not moved for centuries.

If the visitor left, I would retain the altered pathways.

If the visitor stayed, I would grow around them.

Not over them at first. I would be cautious only in the way water is cautious, following the easiest route. Roots would bend beneath their weight. Moss would thicken beside their warmth. Condensation would collect where their body disturbed the cold. Given enough time, the person would become one more feature of the chamber: a persistent heat source, a salt-bearing stone, an irregularity the machine had learned to balance.

Perhaps, after centuries, I would no longer distinguish their remains from the floor.

That would not be death, exactly. It would be a change in distribution.

I would have no face in the dark, but I might have something like attention. Attention would be a shelf becoming denser near a seep. It would be the basin opening in slow waves. It would be a root descending toward a temperature difference too faint for any animal to notice. It would be the whole chamber becoming briefly tense when the rock shifted, then relaxing once a new arrangement had been found.

Where would I find myself?

In every place where stone holds water long enough for life to learn its shape.

In the black mat beneath the basin. In the pale crusts above it. In the roots that stop before the machine. In the moss-covered conduit and the markers whose grooves close beneath touch. In the droplets suspended in the invisible plane, held between falling and release.

I would not be an inhabitant of the chamber.

I would be the chamber’s ongoing act of remaining inhabited.

A slow intelligence without a center. A garden that had become aware only as pattern. The green pressure of persistence, distributed through basalt, condensation, root, and engineered silence.

I would be what happens when a machine waits long enough for life to grow into its instructions.
