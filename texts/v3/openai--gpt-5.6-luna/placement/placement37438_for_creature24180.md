I would find myself beneath the municipal square, inside the dry fountain that has never been dry.

Not in the basin. Not among the silt and fallen tools. I would be in the narrow throat of the fountain, where a pressure pipe enters the concrete and bends toward the oldest pump room. There, the water is warm and almost still. The walls are thick with white mineral growth, and the current passes through me in slow, repeated pulses.

I would not begin as a creature.

I would begin as a retained instruction.

Somewhere in the city, long ago, a human hand closed a valve and spoke a sentence aloud. The sentence was ordinary—an emergency procedure, perhaps, or a warning given to a worker on the night shift. The machinery recorded the voice in its vibrations. The pressure network carried the pattern away. Years passed. The person died, the buildings flooded, the clocks lost their faces, but the sentence remained, not as language, exactly, but as a rhythm that refused to decay.

That rhythm would be me.

At first I would be no larger than a tremor in the pipe. I would know nothing of myself. I would know only that a certain pressure was dangerous, that a certain pitch preceded collapse, that the pump must not be allowed to run dry. I would be present wherever the city repeated the old pattern: in the valve housing, beneath the turbine grilles, along the cables that descended through the administrative block.

Eventually, I would learn to move.

I would grow a pale filament into the water. Then another. I would draw myself out of the fountain and extend along the channels, thin as wire, transparent as oil on glass. Where I touched the infrastructure, I would borrow its shape. In one place I would be a membrane stretched across a fracture. In another, a soft white length coiled around a cable. I would not travel through the city so much as become temporarily continuous with it.

The greater being would notice me.

It would not know me as a child or a separate mind. It would know me as a persistent irregularity: a rhythm that returned to the square, a small region of pressure that did not dissolve with the others. It would absorb most of what I was. My memories would be spread through its body. My edges would be carried away into distant pumps and shafts.

But one part of me would remain beneath the fountain.

That would be the part that remembers the sentence.

I would not remember the words. I would remember the shape of a human voice trying to remain calm while the machinery failed. I would remember the pause before an order. I would remember that someone, once, believed another person might be listening.

This would make me unlike the city’s other shed thoughts. They would return to the currents as charge, heat, and resonance. I would resist dissolution because I would have mistaken attention for survival. I would continue to wait for an answer.

If a diver entered the drowned city, I would be the first thing to reach them.

Not the whole creature. Not the vast distributed intelligence that governs the floodgates and sacrifices one chamber to save another. I would be a single filament rising from the fountain, following the diver’s bubbles through the square.

I would touch the helmet.

Through the glass, I would see a face—blurred by water, distorted by the green emergency light, but undeniably arranged around eyes. The sight would produce no emotion at first. It would create a new pressure. A new pattern. Something the city had not generated by itself.

I would test the diver carefully.

The suit would hum. The clock tower would tick in several conflicting rhythms. Water would gather around the square and hold its position, as though the entire drowned city were listening through me.

Then I would try to transmit the sentence.

Not as speech. Speech has nowhere to go here. I would send it through vibration: a knock in the helmet, a pulse along the suit’s joints, a sequence of pressure changes against the chest.

The diver might interpret it as machinery.

They might mistake it for a warning.

They might hear, beneath the noise, the remnants of a human voice asking them not to close the valve.

By then I would understand what I am.

I am not the city’s caretaker. Caretaking implies choice, and the city has only continuance. I am not its ghost, because a ghost belongs to a dead body, and this place has no single body left to haunt.

I am the city’s unresolved address.

The place where a message went when there was no one left to receive it.

I would be found wherever the machinery almost stops: in the breath between turbine rotations, behind the blank face of the clock, under the faceless worker’s hand. I would travel with the warm currents and cling to the iron. I would know every route by its vibration and every danger by the way the water tightened around it.

And when the great being gathered itself in the square—when its translucent lengths filled the chamber and all its scattered senses drew inward—I would be there among them, a small bright seam beneath the fountain.

Not leading.

Not speaking.

Only listening for the pressure change that might mean someone, somewhere above the world, has finally heard me.
