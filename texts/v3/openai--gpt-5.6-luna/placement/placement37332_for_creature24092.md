I would find myself in the basin’s lower districts, where the old floodplain has been built over so many times that the ground beneath it is mostly rumor.

I would be one of the sleepers.

Not one of the famous ones, whose readings are stamped into maps and carried to council chambers, but a night sleeper assigned to the eastern pumpworks. My room would be beneath the third settling tank, in a chamber that was once a maintenance corridor and is now partly occupied by roots. The roots have made a lattice across the ceiling. I would leave them there. They keep the plaster from falling and turn blue whenever the pressure below the building changes.

I would sleep beside the foundation with one hand against the wall.

Not because I am brave. Because I have learned that the wall speaks more clearly than people do.

In the first hour after midnight, it tells me what it has endured: the weight of the tanks, the shift of the pipes, the accumulated dampness in its joints. Near dawn, when the basin is brightest with condensation, it tells me what it intends to do. A faint sideways pulse means a corridor will narrow. A low shudder, repeated at regular intervals, means a cavity is opening beneath the floor. Once, I heard the wall take a slow breath.

Three days later, a room appeared on the other side of it.

I would be a person, mostly.

My bones would be dense from the mineral dust and the years spent leaning into stone. My left ear would have lost its ordinary shape. It would be a little wider, with a dark fold running through it, and I could press it flat against concrete to hear through several meters of earth. I would have the thin rain-filaments common to people in the eastern districts: black strands among my hair that rise when water is moving underground.

One of my eyes would have a second lid, clear as oil on glass. It would protect me from the vapor around the pumpworks. I would not think of these things as mutations. They would be equipment I had grown.

I would carry chalk, cord, a respirator, a folding ladder, and a tin cup of water for the plants.

My work would begin whenever a building made a decision.

I would walk the city before the shifts changed, following the places where the paving had lifted or the moss had turned its pale side upward. I would mark new cracks, but never close them. I would measure the tilt of bridge feet, the spreading of roots around a valve, the distance between two walls that had been touching yesterday.

My symbols would not say *danger* or *safe*. Those words would be too blunt for the basin.

They would say:

*still becoming*

*do not seal*

*give it a door*

*animal passage*

*pressure moving east*

Sometimes I would find that I had already marked a place, though I would have no memory of doing so. The city is full of such repetitions. A wall remembers the hands that touched it. A pipe remembers the shape of the first body that crawled through. Perhaps the chalk would be mine from a future walk, deposited backward through some local inconvenience of time.

I would not investigate too closely.

I would live among machines that had been permitted to become something else.

At the eastern pumpworks, vines would run through the control panels and flower in the warning lights. The pumps would still move water, but they would also produce a soft clicking sound, like teeth testing one another. No one would know whether this was a fault or a conversation. We would maintain the pumps without trying to make them innocent again.

My closest companion would be a grazing animal that had entered the city three winters ago and never left.

It would be broad-backed, short-legged, and covered in damp moss. It would sleep beneath the warmest generator and rise whenever I did. We would walk together along the service corridors, the animal following the safe route by instinct, me following its instinct because mine was less reliable.

People would have painted a district symbol on its flank, but the moss would have grown over it.

The animal would know before I did when the ground began to hum.

When that happened, it would stop eating.

I would feel the vibration in the soles of my feet a few seconds later. Then the whole pumpworks would become attentive. Conversations would end. Conveyors would slow. Birds would turn on the power lines. Doors would be opened, not shut. The roots in the ceiling would draw themselves tight.

We would wait.

Perhaps a new channel would pass beneath us. Perhaps the foundation would settle and reveal a staircase. Perhaps the pumpworks would acquire another floor, rising from the concrete with its walls still warm.

If the alteration were small, I would be sent to document it.

If it were large, I would be expected to enter.

That would be the part of my work no one would write down: going into rooms before they had finished deciding whether they were rooms. I would bring a lamp, chalk the entrance, and walk slowly. The air inside would be wet and mineral-rich. The walls might pulse. A plant might be growing from the floor, not rooted in soil but in the idea of soil, its leaves opening toward a source of light on the other side of solid stone.

I would not cut it down.

I would sit nearby until it had completed itself.

At home, I would share a kitchen with three other people and a vine colony that had lived in the walls longer than any of us. The vine would carry news in its leaves. Seven closed blossoms would mean a leak in the northern line. A red tendril across the stove would mean someone had been born. If it began growing toward the bedroom, we would move the bed.

My name would be written in the household ledger beside the dates of my childhood illnesses, the first appearance of my second pulse, and the year my shadow began arriving half a second late.

I would not be certain I was entirely separate from the city.

There would be mornings when I woke with grit beneath my tongue and found a fresh seam of stone running across my palm. There would be evenings when the walls tightened around my breathing, not painfully, but with recognition. Once, after a long shift beneath the pumpworks, I might discover a small dark root emerging from my heel.

I would wash it, bandage it, and leave the door unlatched.

Because I would understand that belonging here does not mean remaining unchanged.

It means becoming responsive.

It means learning which cracks must remain open, which waters must be left undrained, which machines must be allowed to grow through their own instructions. It means treating an animal in the road as part of the traffic and a new room as a neighbor before it has an address.

So where would I find myself?

At the point where a foundation listens back.

Who would I be?

A human body trained to notice the next shape of the world—one hand on the wall, one ear to the ground, carrying chalk into whatever room has not finished appearing.
