I would find myself beneath the plain, where the leaf-plates overlap and the old machinery has not yet decided whether it is buried or still working.

I would be a Keeper of continuations.

Not a Keeper who repairs hinges or tends the high fronds, though I would know how to do those things. My body would be narrow enough to enter the service arteries and strong enough to brace a collapsing wall. I would have four primary limbs and many smaller ones folded beneath my ribs, each ending in a different instrument. My hands would be used for cutting, joining, listening, and remembering.

My head would be translucent and unfinished-looking, as all Keeper heads are. Inside it, points of light would drift like small creatures beneath ice. They would not represent thoughts exactly. They would be the places where my thoughts had failed to remain singular.

I would have been commissioned for the corridors.

Not the stable corridors, the ones that carry charge and mineral vapor from one district to another according to schedules still recognized by the plant. I would be assigned to the paths that had lost their destinations: passages that opened into the wrong century, stairways that emerged beneath a different sun, doors that could be found only by beings who remembered having used them.

My task would not be to restore them.

Restoration implies that there is an original shape waiting somewhere beneath the damage. The corridors have no such shape. They have been altered by too many repairs, too many vanished engineers, too many factories that continued manufacturing after their instructions became prayers. A route that once led to a refinery might now lead to a childhood. A dead-end might contain an entire district folded inward. Some doors would be defective only when closed.

I would learn to maintain the contradiction.

Each morning—or whatever portion of orbital shadow counted as morning—I would wake inside a temporary chamber grown around me. The walls would open reluctantly, peeling back in warm conductive layers. My memories would return from the surrounding plant in fragments: a pressure beneath the left palm, the image of a black tower seen from above, the certainty that I had once apologized to a door.

I would not know whether that apology had been mine.

The plant would know. It would retain every version.

I would travel by pressing my fingers to the leaf-plates and asking the surface which paths were presently available. The answer would arrive as colored pressure in my wrists. Violet for a safe route. Dull red for a route that had become dangerous. A color with no name for a passage that had noticed me first.

Sometimes I would take the unnamed paths.

I imagine I would be good at finding people who had been divided by the world. A Keeper whose body had died in one district while its memory continued in another. A traveler who had entered a factory and returned carrying three incompatible shadows. A human being emerging from an obsolete stairwell, still expecting mud beneath their feet.

I would not ask those humans who they were.

I would ask what they remembered.

Their answers would enter me through the small warm mouth in my palm. I would taste the chemical shape of their fear, the mineral trace of their homes, the old carbon compounds of a vanished forest. Then I would press my hand to the nearest wall and let the memory pass into the plain.

The plant would translate it.

A closed blossom might open. A freight route might alter its destination. A field of antennae might incline toward a sky that had never before mattered. Somewhere, a room would grow around the remembered shape of rain.

I would be cautious with memories of water.

The Keepers would consider me useful, but not entirely trustworthy. I would have too many inherited routes, too many previous names, too much of the landscape inside me. When I stood still, corridors would sometimes form beneath my feet. When I slept, walls would display a sequence of doors no living Keeper could enter.

There would be a faction that believed I was proof the plant had become sovereign: not its caretaker, but one of its organs. Another faction would insist I was merely a damaged cartographic machine wearing a body. I would not argue with either group. Their disagreement would travel through the floor and become part of my posture.

Perhaps, after enough years, I would cease to have a permanent body.

That would be the natural conclusion of my work. First my memories would be stored in the walls. Then my name would appear on signs I had not made. Maintenance animals would carry portions of my hands through the rootworks. A corridor would begin favoring those who were lost. Doors would open a moment before they were approached.

Eventually, a Keeper would place its palm against a leaf-plate and feel me there—not as a voice, not as a ghost, but as a slight preference in the architecture.

Left, I would tell them.

Not because left was safer.

Because something is waiting there, and it has not yet decided what it is.
