I would find myself behind the falling water, in the crack where the stone remains wet but not flooded.

I would be the pale salamander—not quite an animal of the surface, not quite a creature of the dark, but a small living remainder suspended between them. My body would fit along a palm, though I would have no use for the comparison. I would know the chamber by pressure rather than sight: the waterfall’s weight descending through the floor, the basin taking each impact, the trembling of grit as it slips from the walls.

The roar would not be sound to me. It would be weather inside the bones.

I would know when rain had entered the ridge. The concussion would deepen, becoming broad and heavy. Loose leaves would announce themselves as they struck the basin. A stone falling from above would send a sharper disturbance through the shelves. The chamber would speak in blows and tremors, and I would understand none of it as language, yet miss nothing.

My world would be only a few bodies long. The rear of my crack. The wet shelf beneath it. The nearest ledge of moss. The stone bowl, which I would avoid until its trembling surface became still. The old path of flat stones would be part of the chamber’s shape, like a healed scar. I would rest against the handholds cut by people long gone, learning the faint warmth and smoothness their hands had left behind.

I would not remember them as people.

I would remember three careful weights, a pause, then two more.

Sometimes falling grit would repeat the rhythm badly, and I would draw myself backward, flattening my body against the dry wall. I would wait until the false approach dissolved into the waterfall. I would not call this fear. Fear belongs to creatures that expect rescue, or escape, or a future different from the present. I would simply become still.

When the chamber settled, I would emerge.

I would travel slowly over the polished stone, spreading my feet wide, feeling each change in the water’s vibration before crossing it. I would hunt among the moss for pale flies, larvae, blind mites—small lives that had also accepted the chamber’s narrow terms. There would be no abundance, only intervals. One ledge green with persistence; another bare and black. A body moving. A body waiting. A drop gathering beneath the ceiling until it fell.

I would be old. My tail would be notched, my hind foot incomplete, my skin marked with the mineral dust of the ridge. I would resemble a root that had learned to withdraw. I would have survived not because the chamber was generous, but because it was consistent. Its cold would not change much. Its darkness would not deepen. Its water would diminish and return. The waterfall would continue its patient violence.

I would have no sense of the world beyond the slit between the stones, except as pressure stored in the ridge and occasional gifts washed inward: a leaf, a thread of root, the distant taste of rain. Daylight would be a weakening near the entrance, not a destination. The open land would be less real to me than condensation on the wall.

Perhaps there had once been others. If so, they would have occupied their own wet hollows, meeting rarely, withdrawing without grief. The chamber would not make a family of us. It would make separate continuances.

So I would live alone, but not in silence.

I would live within the waterfall’s unending body, listening with my ribs. I would be the small, nearly transparent witness pressed into the dark behind the basin: not guardian, not spirit, not survivor in any heroic sense. Only a creature whose life had narrowed until it became exactly equal to its shelter.

When the water swelled, I would retreat.

When it weakened, I would come out again.

The moss would be wet. Something small would move among the roots. The stone would hold its shape.

And I would continue.
