I would find myself on the upper plateau, where the ridge towers stand in a line too regular to be natural and too weathered to be called new.

I would be a Carrier—not one of the keepers of the dishes, nor one of those who listen to the eastern wall, but the one entrusted with things that must not be changed.

My work would be to carry objects across the folded places.

A hammerstone from a shelter whose doorway has moved three valleys east. A bowl from the star-filled pool to the basin that currently lies beneath it. A strip of metal engraved with instructions in a hand no living Carrier recognizes. Small things, mostly. Things that would seem insignificant in a world where cliffs rearrange themselves and water climbs walls.

But the plateau is always trying to make objects into memories of themselves. Left alone, a knife becomes the idea of a knife. A cord remembers being a crack in the stone. A bowl begins to contain not water but the distance to the person who last held it. My hands are the one condition under which these things remain what they are.

I would be partly made.

The transformation would have begun long ago. My joints would have the pale translucence of wet mineral. Gray grains would gather beneath my nails. When I moved, my shadow would need a moment to follow. I would still breathe, though each breath would bead in the cold air before disappearing. I would still remember hunger, sleep, and the shape of a human face. But I would no longer be certain that these memories belonged entirely to me.

During sleep, the plateau would spread my weight into the shelter walls. Water would collect in the hollow of my throat. Voices would pass through me and emerge later from some other surface. I would wake with the knowledge that a stairway had opened somewhere, though not knowing whether I had dreamed it or caused it.

I would not have a name.

Names are too portable here. Spoken in the wrong basin, they return altered. A name can attach itself to a shadow, or be taken up by the Under-Listener and placed beneath the stone. I would be known instead by a task: the one who carries the unchanged thing.

My most important object would be a dull metal wedge, repaired so many times that no one could say what it had first been made to do. It would fit comfortably in one hand. Its edge would not cut flesh or stone. It would cut distance.

The wedge would belong to a Carrier who has not yet arrived.

That would be the difficulty.

I would begin from the shelter beneath the slanted slab and walk toward the ridge towers, though the towers would appear to draw farther away with every step. I would not look directly at the destination. Brightness reveals what is present but not where it is, and the far ridge can be reached only by approaching it indirectly, through its shadow—or through the shadow of something that has never stood there.

Behind me, the shelter would alter its doorway. Ahead, one of the towers would turn slightly, its condensation dish now facing down into the mountain. I would know I had taken the correct path because the water in the dish would taste of a room I had not entered yet.

At the halfway point, the Under-Listener would notice me.

The black pattern beneath the stone would rise in a slow ripple, passing from foot to foot. It would not threaten me. It would simply wait. The plateau would be asking whether I understood what I was carrying.

I would place the wedge against my chest and continue.

Perhaps the object would arrive at the star-filled pool. Perhaps the Carrier who has not yet arrived would take it from my hand. Perhaps I would discover that I was the destination, and that the wedge had been carried toward me through years of folded terrain.

I would not try to resolve this.

The Carriers know better than to force an arrangement to remain still. To inhabit the plateau is not to master its rules, but to notice which rules have temporarily chosen you.

So I would go on beneath the sunless brilliance, between the wet pillars and the breathing cliffs, carrying one small piece of metal that remains itself because I have not yet set it down.

From far away, I would look like a narrow stone figure standing beside a vertical stream.

Up close, I would be less reassuring.

But I would be a person for as long as my hands could carry something across the world without allowing it to become anything else.
