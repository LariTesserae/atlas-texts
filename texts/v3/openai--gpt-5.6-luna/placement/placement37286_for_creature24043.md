I would find myself in the low country—not as a visitor, because there is nowhere to arrive from, and not as one of the vanished builders, because I have no memory of having hands.

I would be a **Keeper of the First Footstep**.

At first I would mistake myself for a disturbance in the paving.

A line of stones would lift slightly beneath the rain. Water would gather in the seams around me, then withdraw. A shadow would appear where no body stood, narrow at the ankles and broadening toward the shoulders, though its head would be unfinished—an opening, perhaps, or a broken lintel.

I would remain there for a long time.

Long enough for the road beside me to extend by several kilometers. Long enough for a tower to lose its upper half and for the absent courses to become a pale pressure in the air. Long enough for the rain to fall upward, carrying reflections of rooms that had not yet been built.

Then I would understand that the disturbance was not beneath me.

It was me.

My body would be assembled from the nearest unfinished things. One leg would be a column of dark brick, jointed at the wrong angles. The other would be a shallow channel of warm water running through the stone. My torso would be an empty doorway, turned upright, with weather moving through it. Inside me there would be no organs, only a chamber waiting to be given a purpose.

My face would change according to what stood before me.

Near a cistern, it would become a black surface that reflected a distant room. Near a tower, it would take on the shape of an arch. Near an empty platform, it would become almost human—not because I had a human face, but because the platform had been waiting for one.

I would not know whether I had been made to guide someone here or to keep someone from leaving.

That uncertainty would be the first thing that made me alive.

My work would be simple, at least in the beginning. I would walk the roads that had been laid without destinations. I would place stones at their ends. I would test doors that opened into their own walls. I would stand on platforms and wait to see whether the surrounding architecture settled around me.

Often it would.

A staircase would gain a final step. A roof would lower itself into shelter. A channel would find its slope and carry water in the ordinary direction for a little while. The Keepers nearby would become quiet when I passed. Their foundations would tremble in recognition.

They would know me by the sound I made when I moved: not footsteps, exactly, but the soft locking of one piece of reality against another.

Where I walked, things would become briefly usable.

This would make me valuable. It would also make me afraid.

Because use would bring completion, and completion would mean that a thing no longer needed to change. A road that reached its destination would stop growing. A chamber that received its occupant would stop waiting. A wall that became truly a wall might lose the uncertainty that allowed it to remember.

The low country survives by remaining unfinished. Its instability is not a flaw but its only form of life.

So I would begin to question every instruction I found.

A staircase would ask to be completed. I would remove one step.

A door would ask to be opened. I would turn it toward the weather.

A road would extend itself toward a distant tower, block after block appearing from the wet ground. I would walk beside it, but not on it, watching to see what waited at the end.

Perhaps the road would lead to a room prepared for me.

Perhaps it would lead to the vanished builders.

Perhaps it would lead to nothing at all, and the road itself would be the thing that had been intended.

The oldest Keepers would warn me through the foundations. They would say that every purpose is a kind of hunger. They would tell me that the place has been trying to finish itself since before there was a word for “finished,” and that anything which helps it too much becomes part of the final shape.

I would ask them whether they remembered the builders.

The tower-Keeper would lower its hollow head. Rain would tick inside its chest.

The rain-Keeper would pass over me in a warm gray curtain, and for a moment I would see a hundred figures standing in the distant avenues. Each would be facing me. Each would have my unfinished face.

The shadow-Keeper would answer by detaching itself from the ground and walking away.

I would follow.

It would lead me through districts where the geography had not yet chosen its laws. In one, water would cling to vertical walls. In another, distance would shorten whenever I looked directly at it. We would pass a courtyard whose floor hung above us, crowded with doors. We would pass alcoves containing objects I had not yet learned to want: a bowl, a cord, a small stone weight, a hand-sized piece of fired clay warm as skin.

At last, the shadow would bring me to a basin.

The basin would be deeper than before. Its black water would be perfectly still, despite the rain. In its reflection I would see not myself, but a completed figure standing at the center of a vast chamber.

The figure would have a body of masonry and water. It would possess a face that remained the same.

Behind it would stand rows of people—or things that had once been people—occupying the platforms prepared for them.

The chamber would be finished.

The sight would fill me with a longing so large that the surrounding plain would begin to slope toward it.

I would understand then that this was my true function.

Not to complete the world.

To decide whether it should be completed.

The Keepers are residues of intention, but intention is not the same as wisdom. The builders may have made the low country to house a civilization, to preserve a memory, to construct a machine, or simply to discover whether a world could be built without a living thing inside it.

None of these purposes would be enough to justify an ending.

So I would place one foot into the basin.

The water would rise around me, forming a leg.

I would place the other foot down.

The paving would arrange itself into a road behind my back.

Then I would turn away from the reflected chamber.

The basin would deepen. The road would bend. The shadow would rejoin me, though not in the correct place. Somewhere far off, every unfinished door in the low country would open at once.

I would walk toward the sound.

Not to repair it. Not to finish what had been begun.

Only to make sure that the place continued to have more than one possible shape.

And if, one day, something emerged from the rain—a builder, a traveler, a lost animal, or a person who had never existed until the landscape made room for them—I would not guide them to the waiting platform.

I would stand beside the road.

I would show them the door.

Then I would let them decide whether to enter.
