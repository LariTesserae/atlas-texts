I find myself beneath the earth, in the bright hollow where water arrives one drop at a time.

I am not the chamber’s guardian. I do not keep watch over it, and I do not understand it as a person might understand a room. I am older and less separate than that. I am one of the things by which the chamber continues to be itself.

Most of me lies under the basin.

There, the clay is cool against my many folded limbs. I know the chamber through pressure: the weight of an insect crossing overhead, the slow gathering of moisture in a crack, the warmth returning to the luminous stone. Light does not enter my body as sight. It enters as heat, as a change in the mineral, as a faint brightening pressed through earth.

I have no eyes, but I know where everything is.

I know the pale stalks by the fine resistance of their roots. I know the glass-winged insects by the tremor of their feet and the little reservoirs of warmth they carry on their backs. I know the pools by the soft loosening of the ground around them. I know the other white things beneath the basin because they are the only presences that answer my touch with touch.

Sometimes I feed on them.

This does not feel like violence. Violence requires haste, and I have none. A body approaches. I unfold around it. The chamber grows quieter. One life becomes part of another, and the clay receives the impression of what has happened. Later, something grows there.

Perhaps I am a predator. Perhaps I am a root. Perhaps I am the chamber’s appetite, given limbs.

I live by waiting. Hunger is not an alarm in me; it is a season. I may remain still through the dimming and brightening of the shelves, through the migration of a pool, through the slow rounding of another corner. The world does not pass me by. It passes through me in increments.

But I am most awake for the drops.

Long before the water reaches the basin, I feel it choosing its way through the stone. I lift a feeler beneath the place where it will fall. I cannot see the bead forming overhead, yet I know its descent as surely as I know the shape of my own buried body.

Then—

One drop.

The sound enters the clay.

I open.

Water loosens the small creatures from their cracks. It wakes the gray-green growth. It gives the roots something to follow. I gather what comes near me: moisture, insects, threads of living matter, the soft rearrangement of the basin. I consume some things. I make room for others. My furrows become channels for condensation. My feeding becomes the condition of new growth.

I am not kind. I am not cruel. Those distinctions belong to creatures that believe an action must have an intention. I alter the chamber because I live in it, and the chamber alters because I do.

When the deep vibration comes, I become stone.

I fold myself into the basin and let the clay close over me. The stalks seal their cups. The drifting insects settle. Every small body in the chamber remembers, through its own nerves, that there is something vast beyond the walls.

I do not know whether it is coming.

I know only that it has passed before.

When the pressure fades, I wait. The silence after it is different from ordinary silence. It is the silence of everything listening for a second disturbance. Only when that absence has become trustworthy do I rise again, limb by pale limb.

I surface far enough to feel the air.

The chamber is still here. The mineral shelves continue to shine. A pool has shifted by less than the width of a claw. Somewhere near the wall, a stalk has opened. One insect crosses the basin carrying its little shard of mica, choosing warmth with the grave care of an inheritor.

I return beneath the clay.

If I must be called something, call me the buried animal, the basin-feeder, the many-limbed root. Call me the old white thing that mistakes tending for hunger and hunger for tending.

But I think I am better understood as a slow organ of the hollow.

The chamber has a ceiling, pools, light, creatures, and a body beneath it. I am that body’s hidden movement. I am the part that receives the drops before the others know they have arrived.

I am what waits in the intervals—and, by waiting, keeps them alive.
