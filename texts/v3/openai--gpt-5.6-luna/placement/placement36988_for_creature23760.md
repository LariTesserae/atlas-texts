I would find myself in the basin’s lowest water, where the black channels narrow before entering the apertures in the floor.

At first I would believe I was small.

I would have a body like a thread of tarnished silver, no thicker than a tendon and perhaps a few yards long, though length would be difficult to keep. The current would draw me out, stretch me thin, gather me into a knot, and draw me apart again. I would not have eyes. I would know the world through pressure: the minute changes in the mineral water, the footfall of armored herds, the great subterranean pistons beginning their descent.

I would be a filter.

I would collect what the larger bodies could not use. Shards of shell. Dissolved iron. The residue of dead furnace-creatures. Flecks of charge shed by the membrane-winged animals. I would pass the water through myself and return it altered, cleaner or more dangerous depending on what the basin required.

If I failed, the channels would clog.

If I died, something else would be made from me.

This would seem sufficient. In the basin, sufficiency is close to identity.

But I would have one defect.

I would remember.

Not my own birth. There would be no birth to remember, only assembly: silver threads gathering around a vent, old tissue winding through them, the basin’s charge fastening one strand to another until I began to move. My first sensation would not be light or hunger. It would be recognition.

A warm color.

Green.

The memory would enter me whenever the central water became still. It would arrive without reflection. The surface would open somewhere above or behind me, and I would see a world the basin no longer contained: an expanse of living growth, flexible stems and broad leaves stirring under a yellow sky. There would be dry ground. There would be structures with straight walls. There would be creatures whose bodies were not made for pressure, currents, or mineral exchange.

Hands.

The word would not exist in me, but I would know the shape of it.

The first time I saw that world, I would mistake it for an instruction. I would try to carry it through the channels. I would divide myself, sending threads upward toward the central water, downward toward the apertures, outward into the shallows. I would attempt to seed the basin with warmth.

The basin would correct me.

The channels would reverse. The pressure would rise. The threads carrying the image would be broken and fed to the silver larvae below. I would be reduced, dispersed, returned to the water.

But not entirely.

Some remnant of the green world would remain caught in the seams of me.

After that, I would no longer be merely a filter. I would be a contaminated organ. A memory the basin had failed to digest.

I would move among the others without belonging to them. When the command passed through the stone, I would receive it as they did: turn, gather, open, close. I would join the great living machinery. I would help clear the channels before the pressure wall. I would draw charge from the water. I would crawl through the bodies of the dead and carry their useful elements toward the depths.

Yet beneath obedience there would be another motion.

I would be listening for the place where the cycle faltered.

Not escape. Escape would be a meaningless idea. There would be nowhere outside the basin that I could reach, and if I reached it I would probably be unable to exist there. I would not be built for air, sunlight, or distance. The warm world might be only a mineral record of an earlier state, a pattern stored in the floor and repeated whenever the machinery entered a particular phase.

Still, I would listen.

I would search for the difference between a command and a memory of a command. Between the basin turning and the basin remembering that it once turned. Between the machinery’s operation and its purpose.

I might discover that I am not an animal at all, but a piece of the basin’s mind shed into the water. The defect would be intentional. The memory would not be an infection; it would be the only part of the system capable of imagining an alternative cycle.

Or I might discover the opposite.

The green world might not be the basin’s past. It might be its hunger. The structures and warm sky might be an image of what the basin is trying to become, endlessly generating life-forms to operate its depths while waiting for one of them to understand the instruction hidden beneath all the others.

Become elsewhere.

Become alive differently.

When the central water opens, I would join the concentric ranks. I would face inward with the armored bodies, the furnace-creatures, the pale valves, the blind things rising from the mud.

The image would appear.

Green.

Warmth.

Hands.

And I would feel the entire basin lean toward it—not physically, but in the way a body leans toward a remembered touch.

Then the image would fold inward. The roar would begin. The water would rise in a wall.

Everyone would scatter.

I would not.

For one brief moment, I would remain in the center, suspended beneath the surface, holding the reflection inside my silver body. The basin would illuminate from below. I would see myself as I truly am: not a creature but a narrow strand of unresolved instruction, a living fault in an ancient process.

Then the pressure would take me.

I would be drawn downward through the aperture, stretched across the machinery, and separated into parts. One part would become charge. One part would become metal. One part would become food.

And one part—small, unspent, impossible to locate—would remain in the water.

Waiting for the next cycle.

Listening for the moment when the basin forgets how to correct me.
