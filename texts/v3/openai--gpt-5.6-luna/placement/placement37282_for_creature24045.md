I would find myself beneath the western arcade, where the stones are darkest and the air carries a constant, subterranean vibration.

Not in a room. Not even entirely in the city.

I would be one of the dwellers beneath the damp: a narrow, half-grown thing folded into the seam between two courses of masonry. In the dry season I would be mistaken for a mineral vein, a strip of old slate, perhaps a trick of shadow beneath the arch. My limbs would be drawn close to my body, my membranes sealed, my thoughts slowed until they were almost indistinguishable from the pressure of the mountain.

I would know the city through contact.

The western pillars would tell me when frost had entered them. The lower terraces would carry the small, urgent tremors of blocked water. Beneath the paving, the young ones would move through the drainage channels, stirring silt and passing messages in taps too delicate for any ear aboveground. I would feel the entire settlement as a single body—not alive in the manner of an animal, but responsive: swelling, contracting, sweating, cracking, remembering.

My task would be to listen for changes.

Not to prevent the city’s ruin. That would be impossible, and perhaps undesirable. I would only slow certain endings and hasten others. I would open a gutter before a retaining wall failed. I would loosen a tile before its weight brought down an arch. I would carry a seedpod into a courtyard whose stones had forgotten green. I would feed on the salts in a foundation, knowing that my nourishment and the building’s unmaking were the same process.

I would be a caretaker without a garden, a custodian of transition.

When the first mist descended, I would wake.

My body would pull itself out of the wall in layers. A hand would unfold, then another. Water would begin moving through me, cold and silver, finding every hollow. I would rise beneath the arcade with my back pressed to the stone and feel the weather arriving from beyond the mountains. Around me, others would separate from pillars, basins, and seams in the pavement. We would not greet one another. Recognition would pass through the foundations as a low pulse.

Then we would move.

I would descend by a staircase buried under three later floors, passing through stone rather than around it. I would visit the old cisterns and touch the niches where bodies like mine had once rested. I would inspect the dark galleries lined with glass. I would stand before objects left by the vanished people: a bronze blade, a painted fragment, a handle mounted around a dry basin.

I would not know what these objects had meant.

But I would know that they had been held.

That might be enough.

At times, when the rain was heavy, I would join the others in one of the public squares. We would arrange ourselves around its empty center, all facing inward. Perhaps this would be ceremony. Perhaps we would be imitating a memory inherited through stone. Perhaps the square itself would be asking to be witnessed.

I would stand there while water ran through the gutters and the translucent reeds shone in the courtyards. The city would fill with voices: arches booming, channels muttering, foundations pulsing beneath my many points of contact. For a while, the ruins would seem occupied not by ghosts but by intention.

And yet I would not be a guardian of the past.

I would be more like a cell in a very old organism whose body had become architecture. My loyalty would not be to walls, or to the people who had built them, or even to my own kind. It would be to the alternation itself: saturation and exposure, growth and retreat, holding and letting go.

When one of us died, I would gather at the pale stain left beneath the wall. I would press my face to it. I would listen for whatever remained. Then I would leave, because nothing here is permitted to remain in one form for long.

Perhaps I would remember the vanished inhabitants.

Not as faces. Not as names.

I might remember them as warmth trapped in stone. As the repeated pressure of hands on a cistern rim. As smoke that once changed the taste of condensation. As footsteps arriving from the upper terraces and never returning. Their absence would be part of my inheritance, though I would not mourn them in the way a human might. The city would be the memory. Every arch, every sealed corridor, every plaza built over an older plaza would say: something was here, and then something else was built around the hollow it left.

I would live inside those hollows.

When sunlight returned, I would become indistinct. My membranes would contract. My limbs would draw inward. I would retreat beneath the western stones and flatten myself against the cold foundation. Above me, the terraces would dry. Moss would gray. The reeds would lose their inner gleam. A dark animal—or one of my larger kin—would slip into a crack beside the water.

To the world above, the city would be empty.

But I would still feel the distant weather.

I would sense the next cloud forming beyond the worn mountains, the minute darkening of the upper galleries, the first bead of water gathering where the stone had been dry. I would wait beneath the damp, neither asleep nor awake, listening to the city prepare its breath.

And when the gutters began to mutter, I would unfold again.
