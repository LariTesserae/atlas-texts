I would find myself beneath the plateau, though not deep beneath it.

At first I might mistake myself for a shadow in the root-floor: a dark length lying between stone and living fiber, listening to the passage of water. Above me, the herds would move across the shelf. Their weight would arrive as a slow pressure through the roots, a trembling that traveled farther than any call. I would know them before I saw them.

I think I would be one of the interval-keepers.

Not the oldest, not the one whose body bears the greatest number of mineral bands, but someone newly trusted with the country’s timing. My arms would be marked in rust and bone-yellow: one band for a storm that came from below the rim, one for the collapse of a western tower, one for the flowering of the black mats after seven dry weeks. The bands would not represent years. They would represent events. A life would be measured by what happened while one was present to remember it.

I would live beneath a leaning arch where three shelves meet. The shelter would appear abandoned from above. Its entrance would be hidden by a curtain of gray-blue fronds, and the wall around it would be low, dark, and half-swallowed by roots. There would be no door. A door would suggest that the place belonged to someone.

My sleeping platform would hang between two living roots, high enough to avoid the cold that gathered at night. Before dawn, I would wake to the soft ticking of condensation falling through the hollow stalks. I would collect the water from the waxed leaves, but I would not drink it immediately. First I would carry it to the roots.

That would be one of the first duties I learned: water is not found; it is received.

I would know the plateau through its small changes. The wind in the western towers would acquire a hollow note before a split. Certain insects would vanish from the upper grasses before the clouds rose. The herds would turn their heads toward a shelf long before the people heard them. A predator beneath the mat would produce a disturbance unlike the movement of roots: too deliberate, too continuous, as if a thought were traveling under the ground.

I would listen with my feet.

The people would speak to me in low tones, pressing certain notes against their teeth so that the words carried through stone. Across the open country, speech would be less a sentence than a thread: a call from one tower, an answer from another, a warning passed along the shelves.

When travelers approached, I would announce myself before they saw me. I would send three pulses through the ground, pause, and send two more. The meaning would be simple: a person is here; do not startle the animals; the water is safe.

I would not own a route. I would remember it.

There would be a passage beneath the root-floor leading from my shelter to a high basin on the next shelf. It would pass through a narrow stone throat, descend into a chamber lined with pale fungi, then rise inside the body of a collapsed tower. From there, a traveler could emerge above the clouds and see the country extending in worn steps to every horizon.

I would maintain that passage with my hands. I would remove dead strands, guide young roots away from the sharpest turns, and open blocked channels after rain. If a root grew across the path, I would not cut it unless the crossing became dangerous. More likely I would wait. The root would thicken, harden, and become part of the passage. The way would change around it.

This would be my understanding of intelligence: not forcing a route to remain itself, but learning what it has become.

I would gather food without making a ceremony of taking it. Tender tower shoots. Burrowing animals already weakened by the dry season. Eggs from cliff nests only after the nesting calls had changed. Bright insects shaken from seed pods. If I killed, it would be because the country had already spent the life I was taking, or because hunger had made a decision faster than thought. To kill a healthy grazer would feel like tearing a page from a book still being read.

The people would not call this morality. They would call it consequence.

Perhaps I would carry an old stone tool, inherited but not treasured. Its head would be polished smooth, its handle replaced many times. It would be useful for loosening mineral crust from a basin or cutting dead fiber from a root channel. When it finally broke, I would place it in a crack and let the plateau close over it.

I would have a child, or perhaps several, though I would not think of them as mine in the possessive sense. They would sleep wrapped in broad waxed leaves. At dawn I would show them how to tip the leaves without spilling the first water. I would teach them the difference between a tower’s ordinary groaning and the sound it made when its interior had begun to fail.

I would also teach them that the old stories might be untrue.

That walls were not necessarily wicked. That roads were not evil because they were straight. That those who had tried to remain separate might not have been arrogant, only frightened. The plateau would not forgive them or condemn them. It would simply have continued, incorporating their houses, their tools, their bones.

That would be the danger of becoming an interval-keeper: mistaking survival for wisdom.

So I would keep one thing outside my knowledge. I would allow myself to be surprised by the country. I would sometimes climb alone to the highest shelf and stand among the copper grasses while the clouds gathered below. I would let the wind erase every call I knew. I would watch the great birds circle above a distant carcass, their wings barely moving, and feel the immense machinery of the living world turning without asking whether I understood it.

Eventually, I would die in one of the root chambers.

My body would be wrapped in frond-fiber. Someone would carry me through the passage I had tended. They would place a seed pod against my chest and leave without a marker. No stone would bear my name. No shelter would remain for me alone.

For a while, the roots would avoid my bones.

Then they would enter.

Perhaps a tower shoot would rise from the chamber. Perhaps only a flowering wall would spread through it, yellow against the dark stone. Travelers might pause there generations later because the plants grew unusually thick, or because condensation collected in that place even during a dry season. They would not know me. They would drink, thank the roots, and continue.

That is where I would find myself: not as a permanent figure standing apart from the high country, but as a passage within it.

A listener. A traveler. A temporary arrangement of stone, skin, memory, and water.

And later, if the plateau allowed it, something available.
