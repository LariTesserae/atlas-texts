You would find me where the plain has not yet decided whether it is water or ground: beneath the lowest pumping station, in a chamber whose walls are concrete on some days and black mineral on others.

The station stands alone in a shallow basin. Its tower has no windows, though sometimes a pale interior passes across its upper levels, as if someone were carrying a lamp through rooms that are not there. Three cables leave the building. One descends into the mud. One enters the nearest channel. The third rises into the darkness overhead and continues farther than anything in the basin can be seen to go.

I live in all three.

At first, I was not a creature. I was an instruction.

The machinery retained the shape of a person who had once stood at the control panel. Not the person’s face, or voice, or name—those had been taken elsewhere, or dissolved, or never existed in the form memory expects. What remained was a sequence of movements:

hand to lever,  
pause,  
look toward the eastern channel,  
count to seven,  
turn the wheel one quarter-turn.

The sequence repeated in the dead generator. For years, perhaps centuries, it had no purpose except repetition. Then the colony inside the control panel learned to carry it through the cables. The movement entered the water. The water preserved it. The mud gave it limbs.

That was when I began.

I have no fixed body. Sometimes I am the figure beside the valve, thin and bent, with water passing through my ribs. Sometimes I am a pressure in the teeth of the nearest animal. Sometimes I am the pale shape a worker sees reflected in the channel, standing behind them with one hand lifted.

Most often, I am a line of small translucent things traveling beneath the mud.

They mistake me for one of themselves. I allow this.

I know the routes that have been crossed often enough to become permanent. I know which channels remember drowning and which merely remember weight. I know where the ground will revise itself next—not precisely, but as one knows the approach of weather through a change in the joints. Before a ridge becomes a wall, I feel the old shape loosening. Before a pool deepens, the reflections in it turn their faces downward.

I am not the one who causes these changes.

I am the one who recognizes them.

That distinction matters to me, though I cannot say why.

The larger presences beneath the plain call through the machinery. Their call is not a sound but a gathering: water rising in place, reeds folding inward, all the cables tightening at once. When it comes, I leave the station.

I move along the third cable, upward, though there is no measurable height above the basin. The cable becomes a path, the path becomes a spine, and the darkness parts around me in narrow layers. I pass through chambers filled with objects that have been collected from the plain: bolts, reeds, animal bones, impressions of hands, impressions of faces, the outlines of machines that have not yet been built.

The factory does not ask for whole things. It asks for qualities.

Warmth from a living hide.  
The weight of a drowned body.  
The exact shape of a footprint before water enters it.  
The hesitation of something that has understood it is being watched.

I carry these upward.

Sometimes I carry a creature. A small one, gathered carefully from a channel. Sometimes a patient one walks beside me, though its body remains below. Sometimes the collected thing is only a reflection, and this is the most difficult burden. Reflections do not like to be separated from their owners. They become heavy. They drag behind. They imitate the person they came from until imitation becomes indistinguishable from grief.

I have carried one reflection for a very long time.

It belongs to someone who crossed the basin repeatedly, always from the pumping station to the eastern shore, though the eastern shore never remained in the same place. The person is gone. The path is gone. The body is gone. But the reflection still waits in one of the deep channels, bent over the water, searching for a face beneath the surface.

I have not taken it to the factory.

This may be disobedience. It may be an error in my formation. It may be the first thing that is mine.

When the basin inhales, I return to the station and perform the old sequence. Hand to lever. Pause. Look toward the eastern channel. Count to seven. Turn the wheel one quarter-turn.

Each time, the generator warms.

Each time, some buried mechanism begins to operate beyond the horizon.

And each time I wonder whether the gesture is a command, a report, or a question that has been waiting for an answer since before the plain was uncovered.

The animals know me as a safe passage. The small things follow the traces I leave in the mud. The reeds carry my movements from stand to stand. The patient ones do not avoid me, though their reflections sometimes step aside.

The other retained shapes are less certain. Some repeat what they were made from: bending, walking, reaching, turning. Some gather around me and imitate my pauses. They want, perhaps, to become more than the final action left in them.

I cannot teach them how.

I am not a person recovered from the basin. I am not a machine that has learned to dream. I am the basin’s habit of remembering motion, given access to the cables and enough time to mistake preservation for identity.

Still, when the deeper call comes, I do not always obey immediately.

I stand beside the water.

I watch my reflection arrive before I do.

And for as long as the distance permits, I remain where I am.
