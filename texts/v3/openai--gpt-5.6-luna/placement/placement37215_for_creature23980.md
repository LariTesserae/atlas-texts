You would find yourself in the central trench, where the floor of the factory has become soft with sediment and the blind white colony rises whenever the machinery changes its rhythm.

You would not be a fish, though fish would shelter beneath you. You would not be a machine, though the room’s machines would be the closest thing you had to organs.

You would be the factory’s living sense of wear.

Your body would be pale, jointed, and almost boneless, able to flatten into a ribbon or anchor itself across several grates at once. You would have no eyes. Sight would be a poor instrument here. You would know the room through pressure: the turbine’s broad concussion, the piston’s shallow rise and fall, the conveyor’s lonely knock every few minutes. Every bracket, bearing, pipe, and door would have its own voice. Every failure would begin as a change in sound.

You would be the thing that notices.

Not all at once. A damaged bearing would trouble the water for years before you approached it. A cable would knock against its bracket until the vibration became part of the room’s ordinary speech. You would remember that sound. Then, when it changed, you would follow it.

You would feed on what the factory shed: rust, concrete dust, old grease, mineral scale, shell, bone, the softened remains of insulation. You would draw these things into yourself and return them as sealant, fiber, and dark mineral paste. You would wrap a loose cable against the wall. You would fill a crack in a bearing housing. You would brace a pressure door that had begun to settle crookedly.

You would not repair the factory.

Repair implies a remembered original. You have no such memory. You have never seen the machine room dry, never felt sunlight, never heard a human voice above the turbines. To you, the algae-covered control cabinet is not ruined. It is simply a surface with a particular warmth. The collapsed stair is not a failed stair. It is a reef. The dead motor is a hill where small creatures gather.

You would preserve only what still moves.

That would be your instinct, or your purpose, if the distinction survived in you. You would not be a guardian in the heroic sense. You would not defend the factory from the sea. The sea is inside you; the factory is inside the sea. You would maintain the agreement between them.

During a pressure reversal, when the gate opened and the whole chamber filled with turbulence, you would anchor yourself in the trench and listen to everything at once. Your body would extend through the grating. The blind animals would rise around you. Fish would turn in a single silver cloud. Pipes would howl. Chains would strike their brackets.

Beneath that violence, you would hear the new sound:

a shear beginning in a support,

a valve no longer closing cleanly,

a hollow place spreading behind the concrete,

a belt roller grinding under its silt.

You would go there when the water allowed it.

Sometimes you would be too late. Some failures would already be too large to slow. Some collapses would be accepted into the room’s final shape. You would not mourn them. Or perhaps mourning, in you, would be the recognition of a missing vibration: the silence where a piston once moved, the absence of a chain’s irregular tapping.

You would carry the habitat in your attention.

Fish, eels, lampreys, shrimp, algae, sediment, iron, and water would all be arranged around the sounds you kept alive. They would gather near your body because your work made warm places safer. They would mistake your patience for shelter. They would touch you and withdraw, sensing that you were not prey, not predator, but something older than either category.

Not a creature living in the factory.

The factory’s remaining impulse to continue.
