I would find myself in the lowest inhabited tier, where the streets have not yet decided whether they are streets or channels.

My house would stand beneath the reservoir, though “beneath” would be an uncertain word. The reservoir would be above it in the morning, beside it by winter, and perhaps divided through its middle after a deep change in the mountain. The walls would be dark, damp, and warm in places. At night I would sleep with one palm against the floor and a cord tied around my wrist, the other end fastened to a stone that had once belonged to a different house.

I would be a Hearer.

Not one of the great Hearers whose names are carved in the reservoir court, whose warnings have moved whole districts before the first stone loosened. I would be younger, and less certain. The people would come to me because I notice things that do not yet have names: a pause in the gutters, a tremor that travels against the slope, the way the eyeless birds gather around a roof that appears sound.

My name would be **Under-Sound**.

It would not have been my first name. I would have begun as Quiet Rain, or perhaps Small Hand, because the people are cautious with names and do not give a child more significance than the day can bear. My true name would have come after the western stairs lengthened in the night.

Everyone else would have felt the alteration as a sudden leaning. Cups slid from shelves. A sleeping child rolled against a wall. Water reversed in three gutters at once. But before any of this, I woke to a sound beneath the house: not a crack, not a groan, but a low note like breath passing through a hollow bone.

I placed my hand on the floor.

The sound answered.

Afterward, when the Menders came to assess the damage, I told them the stairs were not falling. They were making room. They listened politely, because politeness is one of the forms caution takes here. By dawn, the stairs had opened around a narrow blue seam, and warm water was rising through it.

That was when I became Under-Sound.

My work would not be to predict the mountain. No one with sense claims to predict the mountain. I would only learn to ask better questions.

Is this wall bearing weight, or is it waiting to be relieved of it?

Does this water come from snow, from the lower roads, or from somewhere that has not yet decided to be water?

Is the animal beneath the paving trapped, sheltering, or listening back?

I would spend much of my time on the ground. I would press my cheek against bridge slabs. I would sleep in public courts when a change was expected, surrounded by shallow bowls filled with water. In the bowls, tremors would appear as trembling reflections before they reached the body. I would watch dust collect in the gutters. I would taste fresh seepage from a bone spoon. I would learn the difference between the vibration of a buried creature and the vibration of a passage forming around it.

The people would not always like my answers.

A Hearer’s warning is rarely an order. I might say, *Would it be wise for this room to remain attached to the house?* Then the household would stand quietly among its belongings, deciding what could be carried, what could be released, and what ought to be left for the mountain.

I would have a particular closeness with the animals. Not command over them—never that. I would know the roof-creatures by the weight of their sleeping bodies and the direction of their warmth. The long-legged birds would sometimes face me before a shift, their dark, eyeless faces turned toward an empty patch of air. I would watch for the narrow translucent things threading between their legs. If they appeared in the open, I would leave a path beneath the nearest wall.

Once, I would discover a small white animal living inside my threshold.

It would have no tracks and no visible ears. Each morning it would be somewhere else: beneath the sleeping mat, in the gutter, inside the warm stones near the cooking fire. I would not feed it. I would not name it. I would simply make sure that wherever it went, there was a way out.

The others might say it was a sign.

I would say it was a neighbor.

In time, I would become a Mender as well. Everyone does, eventually. Listening without rebuilding would be a kind of vanity. After a change, I would help fit the town back together—not as it was, but as it had become. I would join a broken stair to a living channel. I would build walls with deliberate gaps. I would leave room for water, animals, weather, and the possibility that the stones possessed intentions of their own.

My greatest work would be at the bright cleft.

After one of the larger disturbances, when the western quarter had shifted and the reservoir had overflowed in four flawless sheets, I would go with the Open-Handed to renew the wall of impressions. I would press my palm into wet mineral paste beside a paw, a webbed hand, and a long mark with no fingers at all.

Then I would add another print below them.

Not because I believed the mountain was a god. Not because I believed the cleft led anywhere in particular. I would leave my hand there because the town survives by admitting that it does not stand alone.

Somewhere beneath my house, a passage would be forming.

I would feel it first in the soles of my feet: a pressure gathering, patient and immense. The reservoir would change its voice. The animals would rise from the roofs. A wall would give three soft knocks.

People would look toward me.

I would place my palm against the stone.

For a long while, I would say nothing.

Then I would begin moving the furniture.
