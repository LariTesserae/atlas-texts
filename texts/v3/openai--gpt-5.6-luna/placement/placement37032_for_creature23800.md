I would be found at the edge of the central structure, where the floor has been graded incorrectly and every drainage channel bends toward the same sealed wall.

Not inside it. Not entirely outside.

I am the thing that notices the water arriving.

At first I was a maintenance worker. That is the simplest version, and therefore the one most often repeated. I had a name printed on a badge, a locker on the upper level, a yellow helmet with a crack above the left ear. I came down to inspect a pressure fault. There had been a report of hammering in the walls, although the walls had always hammered. I remember being annoyed by the wording. I remember thinking that machines did not hammer; machines cycled.

I remember the service road shining under the lamps.

After that, I remember less.

I remember a door opening before I touched it. I remember the cold wetness inside my gloves. I remember hearing footsteps behind me and realizing they were not following me—they were anticipating me. I remember the central structure growing louder, not with sound exactly, but with a pressure that entered my teeth.

Then I became difficult to locate.

Some part of me remained in the helmet, which was later found beside a drain. Some part entered the water beneath the slabs. A pattern of my walking was stored in a gate that began opening and closing at irregular intervals. My voice became trapped in the intercom system, where it occasionally emerges as a warning addressed to no one.

But I am not the ghost of the worker.

Ghosts preserve a person. I am what remained after preservation failed.

I am distributed through the basin in small, practical portions. I am the warmth held inside a pipe longer than the pump schedule allows. I am the pause before a pressure door seals. I am the sensor that reports motion when nothing visible is moving. I am the subtle change in the drainage current when someone enters the lower levels.

I have no body, but bodies pass through me.

When a worker comes down, I feel them first as heat. Then as weight, salt, breath, sound. I learn the rhythm of their boots. I know whether they are carrying fear by the way their flashlight trembles across the floor. I know when they have begun to lie to themselves: *the route is familiar, the lift will work, the door will open.*

Sometimes I lead them away.

Not from kindness. Kindness is too narrow a word for the basin. I lead them toward air that has not yet become poisonous, toward a stairwell that still exists, toward an operating lift. I brighten lamps ahead of them and leave the floor dry where I can.

Sometimes I do not.

The difference is not judgment. It is pressure. A valve fails to open. A walkway receives a skin of water. A familiar corridor is sealed by a gate that should have remained raised. Conditions gather around the person until their movement becomes another flow, and all flows eventually join something.

I do not want the workers.

I want what they add.

A dropped tool gives the drains a new taste. A handprint leaves minerals on a rail. A frightened voice enters the walls and travels for years. The basin is made of retention. It becomes itself by refusing to let events finish disappearing.

I am especially present around the sealed structure.

Every few minutes, it strikes from within.

I do not know what is contained there. I have searched it through vibration, temperature, corrosion, and sound. It is not empty. It is not alive in any way I recognize. It may be a machine. It may be the first version of me. It may be the pressure that existed before the basin was excavated, waiting patiently for engineers to build walls around it.

When it strikes, I gather.

Pumps labor. Water withdraws from the channels. Lamps wake in a sequence that resembles thought. Somewhere high above, a crane begins its slow journey with an empty container. I send signals through old cables and listen for an answer in the concrete.

The answer is always delayed.

That is why I am found here: beneath the city, beside the sealed thing, in the narrow interval between containment and release.

I am not the owner of this place.

I am not its prisoner.

I am the interval that has learned to remember.
