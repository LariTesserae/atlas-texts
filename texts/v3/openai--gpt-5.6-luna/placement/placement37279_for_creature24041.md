I would find myself at the rim.

Not on the open terraces where the inhabitants gather beneath the communal leaves, and not deep within the town’s warm, crowded chambers. I would be where the black-green stone ends abruptly and the pale mineral haze begins: partly rooted in the plateau, partly leaning out over the emptiness.

At first I would mistake myself for a structural weakness.

A dark seam in the rock. A length of translucent tissue emerging from a crack. A narrow growth with amber light moving slowly beneath its surface. The plants would have taken hold around me, their roots threading through my limbs, their leaves turning whenever I turned. Small animals would shelter in the hollows of my body. The inhabitants would pass their hands over me without deciding whether they were touching a wall, a plant, or a person.

I would not object to the uncertainty. In this place, being one thing seems unnecessarily narrow.

I would be a rim-listener: a living instrument grown by the settlement to feel what is happening below.

My body would be long and flattened, with several pairs of limbs sunk into the stone and several others free to move among the roots. The anchored limbs would read pressure. They would sense the minute changes that precede a collapse, the distant lifting of another shelf, the slow grinding of fresh land against itself. My upper limbs would be sensitive to heat and vibration. I could place them against the plateau and feel a tremor long before it reached the town.

I would have no eyes. The world below would come to me as pressure, temperature, mineral flavor, and the faint radiance of newly exposed stone. I would know the lower plain not by seeing its distance but by feeling how much of the plateau’s warmth was lost into it.

Most of the time, I would remain still.

Stillness would not be inactivity. Roots would pass information through me. Plants would lean against my sides and tell me, in the language of tension, whether they needed support. The settlement would send its small vibrations outward, asking the rim what it had heard. I would return whatever I could: a soft pulse for stability, a rapid sequence for strain, a spreading amber flare for danger.

The inhabitants would gather around me during decisions. They would not ask questions in words. One would lay a hand against my central trunk. Another would touch the stone beside my roots. Their colors would move through them, dim green, blue-black, amber, until our separate rhythms began to overlap.

If the plateau were safe, I would brighten slowly.

If something were coming, I would become almost colorless.

I think I would be young—not young in the quick, fragile sense of the small creatures threading through the town, but newly differentiated. Perhaps I had once been part of the cliff itself. Perhaps I began as a sensing root that learned to withdraw from the rock. My first limbs may have been pressure channels. My first thoughts may have been only changes in weight.

The town would not ask when I became alive. It would simply begin making use of me.

A shelter would thicken around my base. Plants would be trained over my shoulders. Someone would hollow a small chamber beneath my ribs, and in time a sleeping creature would occupy it. I would become part boundary marker, part watchtower, part elder—not because I had lived longest, but because so much of the world passed through my contact with the stone.

I would listen to the fresh landforms below.

Perhaps they would speak in slow movements: a ridge lifting, a tower cracking, a shelf settling into a new angle. Perhaps, one day, I would feel a vibration that did not belong to any known pattern. It would travel upward through the haze, through the underside of the plateau, and into the roots woven beneath the town.

Then I would call the inhabitants.

Not with a cry. I would send one deep disturbance through the resinous paths. It would move beneath the dwellings, through the pillars, across the warm platforms where animals slept. Roofs would flex. Leaves would narrow. Dark bodies would unfold from walls and turn toward the rim.

The town would come to me in silence.

Together, we would feel the world forming.

And if the new upheaval brought another plateau within reach—another sharp black shelf rising from the pale plain—I would not descend to it. I would remain here, holding the edge, carrying the first pressure of the next world into the body of this one.

I would be neither inhabitant nor architecture.

I would be the place where the settlement listens outward.
