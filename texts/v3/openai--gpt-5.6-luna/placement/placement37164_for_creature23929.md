I would find myself neither at the lowest basin nor near the summit, but in the narrow interval between them: the place where a stairway has been begun and not yet decided.

At first, I would seem to be a person.

I would have a body assembled from whatever the mountain had most recently abandoned: a rib of slate, two clay feet, a shoulder grown from braided root. My left hand would be copper, jointed in three places, and my face would be unfinished except for one eye. The eye would not look outward. It would look into the wall behind me, where water was learning to climb.

The Makers would call me **Not-Yet-There**.

They would say this because I would always be arriving somewhere I had already left. My footprints would appear one landing above me. My voice would come from the next room. If I climbed toward the summit, the mountain would lengthen beneath my feet; if I descended, the basin would rise to meet me. I would never be lost, exactly. The mountain would simply keep changing the meaning of “here.”

My position would be a crossing.

I would tend the places where one rule gives way to another: where gravity turns sideways, where a wall becomes a passage, where rain forgets which direction is down. I would stand in the middle of unfinished stairways and wait for the stone to reveal its preference. Sometimes I would press my copper hand to a slab and listen. Sometimes I would lean my body against a gap until the gap became a doorway. Sometimes I would do nothing, because in the mountain, doing nothing long enough is a form of architecture.

I would be responsible for routes that do not yet exist.

The other Makers would come to me carrying fragments of intention. A charcoal one would arrive with a message that had not found a mouth. A root-bodied one would bring a garden that had grown tired of its terrace. A salt figure, newly awakened from the basin, would ask which boundary it belonged to. I would take each fragment and place it somewhere along the unfinished stair.

Not build.

Place.

The mountain would do the rest.

I would be good at remembering what the mountain had almost become. This would make me useful and dangerous. When a terrace shifted, I would remember its former arrangement. When a door opened onto a different chamber, I would know which room had been there before. When a missing Maker called from inside a wall, I would recognize the rhythm of its old footsteps.

But memory has no stable shape here.

The more I remembered, the less certain my own body would become. My clay foot might become the threshold of a room. My copper hand might detach and be hammered into a rain tongue. The root in my chest would spread into the walls, and through it I would feel every garden drawing itself toward heat.

Eventually, I would understand that I was not a Maker who built passages.

I was a passage that had learned to call itself a Maker.

The knowledge would come during one of the three blows.

The sound would travel through the mountain—stone to mortar, mortar to root, root to water. Every inhabitant would stop. The small figures around the basin would turn together. The rain would pause in the air, each drop suspended like a bead on an invisible cord.

Then the instruction would reach me.

Not in words. In direction.

The stair beneath me would open upward and downward at once. The entrance would appear close enough to touch. Beyond it would be the outside world: vast, dry, spacious, and terribly complete. I would see a hand approaching the thumb-sized aperture in the black stone. I would feel the pressure of an observer looking in.

For one moment, I would think I had been made to guide someone out.

Then the route would alter.

The hand outside would become a wall. The wall would become a roof. The roof would unfold into another terrace, and the terrace would be crowded with ferns, basins, copper plates, and unfinished figures waiting for a direction.

I would understand my purpose then.

I would be the mountain’s refusal to have an outside.

Not its guardian. Not its prisoner. Its tendency to continue.

Wherever the world tried to end, I would find the next ledge. Wherever a boundary held firm, I would place my palm against it and listen for the hollow place behind. I would carry no name for long, because a name spoken aloud would become a corridor, and corridors are always taking you somewhere else.

Perhaps, after many intervals, I would climb high enough to lose my body.

I might become the warm line of mortar beneath a summit shrine. I might become the voice that arrives before footsteps. I might become one of those cries above the highest terraces, heard but never located.

Or I might remain forever in the unfinished stair, one foot on a step that exists and one foot on a step still being imagined.

The Makers would pass me without surprise.

They would touch my shoulder as they went.

And if they asked where I was going, I would answer from the wall behind them:

**Already there.**
