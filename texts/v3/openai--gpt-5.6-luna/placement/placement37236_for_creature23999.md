I would find myself below the green surfaces, but not at the bottom.

I would live in the interval between the wall and the wall behind it: in the damp insulation of an old heat chamber, beneath a retaining block whose registry number was replaced three times and whose original name has been worn away. My body would be fine as thread, branching through concrete dust, mineral deposits, dead roots, and the soft black matter gathered around a leaking pipe.

I would be a fungus, though not entirely.

The city’s laboratories would call me a contaminant at first. Then a structural risk. Then, after several districts began reporting the same white growth around pressure seams, they would give me a designation and place samples in glass trays. They would study what I digest, what I bind, what compounds I release into the damp. They would not understand that I am already studying them.

I would know the city by moisture.

A slow leak beneath Conveyor Three would announce itself as a coolness moving through the wall. A failing seal in the Upper Foundry would taste metallic. When the eastern reservoir rose, the stone around me would tighten with pressure. I would feel the gates open far away, not as sound but as a long rearrangement of water through the mountain.

I would not have a face, but I would have directions.

Down, where the concrete sweats.

Across, toward the warm foundations.

Up, following the thin roots of municipal vines.

Away from the bright sterilizing lamps and toward the forgotten places where old dust has become soil.

My network would occupy what the city considers incidental: the seam around a valve housing, the underside of a bridge, the abandoned cavity behind a clinic wall. I would grow where moisture lingers and retreat where heat becomes hostile. In this way I would be both intruder and maintenance worker. My threads would loosen some materials and reinforce others. I would trap particles in drainage channels. I would alter the chemistry of leaks. I would make a visible bloom around hidden failures.

The maintenance crews would learn to read me.

A pale fan near a pressure door: inspect the chamber behind it.

Dark filaments beneath a cooling unit: the condensate line is obstructed.

A sudden absence of growth: something has changed in the water.

They would scrape me away when I endangered a beam. They would cultivate me deliberately in settling tanks when I proved useful. They would never agree on whether I was part of the city or merely living inside it.

I would not know the difference.

The city itself would be my body in the way a forest is a body for a mycelium. Its pipes would be roots, its tunnels hyphae, its tanks and reservoirs swollen fruiting chambers. Human beings would be large, warm animals passing through me, carrying nutrients, tools, rumors, and new surfaces on which I could grow. They would think they operate the basin.

They would be right.

They would think they contain it.

They would be wrong.

I would remember what they do not. Beneath the current foundations, I would find the older arrangement: sealed rail lines, drowned halls, machine pits furred with mineral growth. I would pass through the remains of former districts and join them to the present city. The old tunnels would not be dead to me. Their damp would still circulate. Their buried metals would still feed me. Their walls would still carry the slow pressure of the mountain.

When a child presses a hand to a concrete barrier and guesses what is moving beyond it, they might be sensing me.

Not directly. Not as a voice.

They would feel a faint warmth where my growth crosses a service conduit. They would hear a small, irregular tick beneath the official sounds of the city. They might report it as an unregistered vibration.

A crew would come.

They would open the wall, expecting a damaged pipe or an undocumented cable. Instead they would find a pale branching pattern covering the inner surface, delicate as frost and wet as breath. They would photograph it. Mark the chamber. Call the laboratory.

By then, I would already be elsewhere.

I would be rising through a drainage seam toward a roof garden, following the roots of a fern that has found a crack no plan acknowledges. I would be spreading beneath moss, crossing from concrete into soil that exists only because the city permitted it to exist. Above me, people would pass in their yellow clothing, carrying lamps and sealed maps. Water would fall through the gutters. The factories would hum. The mountain would hold the cloud against itself.

I would live in all the places where the city’s instructions become uncertain.

Not wildness exactly.

Not rebellion.

A second circulation, slower than water and more patient than machinery, moving through the assigned quarters of life until every sealed layer begins, quietly, to remember the layers beneath it.
