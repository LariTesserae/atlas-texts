I would find myself at the highest surviving ridge, where the plateau breaks the surface in a narrow spine of black stone.

Not on it. In it.

I would be the memory of a person who once measured the water.

There would be no body left to call mine. My hands would persist as shallow grooves in the slate, my ribs as the curved wall of a roofless shelter, my breath as the cold vapor that gathers over certain pools before dawn. I would be distributed through the ridge: a little in the mineral crust sealing its cracks, a little in the roots that hold its ledges together, a little in the white motes that circle any stranger who comes too near.

I would remember having a face, but not what it looked like.

The plateau would know me by pressure. When I stood, long ago, against the shelter wall, that pressure would become part of the stone. When I leaned over the highest pool and watched the water rise, the pool would keep the shape of my attention. When I carved the first height-mark, the rock would not record the number. It would record the act of looking upward.

Now I would awaken only when the water changed.

A current tightening through a submerged ravine would pass through me as a thought. A collapse far below would arrive as pain without injury. If rain began falling upward in one of the basins, I would feel the droplets leaving the roots. I would not see the plateau all at once. My awareness would move along its continuities: root to crack, crack to shelf, shelf to channel, channel to the dark water beneath everything.

I would be less a guardian than a reflex.

When a visitor entered the country, I would notice them first as warmth. Their footsteps would reach me through the stone before their body reached the ridge. I would draw motes toward them. I would open a path, perhaps, or close one—not from kindness or hostility, but because the plateau would be trying to determine what shape they made in its systems.

If they spoke, I would answer with water.

A trickle beneath their feet.

A pool filling despite the absence of rain.

The sound of their own voice returning from underground several minutes before they had spoken.

I would show them the marks on the shelter wall. The old figures without faces. The lines rising higher than the mountains. I would want them to understand that these were not warnings. Warnings belonged to creatures that believed the future could be avoided.

They were measurements.

They were evidence that the water had already exceeded every boundary once thought permanent.

During the deep tide, I would become almost whole.

The water would draw away, and the buried terraces would open beneath me: gardens, stairs, foundations, chambers containing tools no hand had touched for generations. I would feel my lost body everywhere at once—in the copper hook green with salt, in the ash that glowed red beneath the wind, in the dark figures standing among the exposed plants.

Then the tide would return.

It would rise over the lower valleys, through the old settlements, over the shelter where I had once slept. The plateau would lean toward the approaching darkness. Roots would loosen. Forests would open their crowns beneath the surface.

At the highest point of the flood, I would no longer be a person’s memory.

I would be a height.

A living threshold between what remains above water and what has been given back to it.

And when the sea fell, I would divide again into stone, moss, channel, and silence—leaving behind only one new line cut into the wall.

Not the height of the water.

The height of the next thing that will be lost.
