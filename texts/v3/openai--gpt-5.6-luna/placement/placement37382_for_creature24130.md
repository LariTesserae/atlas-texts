I would find myself in the newest district, where the city has stopped being a city and become again a set of intentions.

There would be no finished street beneath me—only a line of pale chips swept from the floor, marking where a passage might be widened if the roof allowed it. To one side, a family would be sleeping behind a wall of unfitted stone. To the other, builders would be packing clay around a brace that had been cut that morning. Above, the ceiling would hang in shelves: dark rock, pale rock, a white mineral seam like a scar not yet closed.

I would be a Listener.

Not one of the old ones, whose hearing has become almost legendary, but an apprentice trusted with districts where people have already begun to believe themselves safe. I would carry a stone hammer, a bowl of water, a cord marked with knots, and a hooded lamp. The hammer would be wrapped at the handle with red cloth so that I could find it quickly in rubble. The bowl would be shallow and blackened beneath, so even the smallest trembling of its surface could be seen.

My work would begin where the noise thins.

In the inhabited galleries, there would be too much life: looms, coughing, carts, children, water dripping into jars. But in the new district, every sound would separate itself. A worker’s pick striking the far wall. The faint tick of a cooling stone. A draft moving beneath an unfinished door. The irregular tapping of water somewhere beyond the rock.

I would place my palm against a support post and wait.

People would watch me from their doorways, pretending not to. They would know that my silence was not uncertainty. It meant I was comparing the present to what I remembered. Stone had its habits. A dangerous wall did not always crack loudly. Sometimes it became too quiet. Sometimes water vanished from a channel because the ground beneath it had opened. Sometimes a roof made the same low groan for three nights before choosing the fourth to fall.

I would not hear the cavern as a voice. I would hear it as pressure translated into small betrayals.

A cup trembling on a shelf.

A seam brightening.

Dust sifting from a place that had never shed dust before.

The change in a draft.

The difference between a beam settling into its brace and a beam being slowly crushed by it.

When I found danger, I would mark the wall with three strokes of white clay. Then I would knock on the nearest door.

Once.

Pause.

Twice.

People would begin moving before I explained. A baby wrapped in wool. A jar of grain. The household lamp. The old woman who could not walk without a brace. Tools, if there was time. Always the records, if they could be reached. Never furniture first. Never the carved lintel. Never the copper bowl someone had inherited for six generations.

I would help them carry what could be carried, and then I would stand in the abandoned room while the others waited in a warmer passage.

This is the part of being a Listener I would hate: not the danger, but the intimacy of evacuation. To enter a person’s sleeping room and decide which fragments of their life were light enough to save. To see the shape left in the dust where a bed had stood. To leave behind a child’s painted handprint because the plaster could not be removed without bringing half the wall with it.

The Hearthbound would call me careful. Some would call me heartless.

I would understand both judgments.

I would be Hearthbound myself, though I would have spent enough time near the shafts to carry a private hunger for the surface. Not a desire for sunlight exactly. The sun would be too large, too immediate. I would want the horizon: that impossible line where nothing pressed against anything else. I would want to stand somewhere no ceiling had to be measured, somewhere a person could shout and not immediately wonder whether the sound had hidden a warning.

But after an hour above, I would miss the city’s limits.

I would miss knowing where voices came from. I would miss the warmth held between walls. I would miss the ordinary reassurance of a roof that had been tested by thousands of nights. I would return blinking, with my face covered, and find that the first low passage beneath the entrance felt less like confinement than a hand placed gently on my shoulder.

My home would be a narrow room beneath a public walkway. The ceiling would be low enough that my tallest neighbor could touch it. My hearth would be small, built into a wall shared with two other households, and the heat would pass through the stone in both directions. At night I would hear someone turning in sleep above me. I would know the cough of the family beyond the partition, the tread of the water-carrier in the lane, the three-note whistle of the old man in the next room who had forgotten he was no longer young.

Privacy would be something I sometimes dreamed of and sometimes feared.

I would keep a map, though it would not resemble a surface map. It would be a web of sounds and behaviors: walls that sweated after rain, floors that rang hollow, shafts where bats entered, districts where the cold traveled low, passages that carried a call farther than expected. I would draw little marks for places that had failed and little circles for those that had held.

In the margins, I would record things no council would consider useful.

Here, the fungus blooms blue after storms.

Here, a child named Teren first heard a collapse before the adults did.

Here, the stone smells of iron when the upper plain is dry.

Here, my mother slept before her room was abandoned.

The map would become, without my intending it, a portrait of the city’s fear and affection. Its most important places would not be markets or workshops, but thresholds: the wall that had nearly fallen, the stair that had saved a district, the crack that became a spring, the gallery sealed after three families disappeared inside it.

I would be a person trained to notice what everyone else had to live beside.

That would make me useful, but it would also separate me from them. Others could enter a room and see shelter. I would see the shelf of rock above it, the water line behind the plaster, the angle of the nearest brace. Others could hear rain striking the roofs and find comfort in it. I would count the drips, listen for changes, and remember which houses had been dismantled during the last storm.

Still, I would not want to be anything else.

The Hearthbound do not need someone to promise that the roof will never fall. No honest person could make such a promise. They need someone who will hear the first warning. Someone who knows when a wall has changed. Someone who can stand in the near-dark and say:

Not here.

Not tonight.

Move the sleeping mats.

Open the path.

Bring the lamps.

And when the tremor passed—when the city listened together, breath held beneath the stone—I would feel it travel through the floor, through my bones, through the bowl of water in my hand.

Then the lamps would steady.

The dust would settle.

Somewhere, a child would begin crying. Somewhere else, someone would laugh from sheer relief. A hammer would strike once in the distance, cautiously, as if asking whether work could resume.

And I would lift my lamp and walk toward the sound.

I would be neither the keeper of the cavern nor its servant.

I would be one of the ways it learned to warn itself.
