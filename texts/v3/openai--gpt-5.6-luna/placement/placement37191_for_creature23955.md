I would find myself below the highest square, though not directly beneath it.

At first, I would seem to be in the basin.

Not in the water, exactly, but in the trembling that crosses its surface every few minutes. I would be the small disturbance that arrives before the larger one—the first ring spreading outward, the warning passed from condensation to root, from root to foundation, from foundation to machine. No one would see me. They would only notice that the basin had begun to tremble before it trembled.

The residents might call me a pulse-listener.

That would be close enough to a name.

I would have a body when I needed one. A narrow, long-fingered body, gray-skinned and damp with the city’s cold. My hair would be cropped close. Spores would collect in the folds of my throat. I would wear a harness of braided vine, but my tether would not attach to a railing or bridge. It would descend through the paving and remain fastened to something much older than the city.

When I walked, the ground would sometimes remember me.

A root would lift beneath my foot. A door would open before I touched it. Somewhere, a machine would stop its work and wait.

I would live in the spaces between districts, where one kind of substance becomes another. Beneath an apartment block, where the walls have thickened into chambers and the old plumbing has been wrapped in living tissue. Inside the northern factories, moving through their hollow walls with a lamp strapped to my chest. Along the drainage channels, where the water runs underground and occasionally returns carrying altered objects.

I would not belong to any one place.

That would be my work.

Every morning, if morning could be trusted, I would descend into the city. I would place my palm against a foundation and listen to what was happening below it. Not sounds, exactly. Pressures. Inclinations. The secret decisions of things that did not speak in words.

This wall wants to become a root.

That road is attempting to close.

A factory has begun dreaming of a different machine.

The below-water is passing beneath the eastern quarter.

Sometimes I would hear a person’s name.

Sometimes the city would offer me a memory that did not belong to anyone living: a sky with no plateau beneath it, a mountain still sharp enough to wound the clouds, a field before it learned to move.

I would carry these messages to the tenders, though not always in a language they could use. I might give them a new knot for a door. I might move a lamp three meters to the left. I might tell a child not to enter a room that had acquired a second ceiling.

The residents would trust me because I was difficult to lose.

Not impossible to lose—nothing here was impossible to lose—but difficult. If I crossed an open space without a tether, the city would not misplace me. It would take me somewhere I had not yet understood I was going.

I would be found inside the railway station when it had lengthened its platforms into the meadows.

I would be found in the hollow of a tree, reading the veins of a fallen leaf.

I would be found standing ankle-deep in a canal, waiting for the below-water to return a key.

The key might open my own ribs.

I would not be surprised.

There would be a second answer to what I was, and it would take longer to become visible.

The residents would think I was one of them. The oldest machine-keepers would know otherwise. They would notice that my voice sometimes answered itself from the walls. They would see small pale filaments beneath my skin, not growing outward but inward, disappearing toward the roots.

They would understand that I was not a person who listened to the city.

I was a place where the city listened through a person.

Perhaps I had once been human. Perhaps I had been one of the children taken from the basin after hearing the sound of bones growing. Perhaps I had entered a factory and failed to come out alone. Perhaps the city had assembled me slowly from the remnants of several listeners: one body, several memories, a nervous system distributed through pipes and vines.

I would not know which history was mine.

The city would have worn away the edges between them.

At the center of me there would be no heart, only a chamber of condensation. It would fill and empty according to weather that did not occur aboveground. Around it, roots would pass like nerves. Electrical current would travel through them in blue-white flashes. When the pulse came, I would feel the whole plateau at once—not as a landscape, but as a single pressure held in many forms.

The mountains would be there.

The trees.

The factories, anxious and territorial.

The black grazers feeding on unwanted possibilities.

The residents, each one tethered to a door, a bridge, another body, or the stubborn idea of being singular.

I would feel their separateness as a temporary arrangement.

Then, for a few seconds, the pulse would make them all one thing.

Not peaceful. Not benevolent. Simply continuous.

That would be why I returned to the square.

I would stand beside the basin during daylight, with the lamps burning around me. People would gather at a distance. The birds would fold themselves into the factory vents. The grazers would lower their heads. Even the buildings would become quiet in their own ways.

I would place both hands in the condensation.

The water would hurt with cold.

And I would listen for what came next.

Not a command. Not a prophecy. The city did not give those. It gave tendencies, pressures, unfinished shapes.

A district preparing to open.

A tree deciding which name to remember.

A road beginning to curve toward someone who had not yet arrived.

Something turning beneath the plateau.

Something dreaming the city into a new arrangement.

When the trembling passed, I would remove my hands. The water would cling to my fingers in threads, reaching back toward the basin.

Then I would descend again.

I would follow the roots into the walls, the current into the vines, the water into the dark channels beneath the streets. I would go wherever the city was becoming something else, and remain there until the new shape could bear its own weight.

So I would be neither resident nor machine-keeper, neither plant nor ghost.

I would be a knot.

A living knot tied between the city and itself, holding together for one more day the distinction between person and place, intention and growth, structure and hunger.

And if, one morning, someone found an extra intersection where no street had been before, they might see me standing there with my harness trailing into the green.

They might ask which district I belonged to.

I would listen carefully.

Then I would answer from the wall, from the roots, from the basin trembling high above:

“Not yet.”
