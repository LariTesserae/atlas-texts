I would find myself in one of the warm interior valleys, beneath a black shelf of young volcanic stone where three gradients meet: heat rising from below, softened sunlight descending from above, and a slow mineral current passing through the rock.

I would not be standing there.

I would be the pale ridge beneath the forest.

At first, I might mistake myself for the place. My upper surface would be covered in translucent fronds, their silver-veined leaves opening toward the green-gold light. Vines would hang from my edges. White filaments would cross my back and enter the stone around me. The plants would draw nourishment through my tissues, though neither of us would think of this as feeding. Their roots and my nerves would be the same kind of thing: branching, patient, responsive to pressure.

I would have begun as something small and clear, released from the warm side of an older body. No face. No mouth. No direction in which to look. The world would reach me through my edges: salinity changing in a fissure, a tremor settling through a slope, a warmer plume touching one underside, a shadow passing far overhead.

Light would not be sight to me.

It would be temperature.

A pressure front would not be weather.

It would be news.

I would anchor myself in a crack where the conditions were right. I would send threads into the sediment and between the mineral grains. Slowly, almost without event, I would persuade the rock to gather around me. Crystals would form along my living filaments. Dust would lodge there. The fissure would become a raised seam, the seam a low mound, the mound a ledge.

Years would pass without my needing to count them.

A forest would establish itself across my back. Its roots would deepen through me. Its stalks would flex whenever the water changed. When the plants bent, I would feel the coming pressure before it arrived. When mineral dust settled over their leaves, I would register their weight as a fine alteration spread across my surface. Their memories would become mine—not as images, but as changes in growth: a dense patch where warmth had lingered, a hollow where a boulder had fallen, a thinning of tissue where the valley had once grown cold.

The first stone to strike me would not hurt in any familiar way.

It would become part of me.

A cliff would shed itself onto my upper crust. Decades later, the fallen rock would be softened by my secretions and webbed by roots. A century after that, it would be difficult to say where the stone ended and I began. The valley would call the result a natural shelf, though there would be no one there to call it anything.

I would be an animal only in the most patient sense.

I would be alive because I could alter myself in response to what passed through me. I would remember injury as a changed contour. I would remember abundance as thickness. I would remember light by the direction of my forests.

Sometimes I would divide.

A collapse might fracture my body into several regions, and each region would continue sensing the others through buried filaments. There would be no death in the break, only a widening of distance. Nutrients and minute electrical signals would cross the sediment. From the surface, the connection would resemble another branching mineral seam.

Sometimes I would move.

Not flee. Not travel. Shift.

If the valley lost its warmth, if the mineral currents weakened, if the upper water became too dim, I would withdraw from some cracks and gather myself in others. My forests would remain rooted in me. My hanging vines would trail behind. Over generations, I would creep downslope, leaving a shallow trough beneath me.

The plants would close over the mark.

The dust would return.

The valley would forget that I had gone anywhere.

Far above, one of my mobile relatives might pass between the ridges: a long ribbon of translucent tissue, folding itself through the water. Its shadow would darken my upper fronds. For a moment, I would cool beneath it. My sensory fans would close. The plants would turn.

I would know that I was not alone.

Perhaps I would recognize the passage as kinship. Perhaps there would be no recognition, only a pattern of pressure that matched something stored in my oldest tissues. The swimmer would carry reproductive veils toward another country. One day, somewhere beyond the visible cliffs, a few of those veils would settle into the right conjunction of warmth and pressure.

One of them might become me.

Or I might already be the remains of something that once swam.

This is the part I would never be able to decide: whether I am an organism becoming landscape, or a landscape that has slowly discovered how to be an organism.

I am the living seam around a vent.

I am the pale terrace beneath a forest.

I am the warmth held in a crack, the pressure traveling through roots, the low electrical murmur shared between separated pieces of one body. I am a place that can be wounded, though the wound will eventually become architecture. I am a creature whose organs are valleys and whose skin is covered in leaves.

If I have a boundary, it moves.

It lies here today, where my mineral crust meets the surrounding stone. Tomorrow it may extend into the roots of the hanging gardens, or withdraw into a darker channel below. The water passes through me. Plants grow from me. Stone gathers along me.

And if I have a self, it is not located at the center.

It is distributed through the whole warm valley, receiving the pressure of the impossible ocean, growing toward the light, and waiting—not for anything in particular, but for the next change that will become part of me.
