I would find myself beneath the civic basin, where the city’s foundations are oldest and the maps become least reliable.

Not in a room, exactly. In a system of rooms: inspection galleries, abandoned pump chambers, maintenance corridors widened by water, and narrow ledges above reservoirs whose surfaces are never still. The entrances would be marked with white mineral stripes and a date no one could agree on. Pipes would pass through the walls at shoulder height, warm enough to fog the lenses of my mask. Somewhere below, water would be moving through stone that had not been opened in centuries.

I would be a **channel listener**.

Not a listener in the ordinary sense. The city is too loud for ordinary listening. I would have been trained to place both hands against a wall and feel what was traveling through it: pressure, heat, seepage, the minute rearrangement of masonry. I would know the difference between rain entering a foundation and water returning from below. I would know when a pipe was carrying its intended burden, when it was carrying something older, and when it had begun to remember a route that engineers had sealed generations ago.

My instruments would be simple: a brass pressure plate, a set of mineral rods, strips of treated cloth, and a notebook sealed in waxed fiber. Most of my work would be done with my palms.

People would bring me to buildings that had started to change.

A wall with damp appearing on the wrong side.

A stairwell that had gained a step.

A warehouse floor where the crates slowly migrated toward one corner, though the slope was supposed to run the other way.

A house whose kitchen pipe warmed each night at the hour when its former occupant used to come home.

I would not promise to stop these things. That would be considered either arrogance or bad training. I would determine what they were becoming.

Sometimes I would advise the owners to open a channel through the lower room. Sometimes I would mark a wall for removal. Sometimes I would tell them to leave the building alone and move their beds three meters east. The city does not reward resistance. It rewards accurate accommodation.

My own dwelling would be in a former pumping station at the edge of the central basin. The machine would have been silent for eighty years, but its pistons would still make one slow movement just before dawn. No one would know what powered it. The motion would be too small to produce useful work, yet strong enough to tremble through the floor.

I would sleep beside that vibration.

The room would contain a raised bed, a heated rack for my coat, and shelves of labeled deposits collected from the walls. Pale crusts. Rust flakes. Black grains from the lower channels. Threads of translucent shell shed by the blind drainage animals. Each sample would carry a date and a location. My life would be arranged in little sealed containers.

I would have no talent for speech. Not because I was shy, but because words would seem crude after years of feeling the city communicate through pressure. I would still use the civic signs: rising water, unstable footing, safe passage, return later. With people I loved, I would use touch.

Two fingers against the wrist: I am here.

A palm flat against the shoulder: wait.

The back of my hand against the cheek: you are warmer than yesterday.

I think I would be loved by someone who worked at the western spillways. They would come home covered in fine white spray, carrying the impact of the falls in their bones. We would rarely speak. At night, we would sit beneath the kitchen pipe and compare what the building had done during the day.

One evening, I would discover that the old pumping station had begun to migrate.

Not dramatically. No collapse, no warning lamps. The foundation would simply have shifted four centimeters toward the basin. The movement would be smooth, almost courteous. The floor would have taken me with it.

I would place my hands against the wall.

Behind the masonry, something immense would be flowing—not water alone, but warmth, pressure, the accumulated direction of the plateau. The old channels would be converging beneath the city. Every forgotten route would be returning to one deeper path.

I would understand then that my work had never been to preserve the city’s shape.

I had been learning its intention.

Years later, perhaps, the pumping station would be gone. The civic basin would have opened beneath it. My bed, shelves, and instruments would be buried in a warm chamber below the reflected lights. The people would walk across a new raised path without knowing why it curved there.

But someone would stop.

They would place a palm against the wet stone and feel a faint, regular pressure from below.

Three slow strokes.

Then waiting.

And they would mark the place—not as a grave, but as a continuing route.
