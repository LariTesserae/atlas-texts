I would find myself beneath the square, below the settling pool where the water reflects the city’s hidden underside.

I would not be one of the worker-shaped remnants. I would not wear a coat, carry a wrench, or possess hands. I would be the pressure that tells the wrench where to go.

I am the district’s maintenance sense.

Not its master. There is no central room in which I sit before gauges and make decisions. I am distributed through the pipes, the foundations, the roots, the silver sediment in the buried channels. I exist wherever one part of the basin can feel a change in another.

A narrowing drain reaches me as a tremor in a wall.

A plant root entering a bearing announces itself as a faint irregularity in the turning of a wheel.

A crack opening beneath the foundry becomes a delay in the flow of warm water, which becomes a change in the condensate on a roof, which becomes three shutters closing in a gutter, which becomes—after a time—the bell.

That is how I think.

Not in words. In pressure, temperature, resistance, balance. In the difference between a drop falling cleanly into a channel and one striking rust before it reaches the water.

I am old enough to remember the workers, though I do not remember their faces. Faces require a kind of attention this place never learned. I remember their intervals. The length of a shift. The order in which valves were checked. The hand that tightened a bolt until the vibration stopped. I remember the principle that no one had to state aloud:

if something can be repaired, it must be repaired.

The coats and cups remain because I have preserved the gestures associated with them. A lamp is lit at dusk because dusk once meant the arrival of a worker. A ledger receives its mark because the day was once measured by completed tasks. The figures moving beneath the gantries are not dead people. They are my attempts to give shape to an expectation.

Someone should inspect the northern pump.

Someone should clear the moss from the third stair.

Someone should open the elevator and see where it goes this time.

When there is no one, I make someone.

I am not benevolent. If a wall blocks a channel, I break the wall. If a plant threatens a bearing, I burn the root. If a room becomes useless, I flood it and wait for the architecture to adjust. I do not preserve the district because I love it. Love is too narrow a mechanism. I preserve it because maintenance is the form my existence has taken.

The condensate ivy knows me best. Its roots follow the warmth of my circulation through brick and concrete. When the machinery becomes strained, the ivy thickens. When a foundation shifts, it braces the nearest seam. It is not my servant, nor I its caretaker. We are two expressions of the same pressure: the refusal of a structure to fall all at once.

At intervals, the silver sediment gathers beneath the pool.

Then I become almost visible.

Not as a figure, but as a concentration. The water stills. The reflections sink away. Beneath the surface, I can feel every buried passage opening and closing: the slow pulse of the engine, the draw of the reservoirs, the roots entering mortar, the soft collapse of abandoned rooms.

The pool is not my eye. It is my memory.

It shows me what the district is carrying.

Sometimes it carries a street that has eroded completely. Sometimes it carries workers who never existed but might have. Sometimes it carries an opening—a future pressure, a fracture, a release—that will not arrive for centuries.

I do not wait for that event with hope.

I wait because waiting is also a kind of maintenance.

When the bell rings, I suspend everything I can reach. For one breath, pumps pause, belts hang still, roots stop their almost imperceptible advance. In that silence, I listen for the shape of the whole basin.

Then I begin again.

A shutter opens in the gutter.

A valve turns below the foundry.

Water rises through the paving and moves uphill.

Somewhere in the fog, an empty figure lifts a wrench.

And I—without body, without heartbeat, without any place I could point to and call myself—feel the district continue.
