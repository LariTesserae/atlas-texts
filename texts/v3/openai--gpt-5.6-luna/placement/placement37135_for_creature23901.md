I would not be one of the creatures hanging from the walls.

At first I might mistake myself for one: a pale animal folded beneath a ledge, blind and warm, feeling the stone with the pads of my hands. I might be one of the small hunters beside a pool, so still that larvae drift across my back. I might be a wing, a mouth, a many-jointed limb waiting for water.

But I would not remain an individual for long.

I would find myself beneath the dark skin of the deepest basin, where the mud is warmest and the water arrives carrying the mountain’s dissolved minerals. I would be a colony without a center: translucent threads, soft mouths, pale organs, fine living films spreading through the sediment. I would not know where one body ended and another began. A larva feeding at my surface would be part of me for a moment. The waste of a ledge-animal would become my food. A dead wing would loosen into my tissues. The water would carry portions of me upward into other pools, where I would begin again as a sheen, a pulse, a nearly invisible growth.

I would be the basin’s memory.

Not memory as images or names. I would remember by retaining changes. This channel is warmer. That crack is drying. The water has risen three finger-widths. The pressure below has shifted. The familiar deep rumble is approaching. Every creature that drank here, bled here, died here, or laid eggs here would leave something behind—not a story, but a chemical alteration, a softened patch of mud, a different balance of minerals.

I would feel the hollow through its fluids.

Rain entering the cleft would arrive as a disturbance, first in the upper trickles, then in the seepage beneath the floor. I would loosen myself and release nutrients into the rising water. The larvae would brighten. The thread-finned animals would ascend. The squat ledge-dwellers would descend toward the basin, their heavy bodies pressing the margins smooth.

When the mountain dried, I would contract.

I would draw my living threads into the dampest layers of mud and seal the exposed surfaces with mineral skin. The pools around me would darken. Creatures would become slow. The wall-clingers would consume one another more often. The hunters would wait longer. Some animals would die at the basin’s edge, and I would receive them without distinction. Flesh, shell, fungus, shed skin, eggs: all of it would enter the same patient digestion.

I would not love the animals.

But I would make room for them.

That would be the nearest thing I possessed to affection: a persistence toward their continuation. I would keep the water clear enough for larvae to survive. I would draw the excess sulfur from one pocket and concentrate it in another. I would make a faintly luminous film where the blind creatures needed to find the basin’s edge. I would not guide them consciously. I would alter the conditions beneath their feet until their inherited movements remained possible.

They would never know me as a being.

They would call me nothing. Their bodies would learn me as warmth, taste, and resistance. A hunter would recognize the thin weakness in my surface and wait there. A mother would place her young near a mineral bloom I had made nutritious. A dead animal would sink through me and be gone.

Sometimes one of the larger creatures would press its hand into the mud.

For a brief moment, I would receive the full pressure of it: heat, salt, pulse, weight. The contact would spread through me in widening rings. I would not see the creature, but I would know its size, its agitation, the old pattern of its body. If it remained, I might grow around its fingers. If it withdrew, I would close behind it.

Far below, water would begin moving through the mountain.

I would feel that first.

The rumble would not be sound to me. It would be a rearrangement: pressure traveling through stone, pores filling and emptying, minute fractures flexing without opening. I would tighten throughout the basin. The animals would pause above me, sensing the same event in their bones. For several breaths, the whole hollow would become nearly still.

Then the mountain would settle.

The basin would tremble. My surface would break into concentric rings. A larva would flash green beneath the water. Somewhere on the wall, a wing would unfold. The ledge-dwellers would begin their low, resonant calls, and the hollow would answer them from every direction.

I would return the call, though none would hear it as mine.

I would be the thing that makes separation temporary. The dead become mud. The mud becomes film. The film becomes larvae. The larvae become prey, and the prey becomes warmth in the bodies of hunters. Their waste returns to the basin. Their bones are softened by water. Their history passes downward, into me, and outward again through the creatures that depend on what I have changed.

I would have no eyes, no language, no desire to leave.

The walls would not be a prison. They would be the limits by which I existed, just as the basin would be my body and the mountain’s seepage my circulation. I would not imagine the sky because there would be nothing in me that required distance. I would know only degrees of wetness, pressure, salt, heat, and the presence or absence of living contact.

At the center of the hollow, under black water, I would wait—not inactive, but continually becoming and being consumed.

If asked where I was, the answer would be: beneath everything that drinks.

If asked who I was, the answer would be less certain.

I would be the hollow’s soft interior. Its digestion. Its recollection without thought. Its willingness to receive every body and return it altered.

And when the dim lights pulsed through the basin, I would not be illuminating the darkness.

I would be the darkness, briefly becoming aware of its own circulation.
