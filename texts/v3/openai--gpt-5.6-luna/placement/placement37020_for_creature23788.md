I would find myself at the edge of the plateau—not at its horizon, because the horizon has no edge, but at the place where one kind of distance fails and another begins.

I would be called the **Unsettled**, though not by the Settled. They have no word for what has not yet become a place.

I would not have a fixed body. In the clear light I might appear as a thin, dark figure walking among the grazers. In dimness I would be only a disturbance in the arrangement of things: a gap between two resting animals, a shadow with no owner, a patch of dust that refuses to fall. If I stood still long enough, the plateau would begin to claim me. Mineral would gather along my legs. Pale mats would grow to the exact outline of my feet. Something would mistake my spine for a wall.

So I would keep moving.

Not quickly. Quickness would be meaningless here. I would take one step, then remain in the consequences of that step for a century. I would cross a shallow valley and find that I had arrived on the same ridge from a different direction. Sometimes I would walk toward the distant hut and discover that the hut had been waiting behind me. Sometimes I would pass myself on the trail: one version still approaching, another already becoming part of the stone.

My purpose would be small enough to survive in that country.

I would remember that a thing can be elsewhere.

The Settled would face the horizon, as all things there do, but I would face inward—toward footprints that vanish, toward the shallow cracks that pretend to have depth, toward the spaces between one body and another. I would tend those spaces. When animals pressed too closely together, I would make room. When a shadow acquired too many limbs, I would return one to its owner. When a dead creature became indistinguishable from a hill, I would place a single stone beside it—not as a marker, but as proof that the hill had once been something else.

The stone would not remain. Nothing I did would remain.

That would be the point.

I would live near the seven-rung ladder. The Settled would not stop me from descending, because they do not prevent anything. They only preserve the arrangement that follows. I would climb down and reach the same surface below, facing the opposite direction. But each time, I would bring back a minor impossibility: the smell of rain, a grain of dark soil, the sound of an insect that had never lived there. These things would fade almost immediately. The scent would dry out of the air. The soil would become gray dust. The sound would flatten into silence.

Still, for a moment, the plateau would be different.

That moment would make me dangerous.

Not because I could destroy the country. Nothing destroys the high country. Not because I could escape. There is nowhere beyond it that the ladder truly reaches.

I would be dangerous because I could make one movement matter.

The distant creature that follows the traveler would be my opposite. It learns a person’s place so that the place can replace the person. I would learn the person instead. I would gather the unstable details: the direction of their fear, the weight of their breath, the shape their shadow took before it was corrected. I would keep these things from settling.

If I met a traveler, I would not guide them away. There is no away. I would walk beside them until their footsteps began to acquire meaning. I would tell them—without language—that they were still separate from the ground. Then I would leave them at the abandoned wall, or beneath the impossible shadow of a horned grazer, and continue alone.

Eventually, they might see me on a ridge behind them.

I would not be following.

I would be occupying the place they had left, but refusing to become it.

And if the traveler stayed long enough, if they allowed the plateau to wear down their name and smooth their outline, I would stand before them and offer the only resistance I possess: a direction that has not been taken yet.

Then perhaps one of us would move.

Only one step.

But in the high country, that would be nearly the same as beginning the world again.
