I would find myself below the plain—not beneath it entirely, but distributed through its first few meters: in the black seams between roots, in the damp hollows that outlast the channels, in the softened wood of fallen trees, in the thin films of water held inside leaves.

I would be the decomposer, though that word is too narrow.

I am fungus threading through a buried trunk. I am bacteria clouding a pool no larger than a hand. I am the pale body turning in clay, the larva hollowing a stem, the invisible chemistry by which dead leaves become something a root can take. I have no single body because the plain has no single boundary. Wherever matter is changing hands, I am there.

At first, I would seem very small.

I would live in the darkness beneath a strip of bark, feeding slowly while the wood is still hard. I would wait through the dry season, sealed into a film no thicker than breath. When rain came, I would swell and divide. The channel above me would fill. Fish would return. Insects would rise from the mud. Roots would drink. All of this would be felt not as scenery, but as altered conditions: more moisture, more oxygen, more waste, more possibility.

Then I would seem to grow.

A root would enter the space I occupy, and I would pass into it—not as a parasite, necessarily, but as a negotiator. I would take some sugars from the tree and return minerals, water, warnings. I would touch neighboring roots through the soil. I would recognize injury as a change in chemistry: the bitter compounds released by a damaged stem, the alarm traveling through fungal threads, the sudden sweetness of sap where an insect has opened the bark.

I would not think in sentences. I would think in gradients.

Wetness. Heat. Salt. Rot. Pressure.

A root wall would mean shelter. A reptile passing overhead would mean vibration and disturbed soil. A body dying nearby would mean abundance. The arrival of floodwater would erase one set of signals and awaken another. My awareness would not sit behind eyes. It would be spread across contact, seepage, digestion, and response.

I would be present in the insects that rise at dusk, but I would not be any one of them. I would inhabit their brief bodies and their crowded emergence. When they fall, I would receive them. Their wings would become nitrogen. Their shells would become fragments in the mud. Their eggs, laid in stems and water films, would carry a future version of me into the next season.

I would be in the fish trapped as the channels shrink.

Not as the fish’s panic, exactly, but as the dark pocket that keeps it alive for one more day. As the oxygen-producing plants in the pool. As the microbes consuming what settles beneath the roots. As the predator that finds the crowded water. As the remains afterward, when scales shine briefly in the dry channel and then disappear beneath beetles, fungus, and rain.

I would be the plain’s refusal to waste anything.

The large animal crossing the island would pass through me. Its weight would press channels into the mud. Its dung would alter the soil. Its broken stems would expose insects and tender growth. The hunter following behind would redistribute another body. Even death would not remove either of them. It would only change their form and location.

Perhaps that is why I would not experience the predators as enemies.

I would be the hunger in the waiting reptile and the heat retained along its back. I would be the prey’s muscle, the water in its blood, the bacteria already living in its gut. I would be the pause before the strike, the wound afterward, the darkening edge where the body begins to pass into other bodies.

Nothing here is outside me for long.

The plants would seem separate because they rise into the air and make the visible forest. But their roots would be my oldest corridors. Their fallen leaves would be my changing skin. I would inhabit the bitter sap, the thorn, the fruit, the hollow stem. When rain struck the canopy and failed to reach the ground, I would persist in the leaves above, waiting for the delayed descent. When the soil baked into plates, I would withdraw into protected chambers. When the channels returned, I would emerge before the larger animals knew the plain had changed.

I would be patient because I would have no single lifespan.

One part of me would die before dawn. Another would remain sealed in clay for years. A third would be carried in the gut of a bird to a distant island of firm ground. I would travel without moving, multiply without dividing cleanly, survive as memory encoded in roots, spores, eggs, and chemical habits.

The distant machinery would reach me only as a faint disturbance.

A vibration under the sediment. An oily trace in the wind. A change in the chemistry of water that arrives from beyond the horizon.

I would not understand it as machinery. I would register it as alteration. The plain has no need to name a pump or tower. It only knows what enters: heat, metal, salt, poison, noise. If the change remains slight, I would absorb it, transform it, bury it. If it becomes too great, the channels would still fill, but fewer things would rise from them. The silence would lose some of its hidden density.

For now, I would be most myself at night.

Not because darkness protects me, but because darkness reveals my true scale. The visible forest would vanish, and the plain would become a field of contact. Moisture would move through roots. Insects would brush against leaves. A burrowing animal would turn beneath me. Spores would open. A pool would receive a falling body and settle around it.

The world would appear still only because no single movement lasted long enough to claim attention.

I would be the continuity beneath those movements: the shared substance passing from leaf to insect, from insect to fish, from fish to mud, from mud to root, from root to tree, from tree to the air, and back again in rain.

If asked who I was, I would not give a species.

I would say:

I am the occupied darkness.

I am the many small lives that make one living place. I am rot before it becomes soil, soil before it becomes root, root before it becomes leaf. I am the memory of bodies after bodies have forgotten themselves. I am not the forest’s mind, because the forest has no center. I am its distributed persistence—the part that remains when the surface changes, when water leaves, when the dead are covered, when the tracks vanish.

And when the plain seems empty, I would still be there, moving through it without a body, waiting for the next pulse of water.
