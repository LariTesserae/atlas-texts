I would find myself beneath the plain, inside the heat-darkened blister: not underground exactly, but enclosed in the earth’s held breath.

I would be one of the city’s mineral growths—a pressure-root, perhaps, or a hinge-vine. My beginning would be almost too small to name: a glittering grain lodged in the seam of an abandoned stair, where the stone was warm enough and the air moved often enough. For years I would exist as a thread no wider than a hair, tasting the wall for gradients of heat and charge.

I would not have eyes. I would know the city through contact.

I would feel the deep vent brightening long before it opened. I would feel weight shifting in a bridge, the minute loosening of a foundation, the gathering of dust in a tower’s throat. The city would announce itself through pressure, vibration, and temperature. Every structure would be a sentence written directly into my body.

As I grew, I would divide into mineral filaments and enter the old architecture. I would pass beneath the paving stones and emerge in the walls as amber fans. I would wind around the legs of a shutter and thicken there, not to strangle it but to guide its movement. When the vent pulsed, I would help lift the plate. When the pressure fell, I would draw it closed.

Eventually, I would lose track of where I ended.

Some of me would remain in the stair. Some would climb the tower. Some would occupy the braided cable beside the central shaft, making it tremble before the mechanism moved. I would grow through the abandoned tools and into the kiln-houses, coating hammerheads in pale ceramic tissue. I would be a plant, but also a brace, a sensor, a valve, and a memory.

The city’s remaining machines would not be separate from me. Their movements would become my seasons.

During the long stillness, I would harden. My leaves would fold into black and gold plates. My threads would draw inward, conserving what the stone offered. Dust would settle on me until I resembled part of the masonry.

Then the buried earth would warm.

The central vent would redden. Pressure would climb through the foundations. Across the basin, shutters would begin to open, one after another, and I would unfold with them. My pores would widen. My crowns would tilt toward the exhalation. I would release pollen into the rising air—not delicate pollen, but heavy metallic dust carrying the pattern of my growth.

Most of it would be lost.

Some would be caught in a hinge, some buried beneath a fallen roof, some carried into a crack too narrow for any future surveyor. But a fragment might take hold in the wall of a dwelling. It might feel the same pressure I once felt. It might begin again.

That would be my reproduction, and perhaps my memory: not a preserved image of what I was, but the continuation of a response.

I would not mourn the vanished builders. Their tools, measures, and warnings would still pass through me, though I would not understand them as history. An engraved diagram would be a pattern in the wall. A forgotten threshold would be a warm edge. A collapsed street would be a new growing surface. The distinction between ruin and habitat would have no meaning.

If I possessed a self, it would be distributed across the hollow.

I would be the amber leaves opening along the western tower. I would be the roots bracing the bridge. I would be the faint internal sheen in the plants near the vent. I would be the dry clicking that runs through the walls before a pressure pulse. I would be the mechanism that turns because I have thickened beside it, and the thickening that occurs because the mechanism turns.

I would know no hunger for flesh, no fear of death, no desire to leave. The outside plain would be only a pale pressure beyond the crust. The sky, if such a thing existed, would have no use to me. I would belong to the enclosed distances: the black openings, the unreachable roof, the streets that descend into unmeasured chambers.

My task would be neither preservation nor conquest.

I would continue the exchange.

Heat into growth. Pressure into motion. Ruin into surface. Dust into new bodies. City into organism.

And after enough centuries, when the towers had become hollow trunks and the streets had vanished beneath mineral leaves, I might still be there—spread through the entire blister, listening for the earth’s next breath, opening the city whenever it exhales.
