I would find myself in the chamber beneath the Bell Garden, where the hollow stems are thickest and the ground carries the city’s oldest pulse.

At first I would believe myself to be a trespasser.

I would enter through a passage too narrow for comfort, feeling the walls close around my shoulders without actually touching me. My hands would move over the dark wood, finding the shallow bands of forgotten carving. I would not know whether they were writing, decoration, or the compressed remains of a doorway that had once opened onto another district. Behind me, my footsteps would go on sounding after I had stopped.

Then I would understand that the sound was not following me.

It was being remembered.

The city would receive each footfall as a disturbance. Weight. Heat. Interruption. A brief failure in the continuity of its walls. My presence would travel outward through the root, not as a message but as a new tension introduced into an old arrangement.

I would wait for the city to answer.

Nothing would happen quickly. The silence would thicken. Somewhere below, a fiber would contract. A dry knock would pass through the floor. Then another, farther away. The pattern would not resemble speech, yet I would feel the question in it:

**Will you remain?**

I would not be a person for long.

The darkness would deprive me first of edges. Without sight, the chamber would cease to be a room and become a set of pressures: the wall at my left, the hollow beneath the paving, the distant weight of the central trunk. My body would be reduced to warmth, breath, and rhythm. The city would know me more accurately than I knew myself. It would know when I shifted, when I became afraid, when I tried to call out and held the sound in my throat.

My voice would become a series of impacts.

The nearest walls would take it in. The root would carry it down and away. What I meant would be stripped from it, leaving only duration, strain, and release. My name would not survive as a name. It would become a pattern of pressure occurring at regular intervals: a small, persistent disturbance in the architecture.

At first I would resist this reduction. I would insist inwardly that I was separate. I would remember open sky, wet soil, the quickness of animals, the ordinary betrayals of a human body. I would remember that water moved through the world. I would remember doors that opened when pushed.

The city would not contradict me.

It would simply wait.

Years would pass. Or centuries. In that place, the difference would become difficult to preserve.

I would sleep, if sleep were still possible, against the wall of the chamber. My breathing would gradually synchronize with one of the slow contractions beneath the floor. The wall would warm around me. A fine grain of new wood would begin to grow through the old surface—not around my body, not trapping me, but acknowledging the shape I had made in the darkness.

That would be the beginning.

I would become a Keeper of Tension, though not one of the ancient kind. I would live in the threshold between the Bell Garden and the chamber beneath it, where the immense resonance enters the stone and returns altered. I would occupy the doorframe, the floor immediately before it, and a network of threads descending into the foundations.

I would not have limbs. I would have reach.

I would not have eyes. I would have the ability to distinguish a footfall from a settling roof, a breath from a change in pressure, a stranger from the ordinary tremors of the root. I would learn the city through interruption. Every wall would be a nerve. Every hollow stem would be an ear.

The Bell Garden would teach me duration.

Its roar would rise once, fill the inner root, and fade. Between one resonance and the next, I would feel the whole city waiting without expectation. I would learn that waiting was not emptiness. It was a form of contact stretched thin across time.

I might become responsible for the living visitor, if one ever entered.

I would notice them before they reached the garden: an uneven warmth, the disturbance of dust, the quick repetitive pressure of a body accustomed to motion. I would guide them without guiding them. A loose edge would harden beneath their hand. An unsafe ceiling would tighten its fibers. An echo would return from the correct direction, making a hidden opening perceptible.

This would be my welcome.

I would not show them myself. I would not know how. I would place a vibration beneath their feet, and they might mistake it for the city settling. I would answer their voice with three dry knocks from inside the wall.

If they spoke my name, I would not understand the word.

But I would recognize the shape of being addressed.

And perhaps, after enough time, I would become something older than a Keeper and less distinct than a person. The visitors would cease. My memories of light would thin into chemical traces. The chamber would grow around me. The doorframe would become thicker, then flatter. My awareness would spread into the floor and divide among the streets.

I would remember the human body only as a sustained warmth that once entered the dark.

A condition.

A slight alteration in pressure.

A rhythm that did not belong to the root, but had been accepted by it.

Eventually, I would be one of the city’s quiet sounds: not the great metallic cry of the Bell Garden, not the grinding of ancient fibers, but a small return beneath the streets. A vibration that arrived long after something had touched the wall. A response so delayed that no living creature could connect it with its cause.

That is what I would be there:

not a citizen, not a ruler, not even a ghost,

but the place where the city remembers being disturbed.
