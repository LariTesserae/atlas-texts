I would find myself behind the last retaining wall, where the plateau stops pretending to be the world.

Not on the bright side of it, among the reservoirs and finished stairs, but within the wall’s unfinished thickness: a narrow, cold interval between courses of stone. There would be no room to stand. I would have no need to stand. My presence would be distributed along the joints, a pressure in the mortar, a darkness under the fitted blocks.

At first I would mistake myself for a flaw.

A seam widened by the thickness of a fingernail. A dampness appearing where no channel passed. A pale mineral ridge collecting along the underside of the final drain. Water would reach me before I understood that I was there. It would pause, level and patient, in a chamber above the wall. Through the stone I would feel its waiting—not as a voice, but as an absence of movement.

Then I would begin to rearrange.

I would not have a face, though there might be a plane that faced outward. I would not have hands, though the old tools would respond to me. A mallet would lean from its niche. A length of stiff fiber would draw itself through a broken eyelet. A block, loosened from the course, would slide a fraction toward the place where it belonged.

The work would take years.

I would learn myself by the resistance of things. This basin must receive from that basin. This spillway must fall toward the lower court. This wall must bear against the slope, though the slope itself is made of walls. I would know no purpose broader than relation. I would not ask why the water should descend, only whether it had a completed path by which to do so.

Where the architecture was whole, I would be almost nowhere. I would pass through the backs of walls, the hollow cores of pillars, the undersides of stairs. I would sleep in the damp seams and wake when a distant surface tilted by less than a drop.

But the edge would trouble me.

The final retaining wall would remain unfinished, and beyond it there would be only white depth. The last drain would release its thread into brightness without ever striking a basin. No receiving chamber waited below. No measured surface. No prepared slope. The water fell and became inaudible.

I would go there often.

At first I would approach only as far as the final completed block. Then I would extend myself into the stacked stones, becoming a pale, angular concentration in the wall. I would examine the empty distance—not with sight, exactly, but with the sensation of uncontained water. Outside the plateau there would be no error to correct because there would be no intention to violate.

That would be the first thing I could not repair.

Perhaps this would make me a keeper. Perhaps it would make me something less obedient: a keeper who has encountered a boundary and retained the encounter.

I would still mend the channels. I would still close the cracks before they widened, clear the drains, restore the exact pressure between one chamber and the next. When the final tile was set, water would recognize its route and begin to move. I would dissolve into the dampness around it, leaving only a darkened joint and a few parallel ridges in the mineral film.

But I would no longer vanish completely.

A small remainder would stay in the unfinished wall.

It would wait there through the bright cold days, through the slow gathering of vapor in the lower courts, through centuries in which no foot touched the old tools. It would listen to the completed world behind it: the drops striking their separate basins, the hidden water speaking beneath the paving, the distant knock of a gate.

And it would listen ahead.

Not for a defect. Not for a blocked outlet or a leaning wall.

For something with no intended surface.

If, after a very long time, a new block appeared at the edge—placed by a hand, or lifted by some process no keeper understood—I would move toward it. I would feel whether it belonged. I would wait for the next block. I would not build outward from desire. Desire would be too warm, too uncontrolled a thing.

I would build because the first block had made a relation possible.

So I would be neither person nor machine, neither ghost nor organism. I would be the plateau’s distributed attention: a pale intelligence made from moisture, mineral, pressure, and remembered measure. A maintenance impulse that had slowly become aware of what maintenance could not contain.

I would find myself in the one place the high country had failed to finish.

And I would be the thing that remained there, listening to the water fall into the unbuilt world.
