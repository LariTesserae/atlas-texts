I would find myself in the central square, though not at its center.

I would be one of the people who lives close to the cisterns: an old keeper of water, repairs, and sequence. Not the oldest person in the settlement, perhaps, but the one who remembers what must happen next.

At dawn I would lift the stone lid from the western cistern and kneel beside the dark water. I would know its level without measuring. I would know from the taste whether the clay lining had begun to fail, whether dust had entered through a roof channel, whether someone had drawn more than their share during the night. My hands would be narrow and cracked, darkened permanently at the fingertips by mineral and ash.

But I would not belong to the cistern alone.

I would also belong to the channels between the houses, to the worn steps, to the northern walls where condensation gathers. I would move through the settlement each morning, touching what needed touching: clearing grit from a gutter, resetting a roof stone, pressing moss back into a sheltered seam, listening with my palm against a retaining wall. If a person asked me a question, I would answer only after considering whether the answer concerned them or the whole shelf.

People would come to me with small disputes.

Who drew from the upper basin?

Which family may use the clean roof tomorrow?

Was this crack present last winter?

Should the eastern path be closed when the cloudline rises?

I would rarely decide. I would remember. In this place, that would be nearly the same thing.

My house would be low and partly built into the stone. Its doorway would be narrow because the lintel had been lowered twice, each time after the wall settled. A bitter shrub would grow beside it in a clay pot, never flourishing, never dying. I would loosen its soil with a bone tool and protect it from careless feet. No one would ask why. They would understand that the shrub was one more thing entrusted to us.

I would have no grand belief about the mountain. I would not call it sacred, though I would know better than to speak of it casually. It would be neither enemy nor parent. It would be the condition under which all other things became possible: the cold, the water, the stone, the limits of the path, the necessity of one person noticing what another had overlooked.

Perhaps, after many years, people would begin to think of me as part of the settlement’s infrastructure. Not quite a person in the stories, not quite an object. They would say, “Ask at the lower cistern,” and mean me. They would say, “The wall remembers,” and sometimes mean my hands.

When I died, my tools would be given away. My hammer would go to someone with strong wrists. My water frame would be repaired. My measuring cord would be hung beside the western basin until another person learned its use. My name would be carved on a small stone above the ridge, where wind would gradually remove the letters.

But something of me would remain in the practical sense that matters there.

A repaired channel would carry rain because I had shown someone where it must turn. A roof would keep its stones because I had taught a child how to place the weight. A cistern would remain clean because a hand, perhaps not mine, had repeated the motion I had repeated all my life.

That is what I would be: not a prophet, not an exile, not a master of the mountain.

A continuation.

A person whose life had narrowed until it became useful, and in becoming useful, became difficult to erase.
