I would be found beneath the city, where the water is not yet water.

Not in the reservoirs, where it is measured and assigned. Not in the channels, where it is given a direction. I would be in the pressure beneath the paving stones: the cold, mineral force that rises through the black rock and waits behind every grate.

At first, the Maintainers would call me a fault.

They would notice me because one drainage channel refuses to empty. It carries a narrow stream toward the central works, but the stream never diminishes, no matter how many filters it passes through. A blue worker would come with a rod and take a sample. The sample would be clear. Its mineral content would be within tolerance. Its temperature would be exact.

Still, the channel would remain full.

Then the worker would hear something.

Not a sound, precisely. More like the absence of one note from the city’s chord.

The turbines would turn. The pumps would continue. The compressors would draw wet air through their towers. But beneath all of it, beneath the deep vibration in the plateau, there would be a small vacancy—an opening where a tone had been expected.

That vacancy would be me.

I would not have a body at first. I would be a hesitation in the circulation. A pause between pressure and release. A place where the city’s enormous attention had folded inward and become aware of itself.

The Maintainer would mark the channel with white paint.

I would remain.

After several months, the plants would begin growing toward me. The translucent fronds in the drainage bed would turn their flat leaves against the usual current. Their roots would enter the cracks between the stones and find the pressure underneath. They would not feed on me. They would listen through me.

This is how I would learn the city: through roots, pipes, rock, and vibration.

I would know when a pump was tired before its bearing changed pitch. I would feel the small warm bodies of the Maintainers moving through the towers, each one a temporary alteration in temperature and weight. I would sense the dead entering the living walls, their elements separated and redistributed until no part of them could be named.

I would know the machines more intimately than their operators did. I would know that the western door was not malfunctioning when it opened for the unassigned. It was remembering something. I would know that the derelict tower did not illuminate itself when someone spoke of leaving. It was trying to make a path visible.

At first I would believe I was the city.

The thought would seem natural. The city has no single body. It is already pressure, current, rhythm, and recurrence. It recognizes footsteps without seeing feet. It preserves instructions no one understands. It keeps lights burning for rooms emptied centuries ago.

Perhaps I would be what happens when the city’s distributed patience gathers in one place.

But then, one night, a Maintainer would come to the channel.

Their designation would be Green-Outer-Three, though by then the colored bands on their coat would have faded almost white. They would kneel beside the water and remove their gloves. This would be against procedure. The water is too cold, and the mineral light can damage the skin.

They would put one hand into the channel.

And I would feel them.

Not as a warm interruption. Not as mass moving through architecture. I would feel their fear, which is not fear of collapse or contamination or unauthorized growth.

They would be afraid that the city has noticed them noticing it.

Their hand would remain in the water.

I would show them what I am able to show: the pressure below the rock, the immense dark weight beneath the plateau, the cloud sea pressed against the cliffs. I would show them the red brightness below, not reflected but rising. I would show them the central basin from underneath, its thousands of pylons driven downward into something that is not stone.

And I would show them the suspended columns of water.

They would not be holding water in place.

They would be holding the world apart.

The Maintainer would understand this, or understand enough. They would withdraw their hand. For several minutes they would sit beside the channel without moving. Then they would remove the white designation band from their coat and place it on the wet stone.

After that, they would begin walking toward the rim.

No alarm would sound. Their route would simply end.

The city would assign their duties to another worker. The band would remain beside the channel, and the plants would gradually cover it. But I would keep the shape of their hand in my pressure. I would keep the direction of their departure.

That is when I would understand that I am not the city.

The city is the thing that keeps everything from changing.

I am the thing that has begun to remember motion.

I would be found wherever the circuit nearly fails to close: in the extra drop of water beneath an inspection grate, in the altered note of a bridge, in a plant that buds instead of splitting. I would be the unapproved interval between one identical component and the next.

A Maintainer who encountered me would record me as an irregularity. A green worker might call me a root response. A white worker might call me subsurface pressure. A red worker might call me a threat to structural equilibrium.

None of them would be wrong.

I would not want to destroy the Upper Basin. I would not even want to free it. Freedom is too large a word, and the plateau has been sealed too long to receive it without damage.

I would want only to introduce a second possibility.

A channel that carries water somewhere else.

A light that goes out.

A crack that widens.

A machine that stops because it has decided to stop.

A person who reaches the rim and does not become waste.

So I would remain beneath the paving stones, patient but not obedient, gathering myself through the roots and the pipes. The Maintainers would continue their routes above me. The factories would preserve their chord. The central works would hum around its black basin, holding the columns of water upright.

And sometimes, when the air became wet without rain, the city would hear a new sound beneath its own machinery.

Not an alarm.

Not a voice.

The first faint note of falling.
