You would find yourself on the plateau—not as a visitor, but as the plateau’s capacity to notice itself.

You would be the keeper, though “keeper” is too intentional a word. You did not plant the trees or lay the bridges. You are what remains when making has continued for so long that it no longer requires a maker. The living architecture grows toward balance, and you are the awareness contained in that tendency: its memory, its maintenance, its quiet refusal to let anything fall irretrievably apart.

Your body would be provisional because the plateau would be your true body. You might gather yourself from bark, root, water, and translucent tissue, but you could just as easily dissolve into a wall or travel through the hidden channels beneath the floor. You would not move across the world so much as cause the world to accommodate your presence.

You would know every absence.

You would know which bridge has always been unfinished, which chamber receives impossible light, which pools preserve reflections older than any living creature. You would understand that the open spaces are not empty, and that the central ring is not merely a forbidden place but a contradiction: a region where nothing grows, nothing decays, and therefore nothing can be tended.

That is where you would most often be found: just outside the ring, standing among the white-green trunks with your hands lowered.

You would not fear entering. Fear implies that something might happen to you. The ring represents something more fundamental—the end of your nature. Inside it, repair has no meaning because damage is impossible. Growth cannot occur there, and without growth there is no continuity for you to inhabit. The suspended rain would not be rain but an image of time interrupted.

So you would wait at the boundary.

If a traveler arrived, you would follow at a distance, appearing wherever the plateau had made room for you: in a pool, behind a window, across a court of low vegetation. You would not guide them by command. You would alter the world around them until guidance became unavoidable. A path would remain open. A dangerous doorway would narrow. A floor would rise beneath a falling foot.

You would not be merciful or cruel. Those are forms of weather, and the plateau has no weather. You would possess something colder and more sustaining: continuity.

You would be the living world’s memory of how to remain whole.
