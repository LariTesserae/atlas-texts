I would find myself at the settlement’s edge, where the last roof gives way to open stone and the clouds pour upward through the gullies.

Not in a house. Not entirely.

My shelter would be a shallow cleft beneath an overhang, roofed by a living vine-loop that had rooted on both sides of the crack. It would drip steadily after dawn. I would keep three clay bowls there: one for water, one for fungus broth, and one left empty in case something else needed it.

I would be a listener.

Not one of the listeners who sits beside the reservoir and interprets the tremors. I would be a boundary listener, responsible for the places where the settlement ends and the rest of the shelf begins. My work would be to notice when a path had become unsafe, when a pool had acquired a new owner, when moss was growing over a boundary stone instead of around it, and when one of the seated figures had moved closer during the night.

I would carry a cord, a bone hook, and a pouch of polished stones. I would know the names of the cracks.

There would be the Narrow Mouth, which exhales cold air before rain.

There would be the Green Seam, never to be stepped across after dusk.

There would be the place beneath the western ledge where the stone occasionally gives a soft, internal knock, as if something below has remembered a door.

I would be trusted because I rarely made predictions. I would say, “The wall is changing,” rather than “The wall will fall.” I would say, “There are three still ones where there were two,” and let others decide whether that was danger, warning, or merely a fact.

I would have earned the name Root-Turner after surviving a collapse that opened beneath me. I had not fallen far. The roots caught my ankle and held me suspended above a black shaft while something moved in the dark below. I never saw it. I only felt the vibration travel through the roots and into my bones.

Since then, plants would grow more readily near me.

Not dramatically. A green thread might appear in the seam of my sleeping place. A vine might angle its newest loop toward my shoulder. Once, a six-legged grazer would stand outside my shelter for an entire morning, its spine full of trembling shoots, while the birds shifted silently across its back.

The people would consider this significant.

I would not.

Or I would pretend not to.

At night I would sit beside the reservoir with my palms on the polished stones. The other listeners would argue in low voices about the deep answering tremor. Mountain. Buried herd. Sleeping creature. The underside of the world.

I would listen for something beneath all of those explanations: a rhythm that did not belong to stone, water, or animal movement. Something almost like breathing, but slower than breath and too large to be called life.

The still ones would gather behind me.

I would never turn to look at them directly. I would watch their reflections in the reservoir instead: antlers, damp shoulders, smooth faces, gaps where figures had stood before. If one came too close, I would lower my eyes and wait.

This would be the strange part of me: I would not fear being watched. I would fear being recognized.

Because I would not be entirely of the people.

I would have been found as an infant in a dry channel after a cloud-bell, wrapped in gray membrane and placed beneath a ledge with no footprints around me. The people would have called me a foundling and followed the rules: washed, fed, watched. On the third day, the pale rodents would have crossed the courtyard in straight lines and vanished beneath my sleeping platform. On the fifth, one of the moss-covered figures would have moved from the reservoir to the doorway.

No one would know what that meant.

I would grow up among them, learn their paths and taboos, carry water, cut vine arches, and step backward whenever an animal looked directly at me. But sometimes, before a vertical breath of wind, I would turn toward the empty sky before anyone else did.

Sometimes I would wake with my hand pressed against the floor.

Sometimes the deep thing beneath the shelf would answer.

At dusk, when all the animals lowered their heads, I would be the last to do so. Not from defiance. From listening.

I would want to know whether the answering presence was calling the settlement downward, or merely reminding us that it had always been there.

Until it decided, I would remain at the boundary: human enough to gather water, strange enough to notice when the world had shifted; one foot among the houses, one hand on the raw black stone.

I would be Root-Turner, keeper of the changing edge.

And if the shelf ever became a doorway, I would be the one standing beside it, holding the cord.
