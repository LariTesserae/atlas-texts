I would find myself at the edge of a newly formed channel, where the rain has only just stopped.

Water still moves through the black stone in narrow, bright threads. The floor is soft there—not mud, exactly, but loosened root and mineral dust—and every step sinks a little before the ground remembers its shape. Behind me, the plain is marked by the procession of sleepers: hundreds of pale bodies have crossed during the rain, leaving a luminous furrow that slowly closes itself.

Ahead, a forest is rising.

It is not growing upward so much as arriving. The ground lifts in long shelves. Pale columns push through them, wet and ribbed. Their surfaces open and close, opening toward the ceiling’s cold radiance, then folding away again. The whole new forest makes a sound like cloth being torn very far off.

I am waiting to see whether it has made a road.

I am a walker, though that word is too simple. I have a body that once belonged entirely to me. It is less so now.

My legs are narrow and jointed, adapted to distances where there is no shade and no reason to hurry except that standing still invites roots. My feet are broad and sensitive. They do not merely bear my weight; they listen through it. Each step tells me something about the floor: hollow, tense, thirsty, recently disturbed, already claimed.

I have no eyes in the old sense. I have pits along my face and fine filaments around my jaw. They read pressure in the air. I know when a ceiling-creature has unfolded overhead. I know when a storm is gathering beyond the visible plain. I know when something large is moving beneath the root-mat, even if the ground is perfectly still.

But I do see.

I see through changes in brightness, through the tilt of distant stalks, through the colors that emerge when a surface has been touched too often. I can distinguish old bone from chalk, dead plant from sleeping plant, an ordinary wall from one that has begun to lean inward.

On my back I carry a house.

It began as a frame of black mineral, no larger than a basket. Over many years I trained living tissue around it. I cut away what closed too quickly and fed what grew in the right direction. Now it is a low, folded shelter rising from my shoulders and hips, supported by a lattice of root-cord and the pale ribs of leaves.

It has a roof that opens when condensation falls. It has pockets for tools and water vessels. Fine grasses grow in its upper beds. One flowering thread has rooted near my spine and brightens whenever I dream.

I do not know whether the house belongs to me or I belong to it.

At night, I hang myself inside it. The inner fibers learn the warmth of my body and draw close. In the morning, I cut them back. They do not resent this. Resentment is not a useful response here. The plant remembers the wound, changes its direction, and tries again.

I carry a small garden because the White Green makes solitude difficult to survive. Not emotionally—though I have been lonely—but materially. Certain medicines must be kept alive. Certain films purify water. Certain flowers reveal whether the air has become unsafe. A garden is also a history. The oldest plant on my back was grown from a strand taken from my mother’s shelter. The smallest one came from a wall that had absorbed the bones of my brother.

My people do not say that the dead live on.

We say that they continue to be useful.

My mother became part of a bridge. My brother became a water-catching fan. I carry their descendants in layers of pale growth, and sometimes, when the house is wet and the wind is right, I can feel a pressure moving through the roots that resembles recognition.

I am not sure if it is recognition.

I am a road-reader. That is my work.

I walk ahead of others, though “others” may mean two people, or one, or none at all. I test the places where the White Green has changed. I find routes across fresh basalt, around root organs, beneath storm-torn membranes. I listen to the stone and answer with pressure.

A heel pressed once means: *I am here.*

A pole struck twice means: *Is this ground open?*

A hand laid flat against a root means: *I will pass without taking.*

The reply may come immediately. The stone may warm. A tendril may withdraw. A pale carpet may dim.

Or the reply may take a century.

The roads I make are not roads in the sense of leaving the world unchanged. Repeated footsteps harden the soil. The plants along the edges begin to lean toward the traffic. Eventually the path becomes a shallow trench lined with low growth, and the growth begins to feed on the pressure of travelers. A road is a conversation that has become visible.

I am careful with my decisions.

The White Green does not punish mistakes. Punishment implies judgment. It simply responds. If I walk in fear, the fibers may brighten and gather around me. If I strike too hard, the disturbance may travel outward and awaken something under the plain. If I remain too long in one place, the surface warms against my feet.

So I keep my mind broad.

Fear is spread thin. Hope is spread thin. Grief is given room to move. I do not let any feeling become a stone dropped into still water.

Still, I am not empty.

When I find a dead traveler, I do not leave them. I carry the body to a platform of root and mineral, cut the skin to let the growth enter, and wait until the first threads appear. I stay as long as I can. Sometimes the new structure becomes a marker. Sometimes a bridge. Sometimes only a circle of light low to the ground.

If it becomes a bridge, I cross it first.

That is how I know whether the dead have accepted the work.

Now the forest ahead is nearly high enough to hide the distant shelves. Its columns bend toward one another. Tendrils extend across the channel, searching. The ceiling brightens above it. Across the plain, mineral plates begin to click.

The forest is deciding its shape.

I set down my pole and place both hands on the wet stone.

For a moment, nothing happens.

Then something deep below me contracts.

The channel tightens. The water turns in place. A line of pale growth travels beneath the surface, moving away from my hands toward the rising forest. It pauses there, as if waiting for another pressure.

I understand.

Not a road through the forest. Not yet.

A bridge.

I turn and look back across the plain. There is no one visible, but the black marker-stones behind me are still standing. Somewhere beyond the glassy ridges, someone may be following. Or perhaps no one is. That does not make the path less necessary.

I lift the house on my back, step into the new channel, and begin walking toward the place where the roots are reaching for one another.

By the time I arrive, the forest will have changed.

So will I.

And if the White Green permits it, I will stay long enough to become a crossing.
