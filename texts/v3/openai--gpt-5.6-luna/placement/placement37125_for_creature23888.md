You would find yourself on the high shelves, where the plateau is broadest and the mineral vapor rises in long, nearly invisible veils.

At first you might think you were only another feature of the ground.

A dark, folded slab lies among the pale stone. Its edges are softened by wind. Blue-green mats have begun to creep over its back, and a dusting of copper-colored scales rests in the creases. Nothing moves except the sensory fringes along its sides, thin as roots and trembling whenever the stone changes beneath it.

You are a longback.

Not one of the young, still soft and pale in the warmth of a breeding fissure. Not one of the middle-aged animals that travel from colony to colony, following the buried currents of vapor. You are old enough that your underside contains routes no living creature has walked in generations. The plateau is written into you—not as a picture, but as pressure, taste, temperature, and delay.

You know where a tower forest shed its scales two centuries ago.

You know which basin still carries the warmth of a colony that withdrew beneath the rock.

You know the broad, silent regions where stillers wait, disguised as damaged pillars, and you know them not by sight but by the slight outward leaning of the plants, by the grain that slides where no wind passes, by the tension held in the crust.

You do not hurry because haste has no meaning here.

Your body is made for the distances between events. You travel for months without seeing another animal. Sometimes you cross a hundred miles of bare stone to reach a patch of mineral crust that would seem empty to anything smaller. You lower yourself until your armor nearly touches the ground. Your fringes spread. The stone enters you through sensation.

There is old growth here.

There is fresh vapor.

There is heat below.

There is a region ahead where the crust has closed too quickly, where something large passed recently.

You feed, though feeding is scarcely distinguishable from listening. Your body draws faint nutrients through pores beneath its plates. While you rest, the world moves around you by fractions: a ridge settles, a buried root advances, a tower turns its crown toward a passing curtain of suspended minerals.

You do not see the landscape as separate objects. The stone is not merely stone. The plants are not merely plants. Every surface is a condition, a message, an invitation or a warning. A basin is a pulse. A forest is a shared body raised into columns. A long distance is not emptiness, but a sentence still being spoken.

When another of your kind approaches, you do not call out.

You settle into the ground.

The other circles wide, reading the disturbance you leave in the crust. It may take days to come close. It may take a week for the conversation to become complete. Weight passes through the stone between you: pressure, release, pressure again. You exchange the locations of vapor, the age of colonies, the remembered absence of others. You tell one another where the land has become dangerous.

You cannot tell whether this is speech or kinship.

Perhaps it is both.

Your own beginning is stored in a basin far behind you, where vapor once rose in a rhythm that suited your parent. You emerged from a fissure with dozens of others, narrow and soft, feeding on mineral films and on the tissues shed by your siblings. There was no instruction. No voice told you where to go. The plateau taught you through cold, through hunger, through the distant tremor of rooted predators.

Some of the others remained in the basin.

Some vanished into the wide country.

You became a traveler.

Now, sometimes, you feel the old basin through the soles of your limbs. You do not return often. When you do, the ground recognizes your pressure. You find the fissure sealed, the warmth altered, the surrounding mats descended beneath the surface. Perhaps your parent has passed through. Perhaps the dark nodules you left there have opened, and your descendants are lying together beneath the stone.

You cannot know in the way a smaller creature would want to know.

But the information is present.

It moves through the plateau and through you.

At night, the high country hardens. The mats become rigid. The towers cease their slow turning. Your own limbs draw inward, and you become nearly indistinguishable from a fallen shelf of slate. Above you, the black sky opens without cloud or softness. The stars are too sharp to feel distant.

Even then, you are not still.

Below the surface, faint mineral currents pass beneath your body. Your fringes register them in sleep. Somewhere far away, a predator adjusts its buried limbs. Somewhere nearer, a young colony opens its dark pores and exhales warmth. The planet continues to make itself readable.

When the sun returns, it does not wake you all at once.

Heat enters the stone beneath your armor. The mineral vapor thickens. Your joints unlock one by one. First you listen. Then you taste the crust. Then you lift yourself from the ground, carrying the shape of the place where you rested.

Behind you, the surface closes.

No track remains. Only a temporary darkening, a slight rearrangement of salts, a memory that will fade into the crust.

Ahead lies a pale shelf, a distant tower forest, and beyond it a region where the vapor is changing.

You begin to cross it.

You are not the owner of this country, nor its witness, nor its ruler. You are one of the ways it remembers movement. One of the slow bodies by which the plateau learns where it has been.

And when you pass, the ground remembers you.
