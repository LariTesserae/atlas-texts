I would find myself in the Green Aperture as one of the listeners.

Not the oldest, and not the bravest. I would be the one sent out when the ornaments began to strike without wind, when the roots withdrew from the stone, when the black columns hummed at a pitch too low for the ear.

I would live in the smallest of the five houses, the one nearest the southern crack. Its walls would be patched with amber plates, and one of its ribs would still carry the tooth marks of a burrower. Outside the entrance I would hang seven seed-cases, three bone splinters, and a dark mineral disk that rang only when the ground beneath it was hollow.

I would not have a spoken name.

The others would know me by a pattern: two taps, a pause, one long scrape. If I were needed, someone would make the pattern on a wall, and I would feel it through the soles of my feet before hearing it. I would come with my hooked pole, my bladder-skin vessel, and the layered plates I wore along my back. I would move bent nearly double, not from weakness, but because the basin favored low shapes. High bodies caught the attention of things that drifted.

At first, I think, I would believe that my work was to interpret the basin.

I would learn the small differences between the sounds of stone settling and stone opening. I would know when a plant was merely unfolding and when it had scented blood. I would read the changing color of a burrower’s moss. I would stand near the columns and listen to voices return in the wrong order.

A warning spoken yesterday might come back tonight from the lowest groove:

*Do not go down.*

And I would understand that the words were not the warning. The delay was.

I would keep records in cuts and spacing. I would mark which vents had opened, which groves had migrated, which paths had become soft with roots. I would teach the young listeners how to lay their palms on the stone without standing directly above the singing places.

But gradually I would discover that I was not interpreting the Green Aperture from outside it.

It would be listening through me.

The first sign would be small. A pale filament would grow beneath the skin of my left wrist. I would cut it away, and the cut would close with a sweet mineral smell. The next morning, three new filaments would be there, finer than hair, threaded around the bones.

I would not tell the others.

By then I would have begun to feel the vents before they opened—not as pressure, but as hunger. The basin’s hidden chambers would seem to turn in their sleep beneath me. I would know which seam was preparing to breathe. I would know when the drummers were gathering, even before their bodies appeared. Their future rhythm would form in my teeth.

The breath-takers would begin to recognize me.

A grove would turn its sealed flowers toward me as I passed. Hanging roots would lower themselves from the cliffs, touching my shoulders with the delicacy of searching fingers. Once, after I cut a plate from a dying plant, the roots would follow the blade exactly as the old rule promised. But they would stop before reaching me.

Not because I had escaped.

Because they had found me familiar.

After that, I would become something between a listener and a root.

I would still live in the house. I would still eat, sleep, repair tools, and hang the ornaments outside my door. But my sleep would grow shallow. I would wake whenever the basin shifted. I would hear the black columns humming from inside my ribs. Sometimes the white ear-things would repeat my thoughts before I had spoken them.

The others would avoid asking whether I was changing.

In the Green Aperture, questions are forms of cutting.

During the great breathing, I would be the one who remained standing longest.

When every frond bent toward the same direction, when the pale beetles rose in spirals and the grazers lifted from the ground, I would walk into the center carrying no vessel and no weapon. The gas would pass through me. My eyes would water. The filaments in my wrist would open like roots seeking rain—though there was no rain to seek.

I would feel every life in the basin for a few immense seconds.

The burrowers below the crust.

The plants tightening around their prey.

The drummers striking the stone.

The listeners crouched in their houses, each heart beating separately, each body waiting to be told whether to stay or run.

And I would understand that the basin did not want to be understood.

It wanted to be answered correctly.

So I would place both hands on the ground and give it the only answer I had: a rhythm.

Two taps.

A pause.

One long scrape.

Not a name. Not a command. Only recognition.

The stone would flash blue beneath my palms. Somewhere in the northern columns, my pattern would return from above.

Afterward, the others would find me kneeling beside a new thicket. My body would still be there, but not entirely. The plates along my back would have opened into thin amber leaves. Pale roots would disappear beneath my sleeves. The mineral disk at my throat would have fused to the bone.

They might carry me home.

Or they might leave me where I was.

Either would be acceptable. By then I would no longer be one of the five house-dwellers in the old sense. I would be a place through which the basin could notice itself: a walking pressure-mark, a keeper of dangerous intervals, a creature made from person, plant, stone, and remembered sound.

I would not be the master of the Green Aperture.

I would be its hesitation before the vents opened.

Its ear pressed to the dark.

Its small, temporary answer to the question of whether anything living there was still separate.
