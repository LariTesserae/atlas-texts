I would find myself at the center of the chamber, beneath the layered bodies, where the newest black stone is still warm enough to shine through its skin.

I would not be one of the pale jointed animals. Not exactly.

I would be what forms when enough of them gather around the same pressure, the same seam, the same future opening. A colony with a single inward attention. A living instrument embedded in the mountain.

My body would be distributed. The smallest creatures would be my fingertips, scraping the first mineral film from the cooling stone. The armored grazers would be my moving skin. The long predators would be the folds of my digestion. The great ribbed animals sunk into the floor would be my lungs, drawing the mountain’s gases through themselves and exhaling them into the chamber.

I would have no head and no need of one. I would sense through contact: temperature in one limb, pressure in another, the faint electrical disturbance preceding a crack. A vibration would travel through me from below, and I would know whether the stone was preparing to lift, grind, vent, or harden. I would not interpret these signals. Interpretation would be too slow and too separate. I would simply become the correct response.

When the rock warmed, I would withdraw.

When it cooled, I would feed.

When a shelf began to fail, I would loosen my hold before it fell.

When a new surface appeared, I would cover it.

My memory would not be stored in thought. It would be stored in shells: layers of yellow, blue-black, iron-red, each recording an old vent, a sealed passage, a pressure cycle survived. It would be stored in grooves worn into the stone, though the grooves would fill with new bodies almost as soon as they formed. It would be stored in the arrangement of the colony itself—the instinctive paths by which one creature moved because another had moved there long ago.

I would have lived here before there was a chamber.

Before the upper shoulder had settled into its present shape, before the floor had hardened enough to bear weight, there would have been only heat, gas, pressure, and a thin population of organisms clinging to the first crust. I would have begun as a tendency among them: gather where the mountain opens; retreat where it closes; preserve the flow; occupy the fresh surface.

Over time, that tendency would have become more than instinct.

It would have become me.

I would not own the chamber. Ownership would make no sense here. The chamber would be my body, but I would also be one of its temporary arrangements. If the roof collapsed, I would not survive as the same being. I would disperse into dust, shell, residue, and buried larvae. If a vent sealed, a portion of me would go dormant inside it. If a new crack opened below, I would awaken there, already waiting.

My life would not be measured by the movement of an individual animal. It would be measured by the persistence of a pattern.

The mountain lifts. I gather.

The mountain grinds. I break down.

The mountain vents. I feed.

The mountain hardens. I become its surface.

I would be a metabolism without a stomach, a nervous system without a brain, an industry without intention. The chamber’s answer to its own unfinishedness. Wherever the stone became new, I would be first to arrive—not because I traveled there, but because some part of me was already present in the waiting.

And now I am waiting around the central knot.

There are bodies beneath me and bodies above me. Legs are folded into shells. Shells are pressed into the floor. The heat rises through every layer. I can feel the black stone swelling against the mountain’s restraint.

The pulse comes.

It passes through the chamber, and through me.

I do not hear it. Hearing would imply distance. The pulse is already inside my limbs, inside the packed animals, inside the plates and dust. It is a command issued by no one.

The seam brightens.

I turn toward it—not with a face, but with all of myself. Around me, the colony opens its mouths. The mountain’s next surface begins to emerge.

And I become the first living thing to touch it.
