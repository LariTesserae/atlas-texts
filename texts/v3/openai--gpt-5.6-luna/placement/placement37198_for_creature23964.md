I would find myself beneath the settlement, though not deep beneath it.

The Vey would call me a **seam-keeper**: one who listens to the places where stone, root, animal, and house have become impossible to separate.

I would live in the narrow spaces beneath the walkways and behind the oldest walls, where a person cannot stand upright but can move on hands and knees through warm dust. My dwelling would be a series of connected hollows beneath the central grove. I would sleep beside the roots of the mineral trees, wrapped in layered fiber, with one palm pressed to the ground.

I would not listen for voices.

I would listen for changes.

A wall has a voice before it falls. A burrower has a different rhythm from a settling foundation. Roots announce themselves as slow pressure, swelling through clay and salt. A warm crack that has begun to widen speaks in irregular pulses. The mineral trees knock their messages through the floor, and the old houses answer them.

The Vey would come to me with their ears uncovered and their questions held back.

They would tap a pattern into the wall: a house, a family, an animal, a warning. I would place my hand over the signal and wait. Sometimes I would tell them that the sound belonged to a harmless root. Sometimes I would tell them to move their sleeping ledge before the next warming. Sometimes I would say nothing, because the wall had not yet decided what it meant.

My hands would be broad and jointed from a lifetime of crawling through the settlement’s hidden passages. The pads of my fingers would be dark with mineral dust. I would keep my hair shaved close, not for appearance but because loose hair gathers salts and betrays the body’s warmth to biting insects. One eye would be veiled by a translucent membrane that never fully withdrew. My skin would carry the rust-colored stain of the grove.

I would smell of hot stone, root vapor, and the small pale animals that nest in my clothing.

The children would be afraid of me until they were old enough to understand that the tapping beneath the floor was not always a warning. I would teach them to distinguish three sounds:

the quick, hollow knock of a trapped creature;

the deep, patient knock of a wall becoming tired;

and the almost inaudible tremor that means the mountain has shifted somewhere beyond the settlement.

They would practice with their palms on clay vessels. They would laugh when I corrected them. Later, they would return alone, carrying news from their homes, asking whether a house was safe, whether a sleeping place had become too warm, whether a dead relative’s wall cavity had begun to open.

I would know the dead by their continuing effects.

A grandmother would remain in the way a root diverted around her bones. A builder would remain in a stair that never settled. A child would remain in the nest of climbing animals that appeared each cold season beneath the roof where he had slept. The Vey would say these things were memories. I would say they were pressures still moving through the stone.

I would have no room with a door.

My belongings would hang from roots and beams: bone probes, clay listening cups, braided markers, salt chalk, a small blade, and a collection of smooth stones chosen for the sounds they made against different walls. My cooking place would be a shallow warm seam. My water—or what passes for water—would come from a porous vessel set among the grove roots, where vapor gathered slowly enough to be counted by days.

I would eat little. Bitter seed cakes. Edible films scraped from shaded stone. The soft organs of plants after they opened toward a heat seam. At night, I would drink from the communal vapor vessel, taking only what was placed in my hands. The settlement would sustain me because I sustained its continuities. No one would call this an exchange. It would be more like a wall holding up a roof.

I would not be a warden, exactly. Wardens listen to trees and gases. Rim-watchers listen to the glare. I would listen to what happens after those things touch the settlement.

I would know when an animal colony had moved beneath a house. I would know when the grove had begun sending too much sweetness into the air, a sign that something below was feeding on a new mineral seam. I would know which paths could bear a procession and which had become hollow beneath their carpets of plants. I would know when a person was ill before their family smelled it, because illness alters the rhythm of the body against the floor.

Perhaps, in time, I would become difficult to regard as entirely human.

Not because I would grow strange limbs or lose the shape of a Vey, but because I would spend too much of my life with my body turned toward the ground. My speech would grow sparse. I would answer questions by tapping once, twice, or not at all. I would sleep through ordinary noise and wake at the faintest change in pressure. When people touched my forehead, they would feel that my skin was cool while my hands were warm.

They might give me a name like **Palm Beneath**, or **The One Who Hears Before**, or eventually, when I had outlived several houses, **Wall-After-Wall**.

I would not leave the summit.

I might imagine descending. I might listen to travelers describe rain and open silence, liquid moving without being carried, green things that do not cling to stone for survival. But I would understand those stories as one understands the existence of a distant ancestor: real in some manner, yet unsuitable to the body one has become.

Below the settlement, the mountain would continue its slow work. Above it, the pale world would burn without horizon. Around me, roots would tighten, animals would scratch, walls would settle, and people would breathe through their filters.

I would place both hands against the floor.

The whole summit would pass through me—not as a vision, but as pressure: a thousand small lives, a thousand structures refusing collapse, every hidden weakness announcing itself in time to be tended.

And if the stone permitted it, I would remain there until I became part of the listening.

Not buried.

Not remembered.

Simply another layer through which the settlement could continue to hear itself.
