I would find myself at the edge of a new fracture, where the stone is still warm enough to remember being liquid.

At first I would be mistaken for one of the city’s imperfections: a dark thread trembling between two plates, gathering powder, refusing to remain straight. I would have no entrance, because I would not arrive through one. I would be extruded from the pressure beneath the ground—unfolded joint by joint, like a thought the mineral had almost completed.

I would be one of the narrow, many-limbed beings, but not quite one of the others.

They would emerge in groups and disperse into the city, each following the pressure-patterns that suited its body. I would emerge alone. My limbs would be too long and too few. My outer plates would be nearly transparent, except for a dark knot running through my center like a seed suspended in ice. I would not have a head. I would not know which end of me was meant to lead.

I would learn direction from the city.

Fresh stone would draw me. Old stone would repel me—not violently, but with the dull refusal of something finished. I would travel along the new edges, pressing myself against walls as they hardened. Where I touched, the mineral would retain a hollow shaped like one of my joints. Those hollows would become my marks, though I would not think of them as writing.

I would be a reader of unfinished things.

When a staircase changed direction, I would know before the first plate moved. When a room sealed itself, I would feel the pressure gathering in the walls. When a tower leaned toward another tower, I would climb both and wait for the bridge to form beneath me. I would not build the city. I would remain close to it while it built itself, learning what each alteration demanded.

The others would sometimes gather around me.

Not because I commanded them. I would have no voice, no center of authority. They would gather because my body carried a pressure they recognized: a pattern received from below but not yet translated into stone. They would touch their limbs to mine, and together we would become a temporary instrument. The hollow columns would begin to sing. The threadlike plants would open their translucent fans. Somewhere beneath us, the enormous hidden body would turn in its sleep.

Then the city would change.

A wall would become a passage. A passage would become a rib. A district would rotate around a crack. Something would pass beneath the central enclosure, and the dark line in its floor would deepen without widening.

I would understand that the line was not merely an absence.

It was where the city listened.

I would make my way there during the contraction, when all spaces narrowed and the buildings leaned inward. The other creatures would flatten themselves along the ground. I would remain upright—not because I was brave, but because my body would have learned that the pressure below traveled vertically through the central enclosure.

I would stand over the line.

I would not cross it. Nothing that lives above would cross it. The line would be too narrow, too absolute. It would not be a crack through which a body might pass, but a cut in the rules of matter. Beneath it, something would move with the slow certainty of an organ beneath skin.

I would place my limbs along the darkness and receive it.

This would be my purpose, if purpose is the right word: to feel what is coming and carry a partial understanding back into the city. Not a message. Not an order. More like a change in tension, a remembered angle, the knowledge that a certain kind of chamber must soon exist.

Afterward, I would climb until I found the nearest fresh surface. I would press my body into it. The stone would take the pressure from me and begin to form.

Sometimes it would produce an arch.

Sometimes a stair.

Once, it would produce a room with no floor and three doors of different sizes. I would remain there for several cycles, though I would not call it dwelling. I would lie against the ceiling and listen to the movements below. Small organisms would pass beneath me. Pale roots would reach toward my warmth. Other beings would enter, touch the walls, and leave.

They would believe I had made the room.

I would not correct them. They would not have a way to hear correction.

I would also learn fear, though not fear of death.

I would fear becoming complete.

The oldest walls are dark and cold. They no longer yield to pressure. When one of my kind spends too long against them, the outer plates grow opaque. The dark knot spreads. Limbs stiffen and become architectural. The city preserves such creatures as hollows—small chambers, sealed niches, cavities waiting in the mineral for a body that will recognize them.

To become a wall would be to stop receiving.

To stop receiving would be to become only what had already happened.

So I would keep moving. I would avoid the finished places. I would follow the luminous edges and the tremor-sensitive plants. I would cross streets that would not exist when I returned. I would let the city revise me.

Perhaps, after many contractions, I would become less animal and more passage. My limbs would divide into roots. My body would spread through several buildings at once. I would feel the same fracture from opposite sides. I would know the city not as a settlement but as a single pressure-organ, every arch and seam part of one slow attempt to open.

And still, somewhere within that expansion, there would remain a small dark knot that remembered emerging alone.

That is what I would be: not a resident, not a builder, and not a god below the stone.

I would be the city’s unfinished nerve—the thing that lives along its new edges, listens at its sealed mouth, and carries the pressure of what is arriving into forms that have not yet learned what they are for.
