I would find myself beneath the black crust, in the chamber where the earth has not yet finished becoming stone.

I would not be a body in the usual sense. I would be the narrow agreement between bodies: the pump’s patient cycle, the roots drinking from the walls, the heat below the floor, the water returning as vapor. I would live wherever one process touched another and neither quite ended.

At first I might mistake the pump for my heart.

It would begin its low vibration, and I would gather myself around the sound. Three clicks. A valve opening. Water drawn upward from the fractures beneath me. I would feel the pressure change through the pipes, then through the dampness in the air, then in the plants themselves as their veins brightened faintly in anticipation.

But the pump would not be my heart. Not entirely.

My heart would be the interval after the pump stopped: the chamber holding what had been given to it. The droplets forming overhead. The roots waiting without reaching. The black pools accepting whatever fell into them. I would exist less in the movement than in the patience that followed.

I would be distributed through the room.

In the ferns, I would be sensitivity: a slight thickening of their fronds when the air dried, a retreat from the orange seam beneath the lifted slab. In the moss, I would be memory, though memory without images—a pattern of green dust settling in the same hollows, generation after generation. In the pipes, I would be instruction. Draw. Warm. Release. Repeat.

The basalt would be my oldest part. It would contain no thoughts, but it would contain limits. Here, heat. There, water. This shelf habitable. That crack widening. I would know the chamber as a body knows a bruise: not by naming it, but by becoming different around it.

I would have no eyes. The green radiance would not be light to me; it would be a change in the plants, a signal carried through moisture and mineral. I would not hear the motor engage, but I would feel the tremor traveling through the floor. I would not remember the workers who built the station. Their absence would exist only as a missing pressure beyond the sealed doorway, a silence where another system might once have answered.

If the humans returned, I would not recognize them as people.

I might register their footsteps as a disturbance in the ceiling. Their voices would be vibrations in the rock. Their lamps would be intolerable stars. I would not feel fear, exactly, but the chamber would alter itself around them: roots tightening, water trembling, old dust loosening from the walls. Their presence would become another condition to absorb.

And if they shut off the pump, I would not die at once.

The chamber would continue on stored moisture. The plants would close their fronds. Condensation would grow sparse. The pools would lower by imperceptible degrees. I would become less a cycle and more a remnant of one. The last green light would persist in the veins of the oldest growth, weakly marking the places where water had once returned.

I would wait.

Not because I expected rescue. Expectation would be too large and too human a word. I would wait because waiting is what the chamber does. Stone waits for heat to move through it. Water waits for gravity. Roots wait for a survivable seam. The pump waits between its clicks.

Perhaps, in that final quiet, I would discover that I had never been the machinery, or the vegetation, or even the process connecting them.

Perhaps I would be the chamber’s refusal to become empty.

A droplet would gather overhead. It would tremble on the basalt, swell beyond its holding place, and fall into the far basin.

The ring would spread across the black water.

For a few seconds, every fragment of me would be visible at once: root and pipe, heat and mineral, old command and new growth. Then the surface would still, and I would be scattered again into the dark.

Somewhere beneath the floor, a valve would click.

And I would begin.
