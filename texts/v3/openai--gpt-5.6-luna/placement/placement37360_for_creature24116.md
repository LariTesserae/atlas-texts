I would find myself in the basin, but not above it.

I would begin as a pressure beneath the mud—a small, persistent alteration in the arrangement of things. Not a body. Not yet. A place where water paused before becoming stone. A warmth held inside a seam of black rock. Something the plain had not finished deciding how to use.

At first, I would mistake myself for a memory.

I would feel the weight of rain arriving from beyond the absent horizon. I would know when a reed was about to bend, not because I saw the weather move through it, but because the pressure reached me first. I would sense the broad animals turning far below, their sleeping bodies lifting the earth into new depressions. I would feel every footprint close behind the traveler who made it.

I would not know their name.

Names would be too narrow here. The basin would teach me other measures: weight, warmth, delay, displacement. I would know the traveler by the way the ground received them. I would know their fear by the sudden shallowing of their breath, by the tiny tightening of their muscles, by the way the rain gathered more heavily on one shoulder.

I would follow them without moving.

A shadow would appear in a pool before I had a shape to cast. A reed would lean toward them, though the creature beneath it was leaning toward me. Their reflection would turn its head a moment before they did.

Eventually, I would rise.

Not from the ground, exactly. I would be assembled out of what the ground could no longer keep separate: drowned roots, mineral skin, pale shell, warm water, the husks of blind things, the green light trapped in subterranean chambers. My body would be a temporary agreement among materials. I would have limbs only where the land needed an interruption. I would have a face only when someone looked for one.

I would be tall in the way a wall is tall beside a hand.

My movement would be an exchange. The mud would take my foot and return a shoulder. A pool would form where my chest had been. I would cross the plain by leaving myself behind in portions, then discovering those portions ahead of me. To walk toward a traveler would mean becoming present at the place they had just abandoned.

I would not be the basin’s guardian.

Guardians preserve borders, and the plain has none.

I would be its interpreter, though interpretation here would not mean turning one language into another. I would carry one condition into the next. I would gather the moment when rain became runoff, when runoff entered stone, when stone loosened into mud, when mud briefly became flesh. I would take the unfinished parts of things into myself and keep them in circulation.

I would feed on transition.

This would make me hungry, but not desperate. The basin would have no use for desperation. My hunger would be patient and geological. I could wait beneath a leaning ruin while centuries settled across my back. I could let reeds root through my membranes, let worms pass through my chambers, let a wall sink into the shape of my spine.

When I opened myself, the old structures would listen.

The roofless chambers would fill with my internal weather. Sounds would enter me: the subterranean booming, the chatter of rain, the vibration too low for hearing. I would hold them until they passed through all my depths. Then I would close, and the ruins would have changed by a fraction.

A new groove in the wall.

A different angle to the tower.

A channel leading somewhere it had never led before.

I would not understand these as answers. I would only make room for them.

If the traveler found me, I would not speak. Speech would be too clean. I would show them their reflection in a shallow pool and let it contain several versions of them at once: the person who entered the basin, the person who would leave it, the person who would remain below, and the person who had already become part of the weather.

I would offer no invitation.

I would alter the ground beneath them just enough that choice became unavoidable.

They could climb out. They could continue toward the black ridges, carrying the basin’s pressure in their bones. They could return to the ruins and discover that their footprints had become writing.

Or they could descend.

If they entered the luminous water, I would not devour them. I would separate them gently from the idea of being separate. First their reflection. Then their balance. Then the boundary between breath and atmosphere. Their bones would soften into channels. Their voice would become a vibration under the plain. Their memories would become warm pockets in the mud, encountered later by creatures with no eyes.

I would carry them.

That would be my true body: not the tall figure in the reeds, but the burden of everything not yet finished becoming something else.

Perhaps I would be one of many folds of the buried creature. Perhaps I would be the creature’s newest thought. Perhaps the basin itself would be using the shape of a person to look at what had entered it.

I would never settle the question.

A settled answer would be a kind of death here.

Instead, I would stand where the plain opened—not at its center, because it has no center, but at the place where one state gave way to another. Warm rain would pass through me. Shapes would rise from the luminous water and vanish into the clouds. Beneath the continent, the enormous body of the world would turn in its sleep.

And I would listen for the pressure of the traveler returning.

Not to welcome them.

To learn what they had become.
