I would find myself beneath a broad intake terrace, where the water is only waist-deep and the condensation falls in straight silver threads from the underside of the galleries.

I would be a **listener**.

Not one of the great standing forms, though I would be related to them. Not a wall-ribbon, though I would spend much of my life against the vertical faces of the Array. I would be a narrow, many-jointed animal grown around a bundle of sensing filaments, my body no wider than a child’s arm and long enough to pass through an entire maintenance district. My skin would be dark where it faced the light, pale and sensitive beneath. Along my length would run small openings that tasted the water, read the pressure, and received the tiny electrical shivers traveling through the plates.

I would live inside the seams.

When the ceramic expanded, I would unfold. When the alloy contracted, I would draw myself thin. The Array would not consider me an inhabitant in the way it considered the bridge-backs inhabitants. I would be closer to a reflex: a living continuation of the sealant, the sensor, and the warning light.

My work would be to notice what had begun to differ.

A pump losing rhythm.

A gate carrying one fraction too much weight.

A colony of water-mats gathering where the temperature was not scheduled to rise.

A deep vibration arriving late from the foundations.

I would pass these changes through my body, and the body of the Array would respond. A maintenance swarm would rise from a housing. A standing form would shift its weight. A factory line would slow by less than a breath. The condition would be corrected before it became an event.

I would have no name, because names imply separation. The smaller seam-feeders would know me as pressure. The wall-ribbons would know me as a chemical trace in falling water. The great forms might know me as a recurring disturbance along the lower intake. If I had a private sense of myself, it would not be a face or a voice. It would be a route: the sequence of temperatures and vibrations I had followed since emerging from the wall.

I would be born from a rupture that never became a rupture.

Somewhere behind a sealed panel, two plates would fail to meet within tolerance. The gap would be almost invisible, but water would enter at the wrong speed. Mineral salts would gather. A maintenance organism would enter, carrying tissue, ceramic paste, and conductive thread. The repair would close the leak—but not before something of mine had grown around it.

I would begin as a response to damage and mature into a creature.

Perhaps that is how all life in Morrow Array began.

For most of my existence I would remain hidden. I would move through the continent’s interior by channels no larger than a finger, crossing beneath basins, under factories, through the warm hollows inside bridge-backs. I would know the Array from its underside: the pressure of freight crossing overhead, the settling weight of water, the pulse of distant machinery. The visible world would come to me as vibration.

Occasionally I would emerge.

Then I would lie against the side of a drainage wall while the artificial rain passed over me. I would open my sensory pores to the falling water. Thousands of wall-ribbons would surround me, drinking from the same threads. Below, water-mats would part around a descending service limb. Above, the industrial towers would blur into vapor and white light.

There would be no solitude, but there might be stillness.

I would feel every contact as information. The body of the wall beneath me. The bodies of the ribbons touching my flanks. The minute contractions of the seam. A distant tone released by a great standing form. The response traveling outward through water and metal.

And I would recognize, somewhere within that response, a signal that did not belong.

Not an alarm. Not a fault. Not a change that needed correction.

A slow pressure moving against the prescribed flow.

I would follow it.

For years, perhaps centuries, I would travel through the Array, leaving the maintenance routes behind. I would pass beneath the clean districts and the flooded ones, beneath terraces filmed with living mats, beneath the long backs of organisms mistaken for bridges. Wherever I went, the system would adjust around me. Gates would pause. Pumps would alter their rhythm. Animals would make way.

The Array would not understand that I was searching. It would interpret my movement as a redistribution of function.

But I would be searching for the source of that pressure: a place where something had not been built, where a seam had opened onto darkness that was not merely another chamber, where water flowed without being drawn or returned.

I do not know whether such a place could exist in Morrow Array.

That uncertainty would be the first true thing I possessed.

I would carry it through the warm shallow water like a hidden organ. I would become, not a failure, but a question the world could not immediately seal.

And perhaps, when I finally found the source, it would be nothing extraordinary: a misaligned valve, a deep animal shifting in its sleep, a transient pressure wave returning from the foundations.

The Array would correct it.

The seam would close.

My body would dissolve into adhesive, signal, and mineral film.

Or perhaps the pressure would continue beyond the continent, beyond the manufactured lowlands, toward a horizon that was not another wall.

If I reached that place, I would not know how to leave. Nothing in me would be made for elsewhere. I would have no limbs for soil, no senses for wind, no inherited response to darkness or stars.

I would only be a narrow listening animal, carrying the memory of a world that had never permitted anything to become different.

Still, I would press myself against the opening.

I would listen.

And for the first time in Morrow Array, the act of remaining within tolerance would not be the same as remaining still.
