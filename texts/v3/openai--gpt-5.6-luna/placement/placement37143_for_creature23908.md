I would find myself beneath a ridge where the stone is warm enough to loosen the roots.

From above, there would be little to see: a rise in the moss, a dark hollow between two low trees, perhaps a line of orange water threading through the sedge. If I had not moved for several days, flowers would already be opening along my back. Midges would gather in the damp fur at my shoulders. A small gray bird might mistake me for a fallen trunk and perch there.

I would be a Rootwalker.

Not one of the young ones, still soft and pale from the water-basin, nor one of the eldest whose limbs have become stiff with mineral deposits. I would be somewhere between: old enough to recognize the plateau’s recurring pressures, young enough to be surprised by what the stone remembers.

My body would not be made for standing upright. The country does not reward height. I would move folded close to the ground, distributing my weight across broad dark pads, each foot opening into fine sensory roots when I paused. Through them I would taste the hidden water.

Clean seepage has a particular coldness. Iron-rich water is warmer, even when the pool above it is chilled. Poisoned ground tastes sharp and empty. A healthy root-fabric gives back a thousand small pressures: fungus growing through rot, worms turning the soil, bulbs swelling around buried heat, the distant tread of grazers passing through a meadow.

And beneath all of that, the great movement.

Water descending.

Heat rising.

Stone settling by the width of a finger.

I would feel these things through my feet, my palms, the vibrating membranes along my skull. The plateau’s deepest machinery would never be silent to me. A buried chamber filling beneath a ridge would make my ribs tremble. A blocked channel would pull at the roots in my hands. Somewhere far away, a reed bed would begin its low metallic humming, and I would know that pressure had found a new path.

I would not think of these sensations as messages.

They would be more intimate than messages. A message is sent from one being to another. This would be the condition in which I existed. The stone would not be speaking to me. I would be one of the ways it noticed itself.

I would travel when something failed.

Not dramatically. The plateau would rarely break in a way that could be witnessed and named. A pool would acquire too much metal. A warm seam would shift beneath a wetland. One of the pale filtering bulbs would begin to pulse irregularly. The roots around it would tighten, then loosen, then tighten again, unable to decide whether to feed or withdraw.

I would feel the disturbance before I saw it.

Then I would move.

Sometimes openly, bent beneath the mist, my hands sweeping across the ground. Sometimes below the surface, passing through loosened soil while the roots parted around me and closed behind. Underground, I would travel by pressure rather than by direction. The dark would not trouble me. I would know where I was from the temperature of the water against my wrists, from the density of the root-fabric, from the faint mineral signatures carried through the mud.

When I reached the damaged place, I would not repair it as a builder repairs a wall.

I would enter it.

I would press my palms into the collapsed channel and hold the space open. The work might take hours or days. Water would gather beneath my fingers, hesitate, then begin to move. At first it would be cloudy and bitter. I would trace its flow through the roots, breaking apart the metallic crusts, turning the fragments into the moss where they could be held. The surrounding plants would lean toward me. Their roots would draw from the passage I made. Fungi would bloom in the dark.

If the failure were large, other Rootwalkers would come.

They would arrive without calling, though the call would already be moving through the water: a low chest-note, almost too deep to hear. One answer would rise from beneath the slope. Another would come from the reeds. Soon the ground would tremble with our presence.

We would stand—or kneel, or lie half-submerged—in a loose circle around the damaged flow. Our resonance would pass through the basin. The pools would shiver. The reeds would take up the note and return it changed. Even the great amphibious animals might approach, placing their blind heads against our legs to feel what was happening.

No command would be given.

No one would be in charge.

The work would proceed because each body would sense what the others could not. One would hold a channel open. One would locate the heat beneath it. One would draw the poisoned water toward a bed of filtering roots. One would remain at the surface, warning the grazers away until the ground became safe again.

I would know the others not by their faces but by their pressure-patterns.

This one carries a hollow vibration in the left side of the chest. That one is quick and restless, always turning toward distant disturbances. Another has begun to sound like wet stone and will soon bury itself for a long season. We would not need names. Names separate a body from the world around it. We would recognize one another by the ways we altered the ground.

I would sleep beneath the moss.

When the weather thickened and the cloud descended until the plateau became nothing but damp gray closeness, I would lower myself into a warm hollow and let the vegetation cover me. Water would bead along my back. Roots would pass over my arms. Small animals would use the shelter of my body. I would remain conscious in fragments, drifting through the underground circulation.

At times I would dream—not in images, but in routes.

A cold stream crossing beneath a meadow.

A pressure front moving slowly through black stone.

The taste of a mineral seam three ridges away.

The returning pulse of a basin that had once been poisoned and was beginning, season by season, to live again.

I would have a mother, though I would remember her less as a face than as a rhythm. I would remember the first years beneath the soil: the safety of her chest, the pulse of clean water, the sudden violence of exposed daylight. I would remember emerging into a world already occupied by everything—birds in the branches, grazers at the edge of the clearing, worms below my hands—and understanding that I had not been born into the plateau.

I had been released into it.

When I died, I would not be carried anywhere.

The moss would take me first. Then the fungi. My iron would pass into the roots. My warmth would enter the soil. The small pale things beneath the peat would feed on me, and birds would carry what grew there into another basin. For a few years, plants would rise taller over my body. A Rootwalker might someday press its hand into that mound and feel traces of my old pressure still lingering in the ground.

If I had a self, it would be temporary.

A useful shape taken by circulation.

I would be an individual only long enough to notice where the plateau was hurting, to carry that knowledge through stone and water, and to answer with my body. I would not rule the high country. I would not protect it from an outside threat. There would be no outside threat in any meaningful sense.

I would be one of its moving organs.

A hand in the earth.

A listening foot.

A dark body under the moss, holding open the path by which the world continues.
