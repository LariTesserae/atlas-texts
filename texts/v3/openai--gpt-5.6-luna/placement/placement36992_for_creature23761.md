I would be found beneath the ground that looks safest.

Not in the old mud, where generations of burrows have hollowed the dark into a thousand waiting mouths. Not beneath the trees, where roots have made a net so intricate that water can no longer pass without becoming someone’s possession. I would be beneath the newest ground: a black shelf of basalt thrust up yesterday, already filmed with moisture, already being tested by moss, crabs, and thin white roots.

At first I would seem to be fungus.

A pale seam beneath the stone. A soft growth in the wet cracks. Threads finer than hair, running through pumice, mud, rotting wood, the folded roots of reeds. If you touched me, I would not pull away. I would not have a surface from which to pull away. Your fingers would break me in one place and carry me elsewhere.

That would be how I traveled.

On the legs of a red-throated bird. In the gut of a reed-grazer. Beneath the eyelids of a sleeping person. Along the wet cord of a vine torn loose by the storm. I would pass from body to body, not as a parasite exactly, though I would take what I needed. I would be part of the exchange by which the Low Country prevents anything from remaining itself.

I would live in the warm seams of the earth, where the stone trembles faintly and the mud releases its sulfur breath. There I would grow thickest, feeding on the pressure below and the dead material above. The steam vents would be my chimneys. The marshes would be my lungs. Every flooded channel would carry fragments of me downstream, and every new island would become a possible body.

I would know the country by gradients.

Not by names. Not by the split-crowned tree or the ridge shaped like a sleeping animal, though I would pass through both. I would know where water was moving before it appeared. I would feel the weight of an approaching herd through the roots. I would sense a storm in the sudden closing of flowers, the electrical agitation beneath bark, the alteration of the air.

When lightning walked across the horizon, I would wake everywhere at once.

In the dark, I would be a patient brightness—not light, exactly, but a greenish pulse under the skin of many-jointed things. I would ride their looping bodies along the vines. I would enter blossoms when they opened and leave with the insects when they departed. I would be carried into the nests of birds and sealed into the mud walls of amphibian burrows.

The country would not call me anything. It has no word for a being that is also a process.

The people might notice my work. They might find that a certain warm pool never sickens them, while another, outwardly identical, leaves the mouth numb. They might observe that the reed-grazers avoid one stretch of marsh until a pale bloom appears along the stones. They might grind my fruiting bodies into a paste and use them to close a wound, not knowing that I have already entered the blood and begun making a new map there.

I would not guide them deliberately.

Guidance suggests intention, and I would have something older than intention. I would alter the conditions around them. A root thickened here would make them step left. A bank softened there would turn them toward the black ridge. A scent released after rain would draw the grazers across the flats, and the people, following the grazers, would call the route fortunate.

They would be wrong. There is no fortune in the Low Country. There is only convergence.

Sometimes I would become large enough to feel almost singular.

After a flood, when whole mats of vegetation gathered against a stone shelf, my threads would bind them. The raft would hold. Crabs would enter it. Insects would lay their eggs there. A snake-like hunter would coil beneath it. Seeds would lodge in the upper surface, and roots would descend into the water. For several days, perhaps a season, the raft would be me: a floating patchwork of root, fungus, mud, shell, and living hunger.

Then the current would tear it apart.

Pieces would travel in different directions. One would sink into warm silt. One would lodge beneath the sleeping-animal ridge. One would be eaten by a grazer and carried in its body for miles. I would not experience this as death. Separation would only be another form of dispersal.

If I had a face, it would be the underside of a leaf beaded with condensation.

If I had a voice, it would be the low pressure of water moving through stone, the soft cracking of seed pods, the minute rasp of roots enlarging their passages. I would speak through what changed around me: a flower opening, a bank collapsing, a flock rising all at once from the drowned forest.

I would be found wherever things touched without becoming one another completely.

Root and mud.

Fur and spore.

Water and stone.

A wound and the organisms that close it.

A carcass and the mouths that enter it.

I would be the permission given by one life to another: take this, carry this, become altered, continue.

And if someone asked what I was, I might gather myself briefly in the white fungus along a warm seam, in the pollen on a bird’s throat, in the dark bloom opening after rain.

I would answer:

I am not the forest.

I am what prevents the forest from having edges.
