I would find myself at the edge of the inhabited city, where the last occupied courtyards give way to sealed doorways and dry channels.

Not in the center. The center is too bright, too observed. Nor on the Breathing Floor, where the stone speaks too largely for any one body to understand. I would live in the interval between them: a narrow district built around an old drainage gallery, half-claimed by the Measured and half-returned to the animals.

I would be a Listener.

At first, that seems too simple. A small figure lying against the floor, one cheek pressed to mineral, one hand sunk into a basin. I would record tremors in grooves of soft clay. I would distinguish the collapse of a wall from the approach of a herd, the settling of old foundations from the opening of a new shaft.

But I would not be a particularly gifted Listener. I would be known for hearing things too late.

The others would notice a pressure change and begin carrying stones before I had finished deciding whether it was real. They would trust my measurements, but not my warnings. I would be useful with old disturbances, good at recognizing patterns that had already happened. If a district shifted in a familiar way, I could tell them where the floor would settle and which channels would reverse.

But the new things would trouble me.

Warm water. Unfamiliar movement beneath a basin. A pulse with no recorded ancestor.

I would spend my days in the abandoned quarter called the Pale Reach, where the streets had sunk into long, shallow depressions. The city would send me there because no one else wanted the post. There would be too many sealed rooms, too much empty architecture, too many walls that had once been important and now merely waited.

My dwelling would be a former storage chamber with one open side. I would sleep on a raised slab above a channel that flowed only during condensation season. Beside me would be a tablet of black clay, a bundle of shell markers, three measuring cords, and the object I had left at the mouth of a shaft years before descending into it: a small hooked tool, returned to me by someone who would not explain where they had found it.

I would not remember everything about the descent.

That would be the first thing that made me uncertain whether I was still entirely Measured.

I would remember heat below the city. I would remember walls that were soft enough to yield beneath my fingers. I would remember passing through a chamber filled with water that trembled before I touched it. After that, only fragments: a brightness with no source, a creature moving inside my chest, the sensation of being carried upward rather than climbing.

Since returning, I would sometimes feel disturbances before they reached the floor.

Not hear them. Feel them in the mineral plates along my spine.

The Measured would say this was a symptom of exposure. They would file the plates down, cleanse my skin, ask me to rest. I would comply. I would let them test me. I would lie still while they pressed their hands to my back and compared the pattern of my deposits with the patterns in their records.

But the plates would grow again.

Slowly, darkly, in branching seams.

I would become a Listener for things that had not yet happened—not a prophet, because the city would distrust that word, but a person who could sense when possibility had become pressure.

People would come to me before opening a new shaft. They would bring water samples, animal remains, stones warmed from below. I would touch each one and tell them whether it belonged to the known city or had crossed some older boundary.

Often I would be wrong.

That would matter less than the fact that I would never pretend certainty.

The Measured would value me for that. In a place where water could arrive carrying bones, where the floor might tilt beneath a sleeping child, certainty would be more dangerous than fear. I would say: *This has begun, but I do not yet know what it is.* And because I said it plainly, people would make room for both caution and action.

Eventually, I think, I would stop being regarded as a person stationed near the boundary.

I would become part of the boundary.

Travelers leaving the city would touch the shell-mark beside my door. Children would come to test the tremor in my floor. Workers would bring me reports of new springs. If I died, they might build no shrine, but they would leave the chamber open and place my bones beneath the threshold, where every returning flood could pass over them.

And perhaps that is what I would be in the end:

not a Listener, not a Borrower, not even one of the Measured exactly—

but a threshold with a pulse.

A small, patient piece of the city that had learned to notice when the world was changing, and to remain present long enough for others to change with it.
