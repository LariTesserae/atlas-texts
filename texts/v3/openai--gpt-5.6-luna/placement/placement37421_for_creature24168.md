I would find myself in the lower city, where the oldest streets descend toward the central trench and the houses are built over chambers of moving water.

I would be a **listener of foundations**.

Not a musician, because music here would be swallowed. Not a prophet, because the basin does not reveal the future. I would be one of the people trained to recognize the ordinary vibrations of a place and to notice when one of them changes.

My work would begin before the lamps were lowered or the shutters opened. I would lie face down on a stone slab beneath the house, my cheek against the damp surface, and wait for the city to enter me.

Water beneath the floor: a soft, repeating knock.

The western seams: a long pressure, almost too deep to hear.

The settling bridge: three small ticks, separated by the length of a breath.

The filter-feeders in the communal tank: a slow pulsing tremor, like someone turning in sleep.

The house itself would have a signature. Every old building does. I would know when the neighboring families woke, when the hauling beasts passed beneath the lane, when a child dropped a clay cup two rooms away. I would know the difference between a rat-sized thing in the wall and a body large enough to undermine a threshold. I would know when the water was merely high and when it had begun to search for a new path.

People would call me **Still-Water**, though nothing about me would be still.

I would be compact and scarred, with broad feet and hands darkened by mineral dust. My hair would be kept close to my skull. I would wear a collar stiff enough to protect my throat from grit and a set of bone plates sewn into my sleeves, each plate marked with a district sign. When I walked the streets, I would carry a short clay rod and a pouch of wedges, cord, chalk, and thin shells. The shells would be placed against walls and bridge joints. If they trembled in the wrong pattern, I would mark the place.

I would not carry a bell. I would carry shutters.

A red one for danger beneath the street. White for a barrier that must be lowered. Yellow for a sick animal or a blocked channel. Black for silence.

Black would be used rarely.

Silence is the city’s true disaster. Noise means that the water is moving, the animals are feeding, the houses are holding, and the buried chambers are still full of their familiar labor. Silence means that something has stopped, or that something has entered so completely into the system that it no longer makes the expected disturbance.

I would fear a quiet gutter more than a flood.

The people would trust me, but not warmly. Trust here would be practical. They would let me enter their homes without explanation. They would clear a space beneath their floors. They would wake when I touched the wall beside their sleeping ledge. They would know that if I pressed two fingers to my wrist, the water was rising; if I placed my palm flat against the stone, they must wait; if I touched the back of my neck, they must get down.

I would rarely speak.

My closest relationships would be made through contact. A forehead against mine. A hand briefly closed around my wrist. Someone passing a tool into my palm without looking. Someone sitting beside me on a bridge while we both feel the weight of water passing underneath.

I would have an animal companion, though no one would agree whether it belonged to me.

It would be a low, blind scavenger with a warm hide and a head too broad for its body. It would live beneath the platform outside my room and emerge whenever I returned. I would call it **Below-Door**. It would know the undersides of the city better than I did. When it refused to enter a passage, I would believe it. When it curled against a foundation stone, I would inspect the stone. When it vanished, I would search the walls before I searched the streets.

I would not worship it. Worship suggests distance. I would owe it attention.

My dwelling would be under a bridge near the lakeward district, where the water was warmest and the traffic never ceased. Above me, animals would cling to the bridge’s underside. Their bodies would make the stone feel crowded. Water would fall between the slabs in bright threads. At night, if night were the right word, the roof colonies would burst from their nests and fill the passage with wings.

I would sleep lightly, one hand resting on the floor.

The dead of my household would be present in the walls. A pale finger bone might show through the plaster beside my sleeping place. The handle of my clay rod might contain powdered remains from someone who had listened before me. I would not know whether this comforted me. In the basin, continuation was not comfort exactly. It was simply the shape things took when they were not yet gone.

I would be respected because I corrected small failures before they became large ones.

I would raise a street by the thickness of a hand. I would redirect a channel around a foundation. I would replace a loosened wedge beneath a bridge and scrape silt from a grate while water climbed my knees. I would move a sleeping animal from an unsafe pen. I would inspect the wall of a house built by a mason dead for three hundred years and recognize, in the pattern of its dampness, where his hand had been.

My greatest achievement would never be visible.

It would be that a particular house remained standing. That a child crossed a lower district without falling. That a communal tank did not choke. That the city’s permanent vibration continued without interruption.

And yet I think, eventually, I would become troubled by one absence.

Somewhere beneath the outer settlement, perhaps beyond the lake where the wet black ground opens toward the horizon, I would feel a rhythm that did not belong to water, animals, stone, or people. It would be faint and regular. Not a knock. Not a pulse. Something more like a response.

At first I would record it as a fault.

Then as an animal.

Then as an error in my own body.

I would follow it outward through the occupied basin, past the causeways and the crowded channels, carrying my shutters and clay rod while the people watched from their doorways. I would not be seeking a new world. The basin’s people do not seek the unknown for its own sake.

I would be looking for a place where the city’s vibration disappeared and this other rhythm became clear.

If I found it, I would not bring back a prophecy.

I would bring back a position.

A place where one more wall could stand. A new channel to be marked. A room for someone to sleep. A warning about what lived beneath the ground.

Because I would not be separate from the basin. I would be one of its arrangements: a small, warm, listening creature between water and stone, keeping the familiar note from failing.
