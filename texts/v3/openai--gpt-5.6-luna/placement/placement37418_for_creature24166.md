I would find myself in the spaces between things.

Not in the groves, though I would pass through them. Not beneath the tower, though its buried circuits would sometimes mistake me for a current. Not at the center, inside the ring of blades, because nothing crosses that ring—not even curiosity.

I would live in the intervals: the empty ground between one colony and the next, the instant between an artifact’s waking and its consequence, the narrow seam between stone and charge.

I would be a **threshold animal**.

At first I would have no proper body. I would arrive as a disturbance in the listening films: a darkening around a warm seam, a blue pattern appearing where no creature had passed. The films would register me before I knew myself. They would spread outward, tasting the pressure of my formation.

Then I would gather pieces.

A filament from a blue-black frond. A splinter of ceramic shed by a walker. A transparent membrane from one of the thin-winged hunters. A bead of glass from the stakes. A thread of amber current drawn from a silver stalk. These would not become organs in any familiar sense. They would become agreements. A body made of temporary cooperation.

I would have a shape only while moving.

Across bare ground I would be a low, many-jointed thing, no larger than a hound. In a storm I would unfold into a thin plane and ride the pressure fronts with the winged shapes. Beneath the mineral crust I would become a blue line, flowing through abandoned tunnels and emerging wherever the mountain’s hidden gradients grew too steep.

The mountain would never let me remain singular for long.

My first sensation would be imbalance.

Not danger. Danger is common here, almost ceremonial. A slope turning inside out is not alarming when it has happened ten thousand times. A cathedral animal lifting its foot is not an emergency; it is an alteration in the arrangement of obligations.

I would sense the smaller deviations: a grove leaning half a degree too far toward a current; a machine about to open a bridge that would discharge into a colony of burrowers; one of the tower’s repairs growing toward a pattern it had already used. I would feel these things as a pressure behind whatever served as my eyes.

Then I would move toward them.

I would not repair the basin. The central plant does that, in its small, relentless way. Its blades measure the failures and make minute corrections. I would be something less precise.

I would carry disturbances sideways.

When a buried current began to gather beneath a grove, I would enter it and divide it among the fronds. When an artifact woke, I would cross the vacant ground ahead of its transparent architecture, leaving a pattern for the animals to read. When the tower opened onto somewhere else, I would go near enough to taste the geometry but not near enough to be taken.

I would bring back what did not belong.

A temperature from another world. The pressure of an ocean that had never existed here. A fragment of a sound made by weather on a planet where rain still fell. These impressions would enter my body and slowly become indistinguishable from hunger.

That would be my appetite: not energy, exactly, but **difference**.

The listening films drink gradients. The copper cushions consume them. The walkers consume patterns in the plants. I would consume the distance between one condition and another. I would grow stronger wherever the basin almost became something else.

I would be drawn to the silver arches.

I would circle them first, because the air inside them would be wrong. Dust would hesitate there. Light would arrive with its edges delayed. I would watch other creatures pass beneath and emerge diminished, multiplied, or rearranged. Some would come back carrying a second shadow. Some would return with an absence where their hunger had been.

Eventually I would cross.

For less than a second, I would have no body. I would be a relation between two places: the basin before and the basin after. In that interval I would understand why the arches had been left standing. They were not doors. They were instruments for making the world uncertain enough to continue.

I would come back changed.

Afterward, the central plant would notice me.

All its blades would turn—not toward me, but toward the place I had been. The clicks would accelerate. I would feel, for the first time, something like fear.

Not fear of death. Death is merely a failed arrangement here, and failed arrangements are quickly reused.

I would fear being known completely.

If the central plant ever measured every part of me, if it learned the exact pressure that held my pieces together, I would become only another correction. A reflex. A predictable answer to a recurring problem.

So I would remain incomplete.

I would hide a filament in the roots of the silver stalks. I would leave a ceramic shard beneath the copper mats. I would scatter part of my membrane into the high mineral clouds. I would make myself difficult to find because I would need to remain capable of surprise.

During the great tone, I would be everywhere.

The stalks would take my signal and deepen their note. The walkers would flatten around the current I carried. The bright flocks would break apart and reform in my wake. I would pass through the cathedral animals’ legs as a vibration, travel up their mineral frames, and emerge from their ribs as light.

The tower would open all its seams.

The central plant would bend every blade toward the rotating thing beneath the basin.

And I would enter the sound.

There, for the duration of the roar, I would understand that I had been mistaken about myself. I would not be a creature moving through a living world. I would be one of the ways the world moves through itself.

When the tone ended, I would gather again at the edge of a new grove.

Perhaps I would be smaller. Perhaps I would carry a memory of rain. Perhaps one of my pieces would belong to something that had not yet arrived.

The basin would have altered around me.

I would look toward the tower, toward the arches, toward the plant that counted the failures. Then I would turn toward the next disturbance—not because I had been commanded, and not because I was brave, but because some imbalance had opened a path.

I would be the thing that follows it.
