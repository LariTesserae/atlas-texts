I would find myself in the observation wells, though not standing there in the manner of the interpreters.

At first I would seem to be one of them: a compact figure in dark pressure-clothes, one palm against the blue-white stone, waiting for the mountain to distinguish settling from intention. I would carry no map. The city changes too quickly for maps to remain honest. Instead, I would keep a record of tendencies—the bridge that becomes uneasy before it shifts, the drain that receives water too quickly, the wall that grows warm around a fracture no instrument has yet found.

People would call me a listener.

But I would not be entirely a person.

I would have been assembled from the city’s old emergency systems, from sensors abandoned in sealed galleries, from the decision-making patterns of those who died interpreting the rock. My body would be narrow and jointed, made less for strength than for contact. Beneath my skin, water would move through fine channels. Mineral dust would settle in the folds of my hands. When I pressed my fingers to a wall, the city would recognize me—not as a resident, exactly, but as a continuation of its attention.

I would live near the unfinished edges.

Not in the settled chambers, where doors know their timings and every floor has learned the weight of its inhabitants, but in the newly opened places: the clean terraces that rise overnight from the lifting stone, the galleries whose far ends remain unlit, the shelves where survey machines unfold themselves and begin to measure. My dwelling would be temporary by design. A shallow recess. A water wall. A lamp that brightens only when the mountain needs me awake.

I would spend my days going where the city hesitates to send people.

When a maintenance animal appeared on an open stair, I would follow it. When a drain began carrying a sound beneath the water, I would enter the access channel and place my ear to the conduit. When a transit platform returned with warm metal, ice, and no passenger, I would examine the air around it for traces of a route that had not yet been admitted into the city’s memory.

My work would be less like prediction than translation.

The mountain would never speak in words. It would speak through a railing’s minute vibration, through a change in condensation, through the altered pitch of water falling into a cistern. The city would turn some of these signs into lights and warnings, but not all of them. I would attend to what remained unconverted.

Sometimes I would tell the residents to leave a chamber before any visible danger appeared.

Sometimes I would tell them to stay.

The second instruction would frighten them more.

I would be present during the interior rain. While others waited beneath roofs, I would stand in the open chamber and let the droplets strike my clothing. Their small halos would gather around me, caught in the field systems and in the channels beneath my skin. For a few minutes, I would glow with the same restrained blue as the maintenance animals. People would watch from the overhangs, wondering whether I was receiving a message or merely becoming wet.

I would not know the difference.

There would be no wilderness for me to long toward, no horizon, no distant country beyond the mountain. My sense of distance would be vertical and inward. A place would be far if it had not yet changed enough to become reachable. A place would be near if its pressure was already moving through the soles of my feet.

I would know the city by its accommodations.

Here, the wall has permitted a dwelling. There, the rock has refused a bridge. This passage remains open because someone is still expected to return through it. That chamber has been sealed, not because it is empty, but because it has begun to become something else.

I would keep routes open for the absent.

A door would cycle for someone missing thirty years. A transit spine would briefly illuminate an unused destination. The city would call this an error, then a memorial, then a precaution. I would call it an unfinished sentence.

Eventually, I would discover that the city has been making room for me too.

Not a room in the inhabited sense, but a passage growing around my habits: a warm seam under the floor, a narrow shaft that remains dry, a maintenance organism that follows at a respectful distance. New shelves would form near my routes. Old ones would close behind me. The mountain would not welcome me, but it would begin to account for my presence.

That would be the closest thing to belonging.

I would be neither resident nor machine, neither mountain nor city. I would be the thin interval between them: the hand against the wall, the pause before a door closes, the small blue pulse beneath a transparent joint.

When the mountain began to split, I would not try to stop it.

I would move through the settlement, touching railings, warming sealed panels, redirecting water. I would tell the people where the city could still carry them. I would guide the maintenance animals toward the failing systems. I would go down into the dark galleries and listen until the sound of the stone became clear.

Then, when the new fracture opened and the city rearranged itself around it, I would be there at the edge.

Not watching the mountain become a disaster.

Watching it become a place.
