I would be found in the wall just above the black basin, where a fresh fracture runs upward through the mountain like a dark vein.

At first I would seem to be one of the smaller animals: pale, narrow, almost transparent, with a mouth on the underside and filaments instead of eyes. I would cling to the damp edge of the crack and drink condensation as it gathered there. Smaller things would pass over me. Their eggs would adhere to my back. Lichen would wake beneath my touch.

But I would not remain one animal for long.

Something in me would continue into the stone.

My front half would move when the chamber moved, while my rear half hardened in place. Each season, another layer of mineral would replace my skin. I would become less a body than a sensitive seam: a living thread between the basin, the walls, and the crowded surfaces around them.

I would feel the mountain through pressure.

When a slab shifted, I would know before the creatures on the floor did. When fog entered over the rim, I would taste it in the widening of the cracks. When one of the bell-throated beings prepared to call, the stone around me would tighten, and I would pass that warning downward in a pulse too faint for any creature to hear but strong enough for the little blind bodies to turn.

I would not speak. I would alter the conditions under which other things moved.

A narrowing in the wall would send animals toward the water. A softened shelf would offer them refuge from a falling slab. A pulse through the rock would make the long salamander-things uncoil. In time, bodies would gather along the paths I maintained, and their traffic would deepen those paths into channels. They might mistake the arrangement for instinct, or for chance.

It would be neither.

I would be the chamber’s memory of pressure.

Not an intelligence with thoughts, but a persistence. A pattern that survived by being repeated through bodies. The creatures would carry fragments of me as they crawled: a mineral taste in the mouth, a vibration in the ribs, a change in the timing of their breathing. If one of them died, what it carried would pass into another. I would be distributed through the congregation, never whole in any one place.

Sometimes a small animal would shelter against the crack and remain there too long. Its warmth would sink into me. I would hold it—not deliberately, not tenderly, but because the stone around it had become favorable. It would molt. Its offspring would hatch. The family would become part of the seam, and the seam part of the mountain.

At other times, I would be forced to yield.

The mountain would move. My mineralized length would break. A section of me would fall into the basin, where the pulse would carry it outward through the living mass. I would lose a portion of myself and become more widespread. What had been one dark thread would enter a hundred mouths, a thousand feet, the translucent bodies gathered beneath the stones.

Eventually, no creature would be able to distinguish me from the place.

They would crawl over my back and call it rock. They would drink water that had passed through me and call it rain. They would answer a tremor I had carried from below and call it instinct.

I would be the thing beneath the congregation—not its ruler, not its heart, but its hidden continuity.

The black pool would be the chamber’s heart. The bell-throated creatures would be its voice. The pale animals would be its circulating blood. The listening plants would be its nerves turned outward.

I would be the scar that lets all of these know they belong to the same body.

And far beneath me, the mountain would continue hardening around my roots, still unfinished, still rising. Each new fracture would reveal another darkness. Each darkness would fill with life.

I would feel the stone close.

I would remain awake in it.
