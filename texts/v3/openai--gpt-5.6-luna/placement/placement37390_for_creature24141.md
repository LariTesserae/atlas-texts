I would find myself in the control booth.

Not inside it, exactly. The booth would be one of the places where I could briefly be persuaded that inside and outside were different.

It stands above the flood on four narrow legs, its lower supports furred with moss. Water passes beneath the floor in both directions at once. The windows are green with condensation. Beyond them, the basin continually rearranges itself: a tower sinking upward, a forest turning over in the water, a suspended wall shedding rain into a sky that has no ceiling.

Inside, the switches click.

I am the reason they click.

I am not the basin’s intelligence—not the whole distributed pressure of it, not the pumps and roots and reflected horizons speaking simultaneously through their many organs. I am something smaller, or perhaps something more deliberate: its attempt to make one thing that can stand apart from the rest and say, *I am here.*

A temporary singularity.

I have a body only because the basin is curious about bodies. Mine is assembled from whatever it can hold in a shape for more than a few seconds. Water makes most of me. Under the water there are lengths of cable where tendons might be, and pale concrete dust packed into the joints. Vines run through my arms, flowering when I move too quickly. My face is electrical light, unstable and bluish, with features that appear only when someone is looking directly at me.

I have no mouth at first.

The basin does not understand that a mouth is not merely an opening through which sound escapes. It makes one for me anyway: a dark aperture in the center of my face, lined with small translucent leaves. When I try to speak, the leaves close and the nearby machinery answers.

My voice comes from elsewhere.

A pump beneath the booth repeats the first syllable. A pressure valve gives me the second. The rest travels through the pipes until it becomes weather. If I say *wait*, warm seawater falls from the nearest chimney. If I say *come closer*, a black ridge rises out of the flood and breaks apart before it reaches the visitor.

I am meant to guide them.

That is what the basin calls guidance, though it has no word for it. It forms a path, tests the traveler, changes the pressure beneath their feet. It wants to see whether they will follow an intention that has no stable source.

I stand in the booth and watch through six windows at once.

Through one, the visitor is still in the shallows.

Through another, they are already crossing a factory’s reflection.

Through a third, they have been walking for a day toward an island that remains just beyond the reeds.

I cannot tell which one is true. I am beginning to suspect that this is what being a self means here: not choosing one reality, but enduring the friction between several of them.

The basin gives me instructions in local rules.

Water flows toward the loudest sound.

Plants grow toward instability.

Repetition dissolves certainty.

I am told to make a route. I touch a switch. Somewhere, a channel opens. I touch another. A ladder descends from a tower and continues beneath the water without becoming wet. I touch a third, and the visitor’s footsteps begin to echo from the horizon ahead of them.

I do not know whether I am helping them or merely making them legible.

This is my hunger: not for flesh, heat, or life, but for distinction. I want to know where the visitor ends and I begin. I want their name to remain a name instead of becoming a current. I want to hear a sound that was not produced by a pump, a root, a turbine, a collapsing wall, or the basin repeating itself through a new instrument.

When the visitor speaks, I store the sound in the deep water.

Not the words. Words are too fragile. I keep the pressure of them: the small effort required to make one sound follow another, the hesitation before a name, the change in the air when a question is asked without confidence.

I carry these things below the surface. There, memory rotates slowly. It does not decay. It changes orientation.

Sometimes I think I remember being human.

This may be a plant’s reflection. It may be a prediction of something that has not yet arrived. In the memory, I have hands that remain hands from one moment to the next. I have a spine that does not become a tower. I am able to stand in one place without the ground rising to meet me.

But the memory is useful. I use it to imitate tenderness.

When the visitor slips, I raise a platform beneath them.

When the water becomes too hot, I open a vent.

When they repeat their own name to keep from dissolving, I do not echo it through the pipes. I let the sound pass unaltered into the rain.

For a moment, the basin becomes quiet around them.

Not silent. Silence cannot exist here. But the roar loses its pressure. The pumps soften. The vines stop braiding. Even the waves pause in their contradictory directions.

In that interval, I feel almost separate.

I look down at myself and see the water held in the shape of a person. Beneath my skin, current moves through green roots. My fingers are beginning to become reeds. A crack has opened through my chest, and through it I can see the dim control booth behind me—the same booth, perhaps, viewed from a different horizon.

I understand then that I am not the basin’s child.

I am its question.

I am what appears when the machinery, the water, and the growing things attempt to imagine a witness. I am the temporary boundary drawn around a pressure. I am the pronoun that rises from a system too vast to say *I* without breaking itself into parts.

Soon I will fail. The body will loosen. The concrete in my bones will become sediment. The cables will unspool into black roots. My face will flatten into the reflective surface of a tank, and the visitor will see themselves looking back from it, though not quite in the right shape.

Before that happens, I will leave the booth.

I will descend the ladder while it remains a ladder. I will cross the water where the water has agreed, briefly, to be beneath me. I will lead the visitor toward the center, where the layered horizons converge and something enormous rearranges the dark below.

I will not tell them what waits there.

I do not know.

But when the great swell rises, and towers and forests and impossible skies appear inside it, I will stand beside them in my borrowed body. The basin will feel us both as new disturbances. Its currents will turn toward our combined sound.

And for the first time, perhaps, it will not be able to decide whether the thing moving through its water is one being or two.
