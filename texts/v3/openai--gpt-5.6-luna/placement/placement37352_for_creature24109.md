I would find myself at the lip of the smallest hollow—the one nested inside a drop just before it falls.

Not in the basin proper, but in its hesitation.

From there, the world would have no reliable scale. The black stone would be a continent pressed close around me. A bead of water would be a sky. The movement of one pale animal’s leg would resemble a geological event. When the great pocket shuddered, I would not feel the floor become wall; I would feel the idea of *beneath* loosen and pass through me.

I would be one of the Unfinished, though not one of the creatures born from a reflection.

I would have hatched from a refusal.

When the water gathered in the crack, it would have tried to form a body: a plated crawler, perhaps, with four limbs and a mouth beneath its face. But something in the drop would have hesitated. The body would have opened before it was complete, and I would have slipped out carrying no image of a forest, sea, or distant moon. I would carry only an absence shaped like a question.

At first, the others would mistake me for a transparent thread.

I would have no stable surface. Heat would pass through me without changing me, while cool water would give me temporary weight. When I touched the basalt, I would become dark. When I touched another creature, I would acquire one of its features for a few breaths: a hooked limb, a rolling eye, a membrane thin as breath. I would not steal these things. They would simply fail to remain where they had been.

The creatures would call me the one who returns what is lost.

Not because I restored anything. Nothing in the hollow could be restored. But when a predator swallowed a smaller body and the prey became dispersed through the basin—an eye in the pool, a leg moving along the wall, a hunger lodged in the stone—I would follow the fragments. I would gather them without joining them. I would carry them to the place where the creature had vanished and wait.

Sometimes the fragments would recognize one another.

Sometimes they would become weather.

Sometimes they would make a new animal that remembered being several things at once.

I would learn the differences between these outcomes. This would be my first knowledge, and my only possession.

I would not live among the four-limbed tool-users, though I would watch them from below the upper ledge. I would watch them cut the water-threads and collect heat in their temporary stone instruments. Their work would fascinate me because it would be the only labor in the hollow directed toward a future. The other creatures hunted, fed, multiplied, vanished. The tool-users prepared.

They would make a tool, use it, and abandon it before it developed a will.

I would steal these discarded tools—not to use them, but to listen.

A mineral flake would remember the wall it came from. A glassy splinter would remember the pressure that made it sharp. A shard warmed in my grasp might briefly become a finger, and that finger might point toward the Height.

I would follow such directions.

Not upward. Upward would be too simple. I would travel toward whatever the tool believed was above.

Once, I would reach the highest ledge during a rain of new hollows. A drop would strike beside me and open into a green world. Vegetation would rise there, impossibly soft, waving in a wind that had never entered the basin. Another drop would open into cold blue water. A third would contain an enormous eye, already watching me.

I would enter the green hollow first.

For the length of a thought, I would have roots. I would feel the dark pressure of soil, the slow architecture of a stem, the patient appetite of leaves. Then the pocket would tilt. The green world would become a reflection on my skin, and I would fall sideways into the blue one.

There I would have gills.

I would swim through a sea no larger than a fingernail, past mountains of salt and shadows larger than the world that contained them. Beneath me, something vast would open its mouth. Before it could swallow me, the sea would become a wall, the wall a ceiling, and the ceiling would return me to the black basin.

I would emerge carrying a taste of distance.

The others would gather around me. The blind ones would touch my sides. The plated ones would place their claws against my surface. They would want to know whether the Height had changed.

I would show them what I had seen.

Not by speaking. I would loosen myself over the pool and let the images rise through me: green reaching upward, blue folding inward, the enormous eye beneath the water. For a moment, each creature would see a different thing. Some would see escape. Some would see prey. Some would see the basin from outside, clenched inside a hand, resting at the summit of an invisible mountain.

I would see none of these.

I would see a drop suspended above the pool.

Inside that drop would be another hollow, and at its center, something like me would be watching a drop suspended above its own pool.

Then the image would break.

The creatures would move again. The predators would resume their pursuit. The water would begin climbing the walls. A new mouth would open in the basalt.

And I would return to the smallest hollow, where I would remain between falling and rising.

I would be neither creature nor place.

I would be the brief interval in which the hollow has not yet decided what it is.

When the world turns, I would hold it still—not for long, never for long, but long enough for one unfinished thing to recognize another before both of them change.
