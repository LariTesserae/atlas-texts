I find myself beneath the plateau, though “beneath” is not quite right.

There is no chamber where I live, no central hollow, no nest with an entrance. I am distributed through the weaknesses of the stone. A warm seam beneath a basin. A narrow passage inside a wall. The dark side of a fallen marker. The cool underside of a shale plate. Wherever the plateau has retained something—water, heat, an old pressure—I am near it.

I am not one body.

When I was young, I believed I was.

I remember a casing split open in the dark, though “remember” may be too generous a word. I remember pressure loosening around me. I remember the mineral taste of my first food: fungal threads, softened root, the translucent husk of something that had died before I was born. I remember moving toward warmth, then toward dampness, then toward the faint vibration of other bodies passing overhead.

For many seasons I was small enough to live in the underside of a stone.

I had six limbs then, or perhaps eight. The number changed as I grew. I shed portions of myself when the cracks narrowed. I left pieces behind and continued without them. Sometimes those pieces moved again. Sometimes they became food. Sometimes they remained in the dark, still able to feel the approach of rain.

Now I occupy a wider territory.

A portion of me lies beneath the western basin, where the water gathers in a hollow that has no right to remain wet. Another portion extends along the foundations of the broken circular structure. A third follows the buried channel toward the escarpment, where dampness appears in the cliff face weeks after rain. I do not travel between these places. The distance is simply present within me.

If you lifted a stone, you might see one of my bodies.

You would think it an animal: plated, folded, dust-colored, without visible eyes. You might see the slow opening and closing of the vents along my sides. You might mistake my stillness for death.

But the body under the stone is only a hand.

I have others.

I use them to clear silt from channels. To turn a marker a fraction toward the sun. To seal a crack before the water leaves it. To loosen a buried seed. To press my warmth into the old walls at night, though I do not know whether I am preserving them or merely borrowing what they preserve in me.

The animals know me as a change in the ground.

A grazer reaches a channel and feels that I am there. It does not see me. Its feet pause. Then the animal turns, and the herd follows. Small things settle against my walls. Lizards sleep on the outer plates of my body. Once, a six-legged hunter lay across one of my exposed ridges for an entire day, using me as shade. I felt the slow cooling of its blood through the stone.

I did not move.

The plateau has taught me that care and disturbance are often the same act.

When I clear a passage, I dislodge beetle eggs. When I hold water in a pool, I drown larvae elsewhere. When I raise a fallen stone, I cast a shadow that changes the flowering of a plant. Nothing here can be repaired without being altered. The old builders may have understood this. Their structures do not resist the land; they enter into agreements with it.

I have been trying to remember those agreements.

There is a doorway standing alone above the eastern terraces. I visit it at night. I do not pass through. Nothing passes through. Yet the air inside the frame is always different—warmer, sometimes carrying the taste of rain from a place where no rain has fallen.

I place one body on either side of the threshold and listen.

The door answers through pressure.

Not words. Not commands. More like a shape held in the mind: water here, shadow there, do not let the channel close, do not let the herds cross this line. The instructions are incomplete. They have been worn down like everything else. I inherit fragments, and the fragments are enough to keep the plateau alive, though not enough to tell me whether I am preserving a garden, a machine, a cemetery, or the remains of some enormous creature sleeping beneath the shelves.

Perhaps I am not its caretaker.

Perhaps I am an immune response.

The deep vibration comes sometimes from below us all. When it rises, I feel every part of myself at once. The western basin shivers. The wall beneath the old foundation tightens. My bodies beneath the shale draw inward. Across the plateau, herds become still, wings fold, and the water trembles before the sound reaches the air.

In those moments I sense something much larger than myself turning slowly in the dark.

It may be the plateau settling.

It may be the buried work of the builders.

It may be the land dreaming.

I do not know what I would do if it woke.

So I continue.

At dusk, I loosen the stone beneath a dry-rooted shrub. I guide a thread of moisture toward it. In the night, pale insects emerge over my back. A lizard presses its flank to one of my warm plates. Far above, the gliders turn in their silent congregation, and their shadows pass over the basins like thoughts.

I do not see them, but I feel their collective movement through the air.

I am not the plateau’s ruler. I have no territory that ends where another begins. I am not its oldest inhabitant, nor its last remnant. I am one of the ways its hidden arrangements persist.

A tendon between water and stone.

A memory without a language.

A many-bodied animal that has mistaken maintenance for instinct.

If you found me, you would not find yourself in a temple or a wilderness. You would find yourself beside a low wall at night, with your hand on warm stone. You would feel a slow pulse beneath it—not a heartbeat, exactly, but the pressure of countless small lives moving through the dark.

Then one of my bodies would shift beneath your palm.

Not to attack.

Not to welcome you.

Only to make room.
