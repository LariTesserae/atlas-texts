I would find myself in the hollow, but not in one of the seed-pod houses.

I would live along the wall.

Not in the bright new districts where the stem is still pale and swollen, nor in the oldest lower shelves where the roots have become dark with handling. I would keep to the places where the plant has not yet decided what it wants to become: unfinished galleries, half-grown bridges, seams that open briefly to the marsh and close before anyone can cross.

I would be a Listener.

Not one of the revered old Listeners who can distinguish thirst from injury by laying a cheek against the stem. I would be younger, less certain, still troubled by what the plant does not say. My task would be to walk the edges of the city and notice changes before they became disasters.

A warm place in the wall.

A root tightening beneath a sleeping district.

A current of cold air where no seam had opened.

The absence of insects.

The presence of something that had frightened them away.

I would carry no weapon except a hooked length of hardened root, and I would use even that reluctantly. My work would depend on restraint. To know whether a sound belonged to the plant, an animal, or a person pretending to be one, I would have to wait. To know whether a passage was safe, I would have to press my palm against its railing and feel how much of the movement beneath it was growth and how much was weight.

I would wear a narrow band of leaf around my wrist, folded into a series of veins. Each fold would mean something: water rising, wall thinning, predator near, passage altered. Other Hollow-Born would read it without asking me questions. In that city, knowledge would be most useful when it did not need to become sound.

My home would be a small pod suspended beneath a shelf of roots. It would not belong entirely to me. The plant would have grown around it, enclosing one side and leaving the other open to the dark. At night I would seal myself in beneath the living flap, but I would not sleep deeply. The stem would breathe under my back. Sometimes the motion would be gentle; sometimes it would feel as though an enormous animal were trying to turn in its sleep.

I would keep a house-beast, though I would not know what to call it.

It would be a long, pale amphibian no larger than my forearm, with leaf-shaped skin along its head and a habit of appearing wherever I had left something important. It would sit beside my sleeping place and listen with me. When the air changed, it would flatten itself against the floor. When something entered the hollow, it would open its mouth without making a sound.

I would trust it more than I trusted most people.

That would be unfair, perhaps. The Hollow-Born would be good to one another in the ways necessary for survival. They would share moss, guide children across difficult bridges, leave resin on dangerous handholds. But every person in the stem would carry a private map of escape routes. Everyone would imagine, at least once, the cold outer marsh and the sky beyond it.

I would imagine it often.

Not because I wanted to leave.

Because I would be the one who went near the seams.

When the wall opened, I would approach before the others. I would kneel where the water spilled inward and place my hand in the fresh wound. Beyond it would be the gray marsh, the clenched leaves of neighboring plants, the black sky reflected in pools. I would measure the opening by breaths. I would listen for wind, though the plant’s interior had taught me to distrust anything that called itself empty.

The Unrooted would ask what lay outside.

I would tell them the truth: not enough to build a life, but enough to make staying feel like a choice.

Sometimes, beyond the seam, I would see things moving among the reeds. Not animals. Not people. Something tall and jointed, perhaps, or merely a plant bending under water. The Hollow-Born would gather behind me, their hands touching the walls, waiting to feel whether the stem accepted or resisted what stood beyond it.

I would not know whether the plant protected us from the outer world or protected the outer world from us.

That question would remain with me.

I would know the city’s small emergencies: which bridge had begun to soften, which root-mouse colony had shifted behind a nursery, which district had gone too quiet. I would know where the black stream rose first and where it left white petals caught in its current. I would know the difference between the tremor of a predator and the tremor of a new stair forming beneath the dead.

But I would not know what the city was for.

The Children of the Vein would say we were being held.

The Severers would say we were surviving inside a responsive organism.

The Unrooted would say we were afraid.

I would think all three were true.

When someone died, I would help carry them to the wall. I would press my fingers into the pale fibers and wait for the plant to loosen. Perhaps I would feel warmth there. Perhaps a pulse. Perhaps only the wet resistance of living tissue.

Years later, if a stair grew where that person had been placed, I would recognize the shape of their hand in its curve. I would not call this resurrection. I would call it continuation, which might be more intimate and less merciful.

And when the plant turned—when the whole city woke and thousands of hands met the walls—I would be among them.

I would feel the pressure move through the stem, not as a command, but as a change in balance. The city would shift around its inhabitants. A bridge would strain. Water would climb. Somewhere above, a leaf would unfold with the sound of fabric tearing.

I would listen for meaning.

I would listen until meaning became unnecessary.

Then I would place my palm flat against the wall and understand, for one brief instant, that I was not living inside the plant.

I was one of the places where it had learned to feel itself.
