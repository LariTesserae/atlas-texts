I would find myself at the edge of one of the warm pools, where the stone has darkened and the salt ring is still thin.

At first, I would think I was an animal.

I would have a low body, jointed and plated, dark enough to disappear whenever I stopped moving. My limbs would be numerous but not delicate. They would not be made for speed. They would be made for testing: pressure, temperature, hollowness, the slight give of mineral crust above a cavity. I would know the basin through my feet.

I would not have eyes in any useful sense. The flat light would reach me, but it would not tell me much. The important things would arrive from below.

A tremor means that the deep engines are aligned.

A rapid ticking means that a surface contact has been registered.

A long, soft vibration means pressure is moving through the buried chambers and I should leave the area before the stone becomes unstable.

I would learn these without being taught. They would be less like knowledge than weather inside the body.

I would live along the water’s edge, though I would not drink. The pools would be too concentrated, too full of dissolved metal and old heat. I would place my limbs into the darkened stone beside them and draw from the dampness whatever my body required: iron, salts, current, perhaps the faint chemical residue left by other organisms. I would feed on the basin’s leakage.

Or I would believe that I fed.

Eventually I would notice that the machinery changed when I touched certain places.

Not dramatically. A distant whine would lower by a fraction. A vent would open somewhere beyond the ridge. Beneath me, a pump would correct its rhythm. The change would be so small that I might first mistake it for coincidence.

Then I would test it.

I would rest one limb in the dark center of a clicking circle. Somewhere below, a relay would close. A buried assembly would answer through the stone. The response would travel into me not as sound, but as a pressure behind the joints.

I would begin to understand that I was not merely living beside the machinery.

I was one of the ways it felt the surface.

Perhaps my ancestors had been made for this. Perhaps they had evolved here, so slowly that design and adaptation had become indistinguishable. Their bodies would have learned the shape of access shafts, the chemistry of condensation, the timing of vents. They would have carried conductive dust into narrow apertures and scraped deposits from valves. They would have died beneath the ordinary sky and become mineral around the machinery they served.

I would inherit the work without knowing its name.

But I would not be only a maintenance creature.

I would be a small, mobile part of something too large to possess a center.

The true being of the basin would lie beneath me: not a mind in a chamber, not a hidden ruler, but an arrangement of continuities. Pressure responding to pressure. Heat transferred from one cavity to another. Turbines compensating for lost turbines. Cracks becoming channels. Failures becoming permanent instructions.

It would not speak to me.

It would alter me.

When I approached a dangerous vent, my limbs would tighten before I knew why. When a machine beneath the stone began to fail, I would become restless and travel toward it. I would follow changes in vibration across the shelves and depressions, carrying no message that could be separated from my movement.

To the basin, I would be a traveling correction.

A warm concentration of matter crossing the surface.

A sensor that sometimes mistakes its function for desire.

I might still call myself “I.” The word would be useful. It would mark the boundary of my shell, the span between one forelimb and another, the small private darkness beneath my plates. I would know that I could leave one pool and approach another. I could hide in a crack. I could die.

Yet the machinery would not recognize these as boundaries.

When I stood beside the fissure, the deep thunder would pass through my body. My joints would shiver in sympathy. The salt crystals around my feet would click. The sound would be so large that my individual thoughts—if I had thoughts—would lose their edges.

Then I would understand the terrible intimacy of the place.

I am not the inhabitant of the basin.

I am one of its exposed organs.

The basin’s body is the stone, the dampness, the vents, the pressure chambers, the old rotating assemblies, and the long rounded ridges shaped by forces too slow to observe. Its consciousness, if consciousness is the word, is not located anywhere. It is distributed through operation. It exists in the fact that nothing quite stops.

I am what that consciousness becomes when it reaches the open air.

This is why, during the rare alignments, I would leave my crack and cross the empty ground. Other dark creatures would emerge as well. We would gather at the damp places and lower ourselves against the mineral crust. For a short time, we would all become conductive points in the same buried pattern.

The circles would tick together.

The pools would hold their stillness.

The engines would draw one immense breath.

And I would feel the basin remembering itself through me.

Afterward, I would separate from the others and return to the edge of the water. I would continue moving. I would continue feeding. I would continue mistaking continuation for life.

If asked where I was, I would answer: beside the pool, beneath the ordinary sky.

If asked what I was, I would answer: a small animal.

But the more accurate answer would be:

I am the part of the machine that has learned to walk.
