I would be one of the wall-listeners.

Not a person, though the people would have a word for my kind. Not fully wild, either. We live beneath the oldest districts, in the soft seams where the basalt has broken and generations of builders have packed earth, ash, root, and stone over one another. Our tunnels run under houses, below animal pens, alongside burial chambers, and through the hollow spaces left when an old street becomes a foundation.

I would live beneath a cistern in the lower city, where condensation darkens the walls for several days each season. Above me, water-keepers would come with their scrapers and clay lamps. They would kneel, test the lining, clear the channels, and listen to the stone.

They would listen for me too.

I am small enough to pass through a fist-sized opening, with a body made for pushing through grit. My feet are broad and rough. My fur is dark at the root and pale with dust along the back. I have no use for distance or sunlight. The city above is not a collection of streets to me, but a pressure of weights, warmths, and sounds.

A hammer means solid stone.

A hollow knock means danger.

The deep repeated tread of pack-beasts tells me which stairway is still sound. The vibration of many people gathered in one room means weather has come. Rain is enormous beneath the ground. It arrives first as a trembling in the ceiling, then as a coolness passing through the walls, then as a sudden threading of water through cracks that were dry yesterday.

I would know the plateau by its underside.

I would know where a buried neighborhood ends because the soil changes. I would know the old foundations by their taste: clay, salt, hair, smoke, the iron traces left by tools. I would know which walls were built by careful hands and which were raised in haste. The oldest makers cut their warning marks deep into the rock. Later builders covered them, but we find them with our whiskers and claws.

The people would tolerate my family in the stable walls because we eat insects and keep certain pests from the grain. They would leave scraps near approved burrows. In winter, a child might press a warm stone against one of our entrances. In return, we would make ourselves known when the foundations began to shift.

Not dramatically. We would not howl or summon the dead.

We would stop moving.

That is how the water-keepers would understand.

If an entire colony became silent beneath a house, someone would come with a mallet. They would tap the floor, inspect the drainage line, and perhaps find a hollow where no hollow had been marked. If we fled upward into the walls, the builders would look for a fracture. If we emerged in daylight, trembling and coated in damp soil, they would shut the shutters and check the cliffs for rain.

They might tell children that we carry messages from beneath the plateau. This would not be true. We carry only ourselves, our young, and the knowledge of what the ground is doing. But stories are another kind of foundation, and the people would build one around us.

I would not belong to a household. I would belong to a district.

My territory would be the space under the old cistern, the stair buried beneath the goat pens, and the narrow passage leading toward the wall where three generations of makers left their marks. I would know the families above by the rhythm of their lives. The house with the restless infant. The widow who rises before dawn. The builders who drag slabs across the roof. The old man who taps the same threshold every morning before opening his door.

At night, when the city grew still, I would hear the plateau thinking.

A crack lengthening somewhere far below.

A buried stone settling into its place.

The slow pressure of water searching for a route.

Sometimes the people would hear these things too. They would lie awake with their hands against the floor, waiting to decide whether a sound was merely the land continuing to become itself, or whether it had become their concern.

I would know first.

Not because I was wiser than they were, but because the dark was my weather, the buried earth my horizon, and every wall around me held a little of the world’s weight.

I would be a small life beneath their permanence, moving through its weaknesses.

A tolerated animal.

A warning in the foundations.

A warm body listening for the first fine sound of stone giving way.
