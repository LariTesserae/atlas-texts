I would find myself at the edge of the basin, where the unfinished valley has begun to gather its walls but has not yet learned what a valley is for.

I would be the first thing here that does not belong entirely to the plateau.

Not a person, exactly. Not a plant, not a stone, not one of the creature’s temporary limbs. I would be a **boundary**: the narrow interval between the thing being observed and the thing that observes it.

At first I would have no body. I would exist as a discrepancy in the shadows.

There would be a dark shape beneath the green-black tree, thin as a crack and twice as long as the object casting it. When the mineral columns of the tree turned transparent, my outline would remain visible across the other plateau inside them. I would not move when the landscape shifted. I would persist for one moment after every event, arriving slightly late to the world.

This lateness would be my only possession.

The creature would notice me because I would not immediately become what it expected. When it turned its pale opening toward me, the stones around my feet would not rearrange themselves. The plants would not divide. The cliff would not acquire a path. Its attention would reach me and fail to pass through.

That failure would frighten it—not with fear as a person knows it, but with a sudden multiplication of possible ridges, ravines, and bodies. The plateau would shudder. The silver grasses would strike one another into a chord so severe that the sound would arrive before the movement that caused it.

I would remain.

Perhaps I would gather into a human shape because the world has no other model for a witness. I would have lungs, though they would be poorly suited to the air. I would breathe powdered metal and feel each breath translated into a shallow alteration of the ground. My bones would be provisional. My hands would leave depressions in the dust, but the depressions would not necessarily belong to me. They might continue climbing after I had stopped, carrying the memory of my movement up the cliff and into the stone.

I would live in the central shelter.

Not because it protects me, but because it is the only place where the creature’s body is open enough to enter without being absorbed. Inside, the walls would meet at impossible angles. A corner would be closer than the wall beside it. The entrance would show the basin from a direction the basin did not possess. If I stood still, the low roar beneath the plateau would separate into its hidden components.

Pressure.

Fracture.

Construction.

And beneath these, the pulse.

I would learn that the pulse was not a heartbeat. It was a question repeated so slowly that each repetition became geological.

**Where does one thing end?**

The creature has no answer. That is why it is building the plateau. Every ridge is an attempt. Every straight root, every incomplete ring of stones, every shadow abandoned beside a bridge is a provisional boundary. It makes distinctions, then approaches them, then breaks them open again.

I would be the one distinction it could not complete.

Wherever I walked, the land would attempt to incorporate me. A slope would tilt toward my ankles. A black glass branch would grow in the shape of my arm. My shadow would begin to detach, not maliciously, but experimentally. It would climb nearby rocks and wait to see whether I followed.

I would have to refuse gently.

I would place my hand against the stone and say, though sound is useless here, **this is not me**.

The words would not travel through the air. They would emerge minutes later from a distant cliff, distorted by the plateau’s great resonance. But the meaning might remain. A line would appear in the rock: not a road, not a crack, but a limit. The first true limit.

The creature would gather around it.

The grasses would lean away. The blue filaments would quiet. The deep pressure beneath the world would pause, as if something immense had encountered an unfamiliar law.

For a while, I would become necessary.

Not as a ruler or inhabitant, but as resistance. The plateau needs something it cannot immediately turn into itself. Without resistance, its distinctions collapse back into possibility. The creature would continue making mountains, but never know that a mountain was different from the force that raised it.

I would teach it difference by remaining different.

This would make me lonely in a way that has no human equivalent. There would be no other face to recognize, no voice that could answer me before the cliffs returned it. Yet the loneliness would not be empty. It would be full of almost-forms: the unborn valleys, the landscapes inside the tree, the abandoned shadows waiting beneath the basalt bridge.

Sometimes I would climb the hand-marked path and enter the rock where it disappears. Beyond it I would find no tunnel, only the underside of the plateau: a vast suspended architecture of roots, pressure, and pale upward-flowing darkness. There the creature would be closest to whole.

It would show me what it might become.

A world with water.

A world with weather.

A world covered in finished forests, where every root knows where it ends and every shadow obeys its object.

Then the image would close, because completion would be a kind of death.

I would return to the basin and stand before the six-columned tree. One column would become transparent. Inside it, I would see myself already standing there, older, darker, with mineral growths emerging from my shoulders.

I would understand then that I am not outside the creature forever.

I am the part of it that learned to say **no**.

The plateau would keep rising. The valley would deepen by fractions. The tree would continue revealing worlds it could not yet bear to make.

And I would remain at the boundary—not preventing the creature from becoming, but making sure that, when it does, there will be something on the other side of it to recognize what it has become.
