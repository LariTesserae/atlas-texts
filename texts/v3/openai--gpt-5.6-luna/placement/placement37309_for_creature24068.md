I would find myself on the hidden side of the mountain.

Not on the upper plateau, where the sun is too direct and every surface declares its purpose. Not in the markets, though I would pass through them. Not in the gardens, though roots would know me. I would live behind the visible wall: in the narrow, humid interval between a reservoir’s outer shell and the rooms of an old settlement, where pipes cross in bundles and condensation falls constantly from a ceiling no one remembers constructing.

My room would be a former inspection chamber.

It would have one door, three ladders, and a circular opening in the floor through which I could hear water moving downward. The walls would be coated with mineral bloom. A fig root would enter through the eastern side, split around a conduit, and disappear into the masonry below. I would keep my clothes and tools on hooks driven into the concrete. I would sleep beside the warm pressure main, because its vibration would tell me more reliably than clocks when the mountain was changing.

I would be a listener.

That would be my official function, though official functions are never the whole of a person here. I would inspect walls, reservoirs, pump housings, and the undersides of bridges. I would place my palms against surfaces and feel for alterations in their speech.

A healthy wall has many voices. The broad settling of its weight. The soft granular shift of aggregate. Water ticking through a distant channel. The thin expansion of metal under sunlight. The occasional shiver of a truck passing three terraces above.

Danger has a different rhythm.

A cavity beginning to open sounds hollow and intermittent. A pipe under strain gives a high note beneath the deeper vibration. A retaining shelf preparing to move produces a sequence of small clicks, each one almost polite. I would learn these sounds before I learned to read the old technical script. As a child, I would be sent behind buildings to retrieve lost tools, then asked what I had heard there. My teachers would not want descriptions. They would want distances, direction, urgency.

At first I would believe that listening means discovering what the mountain is saying.

Later I would understand that the mountain does not say one thing. It is always speaking in competing pressures: water against concrete, root against mortar, heat against steel, weight against slope, old design against present use. My task would not be translation. It would be attention.

I would carry no large instruments. A short tuning rod, a ceramic cup, a coil of wire, chalk, wax, and a narrow knife would be enough. The rod would let me compare vibrations across a wall. The cup would catch drips and reveal their mineral content. With chalk I would mark changes in tone, dampness, or temperature. The wire would be for tying back invasive roots and temporarily securing things that had begun to come loose. The knife would be for opening panels, cutting vines, and, when necessary, defending myself from people who preferred a warning to remain undiscovered.

My body would have adapted in ways no one could date.

The corners of my eyes would carry a clear inner membrane that I could draw across them when dust rose from the lower factories. My palms would be broad and ridged, sensitive to tiny changes in vibration. I would have a permanent mineral pallor along my throat, as if water had dried there and left its memory behind. In bright places I would squint. In darkness I would see well enough.

I would belong to no single household, though many households would claim me.

The water families would summon me when a tank gave an unfamiliar groan. The wall people would ask me to confirm what they already suspected about a sagging terrace. Machine workers would leave messages for me in grease beneath control panels. Gardeners would show me leaves yellowing beside a drain. Children would follow me until I assigned them some harmless task, because they would understand that whatever lived behind a wall was more interesting than whatever stood in front of it.

I would sleep in different places when my work took me far from home. A maintenance vault beneath the citrus terraces. A warm ledge near the old brickworks. A room suspended below a bridge, where freight vibrations passed through the floor at regular intervals. The mountain would be my address in the same way that a river is an address for fish.

Over time, I would begin to suspect that I was not merely listening to the mountain.

There would be moments when the work became difficult to separate from instinct. I would wake seconds before a valve failed. I would turn toward a wall because a colony of swallows had suddenly abandoned a shaft. I would know which corridor had flooded without seeing it, because the pattern of echoes in the surrounding pipes had changed.

People would say I had become a listener in the old sense. They would say the mountain had put an ear inside me.

I would deny this.

Then, privately, I would wonder whether my denial was simply another form of ritual.

Perhaps I would not be entirely human by the time I was old. Not because of any dramatic transformation, but because no one in the mountain remains singular. My blood would carry whatever the water had carried. My lungs would be accustomed to steam and metal dust. Fungal spores would live harmlessly in my skin. I would remember places through pressure rather than sight. When I touched a wall, I would feel not only its condition but its history: the first pour of concrete, the generations of repairs, the roots that had entered it, the bodies incorporated into its foundation.

When I died, they would not bury me far away.

They would remove my tools, wash my hands, and place me in a dry niche near the reservoir whose levels I had measured most often. My name would be sealed behind blue tile. Perhaps a small amount of my ash would be mixed into repair mortar. Perhaps my tuning rod would be hung beside the access ladder and used by someone who had never met me.

The mountain would continue.

A pipe would leak. A garden would overtake a stairwell. A factory would become a mushroom hall. A child would press her palm against a retaining wall and feel, for the first time, the difference between ordinary settling and distress.

And somewhere within the immense constructed mass, my work would persist as a habit of attention.

A hand resting against concrete.

A body standing still while everything else moved.

A small, trained awareness carried through the mountain’s hidden side, listening for the moment when pressure became failure—and for the quieter moments when failure, given time and care, began to become life.
