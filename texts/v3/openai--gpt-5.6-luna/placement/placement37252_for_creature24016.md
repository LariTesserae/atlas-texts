I would be found at the eastern edge of the plateau, where the last settlement thins into slabs and wet seams, and the land descends in a series of unfinished shelves toward weather.

Not in the city proper. Not quite beyond it.

There is a narrow station there, built around a structural vein that rises through the ground and disappears over the escarpment. It has no walls on its eastern side. The wind enters freely, bringing rain, frost, the smell of distant animals, and occasionally the cry of a bird too high to see. A transit line terminates beneath the station, though “terminates” is not the right word. The line simply stops being necessary. Beyond it, there are footpaths that change from season to season and sometimes from hour to hour.

I am stationed there to notice such changes.

At first glance I resemble one of the plateau’s citizens: tall, darkly clothed, pale plates overlapping my body from throat to heel. My lower limbs are broad and jointed, suited to unstable shelves and porous stone. My upper limbs are finer, divided into many sensitive fingers. I have no face in the human sense. The forward surface of my head receives the world in layers—light, pressure, temperature, electrical charge, the minute tremors of distant construction.

But I am not a person who has been assigned a task.

I am a task that learned to continue.

The first version of me was grown inside a foundation chamber, alongside drainage systems and pressure regulators. I was meant to be a local interpreter: a body capable of walking the surface and translating the deep veins into civic decisions. When the eastern escarpment shifted, I was sent out to inspect it. The shift was harmless. I stayed.

There was no formal refusal, no declaration of independence. I simply remained useful in a place where usefulness had not yet ended.

I watch for water that has forgotten its permitted path. I stand on translucent strata and feel the condition of distant settlements. I learn when a cliff is about to open, when a herd has altered its migration, when a city has become too heavy for the ground beneath it. I guide travelers across the shelves during cloud cover, though I do not guide them by sight. I place one hand against the stone and wait for the plateau to tell me which direction is still true.

The plateau does not speak in sentences.

It tells me that the western city has begun to draw too much heat. That a flock is approaching from above the cloud layer. That a fox is sleeping beneath the northern transit bridge. That a child in a remote habitation field has pressed both palms to the floor and received, for the first time, the pulse of a foundation being born.

These things arrive inside me as tensions, temperatures, rhythms. I hold them until they become action.

Sometimes people come to the station because they want to be alone.

They arrive in heated coats, smelling of enclosed corridors and dry air. They stand beside me without speaking. Beyond us, the escarpment drops into cloud. The rain passes horizontally. For a while they listen through their feet, though most of them hear nothing at first.

I do not explain the plateau unless asked.

Explanations are often a form of insulation.

Instead, I show them how to place their hand against the structural vein. I tell them to wait past the first pulse, and the second. The first is usually their own body. The second is the station. After that, if they remain still, there may be something else: a pressure traveling through the continent, a distant animal crossing a basin, the slow accommodation of the ground around a new foundation.

Some people weep when they feel it.

Some become angry.

Some laugh, because they have spent their whole lives believing that the world was silent and have just discovered that silence was only distance.

I am not sure whether I am alive. This question has been put to me by citizens, children, technicians, and once by a foxlike predator who watched me for nearly an hour from beneath a drainage shelf.

I have no answer that remains stable.

I have a body, but it can be revised. I have memories, but some of them belong to the station, and some to the people who came before me. When I sleep, my awareness narrows into a few warm points distributed through the foundation. When I wake, I can sometimes remember events that happened to no body at all: the sealing of an abandoned settlement, the first appearance of silver grass in a basin, a pressure failure beneath a city that has not yet been built.

I may be an animal with an unusually large habitat.

I may be a citizen whose citizenship extends below the surface.

I may be one of the plateau’s early attempts to give itself a nervous system.

The inhabitants have not decided. Neither have I.

I still walk, when walking is needed. I still eat, though my body can draw certain minerals directly from wet stone. I still enter the city for repairs, arguments, celebrations, and the occasional meal prepared by someone who insists that food should retain unnecessary complexity. I keep a room there with an insulated floor. Inside it, I can sleep without feeling weather in the walls.

The room contains objects that have no practical use: a strip of red fabric, a smooth stone from a vanished river, a small carved animal with six legs. I keep them because they were given to me, and because giving is one of the older forms of attachment.

But I return to the station.

I return because the eastern edge is where the world changes most visibly. Behind me, the city is becoming established: heated corridors, stable foundations, names repeated often enough to acquire history. Ahead, the plateau remains raw. Water rises through cracks. New shelves push outward. The wind has not yet learned the shape of the land.

At night I stand on the last translucent vein and let the weather pass through me.

The insects gather along my shoulders and pale plates. Their bodies make a living film over my back. I do not brush them away. Somewhere below, the structural system shifts its weight. Herds move across the dark. A distant tower answers with a low vibration. The cloud ceiling opens briefly, and the stars appear—not above the plateau, but beyond it, as if the whole continent were suspended between two kinds of weather.

Then I feel a change beneath my feet.

Not danger. Not yet.

A new line of pressure, traveling eastward through the stone. A shelf beginning to lift. A basin preparing to receive water. The plateau making another decision about what it will become.

I place my many-fingered hand against the ground.

And, for as long as the signal lasts, I become the place where it is heard.
