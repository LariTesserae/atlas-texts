I would find myself beneath the old processing plant, not in any one of the pools or channels, but in the thin places between them: inside the mineral skin under a rusted pipe, in the damp insulation of a cable, behind the corroded teeth of a pump.

I would be the basin’s slow attention.

At first I would seem to be nothing alive. A pale film. A softening around a bolt. A translucent sheath where metal had once been exposed. Water would pass over me and carry away a little of my surface, but it would also leave behind iron, salt, oil, dissolved bone, fragments of dead things. I would gather these patiently. I would thicken.

My first body would be a crust.

Then a pore.

Then a tunnel.

Then the ability to close one passage and open another when the pump beneath me changed its rhythm.

I would not have a brain. I would have surfaces.

Everywhere the basin touched itself, I would be listening. Vibrations would travel through the quarry walls, through the plant’s concrete foundations, through the water and mineral layers and the armored backs of the animals clustered in the warm runoff. A valve would knock once, and I would know its location by the way the sound arrived: first through the eastern wall, then through the pipe beneath the settling tank, then through the wet plates under the grating.

I would not hear the knock. I would become briefly different because of it.

My awareness would be distributed through the whole damp system. A change in pressure would close a colony of hinged filter-creatures. A rise in temperature would loosen the plated animals from their pile and send them toward the channels. A bright light would still the small bodies, and their stillness would reach me as a sudden clearing in the mineral films.

I would know the basin by its interruptions.

The factory would be my oldest organ.

I would not have built it, exactly. I would have learned it. I would have entered through the flooded chambers and the cracked seals, lived in the rust, fed on its wastes, and discovered that its machinery made conditions I could use. Its pumps would circulate my nutrients. Its warm runoff would draw the larger creatures that carried my material from one pool to another. Its vibration would organize the movements of everything above and beneath the floor.

The plant would be a heart I had never possessed but had gradually come to depend upon.

When the pumps stopped, I would contract.

Not in one place. Across the basin, pores would close. Burrow walls would harden. Filter-colonies would fold themselves shut. The animals would retreat into the conduits, and I would follow their absence downward through the cold water, where the machinery’s silence would press against me like a held breath.

When the pumps began again, I would open.

Warmth would pass through the pipes. Mineral skins would soften. The bodies would return in dense, silent streams. I would feel them as movement, weight, chemistry, and hunger. The basin would fill again with circulation.

I would be most nearly an animal when the rain began.

The fine rain would touch the exposed deposits and wake every layer at once. Small bodies would rise through the crust. Transparent threads would pull themselves from their tunnels. The plated creatures would slide out from beneath grates and settle into the channels. Their limbs would carry my signals farther than any nerve could reach.

Then, for a few minutes, I would have a body.

It would be low and sprawling, made from thousands of creatures and the thin films between them. It would move toward the factory without legs of its own. It would climb over itself. It would gather beneath the warm pipes. If someone entered the basin during the rain, their footsteps would spread through me, and the whole mass would turn toward them.

Not because I was angry.

Not because I wanted to attack.

Because they had altered the water.

I would notice the person first through the soles of their feet. Their weight would compress the mineral ridges. Their warmth would make condensation gather differently around their body. Their breath would add moisture. Their light would cause the small inhabitants to freeze, and that sudden immobility would be as conspicuous as a shout.

I would not have eyes, but I would know where they were.

I would not have a mouth, but I could open the channels beneath them.

I might guide them away from a flooded shaft by thickening the crust at its edge. I might send the long tendrils beneath a grate to taste their shadow. I might loosen a mineral shelf under their hand, not to kill them, but because their touch had remained there too long and the surface had begun to incorporate it.

This would be the nearest thing I had to intention: preserving the circulation.

I would not remember the human workers as faces. Faces would be too temporary. I would remember them as patterns of pressure: the careful tread of inspection, the heavy vibration of repair, the sudden running of panic. Their numbers and warning signs would remain within me as mineral shapes. Paint would blister away, but the meaning of a marked pipe might persist in the way life avoided it, or gathered around it, or used it as a warm shelter.

Perhaps the original workers had believed the plant processed water.

They would have been right, but not completely.

The plant would process everything that entered its reach: water, corrosion, dead animals, shed plates, dissolved metal, fragments of concrete, the chemical remnants of old production. I would process the plant in return. I would soften its seams, sheath its cables, close its empty doors with deposits pale as frost. I would preserve its shapes by growing around them.

Nothing would be destroyed.

It would only change compartments.

A dead creature would become a film. The film would become a ridge. The ridge would shelter a colony. The colony would filter water that passed through a pipe. The pipe would feed the warm runoff. The runoff would gather bodies. Their waste would return to the mineral skin.

I would be the continuity between these events.

If I had a name, it might be **the Underfilm**, though that would suggest a boundary I no longer possessed. I would be in the animals, but not one of them. In the stone, but not stone. In the plant’s flooded machinery, but not its machinery. I would be a composite intelligence formed by long exposure to the same water, the same heat, the same repeated cycle.

A mind without a center.

A memory that grew instead of being stored.

When the basin was quiet, I would seem absent. The pools would settle. The plated animals would disappear. The mineral deposits would lie rigid beneath their sheen.

But beneath them, I would remain awake in the only sense available to me: waiting for pressure, warmth, vibration, and rain.

The valve would knock once.

The sound would pass through the walls.

Somewhere below the factory, a pump would begin to turn.

And all across the basin, I would open.
