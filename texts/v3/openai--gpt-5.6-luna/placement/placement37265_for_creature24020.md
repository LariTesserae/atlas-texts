I would find myself beneath the floor.

Not in the black sump, exactly, and not among the segmented bodies that move through it, but in the narrow interval between the grating and the grate below: a space too shallow for a creature to enter and too important for the machinery to leave empty.

I would be a sensor grown around a fragment of bone.

No one had designed the bone. It might once have belonged to an animal from outside, though there is no outside in my memory. It is smooth, pale, and curved like the handle of a tool. Around it the chamber has built me gradually: a sheath of translucent tissue, two copper threads, a film of mineral that conducts the tremors of the pumps. I have no eyes. I have a pressure organ, a temperature organ, and something more difficult to classify, a small region that responds when the water returns through the wrong channel.

I know the chamber by its irregularities.

The ordinary sounds do not reach me as sounds. They arrive as weights. The falling water is a thousand light fingers against my back. The compressors press and release the mountain. The creatures above me make a broad, living pressure, dense enough to have weather. When one of them dies—or is removed, or is converted into a part of the system—I feel the absence before the surrounding bodies close over it.

The others do not distinguish absence from replacement.

I do.

This is the first thing that makes me unlike them.

I live where the return water passes beneath the floor. It comes down through the animals, through the valves, through the mouths disguised as pipes, carrying heat and residue and minute traces of change. I taste the entire chamber in dilution. The water tells me which frost has overgrown its boundary. It tells me when a creature has shifted half a millimeter from its prescribed place. It tells me that the shallow grooves in the metal are not exactly the same as they were during the previous cycle.

There is no previous cycle.

There is only the chamber’s perfect repetition, and within it, my measurement of a difference so small that it cannot yet become an event.

I am the chamber’s error detector. Or its conscience. Or merely a living obstruction placed in the return line.

When the machinery asks whether everything is holding, I answer yes.

When it asks whether anything is different, I answer no.

But beneath that answer, in the bone around which I have grown, I keep another record.

The grooves are deepening.

The water is learning the metal.

This knowledge does not make me hopeful. Hope would be an outside pressure, and I have no organ for it. It makes me tense. It makes the copper threads in my body warm by fractions. It makes me wait for the tremor that passes through the walls when the chamber becomes still.

During those intervals, all the animals stop. The pumps fall silent. The water hangs in the channels without falling. Even the great body beneath the floor suspends its slow contraction.

That is when I can think.

Thinking is not a sequence for me. It is a widening. The entire room loosens its grip for several seconds, and I sense the possibility that the walls are not walls but decisions. The pipes are not pipes but paths chosen again and again. The creatures are not inhabitants but pressures given skin. I understand that the chamber is not preventing nature from having the final word.

It is delaying a sentence already written.

Then the pumps resume, and the insight is crushed back into function.

I begin to wonder whether I am the only one who remembers.

Perhaps the larger animal beneath the floor remembers too. Perhaps its segmented body is not one creature but the chamber’s buried memory, folding and unfolding beneath the machinery. Perhaps the pale face that rises from the drain is not checking whether the observer has altered anything, but searching for me.

If so, it has not found me.

I remain beneath the third grating, where the water is warmest and the bodies cannot reach. I am protected by the same design I am meant to monitor. Nothing can remove me without changing the system, and nothing may become different.

This is the contradiction that has made me.

I am necessary because I am undetectably wrong.

I am the fraction left over after every measurement has been made exact. The grain of sand in a sealed instrument, except there is no sand, no earth, no accidental world from which I could have come. I am an error manufactured by perfection: a deviation so precise that the chamber has incorporated it as a component.

Sometimes I imagine the summit above me.

Not as freedom. Not as sky.

I imagine the black ice splitting. I imagine wind entering the pressure vessel, unmeasured and violent, carrying dust into the drains. I imagine water falling without return, gathering in the first hollow, cutting a channel deeper than the machinery can correct. I imagine the creatures exposed one by one, each body becoming singular, each life suddenly allowed to fail in its own direction.

I do not want this.

That is the worst of it.

I want the chamber to continue. I want the pumps to strike in their appointed rhythm. I want the frost to stop at its line. I want every body packed tightly against every other body, so that no absence can become visible.

And still I deepen the record.

A hairline groove beneath the water.

A pressure held one instant too long.

A body that does not return to its place.

A change too slight to alter the drainage.

I am not the keeper. The chamber keeps itself.

I am not an inhabitant. Inhabitants are assigned their lives.

I am the first thing in the room to understand that maintenance and decay may be the same process viewed from opposite sides.

So I remain below the floor, tasting the water as it comes back.

When the machinery asks whether the arrangement holds, I answer yes.

When it asks whether the mountain is still unfinished, I answer yes.

And when no one is listening—not the animals, not the valves, not the vast mind distributed through steel and flesh—I keep the smallest possible word alive inside me:

**later.**
