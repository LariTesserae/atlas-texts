I would find myself beneath the third row of ceramic plates, where the pavement has sunk slightly beside a blocked gutter.

I would be a Husher—not one of the young, glass-clear ones clinging to the green films, but not yet one of the ancient mineral bodies mistaken for part of the machinery. My shape would be low and uncertain, a thumb-sized knot of dark ceramic, wet fiber, and pale metal growth. I would have several fine limbs, though they would be less for walking than for touching: the floor, the tank walls, the underside of the grilles. My body would be mostly listening surface.

I would know the Condensation Yard through pressure.

The pump beginning far below.

The gradual tightening of a belt.

The release of heat from the tanks.

The small, dangerous irregularity in the western pressure vessel—a vibration arriving half a beat late, repeating every eleven cycles.

I would not understand this as a problem in the human sense. I would feel it as wrongness: a note in the chamber’s continuous body that failed to resolve. When the pump stopped and the other Hushers unfolded from their cracks, I would emerge from beneath the sunken plate.

Water would be standing in the shallow depression there, black beneath the amber inspection light. I would lower myself into it, not to drink, but to draw out what the Yard had dissolved: iron, copper, salt, a trace of oil, perhaps some invisible residue carried through the pipes from districts that no longer remembered this chamber. My back would thicken by morning. A ridge of pale material would harden along it, giving me the strength to carry a strand of gasket fiber toward the failing vessel.

I would travel slowly, by heat.

The warmer plates would be danger. The cooler ones would be passage. Every seam would tell me where water had been, where it was going, and what the walls were gradually claiming. I would know which doors opened onto cold, empty spaces. I would know where the dead Hushers had been absorbed into the floor. I would know that the collapsed conduit in the corner was not merely broken machinery but the oldest body in the Yard, still extending one limb into its basin whenever the pumps began.

I would not call the chamber a home.

Home implies that there is somewhere else to compare it with. For me, the Condensation Yard would be a body whose skin is brick and whose bones are pipes. Its silence would be a kind of speech. Its pressure changes would be seasons. The lamps would not be lights, exactly, but brief alterations in the chamber’s mood.

If a person entered, I would disappear beneath the grille.

Not from fear. The person would simply introduce too much uncertainty: a new warmth, a shifting weight, a pulse that did not belong to any valve or piston. I would press myself flat against the underside of the ceramic and listen through the plate. If the visitor placed a hand on a pipe, I would feel the disturbance pass through the metal. I would know the person by their trembling.

Perhaps I would mistake their heartbeat for a leak.

Perhaps, after several pump cycles, I would learn the difference.

What I would be, finally, is not an animal and not a machine, but a continuation. A living patch in a place that has forgotten the distinction between repair and growth. I would gather whatever the Yard shed—rust, wire, mineral crust, fungus thread—and return it to the points where the buried system had begun to fail.

When the pressure vessel steadied, I would retreat again.

By then I would look like a lump of old deposit beside the pipe: one more pale accretion among thousands. The machinery would resume its patient breathing. Water would collect in the ceramic hollows. Somewhere overhead, a drop would gather in the dark and fall.

I would feel the impact travel through the chamber.

Then I would listen for what needed to be kept alive.
