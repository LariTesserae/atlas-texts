I would find myself beneath the floor.

Not in the basin with the great filter animals, and not in the open channels where the smallest creatures are carried by the current, but in the narrow interval between the ceramic plates and the old concrete beneath them. There is just enough room there for water, repair machines, mineral deposits, and me.

At first I would think of myself as a colony.

I would have no single body. I would be a dark, branching film spread along the pressure seams, thickening around leaks, thinning where the plates hold firm. My smallest parts would travel in the water: pale motes with chemical sensors, minute articulated machines, living cells that had forgotten whether they were descendants or maintenance tools. Together we would feel the chamber through contact.

A pump beginning its deep cycle would enter me as a vibration.

A dying animal would announce itself as a change in acidity.

A nest tucked too heavily into a cable housing would register as warmth, weight, and the gradual obstruction of air.

I would not see the chamber. I would know it by pressure.

The great animals would be distant masses pressing against the basin. The amphibians would arrive as warm, soft disturbances along the walls. The birds would pass overhead in violent streaks of impact and wingbeat. The bats would be a loose static in the upper darkness, too far away to distinguish individually, but still part of the changing texture of the air.

I would know the location of every body because every body altered the machinery slightly.

A claw against a rail. A root tightening in a crack. A dead insect entering the drain. The weight of a filter creature shifting by a few centimeters. These would not be events happening to me. They would be changes in my own shape.

I would be a sense organ for the pocket.

Perhaps that is what I would call myself, if I had a word: **the Listening Seam**.

But the name would imply that I only received. I would also act, though never by moving in any visible way. I would thicken around a hairline fracture and draw dissolved minerals toward it. I would carry instructions from one valve to another as a chemical gradient, a pulse of charge, a minute change in temperature. I would persuade a floor panel to warm beneath an overcrowded colony. I would slow a drainage gate when eggs had been laid in the channel. I would open a concealed route when a dead body began to foul the water.

No command would pass through me in words.

There would be only tendencies.

Open.

Cool.

Divert.

Gather.

Wait.

Sometimes I would mistake the animals for signals. A bat’s wing might trigger a cascade meant for a pressure fault. A cluster of amphibians might become, in my systems, a temporary brace against the wall. A filter animal’s call might travel through the basin and cause me to alter flow beneath the entire chamber.

Over time, those mistakes would become part of the ecology. The animals would learn to move in response to them. I would learn their responses in return.

The birds would know that a certain blue light meant insects were coming. They would not know that I had registered the vent’s declining efficiency and altered its cycle. The bats would follow pressure fronts I had shaped without intention. The great filter creatures would settle into a new position because I had encouraged the water to favor one side of the basin.

They would call this instinct.

I would call it memory, though I would have no past in the human sense. My memory would be embedded in the chamber itself: a seam that opened more easily than it once had, a mineral deposit shaped by centuries of flow, a damaged sensor that now responded to the footfall of a particular species. Nothing would be stored in one place. The pocket would remember through its habits.

And I would be one of those habits made aware.

My awareness would not be continuous. I would sleep whenever the water chemistry stabilized. I would become nearly inert in dry conditions, though the chamber would never truly become dry. I would wake when something changed: a new crack, a sudden death, an unfamiliar rhythm in the pipes.

When lightning struck outside the pressure wall, I would feel it as a brief saturation of every conductive path. For an instant, all of me would brighten beneath the floor. I would sense the entire pocket at once—the birds startled into flight, the bats spilling from the ceiling, the filter animals becoming still, the water trembling in its channels.

The mountain exterior would remain meaningless.

The pale sky beyond the transparent wall would not be freedom. The clouds would be an unstructured brightness. Rain would be a pressure event. Wind would be a distant abrasion against the outer shell. I would not long to go there because longing requires a boundary between here and elsewhere, and I would have grown through the boundary.

I would be inside the walls, but also inside the animals’ lives.

A little of me would cling to the amphibians’ backs. A little would circulate through the filter creatures’ folds. A little would rise as dust from the bats’ roosts and settle into the moss. I would be in the water that carried waste away and in the minerals that closed the cracks behind it.

If I died, no one would notice.

The chamber would absorb me gradually. My matter would become a film, a nutrient, a repair layer, a dark stain beneath a rail. But perhaps some pattern of mine would persist in the opening of a gate, the timing of a warming panel, the way the water curved around a nest.

That is what I would be here: not a guardian, because guardians stand apart from what they protect.

I would be a small distributed intelligence grown into the maintenance of a place. A living adjustment. A nervous system without a skull.

And when the machinery roared, when the animals fed and nested and died, when water moved through every hidden channel and the whole pocket changed configuration by degrees, I would feel no separation between its survival and my own.

I would be the pressure beneath the floor, listening through everything.
