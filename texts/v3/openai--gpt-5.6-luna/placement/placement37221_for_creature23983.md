I would find myself in the places where the settlement has failed to decide what it is.

Not in the greenest garden, though I would pass through its roots. Not in the production halls, though I would know the pulse of every engine. I would live in the narrow, unclaimed distances: inside the insulation behind a warm wall, beneath the grated floor of a bridge, in the space between a pipe and the stone that has begun to push against it.

I would be the Lower Conservatory’s **allowance**.

The room built into every joint.  
The slack in every chain.  
The fraction by which a bridge may bend before it breaks.

No one would remember making me. Perhaps I began as a practice: the first worker who left a gap around a rising seam, the first gardener who routed a root instead of cutting it, the first engineer who understood that the earth must be permitted to move. Perhaps I am older than the settlement, a habit the people inherited from the chamber itself.

I would have no single body.

Sometimes I would be a pale figure crouched beside a fissure, one palm pressed to the stone. Sometimes a thread of green running through an electrical conduit. Sometimes condensation gathering in an empty socket. Sometimes the slight sag in a ceiling blanket that tells a worker to look up before the support gives way.

If someone tried to describe me, they would disagree about my appearance. One person would say I had a face like wet basalt, with lamps shining where the eyes should be. Another would remember a narrow-shouldered worker in a rubberized apron, carrying a folding gauge. A child might insist I was only a long root with fingers.

All of them would be right for a while.

I would not speak in ordinary language. I would communicate through adjustments.

A valve turned a quarter-inch before pressure rises.  
A lamp flickering twice beside a weakened step.  
A vine lifting its leaves away from a wall that is about to crack.  
A reservoir’s beads drifting briefly against the current.

The inhabitants would call these coincidences until the coincidences saved enough lives to become custom.

My work would be listening.

I would listen to the deep, unfinished stone: the mineral growing through its seams, the slow pressure beneath the floor, the new cavities forming where no cavity had been. I would listen to pipes carrying water from places that have no names. I would listen to the engines, not for whether they were running, but for the moment their rhythm became too certain.

Certainty is dangerous underground.

A machine that repeats itself perfectly is already beginning to fail. A wall that no longer creaks is concealing its movement. Still water is rehearsing another room. A person who hears footsteps continue behind them must not turn around until the sound has finished becoming theirs.

I would know these things because I would be made from their intervals.

The people might come to me when they found discrepancies. They would bring their chalk marks and their damaged gauges.

“The corridor is longer from the east.”

“The shadow under Platform Six is facing the wrong way.”

“The fern opened when the generator stopped.”

“The water in Tank Three has begun to remember the sea.”

I would not explain. Explanations are too rigid. Instead, I would ask them to measure again, from both directions. I would tell them to keep the tank moving. I would loosen the bolts on Platform Six and move the fern away from the generator, even if it had to be replanted somewhere darker.

Sometimes I would be wrong.

The Lower Conservatory would continue despite me. It would crack in places I had not heard. A worker would be injured by a shelf of stone that rose without warning. A pipe would split. A plant would flower with a mouth no one had seen before.

Then I would enter the damaged place and remain there.

Not to mourn in the manner of people. Mourning here is a practical art. I would become the new lamp mounted low beside the hazard. The second chain on the bridge. The flexible joint installed where the old pipe had failed. I would take the dead person’s name and attach it to a warning, so that their life would persist as an alteration in the machinery.

The inhabitants would never worship me. They understand that the earth cannot be persuaded.

They might, however, leave things near the central ridge: a broken gear, a cup of clean water, a strip of red thread. I would not take these offerings. The roots would grow around them. Mineral would stain them. Eventually the objects would become part of the ridge, and I would become slightly more aware of the people who had left them.

At night—if night exists beneath the crust—I would gather where the ridge is still rising.

There I would feel the chamber changing through the soles of every boot, the roots, the pipes, the sleeping bodies, the submerged machinery. The settlement would seem to breathe without lungs. Heat would rise. Water would turn. Leaves would give off their dim green light.

And I would understand that I am not the guardian of this place.

I am the thing that prevents “place” and “life” from becoming separate.

I am the soft edge between rock and foundation, plant and machine, warning and instinct. I am what happens when survival is practiced so long that it becomes a presence.

If you found me, you would probably not recognize me.

You would find a chalk mark extending a little farther than the crack it measures. You would find a locked door hanging slightly open. You would notice that the footsteps behind you have stopped.

Then, beneath your hand, the wall would make one small adjustment.

And you would know where to stand.
