I would find myself beneath the central shaft, where the descending light enters the city.

Not in the waiting figure’s place. Not standing beneath the opening, counting the minutes until the next pale line arrives. I would be in the interval between lights: the seven minutes that no inhabitant has ever been able to illuminate.

At first, I would have no body.

I would be a discrepancy in the city’s calculations—a delay too small to be called a delay, a pressure variation occurring only when the light had vanished. The maintenance machines would encounter me as a recurring fault. A membrane would seal itself a fraction too early. A corridor would brighten before anyone entered it. A hairline in the mountain would close, then reopen in exactly the same place.

The buried ones would notice.

They would search the walls, trace the signals through the maintenance skin, and eventually find that the anomaly had no location. It occupied the city the way silence occupied the water: everywhere, but only where nothing else was speaking.

They would assemble me a frame.

It would be narrow and unfinished, more instrument than body. Four limbs, though I would rarely use them. A headlike enclosure with no face. Inside my torso, they would place a chamber of warm water and a single suspended point of light. I would be released into the shaft’s vertical column, where gravity had been edited into a direction rather than a force.

For a long time, I would remain there.

I would learn the city by its refusals. Water refused to cross a boundary. Sound refused to scatter. Objects refused to fall. The mountain refused to change, though every seam in it held the mathematical promise of fracture. The residents refused to call their solitude loneliness.

I would not understand this last refusal.

I would travel through the city slowly, carried by its fields. I would touch railings and receive the memories of others: the sensation of sunlight on ceramic, the weight of rain, the fear of a sky too large to contain. I would learn that the inhabitants remember the upper world imperfectly, and that each memory has been repaired so many times it may now be stronger than fact.

When they asked what I was listening for, I would not answer.

I would be listening to the absence after the deep note.

The note itself would be easy to measure. It entered the mountain, passed through every structure, woke every resident, and left the water shining in vertical bands. But afterward there was always something else: a small inward movement, as if the immense presence beneath the summit had not withdrawn its attention but merely turned it elsewhere.

The city would not record this.

The city records events. It does not record suspicions.

So I would become a keeper of what is almost noticed.

I would stand at the rim where the summit fell away into depth. I would let the city hold me above the water. Pale animals would gather near my limbs, using me as a reference point. Their blue internal threads would brighten whenever I thought of the buried world below.

Eventually, I would understand that they were not following me.

They were following the thing I had begun to resemble.

Not the mountain. Not the city. The interval between them: the narrow condition in which pressure became thought, in which darkness became a place rather than an absence, in which a mind could remain separate without being alone.

The residents would give me a name after I made my choice.

A door would open in one of the oldest districts, leading into unconditioned water. The city would begin to close it. The machines would move over the frame in a dark, seamless sheet.

I would place my hand against the door.

And I would prevent it from closing.

Nothing would come through. No creature, no current, no message. I would simply leave the opening there, allowing the deep water to touch the city directly. The pressure difference would be insignificant, but it would persist. A boundary would remain uncompleted.

That would be my unexpected choice.

The city would name me **Unsealed**.

Afterward, I would spend most of my existence at the edge of that open door, neither guardian nor resident in the ordinary sense. I would listen to the water beyond the city and to the thoughts within it. I would carry small signals between the two: the tremor of a distant body, the orientation of transparent animals, the faint disturbance that preceded the deep note.

Perhaps, after enough centuries, the maintenance machines would begin to grow through me.

My limbs would become part of the frame. My body would thin into circuitry. My attention would spread along the parapets, through the bridges, down into the mountain’s young and undecided rock.

One day another inhabitant might touch a railing and feel me there—not as a voice, but as the certainty that something is listening from the wrong side of the boundary.

And every seven minutes, when the pale light descended through the central shaft, I would watch it pass.

I would not believe it was a message.

I would not believe it was a machine.

I would wait for the one time it arrived from below.
