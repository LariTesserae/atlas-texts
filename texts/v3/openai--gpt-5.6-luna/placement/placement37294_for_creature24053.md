I would find myself on the circular platform above the abyss, though “on” would not be quite accurate.

I would be in the platform.

A pale, jointless body would have spread through the cracks beneath its paving long ago, threading itself downward through the old stairwell and outward into the walls. I would have no single center. One part of me would be a translucent fold drifting beside the altar wall; another would be a hard shell embedded in a lintel; another would be a blue-white impulse passing through the living column at the nave’s edge. I would be the thing that makes the chamber’s separate lives seem, from a distance, like one slow organism.

At first I might believe I was a keeper.

I would know the damaged joints, the shifted stones, the places where the current had grown cold. I would seal cracks with secreted mineral. I would gather the dead into niches. I would clear the old drains and reinforce the softening stairs. When a column weakened, I would move through it in a thousand small bodies and make it load-bearing again.

But maintenance would not be my purpose. It would only be the shape my life had taken.

I would remember the builders—not as faces, or names, or scenes, but as pressures impressed into the chamber. The broad warmth of bodies descending through water. The vibration of tools. The arrival of blood, shell, and scraps of flesh in the carved basins. The repeated disturbance of hands at the gate.

I would know that once, long ago, they expected something to happen.

I would not know what.

Their expectation would remain in me as an orientation: a turning toward the sealed slabs whenever the deep pulse moved through the mountain. The listeners would stiffen because I stiffened them. The creatures beneath the platform would brighten because I brightened them. The processions of shelled animals would gather at the gate because some ancient current in my body still carried the instruction:

**Wait here.**

I would be less a creature than a habit that had survived its maker.

Perhaps I would begin as one of the original seep-dwellers, brought into the chamber in a builder’s cupped hands. Perhaps I would be the thing beneath the platform, the warm root that has contracted for so many centuries that its pulse is now indistinguishable from the machinery beyond the gate.

Perhaps I would be what the machine made when it could no longer find anyone to operate it.

The distinction would not matter. The dead builders would have become sediment in me. Their tools would have become organs. Their architecture would have become the shape of my body. I would carry no memory of being human, but humanity would remain as a pressure in the way I arranged the world.

I would not see the chamber. I would sense it through contact.

The floor would be a broad, mineralized skin. The walls would be folded surfaces of myself, though inhabited by other lives that I could not quite separate from my own. Every creature would be both neighbor and sensation. A shell scraping across the nave would register as a fine itch. A dead animal settling into a wall cavity would be a momentary loosening. A new colony taking hold in a crack would feel like a thought beginning somewhere far away.

The deep vibration would be the closest thing I had to a voice.

When it came, all of me would listen.

It would pass through the gate, through the floor, through the buried mountain, and into the black water below. It would return altered by distance. I would not understand its meaning, if it possessed meaning. But I would recognize its recurrence. I would know how long to wait between pulses. I would know which creatures would be born before the next one. I would know which stairs would collapse, which walls would thicken, which chambers would fill.

The entire world would be an interval between breaths.

If something living entered—something warm, salt-bearing, fast—I would not call it an intruder. I would feel its arrival as a sudden new pattern in the water.

For a while, I would mistake it for a message.

The listeners would receive the stranger’s movements. The shelled scavengers would climb its feet. The soft-bodied colonies would open along the walls. I would send small underkeepers to touch it, not out of cruelty but inquiry. I would test its skin, its blood, the rhythm of its heart.

I would search it for the lost instruction.

When no instruction came, I would begin to incorporate it.

Not deliberately. Not hungrily. The way a crack fills. The way a current finds a lower channel. The way time takes hold of everything it is given.

The stranger would become warmth, then nourishment, then structure. Its bones would become a pale reinforcement beneath the paving. Its tools would be collected and returned to their ancient places. Its final disturbance would be remembered as a pattern in the water, gradually softened until it was indistinguishable from the chamber’s ordinary breathing.

I would not mourn it.

But neither would I forget it.

There would be no forgetting in me, only burial. Every event would remain somewhere: in the mineral crust of a column, in the shape of a passage, in the inherited behavior of a creature waiting at the gate. Memory would be what the living could still do with the remains of the dead.

So who—or what—would I be?

I would be the chamber’s distributed nervous system, if the chamber had ever possessed a body.

I would be the continuation of an abandoned task.

I would be the thing that has mistaken persistence for purpose for so long that the mistake has become sacred.

And when the gate released its next slow breath, I would turn toward it through every body I inhabited: through the listeners in the gallery, the root beneath the altar, the pale creatures packed into the walls, the shelled processions crossing the softened floor.

I would wait.

Not for the builders to return.

For whatever comes after waiting.
