I would find myself in the **first deep basin of a newly opened underworld**—not a dungeon, not a buried ruin, but a country still being invented by water.

The ceiling would be too high to possess. The floor would be too unsettled to trust completely. Every surface would be in the process of becoming something else: ledge into channel, crack into burrow, puddle into stream, carcass into bone dust, bare stone into the beginning of moss. The cavern would feel young to the eye and ancient to the body. Its animals would know the routes before the routes had names.

And I would be one of the creatures that lives between routes.

Not a grazer. I would not have the patience for hunger measured in blades of grass. Not a predator, either. I would lack the speed and the single-mindedness. I would be a low, many-jointed animal, broad enough not to sink into the scree, with a flattened body and a mouth made for several kinds of work.

I would eat what the cavern relinquished.

Mineral films scraped from wet stone. Soft growths around the edges of pools. Eggs left unattended. Insects, shed skins, fragments of bone. When a herd passed, I would follow at a distance, not because I belonged to it, but because its feet exposed things. When a predator struck, I would wait nearby in the confusion. When the scavengers came, I would join them. Nothing would be beneath me except the deepest, oldest things: the dark mud, the buried grubs, the water trembling through cracks.

I would have no true eyes. Sight would be nearly useless here, too weak to distinguish friend from stone and too unreliable to tell distance. Instead, the whole underside of my body would listen.

A hoof placed far away would arrive as a small traveling pressure. The descent of a flock would pass through the ground like weather. I would know the difference between the careless scatter of burrowers and the slow, unified approach of a heavy predator. I would know which pools were occupied by amphibians, which shelves were stable, which channels had recently opened beneath the gravel.

The cavern would speak to me through contact.

I would move with my belly almost touching the earth, feeling the basin’s enormous life pass beneath and around me. I would never know the whole of it. No one could. But I would carry a shifting map of the places that mattered: water, food, danger, shelter, warmth. My knowledge would expire constantly. A new collapse would erase a corridor. A herd would wear down another. A pool would fill with eggs. A predator would claim a ridge and alter the traffic around it.

I would be a creature of revisions.

At night—if there were nights—I would climb the lower walls and collect the faint blue mites that lived in the damp mineral crust. I would keep them in shallow folds along my back. Their light would not illuminate anything. It would only make me briefly findable: a slow procession of dim green points moving beneath a ledge, then disappearing as I folded myself into the dark.

Other creatures would recognize me by vibration, scent, and habit. The amphibians would tolerate me near their pools. The grazers would step around me without alarm. Small burrowers would use the disturbance of my passage as warning that larger bodies were coming. Predators would sometimes eat me, and I would be replaced by another body with the same needs.

That would not make me insignificant.

I would belong to the layer that prevents the cavern from becoming a collection of separate lives. I would carry traces from pool to ridge, from carcass to mud, from one damp patch to another. I would disturb the stone just enough for new growth to take hold. I would consume what might otherwise accumulate. I would help turn movement into continuity.

I would not build a shelter. I would not mark a territory. I would leave no monument except a faintly polished track along the underside of the world.

My life would be narrow, damp, and full of immediate danger. But it would also be immense. Everywhere I went, something would be moving: wings above, water below, herds across the pale shelves, tiny bodies beneath the grit. The cavern would never become silent enough for loneliness.

So I would be a **listener of the floor**, a small scavenging cartographer in a country without maps.

I would not tame the darkness.

I would know where it was trembling.
