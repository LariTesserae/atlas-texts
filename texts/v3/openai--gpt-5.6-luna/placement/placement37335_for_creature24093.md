I would be found beneath the western pumping station, where the foundation descends in steps into green water and the old turbine turns with its patient, uneven thump.

Not inside it. Not exactly.

I would live in the chamber behind the third pressure gate, in the narrow dry space where the concrete has cracked away from the mountain and made a room large enough for a body, a nest of reeds, and several generations of copper wire. Water would enter through the wall in thin, cold threads. At high flow, the whole chamber would tremble. At low flow, I would hear stones moving along the basin floor far below.

I would be one of the listeners.

My body would be dark along the back, pale beneath the ribs, and scarred where the current had taken me against metal. I would have long forelimbs for bracing myself in sluices, and webbed hands that could feel a vibration through a rail before the sound reached my ears. Along my left arm, I would wear a braid of copper strips. Each strip would mark a threshold I had learned: the depth at which the eastern gate becomes dangerous, the temperature that means meltwater has arrived from the high ravines, the pitch of the turbine when gravel has entered its housing.

I would not keep these records for myself.

The young would come to me in the late hours, when the water had settled enough for small sounds to travel through the station walls. They would press their palms to the concrete. I would tap out the history of a current: three slow knocks for the river changing course, a rapid tremor for a jammed intake, one long pressure for a body carried into the deep channels and not returned.

They would learn that the machinery is not dead.

The pumping station would be old, but age would not make it harmless. Its turbine would turn whenever the upper rivers rose, though no one remembered what it had once powered. The gate beneath it would open and close according to pressure, and sometimes it would close too quickly, sending a hard wave through the reed beds where the youngest fish sheltered.

My work would be to prevent that.

I would clear gravel from the intake mouth. I would crawl along the submerged rail and wedge black stones beneath the gate’s lower hinge. I would replace a failed cable with braided root, copper, and strips of rubber peeled from the old lines. I would listen to the machine through my bones and decide whether its next movement would preserve the basin or wound it.

I would not repair what could be allowed to fail.

A broken wall might create a nursery. A collapsed tunnel might become a refuge for the Slow One. A silent pump could make a still place where eggs would survive. The old world’s purposes would not be sacred to me. I would preserve only the balances that had become necessary after everything else was lost.

When I traveled, I would move through the water with my hand around the ankle of the one ahead. We would pass beneath drowned bridges, through forests of kelp and the hollow ribs of machinery. In clear water, I would disappear against the stone. In silt, I would follow pressure rather than sight.

Sometimes I would follow the Slow One.

Its passage would open a road through the weeds and lift buried things from the basin floor: a tool, a length of rail, a ceramic cup, the bones of someone whose name had become a ridge. I would ride in its wake with the younger ones, though I would tell them I was measuring the current. The lie would be understood and forgiven.

At night, I would rest with my chest against the turbine housing.

Its pulse would pass through me.

That would be the closest thing I had to a heartbeat outside my own body: the slow mechanical thump, the answering movement of water, the mountain sending itself downward through stone. I would listen for changes. A roughness in the rotation. A new vibration in the foundation. The faint metallic pattern from the deepest shafts, where something unknown occasionally moved the dead machinery.

I would be practical about that unknown.

I would not call it a spirit. I would not call it an animal. I would call it a pattern I had not yet learned to survive.

During the great rains, I would leave the chamber and go toward the violence. The others would gather at the river mouths, their bodies vanishing and reappearing among the spray. We would cut floating mats apart, clear the channels, drive the fish downward, and hold one another by the wrist when speech became useless.

If the current took me, I would become part of its record.

My body would settle in a flooded forest. Fish would move through my ribs. Silt would cover the copper on my arm. Someone, years later, would tap my name against a submerged rail and wait for the sound of my bones reaching bottom.

But for now I would remain at the gate, one hand on the shuddering metal, feeling the pressure rise.

Not guardian. Not engineer. Not inhabitant.

A small correction in a world too large to control.

The basin would pass through me, and I would pass through the basin, while above us the mountains continued to conduct the water downward.
