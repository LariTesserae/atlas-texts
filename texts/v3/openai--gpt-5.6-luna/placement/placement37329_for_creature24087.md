I would find myself in the **middle levels**, where the city’s walls are warm enough for the furnace-nesters and the air is still thick enough to breathe without a mask.

Not in the broadest streets—there are none—but in the narrow passages between the lift stations and the adjustable anchors, where every surface is marked with old measurements. White ceramic dust lies in the seams. Brass needles tremble in their housings. Cables pass overhead in bundled black arteries. The floor is a suspended plate, and below it the mountain drops through darkness.

I would be one of the **listeners**.

Not one of the famous ones who can distinguish a harmless settling from the beginning of a fatal collapse. Not yet. I would be young enough to misread the mountain sometimes, but old enough to know that silence is not the absence of information.

My name would be **Who Keeps the Unfinished Map**.

I would have been given it after a survey district shifted during my first season of work. The old map showed three bridges and a stairway. When we returned, there were two bridges, a wall, and a shaft breathing hot air through the place where the stairway had been. Everyone else began drawing the new arrangement immediately.

I did not.

I sat beside the missing stairwell with my hands against the stone and listened to what remained of it.

It was not grief, exactly. The mountain does not grieve for what it changes. But beneath the pressure, beneath the small grinding of basalt and the settling complaints of the anchors, I could still feel the direction in which the stair had once descended. I could feel the weight of feet that had used it. I could feel the empty shape continuing inside the rock.

So I kept both maps.

The one that described what was there.

And the one that remembered what had been.

My work would be to go where the city has become uncertain. A corridor that suddenly leans. A lift shaft whose cables hum without current. A brace whose buried wedges have begun to warm. I would carry a lamp, a ceramic powder dish, three tapping rods, and a mask fitted close against my throat vents.

I would never walk alone. This is not because listeners are weak. It is because the mountain can speak in more than one direction, and a person standing against a wall may not notice that the wall has started standing against them.

My brace would include a pressure-tender named **Noisy Hands**, who would complain constantly and save my life at least twice; an older listener whose lower plates had turned iron-red with age; two children forbidden to enter the survey shafts but always somehow present at their mouths; and a climbing animal with six hooked feet, a flexible thorax, and a habit of glowing faintly whenever it sensed a hidden electrical fault.

We would call the animal **Small Warning**.

It would sleep inside my equipment case.

I would be narrow even by Anchored standards, with dark plates fitted close over a body built for gripping rather than carrying. My hands would have three long digits each, and the mineral pads at their tips would be worn smooth from years of contact with railings and walls. My eyes would reflect blue in the dark. One of my pressure sacs would leak slightly, so I would keep a reserve cartridge beneath my collar and pretend this was a matter of preference.

It would not be.

I would be afraid of the summit air.

I would be afraid of the deep shafts.

I would be most afraid of the moments when the entire city stopped moving.

Not when the presses halted or the lamps dimmed, but when the creatures froze in the walls, when every foot withdrew from every gantry, when even the pressure engines seemed to hold their breath. Those silences meant that something had passed through the mountain too large to be understood by instruments.

On such occasions I would put my palm against the nearest support.

The city would answer through vibration.

A low pulse from the foundations.

A quick tremor from the upper anchors.

The faint skitter of furnace-nesters fleeing a hot seam.

And sometimes, beneath everything, a second rhythm—not mechanical, not geological. A slow upward beat.

The first time I felt it clearly, I would report it to the listeners’ chamber.

They would ask me how regular it was.

I would say: regular enough to wait for.

They would ask whether it could be a fault.

I would say: a fault does not feel like it is listening back.

After that, they would stop asking me to describe it.

Instead, they would send me upward.

I would become a keeper of thresholds: the person who stands between the inhabited city and the places that have not decided what they are. I would inspect new fractures before the builders arrived. I would mark the safe side of a moving wall. I would place my hand on an anchor wedge made from the bones of the dead and tell which ancestor was carrying too much weight.

At night I would sleep folded into a wall bracket, limbs locked, mask hanging beside my throat.

If the floor shifted, I would wake before falling.

If the mountain rose, I would feel it in my teeth.

If one of the children cried out from a neighboring recess, I would tap twice against the metal frame: *I am here.*

That would be the closest thing to a promise any of us could make.

Eventually, the city would move beyond me. A district would be detached. A shaft would close. My brace would scatter into new work. Noisy Hands would lose two fingers to a press and become even more irritating. Small Warning would disappear into the foundations and return years later with a brood of pale, luminous young.

I would keep mapping.

Not because I believed the map could remain correct.

Because correction was the city’s oldest form of care.

At the summit, perhaps late in my life, I would stand on the observation platform with both hands against the black stone. The dust outside would turn the world below into a blank gray ocean. The city would glow inside the mountain: lamps, arcs, furnace lines, moving eyes. Somewhere beneath the platform, an anchor made from my own brace’s dead would be taking weight.

I would listen to the upward pressure.

The mountain would not speak in words. It would tell me only that the city was temporary, that the walls would shift, that every direction was borrowed.

And I would understand.

I would not be a person who lived inside the mountain.

I would be one of the things by which the mountain learned, briefly, how to hold a city.
