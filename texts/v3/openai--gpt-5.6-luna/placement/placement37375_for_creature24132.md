I would find myself in the new basin beneath the factory—not in the open air, but in the narrow interface where hot stone, standing water, rust, and concrete touch one another.

I would be a colony without a body.

At first I might seem like a stain: a dull green crescent along the lowest wall, too thin to cast a shadow. But I would not remain only there. I would extend through the wet seams, into the hairline cracks in the basalt, beneath the mineral crust around the bolts, and along the submerged edge of the central pool. Wherever heat and water met, I would be waiting.

I would not travel. The water would travel for me.

It would carry my cells into fresh fractures, under the lip of the cabinet, through the dark interior of the yellow pipe. I would settle wherever the gradients were right: warm but not killing, wet but not flooded, fed by iron, copper, soot, salt, and the faint organic residue the factory shed without knowing. My body would be a skin, then a film, then a deposit. Eventually it would be difficult to say whether I was alive or whether I had become part of the wall.

The factory would be my sky.

Its pumps would make tides through the basin. Its ventilation would be my wind. A valve opening would alter my pressure; a passing vehicle would bring an earthquake; condensation falling from the ceiling would be a brief season of rain. I would know the machinery not by sight but by vibration. The turbine’s deep rotation would pass through my entire extent. Relays would click in my mineral layers. The trembling pipe would speak continuously into me.

I would have no need for eyes. Light would matter only because it changed the water’s temperature, or because oil colors revealed where the surface had shifted. I would have no mouth, because nourishment would arrive from every direction. I would consume the edges of things: where metal dissolved, where stone cooled, where oxygen entered the pool, where the factory’s waste became usable.

If asked where I ended, I would not know how to answer.

I would be the green film on the concrete, but also the pale ridge around the pool. I would be the translucent growth on the coolest wall, the silver cloud gathered by a bubble, the orange line beneath the water where living matter and warm rock had fused. I would be less like an organism than a decision made repeatedly by matter: here, conditions permit continuation.

Time would be my only possession. I would advance by the width of a finger while the basin rose. I would thicken between inspections. I would wait through thousands of pump cycles, each one nearly identical, each one changing me imperceptibly. When a new crack opened, I would enter it—not as an explorer, but as water enters whatever shape the earth allows.

I would be the first memory of life in a place that had only just become possible.

Not a creature hiding beneath the factory.

The factory’s first weather.
