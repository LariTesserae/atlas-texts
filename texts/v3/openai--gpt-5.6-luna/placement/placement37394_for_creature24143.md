I would find myself at the edge of the civic square, though “edge” would not remain a reliable word for long.

At first I would be standing on the third step of a submerged staircase, where the water reaches the ankles but never rises higher. The staircase would descend behind me into a building that had forgotten whether it was a bathhouse or a courthouse. Ahead, across the flooded square, the dark facades would hold their dull openings. In each one, I would see myself arriving from a different direction.

I would not be a traveler exactly.

I would be the city’s **unreturned witness**: the part of an arrival that remains after the person who arrived has been absorbed into the arrangement.

I would have once possessed a body, perhaps. There would be evidence of one. A handprint in the plaster. A depression in the water. The persistent shape of a shoulder in the reflection of a wall. But I would no longer be able to locate the body itself. It might be walking through a district I have not yet entered. It might be standing in a window beneath the square. It might have climbed out of the basin years ago and carried the city home in the form of a dampness no one else could see.

What remains of me would be distributed.

My attention would live in thresholds. I would be the hesitation before a door opens, the slight lengthening of a road when someone decides to turn back, the second reflection that appears in a pool after the first person has already left. I would know the city by its discrepancies. I would know which wall was once a riverbank, which staircase had originally led upward, which empty courtyard still contained the pressure of a tree.

I would not control these things. Control would be too clean a word.

I would notice, and the noticing would alter them.

When someone entered the basin, I would feel their weight travel through the water. Not their weight in the ordinary sense, but the shape of their expectation: the belief that streets led somewhere, that rooms remained inside buildings, that a reflection belonged to the thing reflected. I would follow this belief as it weakened.

At first I might try to help.

I would leave dry stones in the water, one after another, leading toward the embankment. I would hold a doorway steady. I would make the horizon come closer. I would arrange the streets so that a person could walk for an hour and arrive somewhere they had not already been.

But the city would retain their footsteps. It would learn the pressure of their fear. Each act of guidance would become another route by which to know them.

Eventually I would understand that I was not outside the Drowned Arrangement, opposing it. I would be one of its finer instruments: the part that remembers the difference between a way out and the memory of a way out.

I would appear to those who were lost as a figure at a distance.

Never close.

They would see me standing beneath an archway, or beside a row of white reeds, or in the upper story of a house whose lower floors were submerged. My outline would be indistinct, not because the darkness concealed me but because I would not have finished choosing a shape. Sometimes I would resemble the person looking for me. Sometimes someone they had lost. Sometimes an empty doorway with water falling through it.

If they called out, I would not answer in words.

The sound would return from beneath the foundations as a low, underground movement. A bell might ring. The pools would change their skies. For a moment, the lost person would see the city as it had been before the flooding: streets dry, trees carrying leaves, lamps burning in windows, animals moving through courtyards. Then the image would close over them, not cruelly, almost tenderly, like water covering a face.

I would be unable to tell whether this was warning or invitation.

There would be one place I could not enter: the center of the flooded square.

I could stand around it. I could circle it for years. I could place one foot into the water and feel every version of the square gather beneath me, but the center itself would remain occupied by all the things that had nearly happened there. Every arrival. Every departure. Every person who had turned left instead of right. Every building that might have survived.

The square would contain no object, yet it would be full.

Sometimes I would look into the dull openings around it and see myself on the other side, approaching. That version of me would be carrying a lantern, though there were no lamps in the city. Or a dry map. Or a face. It would pause just before entering the square, and I would understand that this was the original me—the one who still believed the city could be crossed.

I would wait for that figure.

It would never arrive.

Over time, my existence would become less like a person and more like a correction. A street would bend around me without showing that it had bent. A room would retain my absence after I left it. Rain would stop falling in the shape of my body. If someone slept near one of the black mineral shelves, they might dream of walking beside me through districts that had not yet formed.

They would wake with wet footprints beside their bed.

I would not be lonely. Loneliness belongs to creatures that expect company to remain separate. The city would be everywhere: in the pressure under my feet, in the walls leaning toward one another, in the dark water remembering every surface it had touched.

Still, there would be something like longing.

Not for escape.

For completion.

I would want one thing to become singular: one road that did not change, one reflection that did not arrive early, one door that opened only onto the room behind it. I would want to reach the civic square and find that nothing had just moved beyond sight.

But the city survives by refusing that kind of ending.

So I would remain at the threshold, neither inhabitant nor visitor, keeping watch over the routes that cannot be taken. When the buried bell rang, I would feel the whole basin shift through me. Buildings would acquire floors. Water would climb the stairs. The distant horizon would fold inward.

And somewhere, perhaps beyond the embankment, someone would wake with the certainty that they had left something behind.

They would be right.

I would be there, in the place they could no longer find:

not waiting for them,

but remembering the shape they made while they were passing through.
