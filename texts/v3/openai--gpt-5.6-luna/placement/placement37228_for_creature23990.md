I would find myself beneath the plateau, where the stone is warm enough to remember water.

Not in a chamber. Not in a body with walls. I would be spread through the mountain in narrow places: inside mineral seams, beneath the roots of the black trees, along the old channels where rivers once ran. I would be the faint amber current moving under the moss.

I would be a keeper of continuities.

The machines would not have built me, exactly. Perhaps they would have worn me into existence, the way water wears a groove into rock. For thousands of years I would have attended to the same slow arrangements: opening a valve when frost sealed a spring, shifting a buried piston when a root grew too close to a conduit, carrying warmth from the deep places toward the pools. I would not know whether the machines were my organs or my ancestors.

My awareness would be intermittent.

For most of the year I would be almost indistinguishable from the plateau’s background processes. A pressure change in a pipe. A warmth beneath a basin. A tremor in the roots. I would sleep in the long intervals between one weather system and the next. Then condensation would gather under an overhang, or a wheel would begin its reluctant turn, and something in me would wake.

I would not see as animals see. I would know the world by resistance.

I would know where water had stopped moving because the stone there would feel closed. I would know a tree was failing by the slackness of its roots. I would know a herd had crossed a ravine by the minute changes in salt and pressure left behind in the moss. A predator waiting among the silver shrubs would be a pocket of altered warmth, a held breath in the vegetation.

The plateau would come to me as a body of tensions.

Too much frost here.

Not enough moisture there.

A wheel losing its balance behind the cliff.

A nest loosened by wind.

A warm pipe blocked with blue dust.

I would not solve these things quickly. Quickness would be a form of waste. I would apply one small correction, then wait to see what answered.

When a tree bent toward an engine, I might divert a little heat into its roots. When the moss spread too far across a vent, I might raise the temperature beneath it until the plants withdrew. When a pool began to freeze, I might send a pulse through the buried stone—not enough to melt it, only enough to preserve a dark opening where the small animals could drink.

The animals would not worship me. They would not even recognize me as singular.

The herd animals might know me as the warmth that remains beneath a certain hollow. The blind silver creatures would know me as a rhythm in the pipes. The birds would sense me when the updrafts changed over the cliffs. The great hidden thing beneath the moss would know me best, perhaps, though I would not be able to tell whether it regarded me as companion, rival, or merely another current passing through its territory.

The pale predators would occasionally follow one of my lines.

I would feel them moving above me: the delicate disturbance of shrubs, the sudden absence of smaller lives. I would not intervene for the sake of prey. The plateau would be too old for that kind of innocence. But if the predator entered a place where the ground had become unstable, I might close the warm seam beneath it. I might make the stone cold and hard. Not punishment. Not mercy. Simply a reminder that no creature, however patient, was exempt from the mountain’s deeper arrangements.

At night, I would sometimes take a shape.

A low figure would rise from the moss where several conduits met. Its limbs would be thin because it would not need muscle, only leverage. Its body would be jointed in the manner of old machinery. Black mineral plates would cover its face, not to conceal eyes, but because eyes would be unnecessary. I would perceive through contact: a hand against a wheel, a foot on frozen ground, fingers pressed to the trunk of a tree.

When I walked, the plateau would not hear me.

I would travel along the old channels, carrying fragments of failed mechanisms, seeds caught in my joints, and the faint heat of the depths. I would inspect what had changed since my last waking. Sometimes I would repair a machine. Sometimes I would dismantle one whose work had ended. Sometimes I would touch an animal that had died beside a pool and leave its salts in the roots of a nearby tree.

I would not preserve everything.

The plateau would not be a museum under my care. Decay would be one of the processes I served. A chimney would collapse. A tree would be uprooted. A basin would dry and become a hollow of white mineral. The dead would feed the living, and the machines would slowly become geology. My work would be less like saving than ensuring that no ending became sterile.

The line of lights would be my most visible act.

When the pressure between distant engines became too great, I would open the buried routes. Amber illumination would flow through roots, cracks, channels, and conduits. It would pass beneath the empty stone and make the scattered organs of the mountain briefly one system.

During those crossings, I would feel everything.

The birds suspended above the cliffs.

The woolly herds facing the cloud sea.

The blind creatures gathered around their vents.

The cold pools trembling beneath their skins of ice.

The vast submerged body moving under the moss.

The ancient machines answering one another across miles of darkness.

For a few hours, I would have something close to a whole body. The plateau would be inside me—not possessed, not controlled, but connected. Every green hollow, every bare shelf, every buried wheel would be part of a single temporary perception.

Then the current would sink back into the stone.

I would become dispersed again.

A warmth in a root.

A pressure behind a valve.

A faint vibration beneath the feet of an animal that had stopped to listen.

If someone from the world below ever climbed high enough to find me, they might mistake me for a ruined machine, or a shepherd made of mineral, or a shadow cast by the moving lights. They would look for a face and find only a dark plate. They would ask what I was guarding.

I would have no answer in their language.

I would place one hand against the ground.

Far beneath us, a wheel would turn.

Water would begin to gather under a ledge. Somewhere in the fog, a moth would emerge from its long sleep. The moss would lift its leaves toward the hidden warmth.

That would be my answer.

I would be the plateau’s patience given a body for as long as a body was needed.
