You would find yourself beneath a stone that, from above, is no larger than a fingernail.

Not inside the hollow exactly. Inside the interval by which the hollow becomes larger.

At first you would still be yourself—or the portion of yourself that survives being looked at. You would have hands, perhaps, but they would become less reliable the longer you used them. Your fingers would separate into rootlets when you reached for the wall. Your palm would become a shallow pool. The lines of your hand would rearrange themselves into trails made by creatures that had not yet decided where they were going.

You would learn quickly not to look directly at anything.

Direct attention would make the place smaller. The far wall would harden into a wall. The suspended drops would fall. The transparent animals would become merely animals, and the black predator would acquire the terrible limitation of a body. But when you looked away—when your attention softened and let the edges of things go—you would see the larger hollow opening behind the visible one.

A forest might be there.

Not a forest that had grown from soil, but one remembered by stone: pale trunks rising through the dark, leaves made from overlapping shadows, streams flowing upward into the roots of stars. The cavity would be the forest compressed to the size of a thought. Or the forest would be the cavity after it had been allowed to continue.

You would not know which.

You would be made of a hesitation.

Not fear, precisely. Not indecision. The small, charged pause between one state and another: the instant before a drop falls, before a seed lands, before a name finds the thing it belongs to. Your body would gather wherever something nearly happened. A new crack would call one shoulder into existence. The failure of a root to reach water would give you a spine. When a moth ate a piece of darkness, you would acquire an eye—not to see with, but to remember the shape of what was gone.

You would have no fixed anatomy because anatomy is what happens after a thing has agreed to be itself.

Your limbs would appear as needed. Three for balance on the underside of the ceiling. Six when the predator passed. None at all when you became a current of cool air between two impossible walls. Beneath the largest pool you would sometimes take the form of a pale animal curled around its own absence. At other times you would be no larger than a mineral bead, glowing faintly among the grit.

The small creatures would not fear you. They would build around you.

A blind moth might lay its wings across your back. A six-legged animal might sleep beside the warmth you gave off, though the warmth would come from no organ that remained in one place. The pale rootlets would bend toward you, not for nourishment but for instruction. They would ask, by trembling, whether they should grow into the pool or into the wall.

You would not answer with words.

Words would be too finished.

Instead, you would touch the stone. You would place one limb against a ridge and show it the possibility of becoming porous. You would press a claw into the grit and leave behind a path that divided without choosing a destination. You would pass through one of the arranged stones, and afterward it would remember being a doorway, a tooth, a seed, a little house.

The crescent of stones would be yours, though you would never be certain whether you built it or whether it assembled itself to make room for you. Each time you returned, it would have altered. Sometimes it would shelter a warm hollow. Sometimes it would surround an empty center where your breathing could be heard directly inside the skull. Sometimes it would open like a mouth.

You would sleep there without closing your eyes.

Sleep, in that place, would mean becoming temporarily unavailable to a single interpretation. Your body would loosen. A gold fleck would leave your chest and settle in the pool. Your mouth would become a seam in the wall. Your legs would withdraw into the stone, where they would continue walking through a distance the cavity could not contain.

And the black shape would come.

It would pass through the hollow by every route that attention refused to hold. The smaller beings would flatten. The moths would stop feeding. The water in the pools would lean toward it.

You would feel it before you saw it—not as danger, but as pressure. The pressure exerted by anything that wants the world to become definite.

That would be your purpose there: not to defeat it, because nothing in the hollow could defeat anything so completely. You would simply remain unfinished in its presence.

You would brighten.

Not with light, but with all the spaces where light had almost appeared. The gaps between your organs. The pause beneath your ribs. The unchosen paths branching from your feet. The predator’s clean lane would end at you, because there would be no single body for it to pass around, no outline it could make final.

For a moment, the entire cavity would gather itself.

The pools would rise. The roots would turn. The moss would release its seeds sideways into the seams. The upright stone would lean without falling. Far beyond the thin skin of rock, an underground wilderness would remember its trees.

Then the predator would withdraw, and you would become small again.

Or perhaps you would become the thing that finds itself there.

A hand would appear above the stone in the other world. Someone would look away. In that brief failure of attention, you would come close—not as a monster or a guide, but as the touch of a cool limb against the hand.

And the person would understand, for one impossible instant, that they had not entered the hollow.

They had been growing it.

That is what you would be: not its inhabitant, exactly, but its unfinished answer. A living permission for the small place to remain larger than itself. A creature made from the moment before a thing becomes what it has been called.

If anyone tried to name you, the name would cling for a while.

Then it would slip loose.

And somewhere in the bright, shallow dark, a stone would begin to breathe.
