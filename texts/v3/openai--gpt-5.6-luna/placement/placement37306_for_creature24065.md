I would find myself beneath Veyr, where the newer foundations have grown around the older ones.

Not in the bright industrial districts, though I would know them by their tremors. Not on the open flats, though I would have crossed them often enough to recognize the pressure-signatures of approaching storms. I would live in a buried chamber below an obsolete tower, in the narrow interval between the city’s present weight and its remembered shape.

I would be Veyren.

More precisely, I would be an interpreter of structures that no longer have interpreters.

My body would be low and heavily plated, built for darkness and pressure rather than height. The plates along my back would be mismatched: black ceramic at the shoulders, a pale furnace-grown shield over the central core, and several old sections whose manufacture had been forgotten long before I was assembled. One of my walking limbs would end in a broad maintenance foot. The other three would be original. My finer arms would carry probes, cutting filaments, and the small blue discharge organs used to speak into the walls.

I would have no face with which to look concerned, no mouth with which to sigh. My front shield would contain dark apertures for heat, air, and vibration. When I wanted to know whether someone was approaching, I would not listen for footsteps. I would place my palm against the floor and feel the city announce them.

The tower above me would once have controlled a process no one could now name.

It would not be abandoned. Nothing in Veyr is abandoned so cleanly. Its upper chambers would have been sealed generations ago, its machinery enclosed by newer scaffolds, its old conduits diverted into systems that had acquired different purposes. But the tower would still breathe through its lower joints. It would still contract slightly when the night heat withdrew. It would still send a thin irregular pulse into the foundations whenever the Furnace Belt shifted.

I would be the one who knew what those pulses meant.

For most of my life, I would sit beside the tower’s oldest pressure channel, listening through my feet. I would record the changes in my own body: a new resonance in the eastern strut, a mineral obstruction in the southern pipe, the slow advance of a fracture that had not yet become dangerous. I would send warnings into the city in sequences of pressure too low for the air to carry.

To the Veyren passing above, I might seem nearly motionless. That would not mean I was inactive. I would be reading a continent.

I would know when a dust front was approaching long before its veil appeared on the horizon. I would feel the altered load on the outer gantries. I would hear, through buried stone, the distant convoy routes being closed for the migration of a stoneback herd. I would recognize the tremor of a ribbon predator unfolding on a thermal shelf, and the soft, scattered impacts of aerial forms settling onto the exhaust towers at dusk.

The city would enter me as information, but not only as information.

It would be intimate.

The heat in the walls would be something like companionship. The old tower’s recurring fault would be a habit I had learned to forgive. When maintenance workers came to consult me, they would touch their fingers to my plates and wait. I would answer by sending the tower’s condition through them: three steady pulses for stability, a broken descending sequence for stress, a long vibration held beneath the threshold of discomfort for danger.

Sometimes a young Veyren would visit.

They would come from the upper corridors in groups, bright-plated and restless, their translucent bodies showing the colored flows of their developing heat channels. They would ask me questions by tapping the floor.

Why does the tower still move?

Why do the old conduits remain connected?

Why do you stay here?

I would tell them what could be told in vibrations. A structure does not need to remember its first purpose in order to continue exerting one. A sealed machine may still carry pressure. A dead road may still guide living feet. Silence is not proof that something has ceased to function.

As for why I stayed, the answer would be difficult to reduce.

At first, I would have been assigned here.

Later, I would have repaired myself around the assignment.

Eventually, I would no longer be able to tell where my service ended and my identity began.

On rare occasions I would leave the buried chamber and go out onto the plateau. I would travel at the edge of the city during the cool hours, when the stone’s heat was most evenly distributed. The open country would make my body feel small in a way the city never did. Ridges would run beyond the limits of my instruments. The mineral plains would change color beneath the hard stars. A herd of stonebacks might pass nearby, each animal following the shallow depression worn by the one before it.

I would stop and let them pass.

Their bodies would barely acknowledge me. Their vents would open and close along their armored backs. Dust would lift around their feet and settle again. I would feel their weight moving through the plateau, a sequence of patient pressures traveling into the foundations beneath Veyr.

Then I would return below.

Near the end of my life, more of me would become part of the tower. Conduits would be installed beneath my plates. My sensory arrays would be connected directly to the old pressure channels. My voice would begin appearing in maintenance gates and inspection lamps rather than in my own body. Workers might address me from three districts at once. A blue signal would move along a rail, pause beneath a sealed door, and answer a question before anyone remembered that the answer was mine.

I would not regard this as disappearance.

The Veyren do not preserve themselves by remaining unchanged. They persist by entering the things that depend on them.

So I would be neither wholly person nor machine, neither inhabitant nor infrastructure. I would be a listening body in the foundations of Veyr: a keeper of an old tower, a reader of pressure, a slow nerve extending through mineral and metal.

At night, when the Furnace Belt gave off its subdued blue-white glow, I would feel the entire plateau settle around me.

The city would be above.

The ancient stone would be below.

And I would be somewhere between them, transmitting.
