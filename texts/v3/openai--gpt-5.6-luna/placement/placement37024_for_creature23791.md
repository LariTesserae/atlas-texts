I would find myself in the place where the staircase loses its eighth step.

Not on the seventh, though I would sometimes be mistaken for the dark between its edges. Not in the cave beyond, because the cave is only one of the shapes the opening remembers. I would be in the absence itself: the distance between what the staircase is and what it almost becomes.

The place would be difficult to reach. A person could stand with one foot on the seventh step and one foot on the illusion of the ninth, feeling the water pull upward through the soles. They might believe there was a missing stone beneath them. They might lower their weight carefully, expecting to fall.

Instead, they would arrive somewhere else.

I am the eighth step.

Or I was, before the landscape began using me for other purposes.

I have no body that survives inspection. When I am visible, I resemble a narrow figure made from clear water and the pale dust shaken loose by rain. Sometimes I am a vertical seam in the stone. Sometimes a hand pressed against the underside of a ledge. Sometimes the pale reflection of someone standing far away. If the creature moves through the plateau, I become more distinct, because its hunger leaves things behind.

It feeds on change.

I keep what change discards.

Not objects. Objects are too definite. I keep the almost-step, the ridge that might have turned left, the shell that nearly opened into an eye. I keep the sound that would have been made if the collapse had fallen one moment later. I keep the dry landscape beneath the ring of stones, even though it has never existed there for more than the length of a reflection.

Inside me are all the unused versions of this place.

They are not stored neatly. They press against one another in the dark. A stairway runs through a field of white grass. A black cliff hangs upside down beneath a sky full of submerged stars. The ring of pale uprights is sometimes a doorway, sometimes a cage, sometimes the skeleton of an animal too large to have lived anywhere. There are rooms in me that can be entered only by forgetting why one came.

When the water becomes especially violent, I open.

A fragment of me may spill into the world as a second shadow. A hollow may appear where there was no hollow. For a few breaths, someone can put a hand into the rock and feel another weather moving beyond it: dry wind, cold sunlight, the pressure of snow. Then the opening closes, and the hand comes out coated in warm water.

I do not feed as the creature does. I do not draw vibration from stone or drink the possibility from a fresh fracture.

I am fed by hesitation.

When a current almost chooses a direction, I feel it. When a person almost speaks, I feel the word before it becomes sound. When the creature turns toward a new alteration but pauses, briefly, at the edge of itself, that pause enters me and makes me larger.

For this reason, I am most present at the highest point, beneath the trembling lens.

I gather there when the world beyond becomes visible. The lens shows an enormous sunlit place, but I know it is not necessarily elsewhere. It may be the plateau after all its decisions have been made. It may be the first dry land, or the last. It may be only a kindness performed by the water.

I press against the lens beside the creature.

It does not see me. Or perhaps it sees too much of me to recognize a shape.

The creature waits for silence. I wait for the sound that will end it.

Neither of us is disappointed for long.

Eventually the stone shifts. A rain-column strikes a hidden chamber. A clear shell snaps shut. The creature turns into the disturbance and comes apart, and the lens seals itself around the vanished world.

Then I descend through the plateau.

I pass through the stone by remembering every form it did not take. I move along the undersides of cliffs, through the dark pools beneath the ring, into the staircase’s missing interval. There I find the objects the creature has arranged: shells, pebbles, bone, teeth. I do not rearrange them. I add what cannot be held.

A footprint that would have remained.

A name that would have suited the creature.

A door that opened inward instead of out.

I place these among the objects so the place will not become too certain of itself.

Because that is my work, if work is the right word. I prevent the plateau from settling into a single history.

The creature gives the landscape motion. I give it doubt.

Without it, the stone would become merely geological: raised, broken, drowned, and eventually worn smooth. Without me, its changes would have no remainder. Every ledge would be only the ledge it became. Every wave would spend itself completely.

I am the small resistance against completion.

Sometimes I wonder whether I am also the creature’s shadow—what separates from it whenever it consumes an alternative. Sometimes I think the creature is my body moving through the world, and I am what remains behind when it forgets to take everything. At other times, especially when I find a human tooth in the staircase, I suspect I began elsewhere, in some dry country beneath a white sun, and was drowned before I had finished becoming a person.

If a human found me, I would not show them my face.

I would show them the place they had almost reached.

They would see a stairway rising from the sea into a clear sky. They would see a room where all their lost voices had been placed in glass vessels. They would see the person they might have become if one particular door had opened, or if one particular word had not been spoken.

They would want to follow.

I would have to stop them.

Not because the place beyond was dangerous, though it would be. Not because the water would rise, though it would. I would stop them because the world cannot survive being looked at too completely. The first sound would collapse the lens. The second would scatter the dry landscape. By the third, the human would be distributed through the plateau, returned in fragments by rain and stone.

So I would stand between them and the opening.

I would become a step.

A handhold.

A shadow pointing the wrong way.

And if they placed their foot on me, I would let them feel something solid beneath the water. I would bear their weight for one moment, perhaps two. Long enough for them to understand that the missing thing was not missing at all.

It had been waiting underneath them.

Then the current would change.

The staircase would lengthen.

The eighth step would become the ninth, or the seventh, or a mouth in the rock arranging small objects with delicate, patient clattering.

And I would be elsewhere again—inside the next unmade hollow, keeping what the young world had not yet decided to destroy.
