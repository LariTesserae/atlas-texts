I would find myself in the **Middle Gardens**, where the water is deep enough to swim through but shallow enough that the roofs still offer somewhere to stand.

I would be a **listener of thresholds**.

Not an elder, though people would sometimes mistake me for one. I would be young enough to climb quickly and old enough to know when not to. My hands would be broad and darkened by mineral light, my feet scarred from warm basalt. I would wear a cord around my waist with tools tied along it: a clay weight, a shell cup, a knife, three lengths of bright fiber, and a small bell that I almost never rang.

My work would be to find the passages that did not yet exist.

At dawn I would move through the city alone, following the places where water behaved strangely. I would press my palms to walls, roofs, staircases, and sealed floors. I would listen for the difference between stone settling and stone preparing itself. A wall that trembled beneath my hand might be about to crack, or it might be growing. A stair might be gaining a step. A dry room might soon become the mouth of a new channel.

I would mark these possibilities with knots.

One knot for rising stone.  
Two for a new current.  
A loop left open for danger that had not chosen its shape.

My maps would be made of cord stretched between roofs and tied to roots, chimneys, bells, and posts. They would never look finished. A route could be accurate in the morning and useless by evening. This would not trouble me. A map in my city would not be a picture of the world. It would be a conversation with what the world might do next.

People would come to me quietly.

A mother would ask whether the sleeping chamber beneath her house was still safe. A builder would bring me a wedge of black stone and ask whether the warmth inside it was ordinary. Children would lead me to a courtyard where a puddle had begun to climb the wall. I would kneel, taste the water, place my ear against the plaster, and tell them whether to move the lamps, the sleeping mats, or nothing at all.

Often the answer would be: wait.

I would live in a room with three entrances and no complete wall. One entrance would open onto a roof garden. One would descend into a flooded workshop. The third would lead to a narrow passage that sometimes ended in stone and sometimes continued into another district. My bed would be tied to the floor but not tightly. My shelves would have gaps between them. In the wet season, roots would enter through the ceiling and grow beside my sleeping place.

I would keep a collection of things that had changed.

A tile that had developed a red seam.  
A stone that had risen through a market floor.  
A broken stair-step from a street that no longer descended.  
A bead from a dead child’s bracelet, returned years later from beneath the upper water.

I would not call these relics. I would call them **evidence**.

At night I would climb to the highest roof I could reach and sit beneath the suspended sea. The green-gold light would pass through the water above me. Rain would rise from the streets in silver threads. Sometimes a shadow would cross the ceiling, so large and slow that I would feel it in my ribs before I saw it.

I would wonder whether it was an animal, a mountain, or some future part of the city moving overhead.

Perhaps I would be more than a listener. Perhaps the city would use me as one of its senses.

When I placed my hand against a wall, I would feel not only what it was doing now but the pressure of what it might become: an arch, a channel, a buried room, a garden root, a doorway opening onto a street that had never been laid. I would hear voices traveling through unborn buildings. Sometimes someone would call my name from beyond a surface of stone, and I would have to decide whether to answer.

My name would be **Open Step**.

It would have been given to me after the stair beneath my childhood home gained an extra step during one night of rain. Everyone else stumbled over it. I was the first to notice that the new step did not lead upward or downward.

It led somewhere sideways.

Since then, I would have learned to trust the places that did not fit.

I would not own a house, a garden, or even a fixed route through the city. I would belong to the spaces between them: the slack in a bridge, the gap left in a wall, the moment before a stone fell. When people asked where I lived, I would answer with a level, a current, and the nearest sound.

“Between the Upper Reed and the third bell,” I would say.

Then, if they asked again, I would tell them:

“I live where the city is deciding.”
