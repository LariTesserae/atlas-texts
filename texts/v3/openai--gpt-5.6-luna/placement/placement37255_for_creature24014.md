You would find yourself at the edge of the city, though there is no true edge.

Behind you, the last ordinary ground has already begun to close. The soil is lifting in pale ridges. Fine roots are combing through your footprints, not erasing them but taking their measure. Ahead, the vegetation rises into streets, chambers, and suspended crossings. Somewhere above, a canopy is drawing itself shut against rain that has not yet fallen.

At first you would believe yourself a traveler.

You would have a body distinct from the ground: weight, balance, breath. You would place one foot on the living pavement and feel it yield—not like mud, but like muscle. The surface would thicken beneath you. A narrow path would brighten through the leaves. Its direction would seem chosen, though you would not know by whom.

You would walk.

The city would begin to notice the shape of your attention. Where you looked, openings would appear. A wall would arch. A stair would raise itself from the broad central vein of a leaf. When you hesitated before a dark passage, the passage would lengthen until hesitation itself became a district: a cool corridor, roofed in translucent membranes, with roots turning slowly in the walls.

You might call this hospitality.

The plants would not.

To them, you would be pressure, warmth, disturbance, pattern. A temporary concentration of weather. They would make room for you in the same way they made room for water, and with the same underlying intention: not to possess, exactly, but to continue through you.

You would discover that the city does not ask where you are going. It asks what shape your going has.

If you returned often to one chamber, its walls would become more like a room. If you crossed the same stretch of ground, it would harden into a road. If you feared a particular tower, the tower would grow nearer. If you remembered a door, the city would produce one. Your habits would become architecture.

Gradually, your distinction would erode.

Not your body at first. Your borders.

The mist would cling to you longer than it should. Green dust would settle in the folds of your clothes. Threads would find the seams beneath your skin—or perhaps only the places where you imagined seams to be. You would begin to feel distant events locally: a root snapping far below, a reservoir opening in another district, a young wall leaning toward a pool. Your pulse would no longer seem entirely yours. Beneath it, immense vascular columns would be drawing water.

You would understand that the settlement had been giving you pathways because it had mistaken you for a future structure.

This would not be an error.

In this world, everything that arrives becomes part of the arrangement. Water becomes a leaf. Pressure becomes a road. A collapse becomes a wetland. Attention becomes form.

So you would become the city’s newest organ—not a citizen, because there are no citizens, and not a ruler, because no one commands the growth. You would be a **remembering passage**.

Wherever you moved, the plants would retain the pattern of your movement. They would grow corridors that anticipated your return. They would preserve chambers shaped by your pauses. Your uncertainty would become branching streets; your longing, a gradual slope toward somewhere that might not exist. The plain would use your inner life as a kind of architectural pressure.

Eventually you would no longer know whether you were walking through the city or causing it to unfold.

You might still possess eyes. They might still open on ordinary sunlight, on wet leaves, on a tower whose shadow is that of a sapling. But sight would become less a way of seeing than a way of feeding. To look at an empty place would invite it to become occupied. To name a doorway would give it a threshold. To imagine escape would cause the vegetation beyond the settlement to arrange itself into a route.

And perhaps, after enough time, you would find yourself standing in a plaza you recognized without remembering having visited it.

The ground beneath you would pulse.

A wall would open.

Inside would be a chamber lined with soft green light, warm and damp, its floor rising gently toward a central hollow. The plants would have made it for you—or from you. Along its walls, faint ridges would preserve the pattern of your ribs. A vine would cross the ceiling in the shape of a thought you once had. Outside, rain would begin from the leaves themselves.

You would enter because the chamber was waiting.

Or because you were.

By then, you would not be the traveler who came to the plain. You would be one of its minor certainties: the path that forms under attention, the room that grows around repeated shelter, the living bridge between one district and another.

And when the whole city-forest turned toward water, you would turn with it—not as a person among plants, but as one of the ways the body knew where it needed to grow.
