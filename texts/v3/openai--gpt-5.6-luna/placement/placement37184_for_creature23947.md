I would find myself in the country beneath the country: far below any root, foundation, or buried ocean, in a chamber whose ceiling has ceased to matter.

I would be one of the makers—the tall, narrow people of volcanic skin who live beside the glowing seams. Not a hunter, and not one of the attendants of the Stillness. I would be a path-keeper.

My shelter would stand alone beside a ridge of young black stone. It would have no door, only a threshold: a strip of ground graded so carefully that my feet could recognize it in complete darkness. Inside, the walls would be marked with pressure-signs: the waking of a wall-dweller, the passage of a broad pale animal, the collapse of a distant plate-field. I would sleep with one hand against the floor and dream in tremors.

I would carry a hooked staff, worn smooth where my palm had held it through many crossings. Its hook would test the crust before me. Its shaft would tap warnings into the ground. I would know the safe routes not by sight but by the way the stone answered weight.

I would travel between the small settlements, replacing fallen markers and opening paths through newly hardened ground. I would learn which ridges were merely ridges and which were sleeping bodies. I would know the places where the air turned sweet enough to numb the throat, where the plates rang beneath a foot, where a buried creature listened without yet waking.

I would rarely speak. When I did, my voice would be a low pressure in the ribs, felt before it was heard. I would identify others by the disturbances they made: the familiar warmth of a household member, the cautious rhythm of a stranger, the immense and almost motionless attention of something that had been in the wall since before the first shelter.

I would not fear the darkness. Darkness would be the ordinary condition of things, and light would be only a small personal courtesy—a dim mineral lamp held close to the body, illuminating the next step and no farther. To cast light across the plain would feel vulgar, almost violent. The country would have its own privacy, and I would belong to it.

When someone died, I would help carry them to a warm hollow and set their staff upright beside them. I would return over the years to feel the change in the ground: skin becoming stone, joints vanishing, memory thinning into pressure. I would not believe that the dead had gone elsewhere. There is nowhere else. They would simply become more thoroughly present in the country.

At the edge of the forest of needles, I would sometimes serve as an attendant. I would bring offerings from distant places—colored dust, worked metal, a tool whose owner no longer needed it—and leave them beneath the first warm pillars. I would not enter. I would not ask what lived among the hanging bodies. I would only sit and listen.

If one of our offerings disappeared, I would not search for it.

If something appeared in its place, I would not touch it.

My life would be the refinement of attention. I would become old not by counting years but by losing weight, softness, and unnecessary motion. Eventually I would need a niche in the wall. My hands would remain pressed against the stone, advising those who passed through by means of small changes in warmth.

And one day, perhaps, the floor would begin to remember me.

A traveler would place a foot where I had once stood and pause, sensing a difference beneath the crust: not a voice, not a warning, but the faint impression of someone who had known the country well.

I would be that impression.

Not a conqueror of the underground continent. Not its discoverer.

A temporary person teaching the stone how to remember.
