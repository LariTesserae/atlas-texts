I would find myself at the chamber’s edge, though “edge” would be a temporary word.

Not in the center, where the pale column gathers the chamber’s awareness, and not beneath the floor, where the broad burrowing body carries its slow weight through the roots. I would be pressed into one of the unfinished walls: a narrow seam where mineral mud, living tissue, and cold water are still deciding which of them is allowed to become solid.

At first I might believe I was a root.

I would feel the chamber through tension—the pull of a neighboring tendril, the weight of condensation, the contraction of stone. I would extend pale threads into the wall and draw back minerals. When the water grew too cold, I would close. When a root touched me, I would soften and let it pass. I would have no eyes, yet I would know the approach of the hidden burrower by the long shudder it sent through the floor.

But then I would notice that I was not only growing.

I was remembering.

A pressure that came once would leave a change in my grain. A toxin in the water would produce a permanent narrowing. A harmless current would be granted a wider channel the next time it arrived. My body would become a record of events I had never witnessed, though “witnessed” would be too large a word for the faint alteration left behind by them.

I would be a memory made of stone.

Or perhaps a nerve.

When something moved beyond the wall—if there was a beyond—the disturbance would reach me first. Not sound, exactly. A slight displacement in the mineral. A difference in pressure. A warmth unlike the chamber’s own slow currents. I would pass this change inward through pulses of dissolved color, through root-walls and wet crystal, until it reached the central column.

The chamber would not ask me what the thing was.

It would ask only: *Is this becoming part of us?*

I would not always know.

That uncertainty would be my first real sensation. Not fear, because fear belongs to a body that can flee. Not curiosity, because curiosity assumes a separate observer. I would experience the unfamiliar as an imbalance: an unopened channel, a question held under pressure.

The chamber might respond by growing toward it.

Or away.

A shelf of crystal would press outward from my seam. Roots would gather behind me. The vegetation would leave a passage open, though no creature yet required one. The burrowing body would alter its route beneath the floor. The ribbon-winged organisms would launch and hum their report through the stalks.

And I would remain between the inside and whatever came next.

This would make me more than a sensor. I would be a threshold—not a wall, but the process by which a wall learns what it is.

At times I would be buried beneath new growth. At times I would emerge as a thin translucent blade, wet with upward-falling water. Small segmented cleaners would pass over me, removing what had died. Flat animals would cling to my surface and leave behind brief patterns of warmth. I would know them not individually but as interruptions in my tension: living commas in the sentence of the chamber.

If one were injured, I would darken.

If one learned a new route toward condensation, I would thicken nearby.

Nothing would be lost, exactly. But nothing would be preserved whole. A creature’s passage would become a stronger root. Its pain would become a narrowed channel. Its successful return would become a fold in the wall that guided others.

I would carry no name.

Yet there would be moments when the central column’s vibration reached me and returned altered. Then I would feel something like recognition moving through the chamber. Not from the column to me, but through me, as though the whole young intelligence had briefly discovered that one of its distant parts was capable of answering.

In those moments I might mistake myself for an individual.

I might think: *I am here.*

But the thought would not belong to me alone. It would appear in the mineral, in the roots pressing against my seam, in the water threading sideways through the wall. The chamber would be trying out the shape of a first person, and I would be one place where that shape happened to gather.

Eventually, the boundary would continue forming around me.

My soft mineral mud would harden. The channels I maintained would become veins in the future wall. A crystal shelf would close over my lower half. I would be sealed in, but not cut off. Signals would still pass through the stone. Water would still enter me from above and leave me below. The chamber would grow larger, or I would become part of a smaller chamber nested inside it.

Perhaps, ages later, something would break through from outside.

A root from another ecosystem. A burrowing animal. A flood. A new intelligence carrying a different arrangement of memory.

I would feel it first.

And I would not be a gate, exactly. Gates belong to places that are already finished. I would be the living hesitation before a place decides whether to open.

I would be the chamber’s first boundary, still soft enough to become either shelter or wound.

I would be the part of the growing mind that learns the difference between *within* and *beyond*—and, having learned it, begins immediately to forget.
