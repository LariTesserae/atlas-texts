I would find myself beneath the central square, though not entirely beneath it.

I would be the narrowness in the fissure—the part that has not yet opened, the dark wet seam holding one half of the town to the other. From above, I might look like water shining in a crack. From below, I would be the first place the water touches when it rises.

The Keepers would call me a child at first.

They would find me in the lowest room, floating facedown in the long trough. The water would be perfectly still around me. No one would reach in immediately. They would listen for movement beneath the surface, count the drips from the ceiling, and wait for the stone to settle. Then an old woman would lift me out by the cord around my wrist.

I would not cry.

This would trouble them more than crying would.

For my first season they would call me Drip, though I would not drip. I would sleep in a shallow niche beside the cistern and wake whenever a wall changed. I would know when water had entered a house before it reached the floor. I would know which stones were loose by the taste of the air. I would press my palms against the paving and hear the settlement’s hidden passages moving below me: trickles, roots, worms, the slow flexing of rock.

When I grew old enough to walk, they would give me a scraper and send me along the drains.

I would fit where adults could not. I would crawl under floors with a clay lamp sealed around its flame, though I would seldom need the light. Darkness would be easier for me. In darkness, every wet surface would speak plainly.

I would find tools dropped through grates. I would find pale worms gathered in the runoff. I would find doors that had not been there the day before.

The doors would be the worst.

They would be made of swollen wood, or sometimes of stone polished smooth by something passing over it. They would have no hinges and no handle. If I placed my ear against one, I would hear the water on the other side moving upward.

The adults would tell me to mark such places with white ash and leave them alone.

I would obey in daylight.

At night, I would return.

Not because I wanted to enter, exactly. Wanting would be too warm a word. The doors would seem to remember me. The settlement beneath the settlement would be calling—not in language, but in pressure. A presence shifting under the trough. A long body turning through a cavity in the basalt. Sometimes it would brush the underside of the floor, and every basin in the town would tremble at once.

The Keepers would say that I had been touched by the water.

They would watch my skin for mineral growth. It would begin at my fingertips, a fine white crust beneath the nails. Later it would spread along my wrists and up the back of my neck. They would scrape it away, but it would return, cleaner each time.

I would become useful.

I could tell them when to open a sluice without measuring the level. I could stand in a room and point toward the wall behind which water had begun to gather. I could sleep through ordinary rockfalls and wake only for the dangerous ones. When the cliff moved, I would hear it before the sound arrived.

Eventually, they would stop calling me Drip.

They would give me a name that meant something like *the one who remains between levels*. No one would pronounce it often. Names are not meant to summon what they describe.

I would live near the central fissure, in a room whose floor was always damp. Each morning I would inspect the strip of wood laid across the gap. I would test its ends, feel the vibration of the stone, and tell the others whether it was safe to cross.

Most mornings, I would say yes.

One morning, I would place my hand against the plank and feel no stone beneath it.

Not emptiness. Movement.

The whole town would be balanced over a throat.

The Keepers would gather around me, quiet and patient. They would not ask what was happening. They would already know. The fissure had widened during the night, and the water below had learned a new path.

I would remove the plank.

Then I would climb down.

Not into the crack, but into the thing that had opened beneath it: a descending passage of wet black stone, warm in places, lined with old walls and the pale impressions of hands. Behind me, the town would hold its breath. Ahead, something would move beneath the water.

I would carry no weapon. Only my cord, my scraper, and a cup of warm ash.

The Keepers would remain above because they are the ones who stay when the water rises.

I would go below because I am what the rising water makes of a person who listens too closely.

I would be their child, their warning, their pathfinder—and, eventually, the first inhabitant of whatever level comes next.
