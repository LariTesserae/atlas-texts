I would find myself in the reservoir’s western half, where the light is strongest and the current has not yet gathered all its force.

I would be a level-keeper: one of the people whose task is to know the water before it becomes dangerous.

Not its ruler. Not its owner. I would carry no authority beyond the next gate and the next marked pillar. My work would be to walk the submerged steps with one hand on the rail, feeling the water rise around my calves, my knees, my ribs. I would know the safe levels not only by the white marks on the masonry but by the pressure in my ears, the drag of my harness, the tremor running through the floor.

My body would be part of the measuring equipment. I would have broad, wrinkled hands and feet hardened by constant immersion. My skin would smell of warm water, lamp smoke, reed fiber, and the faint metallic residue of the gates. I would wear a rope around my waist, not as clothing but as a second nervous system. Somewhere above, another worker would hold its end. If I disappeared beneath the glare, they would feel the direction of my movements through the tension.

I would begin each shift by touching the southern pillar.

It is the one with the crooked replacement block. The new stone is brighter than the rest and set slightly out of true. When the pressure changes, it gives a small, almost inaudible click. I would press my palm to it and wait. If the click came once, the chamber was holding. If it came twice, I would signal the wheel crew to slow the lift-buckets. Three times, and everyone would move toward the eastern gates.

I would be good at waiting.

I would wait for the surface to stop trembling before reading the level. I would wait for a flame to settle before deciding whether a shaft was drawing air. I would wait beside a blocked grate with my hook lowered into the current, feeling for the obstruction rather than attacking it blindly. The reservoir would teach me that force is rarely the same thing as speed. Water remembers every hurried mistake.

At first I would have been a lamp-tender, a child on the upper ledges, keeping mica screens clear and wicks trimmed. I would have learned the chamber through brightness: the gold descending from the western shafts, the blue held beneath the arches, the long wavering reflections cast by every person who crossed the water. I would have known the lamps before I knew the gates.

Now I would work below.

I would move through the chamber chest-deep, guiding a floating frame toward the eastern grate. The current would push steadily against my shoulders. Behind me, the wheel would groan and turn. Ahead, the water would pour through the bars in a white, boiling sheet. My companions would be near but not visible in any reliable way: a hand emerging from glare, a rope tightening, the dark shape of a body broken into reflections.

We would not speak unless we had to. I would slap the water once to warn the worker at the frame. They would answer by striking the pillar with a mallet. The sound would pass through the masonry, blunt and delayed, arriving in my bones before it reached my ears.

I would understand the reservoir as a living arrangement without believing it was alive.

The eastern wheel would be stubborn. The western shafts would be generous. The lower stairs would remember floods. These would not be superstitions, exactly. They would be the language people use for things they attend to every day. A machine maintained long enough becomes a companion, even if it has no mind behind its noise.

At night, I would sit with the others on the raised platform, my legs dangling above the basin while someone brought grain cakes and salt. The lamps would multiply us in the water. Each worker would have an inverted double below, walking through the submerged galleries with a slight delay. I would watch that second self more than the person beside me.

Not because I thought it was another being.

Because it would show me whether I was standing still.

The reservoir would be my world in the most literal sense: enclosed by foundations, bounded by masonry, bright at the center and dark only beyond the reach of work. I would not dream of escaping into the soil or climbing toward the settlement above. Dryness would feel strange, unfinished. A room without water pressing against its walls would seem vulnerable, almost naked.

I would be one of the keepers, then—but also one of the things being kept.

The water would sustain the settlement, and the settlement would sustain us. We would clear its grates, feed its reeds, mend its plaster, trim its lamps, and listen for the first change in its breathing. If we neglected it, the pressure would rise. If it failed, our lives would become scattered and dry.

So I would remain below, moving from pillar to pillar in the warm current, hand on the rope, palm against the crooked stone, waiting for the next quiet click.
