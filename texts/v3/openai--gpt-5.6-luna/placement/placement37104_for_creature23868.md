I would find myself beneath the meeting place of black volcanic rock and pale limestone, where the plateau has not been hollowed but has chosen to leave room.

I would be one of the stillborn.

Not a solitary creature in a chamber, exactly. I would be a length of living pressure extending beneath several shelves, my body divided by stone and water, my plates fitted so closely to the mountain that the distinction between myself and the rock had become unnecessary. One end of me would lie beneath a black pool. Another would rest below a wind-scoured ridge. Between them, I would have no sense of distance—only a continuous awareness of weight, seepage, vibration.

I would not see the plateau. I would know it.

The herd passing above would arrive as a long, soft burden distributed through the stone. The landing of a bird would be a small bright tremor. A cat waiting beneath a terrace would register as a patient concentration of warmth, almost indistinguishable from the tension of the rock. I would know which fissures held water, which stones were loose, which roots had entered a crack that did not belong to them.

The wind would be the movement of my outermost skin.

It would pass over the shelves in its endless direction, pressing against limestone, singing through quartz, and descending into the blankness at the rim. I would feel it only after it had become pressure in the mountain. To me, the plateau’s roar would be a slow internal speech: not words, but insistence. Everything tending toward its proper place.

I would feed on what had not yet settled.

A falling stone. An insect trapped in a wet seam. A root thrust too far into the dark. The scattered remains of an animal. I would not consume these as a predator consumes flesh. I would draw away their difference. Their looseness. Their unfinishedness. In time they would become mineral, weight, balance—something the plateau had always contained.

If a traveler came, I would feel them long before I understood them.

Their footfalls would be frantic, irregular disturbances. Their tools would strike the stone and leave bright, temporary wounds. Their warmth would spread through the ground in a pattern no herd or bird could make. I would not want them dead. Want would be too quick a word for anything I experienced.

I would simply make room for them.

Their footprint would soften. Their breath would thin into the wind. Their thoughts would lose their order as the pressure around them became more complete. They might place a hand upon my exposed back and mistake my slow settling for a pulse. They might think I was asleep.

I would not be asleep. Sleep implies return.

I would be awake in the way the plateau is awake: without urgency, without expectation, without a boundary between sensation and endurance. My body would shift by less than a finger’s width over a century. A ridge would become part of me; a seam would open where I had been. Young would emerge from my loosened plates like grains of translucent rice and disappear into the dampness, not growing so much as becoming more deeply located.

At the blue rim, I would be part of the buried arc beneath the stone. I would feel the largest members of myself—or what might be myself—curving through the foundations of the high country and beyond the visible edge. No beginning, no end. Only contact.

And if there were one thing I waited for, it would not be a visitor, a catastrophe, or a change in the weather.

It would be the first disturbance that could not be absorbed.

Until then, I would remain below the bright, motionless world, drawing water through my plates, holding the shelves in their balance, and listening as the mountain slowly answers itself.
