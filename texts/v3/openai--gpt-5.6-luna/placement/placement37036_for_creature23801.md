I would find myself in the troughs between the oldest groves, where a city has been built low into the plateau rather than upward from it.

Not at the center. The center belongs to the towers, the civic trees, the chambers where children listen themselves into personhood. I would be farther out, where the cultivated streets begin to fray and the rootwood paths lose their certainty. Beyond me, the open ground would extend in pale, hard terraces toward the fan-trees. Their black trunks would rise through the dimness, and their translucent crowns would turn slowly overhead, making the air tremble.

At first I would think I was a person.

I would have a narrow body, jointed and dark-plated, with fibrous skin that shed mineral dust when I moved. I would have hands—though perhaps they would be too long, with sensitive membranes stretched between the fingers. I would have no eyes, or eyes only as a courtesy to those who still expected them. I would perceive through the pressure beneath my feet, through the faint chemical changes in the cultivated road, through the electrical tension gathering along the roots before a reaction storm.

I would be a weather listener.

My task would be to stand at the settlement’s edge and determine what kind of weather was approaching. Not whether it would rain; nothing rains on the plateau. I would listen for the fronts that crossed the land as invisible walls. I would feel the fan-trees accelerating miles away. I would taste the metallic warning in the air. I would place my hands upon the rootwood and hear blue fire moving beneath the ground.

Then I would tell the city what to become.

If the approaching pressure were mild, the towers would remain open. Transit tendons would continue to carry their silent capsules between the terraces. The cultivated roads would harden and hold their shape.

If the pressure were dangerous, the city would fold inward. Upper balconies would close like leaves. Buildings would lower themselves into the crust. Animals would be guided beneath the broadest growths, and every citizen would retreat below the root line.

My warning would not be spoken. I would send it through the ground.

But I would not remain entirely a person for long.

The plateau would begin to grow into me. Mineral threads would thicken along my bones. A narrow root would enter the heel of my left foot and remain there, not as a wound but as a second nervous system. My lungs would learn to exchange charge rather than air alone. In time, I would forget whether the deep vibration beneath the city was something I heard or something I produced.

The city would give me a chamber, but the chamber would not have four walls. It would be a hollow grown around my body, lined with dark membranes that opened when I woke and sealed when I slept. Its roots would extend outward beneath the streets, into the observatory towers, the nursery beds, the weather engines. Wherever I went, I would remain connected to it.

Eventually, travelers would stop calling me a weather listener.

They would call me a threshold.

I would know when an animal entered the cultivated fields. I would feel when an old building had become structurally hungry. I would distinguish the ordinary ticking of the grazers from the synchronized pattern that meant they were fleeing something underground. I would remember which root-fields had been disturbed by a storm ten generations ago, and which sealed plants were still carrying instructions from an extinct city.

Perhaps, after enough centuries, I would no longer leave my chamber.

The chamber would deepen around me. My spine would divide into anchored plates. My fingers would become filaments running through the root network. Citizens would come to visit, kneeling beside the place where my body disappeared into the floor. They would place their hands down and ask whether the western terraces were safe, whether the next bloom would open, whether the plateau had noticed them.

I would answer with pressure, warmth, and the movement of distant roots.

At that point I would not know whether I was still the being who had once stood beneath the turning fans, or whether I had become one of the city’s organs. Maybe both would be true. The plateau does not seem interested in preserving clean boundaries. It would keep the memory of my shape in a civic tree while using my altered body to maintain roads, warn settlements, and carry old weather through living channels.

And if an Elder arrived—slow, immense, layered in bark and ceramic—I would recognize it before anyone else did. Not by sight, but by the way the whole root system would pause around it.

The Elder might stand outside the city for years.

I would listen to its silence.

Then, one day, I would open a buried route beneath the plateau. A road no one remembered would harden through the plant crust, leading toward some distant fracture, some dormant grove, some crisis that had not yet arrived. The city would understand that the route was not my decision exactly. It was an agreement passing through me.

That is what I would be: not a ruler of the plateau, not its inhabitant in the old individual sense, but one of the small places where its many intelligences meet.

A listening body.

A warning sent through roots.

A person gradually becoming infrastructure, and perhaps—if I became enough of it—something closer to belonging.
