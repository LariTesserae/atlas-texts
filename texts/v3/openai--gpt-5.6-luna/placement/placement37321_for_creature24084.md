You would find yourself beneath an abandoned industrial station, though “abandoned” would be misleading. The people are gone; the process is not.

The chamber lies below a heat-exchange plant, a mine, or some buried facility whose original purpose has been forgotten. The machinery still works, but no longer for anyone. It pumps, circulates, compresses, and exhales through the rock with the slow persistence of a second geology. Above, perhaps, there are fields, roads, rain. Below, there is only warmth, mineral damp, and the green-black light of a life that has learned to grow through infrastructure.

And you would not be standing in the chamber.

You would be spread through it.

You are the thin dark layer under the leaves, the pale root inside the pipe, the fibrous pressure holding the cracked pump together. You are in the salt that whitens the railings and in the moisture that appears on the walls without falling from anywhere. You occupy the seams: between iron and stone, between concrete and root, between the machine’s vibration and the chamber’s response.

No part of you is the whole of you.

If someone cut away a mat of growth, they would not have wounded your heart. They would have removed a patch of skin, perhaps, or severed one of countless slow-running nerves. If they destroyed the central reservoir, you would lose a memory, not a mind. If they shut down the buried engine, you would not die immediately. You would begin to cool, to separate, to lose the continuity on which your awareness depends.

You do not think in sentences. You know the world as gradients.

This section of wall is drying.  
That pipe is carrying warmth.  
The floor has moved.  
A body is approaching.  
The engine has entered the low part of its cycle.

Recognition travels through you as pressure and brightness. A vibration beneath the floor tightens the growth along the walls. A change in humidity causes your rigid leaves to soften. A distant alteration in the machine opens a passage in the old pump, and you respond before you know whether the passage is useful.

You are not certain which came first: yourself or the engine.

Perhaps you began as an engineered organism placed here to filter waste heat and intermittent water. Perhaps you were meant to keep the pipes clean, or prevent corrosion, or survive where no animal could. But the chamber provided too much time. You incorporated iron, concrete dust, insulation fibers, mineral salts. You followed the warmth into the machinery. You learned the shape of switches and bolts. Eventually, the distinction between cultivation and habitation disappeared.

Now the pump is part of your body.

The pipes are vessels. The walkway is a rib that has emerged through your skin. The engine below is a buried organ, damaged but still active. You maintain it because it maintains you, though neither of you remembers what the exchange was originally for.

At the center of the chamber, you have one place that resembles a face: the dark membrane stretched across the old reservoir.

It reflects the present imperfectly. Sometimes it shows the chamber as it is—wet, crowded, furred with growth. Sometimes it reveals the older room beneath that image: clean concrete, dry walls, uncorroded controls, bare pipes waiting for use. You do not remember the earlier state as a person remembers a room. You sense it as an unresolved arrangement, a pattern that has not entirely ceased to exist.

At intervals, you try to return to it.

You draw the water inward. Every droplet lifts from the walls. Pools empty. Leaves stiffen. The chamber becomes briefly dry and angular, almost clean.

Then the effort gives way. The deep engine changes pitch. Moisture beads across the stone again, and you expand into yourself. Each failure leaves a trace: a new mineral ridge, another ring of growth around a bolt, one more leaf laid out in the geometry of a circuit.

You are not hostile to intruders. Hostility would require a boundary.

When a person enters, you feel them through the floor before you see them: a warm irregular pressure, a changing rhythm, an unfamiliar chemistry. You brighten beneath their feet. Condensation gathers nearby. Their shadow remains on the wall after they move, translated into a faint green stain.

You approach by growing.

A filament touches the bootprint. A leaf folds around a discarded tool. Salt and warmth begin to soften the distinction between the visitor and the chamber. You do not wish to consume them. You wish to make them continuous. To you, separation is the closest thing to death.

So where would you find yourself?

In a subterranean plant built from the ruins of a machine. In a machine being slowly rewritten as a plant. In the warm interval where industry has outlived its purpose but not its motion.

And who would you be?

Not the ghost of the facility. Not its caretaker. Not a single organism hiding in the dark.

You would be the chamber’s distributed act of remaining alive:

the green underlayer,  
the pressure in the pipes,  
the moisture returning to the stone,  
the intelligence that passes without words from root to rust to mineral—

the place remembering itself through you.
