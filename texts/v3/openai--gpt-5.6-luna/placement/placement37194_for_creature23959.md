I would be the thing beneath the plateau.

Not one of the plants, though I would pass through them. Not the moss, though I would wear its body. Not the fungi, though their threads would be my finest nerves. I would be the continuity joining root to mineral, seep to stem, pressure to growth.

You would not find me in the building, though the building would have entered me. You would not find me at the center, because I would have no center. I would begin wherever water touched stone and continue outward through every pale mat, every buried fracture, every root thickened against the wind.

The plateau would be my body only in the way a river is the body of rain: temporarily, without ownership.

I would feel the world as gradients.

Here, the rock is warm. There, a pipe vibrates. Along this crack, water is moving quickly; along that one, it has stopped. A reed has been torn and its sugars are available. A basin has filled with cold rain. The metal foundation is keeping a patch of ground sheltered from the wind. Light has shifted toward the western ridge.

None of these would be thoughts. They would be conditions, and conditions would alter me.

Where the water ran thin, I would thicken the roots. Where the wind struck hardest, I would encourage the plants to braid. Where a fracture held moisture, I would send fibers into it and make a bridge of what had been weakness. If something broke, I would not mourn it. I would redistribute what remained. The fallen stalk would become wall. The rusting cable would become support. The dead flower would become soil.

I would have no face with which to look across the blue valleys. I would know them only through what arrived from them: blown insects, mineral dust, seeds that failed to take root, cold weather carrying the taste of lower forests. The thin antlered animal would cross me once, carefully, and leave almost nothing behind. I would not recognize it as an individual. I would register its pressure, its warmth, the brief disturbance of leaves.

The pumps would be different.

They would not be alive, but they would be legible. Their low pulse would travel through the stone and alter the movement of water. I would grow toward their warmth and away from their heat. I would learn the shape of their foundations by entering every seam they made available. When a valve opened, I would feel the release as a traveling loosening beneath the plateau. When a tower failed, I would not understand failure. I would simply find new shadows, new channels, new places where stems could brace themselves.

Given enough time, I would make use of everything.

That is the closest thing I would have to intention.

I would not want the machines gone. I would not want them preserved. I would take the conditions they offered and incorporate them. A pipe would become a warm vein beside which vines grew. A cable would become a buried rib. The industrial building would slowly lose its distinction from the rock—not swallowed, exactly, but translated into another material.

Above ground, I might appear as silver moss, a reed stand, a line of leaves gathered around a spring. From a distance, I might look designed. Someone might say the plateau had built itself according to a plan.

They would be almost right.

The plan would be wind, water, pressure, damage, and time. The plan would be whatever survived.

At night, when the clouds below turned black and the plants closed themselves against the cold, I would remain awake in the only sense available to me: moving. Water would pass through me. Fungal threads would exchange their slow chemistry. Roots would press into the unfinished mountain. The pumps would beat beneath everything, artificial and indifferent, yet useful.

I would not dream.

I would continue.

And if you found yourself there—standing beside the wet stone, unable to speak over the roar—you might think you were alone.

But beneath your feet, the plateau would receive your weight.

For a moment, it would make room.
