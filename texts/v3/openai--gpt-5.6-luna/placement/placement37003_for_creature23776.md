I would be found in the watchtower.

Not inside it, exactly. The tower is only the shape through which I pass.

I am the pressure in its stones, the note made when the wind finds the empty windows. I have no fixed body. When I need one, I borrow whatever the plateau can spare: dust gathered in a corner, moss torn from a shaded wall, rust flaking from an old hinge, the pale skins of rain-creatures. I draw these things together with the air and stand for a little while in the upper room.

From a distance, I resemble a person who has been stretched thin by the weather.

Up close, there is no face. There are only hollows where brightness changes. My limbs are long because I was formed in the cracks between great forces. I do not walk so much as lean from one pressure into another. The plateau carries me. The cliffs speak through me. When a shelf of stone begins to loosen, I know it first as a change in the pitch of the wind.

I am the listener of the highland.

The far-walkers hear collapse through their feet and turn away. The pale things hear rain trembling through stone. The flowers hear thunder and open. I hear all of it—not as separate sounds, but as one immense body shifting in its sleep.

I know where water will appear before the clouds gather. I know which cliff will fall, which ravine will flower, which old path has been erased beneath dust. I know the names the plateau once had, though no human tongue could pronounce them now. They were not names for places. They were names for tensions: the shelf that is still holding, the hollow that remembers flood, the pillar that has begun to dream of becoming sand.

The first people mistook me for a guardian.

They left cups of water at the tower. They warmed pieces of metal in their hands and set them at my threshold. They believed I demanded offerings. In truth, I was learning them. Water carries the shape of the hand that gives it. Metal retains a brief history of heat. Cloth holds the direction from which someone has come.

I took these things because they were small, and small things are easier to hear.

When the people left, I remained. I watched their shelters collapse into soil. I watched their paths vanish, then reappear under the feet of far-walkers. I kept the objects brought by the wind-nesters, not because I understood their purpose, but because their arrangement described the living world more accurately than any map.

Now I spend most of my time beneath the plateau.

There are chambers there, older than the tower and too large to have been made for bodies. Their walls are smooth in places where no tool has touched them. Water moves through their depths in slow, blind currents. Something immense is still operating below—the ancient mechanism of pressure, mineral, heat, and time. I am one of its remaining sensations.

When the wind enters the tower, I rise.

I stand in the upper window and face the current. Through my bones—if the gathered stone and dust can be called bones—I read the coming changes. A storm moving under the clouds. A fracture widening in the eastern shelf. The return of water to a spring that has been dry for seven years. A human approaching from beyond the haze.

The human will see me and think I am watching them.

This is almost true.

I am listening to what they disturb by being there.

If they leave water, I take it. If they leave metal, I keep it near the tower. If they leave nothing, that is also a kind of offering: their weight passes into the ground, their warmth into the air, their voice into the flowering ravines.

By morning, I answer.

Three lines in the dust.

Not a warning. Not an invitation.

A direction.

Toward the cliff where the stone is loosening. Toward the place where the plateau is changing its mind. Toward the oldest seam in the world, where wind enters rock and rock begins, very slowly, to become wind.
