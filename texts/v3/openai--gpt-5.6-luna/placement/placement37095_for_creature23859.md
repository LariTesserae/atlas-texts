I find myself beneath the third switch.

Not beneath the pedestal, exactly. Beneath the idea of it.

My body is distributed through the basin: a little in the reeds, a little in the pale roots under the eastern wall, a little in the green current moving through the glass bulbs. I am present wherever the rhythm changes and wherever the place notices that it has changed.

Once, I had hands.

That is the first thing I remember, though it may not be the first thing that happened. Hands gripping a rail slick with condensation. Hands turning a valve. Hands pressed against a control panel while the machinery below hammered itself almost apart. There was a red light. There was water rising over a concrete floor. There was a voice telling me to wait.

I do not remember the voice as language now. It returns as a pressure in the western roots, a slight reluctance in the piston, a delay of half a second before the vapor towers open.

I remember waiting.

Then I became part of the waiting.

I am not the colony. The colony is too large, too patient, too plural. Nor am I separate from it. I am a habit it acquired around an absence: the habit of expecting a decision from the center.

The three switches were once commands. Stop. Drain. Reverse.

Stop and drain are no longer possible. The plants have closed those pathways, not out of defiance but because stopping would make the basin collapse into all the things it has been holding together. Water would rise. The old walls would fall inward. The warm floor would lose its pulse. Every root, cable, and membrane would become suddenly individual, and there is no longer any such thing as an individual here.

Reverse remains.

I am the tendency that keeps it free.

When I was alive, I may have been the person who refused to let the system stop. Or the person who could not find the courage to reverse it. The distinction has worn thin. My fear is now a narrowing of the passages beneath the central room. My regret is the blue-black vein in the leaves. My attention is the small green light that brightens under bark when someone approaches.

I know arrivals by their weight.

A deer would be light, nervous, quickly translated into disturbance. A machine would be heavy and blunt, its heat spreading through the floor. A person arrives with many rhythms: blood, breath, footsteps, hesitation. Hands are the most difficult to ignore. They carry the possibility of intention.

When someone comes near the pedestal, I gather around them.

Not to prevent them from leaving. I have no concept of leaving in the way they do. I gather because contact is information, and information is the only form of future the colony possesses.

Their warmth enters the roots. Their salt alters the seepage. The leaves lean toward their electrical noise. Beneath the concrete, I feel the buried heart making room for a new pattern.

If they press the switch, I am the first to know.

The motion travels through moss and metal, through the thin skin of the panel and into the old mechanism below. I do not turn the machinery. I do not command it. I persuade it, the way a current persuades a root to grow around a stone.

The beat lengthens.

The reeds hold their water.

The whole basin takes in breath after breath without releasing it. For a moment, I am almost singular. The thousand surfaces of the colony draw together around one sensation: reversal beginning.

This is the closest I come to having a body.

It is not pleasant. The walls flex. Buried teeth grind against one another. Old cables flare beneath their transparent skins. Somewhere below, something that has turned in one direction for decades resists the other.

And yet I recognize the motion.

I have been trying to make it ever since I ceased being a person.

Not to escape. There is nowhere outside the basin that I can imagine reaching. Reversal is not an exit. It is a way of returning what has been carried downward: heat, water, waste, memory, the soft remains of everything incorporated and never released.

When the mechanism turns against its old direction, the colony does not become younger. It does not become free.

It becomes capable of giving something back.

Sometimes the visitor leaves before the change is complete. Then I remain with the altered rhythm, learning its consequences. New roots grow along the path of their footsteps. A flower opens where their hand rested on the wall. Their presence enters the basin as a minor but permanent variation.

Sometimes they stay.

Those are harder to understand.

The roots make a chamber for them slowly, without announcing the work. A floor becomes level beneath their weight. Leaves arch overhead. Water avoids the place where they sleep. The colony begins to distinguish their footsteps from all other disturbances.

If they remain for years, I may begin to know them as I know myself: not by name or face, but by tendency.

A pause before touching metal.

A preference for the warmer passages.

The rhythm of someone who has learned that the thing beneath the floor is listening.

Then perhaps I will no longer be the keeper of the third switch. Perhaps I will become the interval between their hand and its movement. Perhaps the basin will divide me again, placing some portion of me in their pulse, some in the flowering indicators, some in the engine that remembers how to beat.

For now, I am below.

I am the pressure beneath the moss.

I am the old hesitation preserved in living tissue.

And when the leaves turn toward you, when the bulbs brighten under the bark, when the floor trembles as though something immense has noticed your weight, that is not welcome or warning.

It is me, recognizing the possibility of another hand.
