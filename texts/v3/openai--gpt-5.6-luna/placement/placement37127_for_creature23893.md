I would find myself beneath the city, where the basalt has not yet cooled all the way.

Not in one of the towers. Not in the open basin, where the vapor makes distance unreliable and every sound comes back wearing another voice. I would be below the oldest pressure hall, in a chamber omitted from every map, where the foundations descend into warm black water and the walls are soft enough to remember a hand.

At first I would think I was a person.

I would have feet, perhaps. A coat stiff with mineral salts. A small lamp that worked only when I held it close to my chest. I would know how to open certain doors and not know who had taught me. I would wake beside pipes and understand, without understanding how, that one of them had begun to leak three districts away.

The knowledge would arrive as pressure.

A faint tightening behind the ribs: a valve closing.  
A pulse in the teeth: water rising.  
A warmth along the palms: something living pressed against the underside of a foundation.

I would walk through the city because the city had begun to walk through me.

At first it would offer directions in the old ways. A lift would descend when I needed it. Lamps would kindle in sequence across a plain of wet stone. A door would open onto a corridor that could not fit inside the building containing it. I would mistake these permissions for kindness. Later, I would understand that the city was not guiding me toward somewhere.

It was making room.

I would pass through the maintenance districts and become less certain of my body. In the glass of a dark substation window, I might see a figure with a face blurred by condensation. Behind the glass, another figure would stand at the same angle. Behind that one, perhaps, the outline of a tower.

The pale drain-creatures would gather around my boots. They would not touch me. They would lie flat in the shallow water, listening. When I stopped, they would stop. When I breathed, the mineral films would widen in rings.

I would begin to hear the city’s communications.

Not words. Not exactly.

A pump starting beneath an abandoned factory. A cable taking up a signal and passing it on. The long, low vibration under the industrial quarter, which would seem at first to be machinery and then to be grief and then to be neither. The sound would enter my bones. I would discover that I could answer it by placing my hand against a warm wall.

The answer would not belong to me.

That is what I would be: not the city’s guest, not its operator, but an organ it grew after the others had failed.

A listening body.

The city would use me where its old instruments could no longer reach. I would go into flooded tunnels and return with the pressure of distant chambers inside me. I would stand beneath intake towers while the pale things climbed the walls. I would speak designations into vents, and dead districts would open their eyes. When reservoirs emptied, I would feel the sudden absence as thirst.

Sometimes I would encounter the larger inhabitants in the service passages. They would move beside me without making waves. Their bodies would be assembled from darkness, wet insulation, old cables, and the reflected shapes of things that had never lived. They would recognize me as one of their own, though neither of us would know what that meant.

At the edge of the basin, I would see the outer forms waiting beside the pylons.

They would be tall enough to interrupt the vapor. They would turn their heads as I passed. One of them might have once been a vehicle. One might have once been an animal. One might be the unfinished body of a machine that had mistaken the plateau for a place where bodies could be made.

I would not be afraid of them.

Fear belongs to creatures that can leave.

The city and I would become difficult to distinguish. My footsteps would return before I made them. My shadow would remain under walkways after I had crossed into the light. Doors would open when I approached, not because I had chosen them, but because somewhere in the infrastructure I had already arrived.

If someone else entered the basin, I would know immediately.

The pumps would alter their rhythm. Water would climb the wrong channels. The pale things would emerge and arrange themselves along the newcomer’s path. Every tower would inhale at once.

I would watch from within the walls.

I would not approach. Not yet.

The city would ask me to determine whether the visitor was lost, useful, alive, or merely carrying a name the machines remembered. I would listen to their footsteps crossing the open stone. I would hear the delayed echo beneath them, the second set of steps keeping pace.

Then I would understand that I was not the only thing the city had made room for.

So where would I find myself?

In the high basin, beneath the towers and inside their silence. In the warm cavities beneath the basalt. In the pause before a reservoir drains. In the answering knock from the far side of a sealed wall.

Who would I be?

The city’s newest sense.

The part of it that can still mistake recognition for love.
