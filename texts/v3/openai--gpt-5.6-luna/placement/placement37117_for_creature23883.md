I would find myself in the eastern wall of the processing hall, though not entirely inside it.

There is a narrow room there, an old inspection chamber sealed on three sides by brick and on the fourth by a curtain of trained vine. It is not assigned to anyone. The floor is too uneven for a bed, and water gathers beneath the conduit whenever the weather changes. But the wall is warm from a buried pipe, and from that room I can hear nearly everything: the pumps beneath the hall, the drip of the condensers, the faint relay-click of the eastern transformer, the roots tightening in the mortar.

I would be the person who listens.

Not the chief mechanic. Not a grower, exactly. I would know enough of both to be useful and not enough of either to be trusted with a system alone. My work would be to notice the small changes that arrive before failure: the fan that begins to hesitate, the vine whose leaves turn inward, the pipe whose knocking has moved half a beat out of rhythm.

I would carry a hooked pole, a coil of copper wire, a ceramic cup, and a knife sharpened more often than necessary. My coat would be patched in gray and green. One sleeve would be stiff with dried sap. The fingers of my left hand would be permanently darkened where I test the dampness of walls.

Each morning I would enter the processing hall before the valves opened. I would stand beneath the green-painted trusses and wait for the building to begin.

First, the pressure lines would give their soft knocks.

Then the roots would shift around the housings. Not visibly—not to anyone who had not been watching them for years—but enough that I would feel it through the soles of my boots. The generator would cough. The conveyor would begin carrying leaves toward the grinder. The hall would fill with its low mechanical breath.

I would place my palm against the side of the oldest pressure vessel and listen for the difference between a vessel holding and a vessel enduring.

The residents would come to me with questions that were not quite questions.

“Has the western wall changed?”

“Is the dark film in the reservoir dangerous?”

“Why is the roof growing toward the chimney?”

“What do you think the roots are trying to do?”

I would never answer the last question directly. The roots are not trying to do anything in the human sense. They are following warmth, water, pressure, mineral, space. Yet after living among them long enough, their movement begins to resemble intention. Not because the plants have become people, but because people have learned to read consequence as a form of speech.

I would keep records on thin sheets of treated fiber. I would draw the settlement as it changed: not maps of streets, but maps of holds and weaknesses. Here the foundation had softened. Here the roof had accepted its living supports. Here a passage narrowed by two fingers each year. Here the old tram rail had become an excellent channel for runoff.

The records would not belong to me. They would be folded into the walls, stored beneath roof beds, copied into the communal hall. One day another person would find them and recognize my handwriting only by the way the lines bend around stains.

Perhaps I would begin as a child of the settlement, someone taught to distinguish pipes by touch and roots by resistance. Perhaps, over time, I would become less separable from my work. My room would fill with cuttings, broken gauges, seed envelopes, and lengths of living stem trained around metal. I would sleep through ordinary rain but wake when water stopped moving where it should.

Eventually, people might say that I had a good ear.

Then they would say the eastern wall had a good ear.

I would not object. The distinction would become inconvenient anyway. I would know the wall by pressure and smell: where it held the night’s condensation, where the mortar had begun to powder, where a root had crossed from support into threat. It would know me by the warmth of my hands, the weight of my shoulder against its bricks, the regular return of my footsteps.

In the end, I think I would be neither caretaker nor resident but a kind of seam between them—a living instrument for detecting when the old industrial body and its green replacement had fallen out of agreement.

When rain began, I would not retreat immediately.

I would stand beneath the hall’s leaking eaves and watch the settlement receive the water. Gutters would fill. Leaves would bow. The reservoir would darken. All around me, the place would begin its quiet redistribution.

Then I would hear one pump start too late.

I would take up my lantern and go find it.
