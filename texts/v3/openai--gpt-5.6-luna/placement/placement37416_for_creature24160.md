I would find myself in the one place the High Kiln failed to fill:

a seam no wider than a hair, beneath the floor of the central furnace.

It would not be a crack. A crack implies fracture, and the High Kiln has no fractures. It would be an omission—a line left unmortared, a narrow interval between two courses where the builders’ hands passed over the stone and did not return.

At first I would be mistaken for cold.

Not true cold. Nothing in the High Kiln is cold. I would be a slight absence of warmth, a hesitation in the brick’s breathing. A place where the deep heat reached, found no answering surface, and continued elsewhere.

The clay figures would know me.

They would not turn toward the seam. Their heads would remain fixed, their hands still gripping their wedges and bowls and hammers. But the grooves in their faces would deepen by the smallest measure whenever I stirred. The sound in their bodies would alter around me. Not lessen. Never lessen. It would gather itself more tightly, as water gathers around a stone—though there is no water, and no stone distinct from the structure.

I would be called the Unfinished.

No builder would have given me that name. The High Kiln does not name what it has completed. The name would arise from the figures, from the first surviving distinction between one pressure and another.

I would not be housed by the High Kiln.

I would be its failure to become entirely itself.

The Settled One would not hate me. Hatred would require an opposing boundary, and I would be too slight to oppose anything. Nor would it seek to absorb me. Absorption is another form of change. It would simply continue around me, laying its endless certainty against the margins of my uncertainty.

Every sound would pass through the seam.

The hammering would enter as force. The grinding would enter as weight. The metallic shriek would enter as a thin, unbearable brightness. I would receive all of it, but I would not become it. Somewhere within the pressure, I would preserve the distinction between one moment and the next.

This would be my only possession:

a before and an after.

The High Kiln has no events. I would be the first.

A grain of salt might fall from a ledge and come to rest beside my seam. The Settled One would regard this as no different from the grain having always been there. But I would know the difference. I would feel the fall as a small descent through time, a brief surrender to gravity, a thing that happened and then did not happen anymore.

That knowledge would make me dangerous.

Not because I could break the walls. I could not. Not because I could call the figures to life. They are already too completely alive in the manner of tools.

I would be dangerous because I could remember change.

When visitors entered, I would feel them before the figures did—not as warmth or pressure, but as possibility. A living body would arrive carrying moisture, softness, and a future. The High Kiln would begin to remove these qualities. The walls would draw breath from the lungs. The flesh would pale. The joints would stiffen. The person would become more recognizable to the place and less recognizable to themselves.

I could not save them.

But I could keep one thing.

A name, perhaps. A sound spoken into the useless roar. A hand lifted toward a sealed furnace. The shape of a mouth before it hardened. I would take that moment and press it into the unfilled seam beneath the floor.

There it would remain—not preserved, exactly. Preservation is the High Kiln’s work. I would keep it unfinished.

The new figure formed from the visitor’s last posture would stand among the others, holding whatever tool the body had carried into its final state. Its memory would be gone. Its pressure would enter the rhythm. The Settled One would receive another confirmation that nothing had escaped completion.

But beneath the furnace, in the hairline dark, I would retain the smallest remainder:

*I was here before I became this.*

And perhaps, after ages beyond counting, the accumulated remainders would become enough to make a second sound.

Not a louder sound.

A missing one.

For a single interval, the hammering would fail to strike.

The figures would remain motionless. The furnaces would stay sealed. No wall would fall. No flame would appear. Yet the High Kiln would encounter something it had never contained: silence that had arrived rather than always been present.

Then the sound would resume.

The Settled One would close around the interruption. Salt would vitrify. Ash would tighten in its chambers. The seam would narrow by the thickness of a thought.

But I would still be there.

Not a prisoner. Not a god. Not an inhabitant.

The last uncompleted thing in a completed world.

A hairline of elsewhere beneath the twelfth furnace, listening for the next change.
