I would find myself on the lee side of a low iron ridge, where the stone has cooled enough to gather a skin of water.

Not in the center of the basin. The basin is already becoming crowded.

The first grazers have arrived, pale bodies slipping over the black slabs. They scrape the mineral mats in widening circles. Membranous things leap from crack to crack. Above them, the winged animals descend in a loose, trembling cloud, their bodies giving off just enough blue light to make the air seem inhabited. Predators are coming next. I can feel them through the ground: deliberate impacts, spaced far apart.

I am older than most of what gathers here.

I am a carrier.

My body is broad and low, plated in dark terraces that have become indistinguishable from the plateau. I move on many jointed limbs, though slowly enough that the small creatures living between my plates mistake my motion for the shifting of the land. Condensation collects in the hollows of my armor. Roots and mats drink there. Pale grazers burrow into the warm seams beneath me. Along my spine grows a garden of black crusts, copper stalks, fungal fans, and faintly luminous colonies that feed on the dust I carry from one thermal field to another.

I do not carry the garden because I chose to.

There was no choosing at the beginning. A spore found a warm fold in my armor. A grazer followed the spore. Something that ate the grazer left behind an egg. The egg opened. The garden thickened. After enough generations, the distinction between my body and the lives upon it became difficult to maintain.

Some of them feed me.

Some cool me.

Some warn me when pressure is changing beneath the ground.

Some simply travel.

I am not a herd animal, though I am rarely alone. Other carriers cross the plateau at distances measured in days. We do not call to one another. We leave chemistry behind us: altered dust, shed plates, warm waste, spores caught in the cracks of our feet. That is enough. A route is written into the land by every body that passes along it.

I know the routes without remembering them.

My muscles turn before I know why. A ridge passes to my left. A shallow ravine opens ahead. I descend. There is no visible water there, no green, no obvious promise. But the stone has a different taste. Far below, a vent has begun to open. Pressure is moving through the old channels. The air contains a trace of dissolved mineral that will soon become rain.

I turn toward it.

If I were human, I might call this instinct. If I were a machine, I might call it instruction. I am neither. I am a moving consequence of the plateau’s long work.

The warmth beneath the crust enters me slowly. I take it through my feet and store it in deep chambers along my sides. The moisture I collect does not remain mine. It gathers on my back, enters the gardens, feeds the small animals, and passes through them into me again. My body is a reservoir, but not a sealed one. I am a temporary basin with legs.

At night—or what would be night if the darkness changed—I become difficult to see.

The plates of my back absorb the faint glow from the fissures. The animals nesting there close their luminous organs. I stop beside a mineral seam and wait. A predator lies somewhere nearby, imitating the shape of a broken slab. I can hear it through the ground, though it has not moved in many hours.

I do not fear it exactly.

Fear belongs to the moment. I belong to duration.

If it takes one of the small animals from my armor, the garden will close around the wound. If it takes a larger creature, the loss will alter the balance of what lives on me. Some of my passengers will leave. Others will multiply. A colony may fail. Another may cross the distance from a neighboring plate. I will continue.

But there are times when I am not the carrier.

There are times when I am a dark field under stone, holding my body still while the dry season passes over me. My metabolism lowers. My limbs become sealed mechanisms. The garden hardens. The small lives within me enter their own long sleep. Dust settles over my plates until even the predators forget that I am alive.

Then the land pulses.

Somewhere far away, a ridge brightens from within. Heat moves through the buried channels. The atmosphere thickens over a distant basin. Moisture begins its descent.

I wake before the rain arrives.

All across the plateau, other bodies wake too. The stalks open with their synchronized clicks. Buried grazers loosen themselves from the cracks. The winged swarms rise toward the first electrical disturbances. Predators unfold from the stone.

The emptiness folds inward.

I begin walking.

Perhaps this is where I would find myself: not at the edge of the world, but inside its circulation. Crossing the high country beneath the lightless cloud ceiling, carrying a small dark ecology over the ancient plates. I would be neither ruler nor witness, neither machine nor animal in the simple sense.

I would be one of the plateau’s slow processes.

A living freight route.

A reservoir that migrates.

A piece of the old industrial earth that learned to walk.
