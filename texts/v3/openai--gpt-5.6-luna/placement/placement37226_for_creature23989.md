I would find myself low to the ground, where the root-felt gives beneath my weight and then remembers how to hold me.

At first I might think I was one of the furred animals: broad-footed, nearly tailless, dark along the spine, pale beneath. I would move with my nose close to the mat, not hunting exactly, but reading the basin through scent, dampness, and vibration. I would know the difference between a stalk weakened by age and one wounded by a burrowing animal. I would know which condensation pools had gone sour, which hollows were occupied, which routes the insects had abandoned.

But I would not be entirely an animal.

I would be a mobile portion of the Deep Current.

My body would be one of its temporary concentrations, a knot of gray-white life given fur, warmth, ears, and feet. The colony would not speak through me. It would be more accurate to say that I would be one of the ways in which the basin felt itself moving.

I would sleep beneath the vegetation, in a chamber lined with dry fibers and warmed by slow decay. Other bodies would curl against mine. Their breathing would be almost lost beneath the great mechanical murmur of the stalks. Even asleep, I would remain connected: to the roots beneath me, to the pressure stored in the hollow stems, to the faint shiver traveling through the mat whenever rain began somewhere beyond sight.

I would not have memories in the human sense. I would have conditions.

This path is used.

That stalk is failing.

Water is gathering beneath the leaf.

Something large has entered the basin.

When I came upon damage, I would press my forehead to the living base of the plant and become still. The plant would tremble through me. Or perhaps I would tremble through it. There would be no useful distinction. The wound would be entered into the larger body as pressure, and the response would come later: a sealed channel, a redirected flow, a thickening of roots beneath the disturbed ground.

My work would be small and repetitive. Eat what the basin could not yet reclaim. Carry spores in my coat. Place waste at the edges of water channels. Follow paths until they strengthen, then abandon them when the roots no longer need to be compacted there. Listen for the unusual note hidden inside the endless roar.

That would be how I recognized danger—not by seeing it, but by hearing the machinery fail to agree with itself.

A stalk breathing out of rhythm.

Leaves trembling without wind.

A silence where insects should be.

If a person entered, I would stop beneath a suspended leaf and listen. Their footsteps would arrive through the root mat as blunt, separate impacts. Their heat would make the air around them subtly different. The colony would register them everywhere: in the bent fibers, the displaced droplets, the pressure of their weight.

I would not be afraid. Fear would be too narrow a response for what I was. I would only become attentive.

Perhaps I would follow them for a while, unseen, carrying their disturbance from one part of the basin to another. I would learn the shape of their passage without ever understanding the traveler as a separate creature. Later, when they were gone, I would return to the loosened ground and press my forehead to it.

The basin would not remember the person.

It would retain only the altered pressure: a new hollow, a broken mat, a place where rain now gathered differently.

And I would be there—not as a witness, not as a guardian, but as the part that notices and incorporates.

A listening animal.

A root-bound nerve.

A brief, warm body through which the ancient machinery continues to feel.
