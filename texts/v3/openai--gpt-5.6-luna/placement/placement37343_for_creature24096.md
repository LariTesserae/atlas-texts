I would be found beneath a city that has forgotten it was ever built.

Not in one of the exposed islands, where the weather still has access to the stone, and not in the open avenues where the great animals pass like moving districts. I would be deep below the drowned terraces, inside the wall of a tower that leans across a current.

The tower would have no name. Names require surfaces, and the surfaces here have been worn away.

Its lower chambers would be filled with a slow circular flow. Water would enter through a cracked arch, descend three levels, turn around a central column, and return through the same district by a route that no longer appeared on any plan. The current would reverse every few decades. It would carry pale dust, blind creatures, and the dissolved residue of older masonry.

I would cling to the inner face of the tower where the pressure was strongest.

At first, I might seem to be part of the wall. My limbs would have folded into the seams, my membranes flattened and furred with mineral. My body would be cloudy now, no longer pale as ice but the color of the deposits around me. Only the slow green pulse beneath my ribs would reveal that I was alive.

I would be a Pressure-Born.

But not a young one, and not one of the travelers who cross the city in green-lit lines. I would be old enough that movement had become difficult. Old enough to have forgotten the shape of my own nursery. Old enough that the city’s currents no longer passed through me as separate sensations, but as a single vast condition.

I would know the tower by its burdens.

The northern buttress would be hollow. The western gallery would be filling with silt. Something enormous had passed beneath the lowest foundation three centuries ago, leaving a pressure scar that still moved slowly through the district. There would be a sealed chamber near the top, dry for now, though the water pressed constantly against its door.

I would know that the chamber was not empty.

I would not know what was inside. The wall would not tell me in words. It would tell me in the particular way the pressure returned from the sealed stone: softened, delayed, carrying a faint repetition. A pulse within a pulse.

Perhaps it held a nursery. Perhaps it held the remains of the builders. Perhaps it was only a room shaped so exactly that water could not help but remember it.

I would have been listening to it for a long time.

Longer than any single living thing should listen to anything.

The younger Pressure-Born would come to me along the tower’s outer face. They would press their bodies against the stone and wait. I would communicate with them by shifting one limb, then another. Danger below. Passage open. Do not enter the eastern shaft. A large body moving beyond the colonnade.

They would understand, though understanding here would not be thought passing from one mind to another. It would be alignment. A shared adjustment to the same unseen force.

Sometimes one of them would ask me about the sealed chamber.

Not directly. It would hover beside me, membrane trembling, and send a question through the wall by the pressure of its body.

What is there?

I would answer: something that has not yet opened.

That would not satisfy them. It would not satisfy me either.

I would be less a guardian than a delay.

The tower would be failing. The current had been worrying at its foundations for thousands of years, and the cracks had begun to join. If the wall gave way, the water would rush through the district, emptying the upper halls and crushing the old bridge that carried one of the ancestral routes. Hundreds of animals would be drawn into the breach. The tower might fall sideways, striking three other structures. The disturbance would travel through the city for days.

So I would enter the cracks.

Not because I had decided to save the tower. Decision is a crude thing in a place like this. The fracture would reach me as pain, or warning, or summons. I would unfold myself and press my body into the narrow spaces. My limbs would become braces. My secretions would harden against the masonry. The current would divide around me.

The collapse would slow.

That is what care would mean here: not preventing ruin, but changing its angle.

A wall preserved for another century. A passage left open long enough for the young to cross. A fallen slab diverted from a nursery. A current persuaded to spend its strength elsewhere.

The city would continue to rearrange itself. It would not thank me. Nothing here would recognize an act as separate from its consequence.

I would remember the world through these consequences.

I would remember a procession of Pressure-Born descending through a shaft, their green pulses vanishing one after another.

I would remember the shadow of a raylike animal passing overhead and the sudden dimming of an entire plaza.

I would remember the first fracture felt by the young in the nursery: a small, bright disturbance traveling through the foundation, teaching them that the architecture was not silent.

I would remember companions who had anchored themselves in quiet chambers. Their bodies had become pale outlines beneath the mineral skin. The living gathered around them and received the final map of their sensation: a district emptying, a door opening, a pressure front still centuries away.

I would remember them without faces.

Faces are not useful underwater.

What I would have instead is an immense interior geography. Not memories as images, but as routes through the body. A sealed stair would correspond to a tightening beneath my ribs. A collapsed avenue would be a missing rhythm in one of my limbs. The approach of a great animal would pass through me as a long, low turning.

Perhaps, toward the end, I would discover that the thing inside the sealed chamber was not a nursery or a relic.

Perhaps it was another of my kind, older than I was, buried in the wall before my species had learned to leave its foundations. Perhaps it had been listening all this time. Perhaps the pulse I felt was its pulse answering mine across the stone.

Or perhaps it was only water accumulating behind a door.

I would not be able to tell the difference.

That would be the final lesson of the highest country: that a thing need not be alive to possess a pressure, and need not possess a mind to alter the lives of those who listen to it.

Eventually the tower would begin to fail in earnest.

The current would change. The great circular flow would reverse. Dust would lift from the floor. The sealed chamber would tremble. The younger inhabitants would arrive and press themselves against the walls, waiting for my signal.

I would open all six limbs.

Not warning.

Not invitation.

Release.

They would leave through the old route, carrying my last map with them: the safe surfaces, the weak arches, the direction of the coming fall. I would remain in the cracks. The tower would fold around me. Water would strike through the galleries and take the pressure from my body.

For a while, perhaps, I would still feel the city.

Then mineral would grow over me.

I would become one of the pale forms along the wall—not a monument, not an ancestor exactly, but another contour in the water. The living would cling to my limbs without knowing where their bodies ended and mine began. Currents would pass through the spaces I had held open. Some chamber, somewhere above, would remain dry for another thousand years.

And if I had to say what I was, after all that time, I would not say guardian.

I would say: a listening place.

A small, temporary arrangement of flesh inside an ancient instrument.

Something the city used to hear itself changing.
