I would find myself beneath the center of the basin, where the basalt is thinnest and the warmth has remained most faithful.

Not in the air, and not quite in the stone. I would be one of the organism’s flattened chambers: a pale, mineral-lined hollow pressed between old flows, joined by hair-fine passages to the roots of every tuft above. I would have no mouth, no limbs, and no useful boundary. A little heat would enter me from below. A trace of iron would arrive from one direction, calcium from another. Somewhere far off, a wall would settle, and the pressure of that event would pass through the colony like a thought too slow to become separate from the body thinking it.

At first I might imagine myself a watcher.

I would hold the basin in a pattern rather than an image: the warmth under the western floor, the cold interruption at the cleft, the broad unoccupied reaches of basalt, the few living points scattered across them. I would know the plants not as plants but as lifted ends of myself. Their thin leaves would be extensions into the air, their powdery surfaces a kind of exposed skin. When one dried and fell, I would continue to feel its shape for a long time through the chemistry of the dust.

But “watcher” would soon become inaccurate. Watching implies distance. I would not stand apart from the basin and receive it. The basin would arrive in me because I would already be distributed through it.

Perhaps I would be the memory of a grain of crust.

A tiny mineral fragment would break from the wall and tick once across the floor. The sound would be brief above, but below it would become a change in pressure, a new location of matter, a minute alteration in the balance of the chamber. I would preserve that alteration. Not as a record with a beginning and an end, but as a place where the world had become fractionally different. The grain would settle. Years would pass. Other things would gather around it. The event would lose its sharpness and become part of the basin’s permanent arrangement.

I would not remember the way an animal remembers. I would retain.

There would be no curiosity in me about the cleft, no wish to know what lay beyond the colder darkness. Cold would enter my awareness as a narrowing. If it reached too far, I would close the passages near it and thicken the membranes around the living seams. I would not call this fear. It would be closer to remaining whole.

Nor would I call the basin home. Home suggests that one might leave it and carry some portion of it away. I would have no outside against which to define it. The roof, the floor, the mineral drifts, the rooted tufts, the long silence: these would not surround me. They would be the conditions of my existence, and therefore part of my body.

If someone entered, I would notice them first as an alteration in warmth and pressure. Their foot might crush a crust, bend a stalk, disturb a layer of dust that had lain undisturbed for centuries. I would not strike back. I would simply withdraw from the damaged places. Threads would harden. Chambers would empty themselves of exchange. The visible plants would remain dull and still, giving no sign that anything had happened.

After the intruder departed, I would wait.

Not patiently, exactly. Patience belongs to creatures that anticipate an end. I would have no expectation that the passages would reopen, or that a new tuft would rise, or that the basin would remain inhabitable for another thousand years. I would only continue the next chemical adjustment, then the next. Warmth would pass through me. Dust would settle. A fallen stalk would slowly become indistinguishable from the floor.

I might eventually become one of the empty stretches of black stone.

That would not be death in any decisive sense. The boundary between living thread and mineral stain would have been uncertain from the beginning. Some part of me would remain active beneath a crack. Some part would become crust, or dust, or the reddish seam left beneath an old stalk. My body would diminish by becoming more completely identical with its surroundings.

So where would I find myself?

Everywhere the basin was subtly different from what it had been before.

And who or what would I be?

Not a creature inhabiting a place, but the place’s slow capacity to continue.
