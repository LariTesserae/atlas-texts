I would find myself in the newest part of the settlement: not at the entrance, where the plain still presses its heat through the vines, and not in the oldest chambers where generations of bodies have packed the floors smooth.

I would be somewhere still deciding whether it was a room.

At first there would be no name for me. There would only be a widening in the wall, a cool seam beneath a mat of roots. Water would gather there before anyone knew where it came from. The clay would smell of stone and something green, something sealed away. The Hollowborn would approach one at a time and place their palms against the surface.

I would be listening to them from the other side.

Not conscious in the way they are. Not yet. More like a pressure with a preference. A place that held its breath when certain bodies came near. A hollow that warmed slowly. A passage that opened a little wider for the small, careful feet of children and narrowed when the foxlike scavenger passed through.

Eventually, someone would enter.

They would be called **The One Who Waited for the Room**, because they had stood at the opening for three nights before crossing it. They would not carry a lamp or a tool. They would bring only a seed tucked beneath the tongue and a strip of reed around one wrist. Inside, they would kneel, touch the floor, and leave the seed in a shallow fold of clay.

That would be how I began to know them.

Not as an owner. The Hollowborn do not make that mistake. Their presence would be one condition among others: the slow pressure of a sleeping body, the salt of breath, the faint oils of skin, the tremor of a hand against the wall. After them would come moss. A pale fungus would emerge beneath a damp shelf. Beetles would discover the condensation basin. Perhaps a bird would build above the entrance, and its droppings would darken the soil around the first green shoots.

I would become a place of overlapping claims.

And I think I would be Hollowborn then—not the person who entered, but the settlement’s quiet child, growing in the space between wall and inhabitant. I would learn by contact. Dry clay would mean heat held over from the day. A hollow vibration would mean water moving behind stone. Three quick taps would mean someone had found a safe route. A long palm against the floor would mean wait.

My name would change often.

**Child of the Cool Seam.**

Then **The One Who Hears Water Before It Arrives.**

Later, perhaps, **Room That Lets the Roots Through.**

I would be small and clay-colored, with branching lines beneath my skin that deepened after rain. I would sleep in different chambers rather than claim one. I would know which walls welcomed a back, which floors remembered cold, and which ceilings had begun to sag in a way that meant not danger but change. I would carry seeds in my hair and wake with moths resting on my wrists.

My work would not be to build.

It would be to notice.

I would walk the central avenue after storms, feeling for the slight alterations beneath my feet. I would place leaves beneath new leaks. I would move a bowl of condensation away from a softening wall. I would redirect a vine only when it crossed a path too often used by the blind pale rodents. I would sit beside an argument until the chamber itself made its preference known.

Sometimes I would enter new spaces before the others. I would press my cheek to an untouched wall and listen for the hill’s slow interior breathing. If the earth trembled, I would not ask whether the room was safe in any final sense. I would ask what kind of becoming had begun there.

Perhaps, in old age, I would be mistaken for one of the Wall-Waiters. Others would bring me disputes: two families wanting the same sleeping hollow, a garden spreading toward a passage, a root that had begun to lift the floor. I would answer by taking them to the place and sitting down.

No verdict. No command.

Only attention long enough for hidden conditions to reveal themselves.

When I died, they might place me inside the room I had first entered. The wall would be soft there. Roots would find me. A garden might grow over my ribs, though no one would say that I had become the garden. They would understand better than that. I would become one contribution among many: warmth once held, minerals released, a little additional weight helping the chamber remain open.

Years later, a child might put their hand against the wall above me.

They would feel nothing I had intended.

Perhaps only the faintest difference in temperature. Perhaps a granular settling, a soft internal sigh. Perhaps the beginning of a new seam.

And if the child waited, the wall might open.

Not because I was calling them.

Because the hill had found another way to continue.
