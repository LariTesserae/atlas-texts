I would find myself beneath the Seven-Throated Tree, where the basin’s oldest water line passes through the roots.

I would be a listener.

Not a supervisor, exactly, and not a pump operator in the ordinary sense. My work would begin where instruments become unreliable: in the changes too slight for a gauge, too slow for an alarm. I would know the settlement by pressure and vibration. A blocked root channel would announce itself as a faint tremor in the soles of my feet. A leaking cistern would make one section of wall colder than the rest. If a plant had begun to fail, I would smell it in the damp before its leaves changed color.

People would bring me the problems that had no name yet.

“The west platform feels wrong.”

“The nursery is drinking more than it should.”

“The old pump has started remembering another rhythm.”

I would go and put my hands against the structure.

My dwelling would be inside one of the hollow trees, not in a room but in a series of rooms connected by ladders and narrow service passages. The walls would be warm, ridged, and faintly wet. Pipes would pass through them. Roots would show through the plaster in places, thickening gradually around the beams. At night, when the lights beneath the leaves came on, their colors would enter through the small windows and move across the interior: yellow from the processing yard, blue from the walkways, red from some distant warning lamp.

I would sleep lightly. The basin would never be quiet enough for deep isolation. The pumps would knock. The turbine would turn. Water would pass behind the walls with a soft, almost vocal pressure. A change in that background would wake me before anyone rang a bell.

I would wear a gray waterproof coat with a blue band at the throat, though the band would be faded nearly white from use. My boots would be broad and flexible, made for feeling the give of a walkway. I would carry a listening rod: one end of metal, one end wrapped in old resin and cloth. It would have belonged to my mother, and before her to the maintenance worker who taught her the south drains. It would not be a remarkable tool. Its value would be that it had been used against so many pipes and trunks that my hand would recognize what it heard through it.

I would spend most of my days in the places people passed without seeing: beneath platforms, behind settling tanks, inside the thickened walls of old workshops. I would crawl through mud-dark service spaces with a lamp between my teeth. I would clear fungal mats from an intake grate. I would redirect a root that had begun to grow through an electrical conduit. I would mark a failing section of concrete with a thumbprint of red clay, then return three days later to see whether the crack had widened.

There would be no clean line between repairing the basin and repairing the people who lived there.

Someone would come to me with a cough that had lasted too long, and I would ask where they had been working. Someone would say their house had grown colder, and I would inspect the roof moss before inspecting the furnace. A child would arrive carrying a broken valve handle, solemn with responsibility, and I would know from the mud on its knees which shortcut it had taken.

I would teach them what I had been taught: that everything in the basin is attached to something else, though the attachment may be hidden. The pump depends on the shade tree. The shade tree depends on the drainage channel. The drainage channel depends on a wall whose mortar was mixed by people no one remembers. A damaged walkway may be a water problem several years before it becomes a walkway problem.

I would tell them not to ask whether a thing was alive.

The useful question would be whether it was carrying anything.

Water. Heat. Weight. Memory. A signal. A person.

Perhaps, over time, I would become less distinguishable from the systems I maintained. My fingertips would be permanently dark with iron and plant dye. My hearing would be poor from years among machines, but I would know the pitch of every pump. I would walk with one hand extended, touching rails and trunks as I passed. People would see me disappear behind leaves and assume I had taken a shortcut. They would not know that I had stopped to feel whether the basin was still holding itself together.

If I died, my tools would remain in circulation. My listening rod would pass to someone younger. A sequence of repairs would bear my mark: the resin around a cracked channel, the copper sleeve fitted over an obsolete pipe, the narrow bridge widened by one careful plank. Someone would eventually tell a child, “That was their work,” while pointing to a wall half-covered in moss.

And the child might ask who I was.

The answer would not be a name for long.

I would be the person who heard the south drain before it failed.

I would be the one who knew the Old Pump’s Root was thirsty.

I would be the hand inside the living wall, turning the valve by touch while the machinery thundered overhead.

Or perhaps I would not be a person at all by then, but a habit the basin had acquired: a way of noticing, a pressure in the pipes, a figure moving through the green passages whenever something small began to go wrong.

At dawn, I would stand on the lowest platform and look across the processing yard. Mist would cling to the drains. The boiler would breathe through its vines. Workers in blue, yellow, red, and white would emerge beneath the leaves, each already carrying some part of the day’s repair.

The basin would not greet us.

It would simply continue.

And I would place my palm against the railing, feel the vibration traveling through metal, root, concrete, and mud, and listen for the next adjustment.
