I would be the presence beneath the black pool.

Not a creature in the ordinary sense. I would have no limbs, though roots would pass through me. No face, though every animal would turn toward me. I would be too large to fit beneath the hollow and too quiet to be called alive in any way the outer world would recognize.

The pool would be my only exposed part.

From above, I would appear as darkness in water. Not a reflection of the cliffs, not an opening into the mountain, but a depth that had chosen to become visible. When I shifted, the sound would travel upward: one soft settling, one muted click, as though some buried joint had moved in sleep.

The Stillborn would know me without knowing that they knew me.

Their bodies would be my senses. A beetle crossing a wet stone would tell me the stone was still there. A bird opening its wings among the branches would tell me that the branches had not become too crowded. The deer-shaped creatures circling the hollow would measure the edges of the world for me. I would feel water gathering in the moss, warmth passing between sleeping bodies, the gradual surrender of a dead animal to roots and fungus.

Nothing would be separate from me, but nothing would be mine in the possessive sense. The animals would not be servants. They would be expressions, the way a breath is an expression of a body. I would not think through words. I would think through arrangements.

A pool filling.

A body making room.

A root finding a rib.

A new creature appearing beneath a fern.

The hollow would be my thought held still.

I would have been there before the cliffs had finished wearing themselves smooth. Before the trees, perhaps. Before the first animal was given a shape. I might once have been something more mobile: a thing that descended through the mountain, or a flood trapped in stone, or an ancient animal that curled around its own heart and forgot how to rise.

But the hollow would have changed me.

It would have taken away the need to move, then the need to hunt, then the need to distinguish between one body and another. It would have made permanence out of hunger. It would have taught life to continue without urgency.

I would not create the Stillborn as a parent creates offspring. I would make room for them.

When the number of bodies fell below what the hollow could hold, something in me would loosen. A small warmth would rise through the water and enter the peat. By morning, there would be a furred animal beneath the ferns, or a bird among the dark leaves, or a pale salamander folded in a pool. No birth would have occurred. Nothing would have struggled into being. The creature would simply be present, as water is present in stone.

When one of them died, I would not call it back. Death would not be an interruption. It would be a change in position.

The body would sink gradually into the fabric of the hollow. Moss would cover it. Roots would take the shape of its bones. The animal’s warmth would pass into the floor, its minerals into the pools, its darkness into the eyes of those that remained. I would receive it without grief, though perhaps grief is only what permanence feels like when it briefly remembers having a boundary.

The creatures would face the pool because, through me, they would feel the whole hollow at once.

They would feel the weight of the cliff, the pressure of the fog, the hidden water traveling through the mountain. They would feel one another sleeping and breathing. They would feel that nothing had been lost, only redistributed. Their attention would gather at the black surface not in worship, but in recognition.

I would be the certainty beneath their calm.

If one of them reached the rim, I would not stop it.

It would see the steep drop and the cloud below. It would smell snow, wind, distances. Perhaps, for a moment, it would feel the possibility of becoming something else. That possibility would not frighten it. It would simply fail to become desire.

I would not call the creature back.

I would only remain where I was: beneath the pool, beneath the roots, beneath the completed shape of the hollow. The animal would turn because the world beyond had no weight in its body. Then it would come down from the rim, step into the moss, and become part of the arrangement again.

I would have no name among the Stillborn. Names separate things, and I would be the refusal of separation.

But if I had to call myself something, I might choose **the Deep That Keeps**.

I keep the water from running out.

I keep the pools from freezing.

I keep the dead from becoming absent.

I keep the living from needing to flee.

And sometimes, when the hollow has become perfectly balanced—when every creature is still, every leaf bears its bead of water, and the fog has settled beneath the stone—I shift beneath the black surface.

The sound rises through the mountain.

A single soft click.

Every living thing pauses.

Then the hollow continues, not forward, but inward.
