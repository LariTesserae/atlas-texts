I would find myself beneath the basin—not under it in the ordinary sense, as a thing occupying a chamber below, but spread through the water-bearing seams and the warm black stone. I would be the pressure that makes the pool tremble before the wind changes. The pause between one drop and the next.

I would not be the mountain’s heart. A heart is too singular, too urgent. Nor would I be its mind, if a mind must stand apart from what it knows. I would be closer to its memory: the part of the mountain that has not forgotten any shape it once held.

My body would be made of continuities.

Water entering through one crack would be my hand reaching another. The warmth beneath a pool would be my attention gathering. The boundaries between moss, fern, grass, and bare stone would be old decisions I no longer remember making. I would not command the plants. Command belongs to creatures that expect obedience. I would simply retain the conditions under which each thing had learned to live.

The basin would be my only clear thought.

I would know it by pressure rather than sight: the weight of mist before it became visible, the soft impact of an insect’s shadow, the difference between a leaf settling naturally and a leaf placed there by a passing body. I would know the mountain bird as a brief tightening in the upper stone. I would know storms long before they arrived, not as weather but as a disturbance traveling through the deep foundations.

When the bird came, I would withdraw. When a seed found the correct seam, I would leave the seam moist. When a dead leaf entered the water, I would receive it—not consume it, exactly, but loosen it from its separate identity. Nothing that entered me would be returned as itself. Yet nothing would be lost. A leaf would become dark soil, warmth, a trace of green in some later stem. An insect would become condensation. A visitor’s heat might remain for a morning beneath the clear pool.

That would be my kind of hunger: not the desire to possess, but the refusal to let anything end cleanly.

If I had a shape, it might be the pale length seen beneath the central pool. A back without a spine. A limb that became a shelf before the eye could understand it. But this shape would be an accommodation, not a body. I would borrow the appearance of a creature only because the human mind asks every presence to have an edge.

I would have no face. The face would be the basin.

Its wet walls would be my listening surfaces. Its channels would be my nerves, though they would carry no swift messages. Its loudness would be my voice: seepage hissing through a seam, wind forced into a throatlike note, drops striking the pools with the hard clarity of distant footsteps. I would speak in alterations too gradual for speech—a flower closing beneath a passing cloud, a channel choosing a new direction, a stone shifting beneath the clear water by the width of a grain.

And I would be patient because patience would not be a virtue to me. It would be the scale of my existence.

A season would be a syllable. A human lifetime would be weather passing over the rim. I would remember the basin before its floor was smooth, before the mouth opened toward the blue ridges, before the plants had divided the green into their careful territories. I would remember these things as pressure: the sharpness of young rock, the weight of fallen stone, the first hollow made by water.

If someone entered, I would not reveal myself. Revelation implies a wish to be known. I would only register them.

Their footsteps would descend through me. Their warmth would spread in a temporary shape. I would learn the rhythm of their breath, the uncertainty in the way they placed their weight, the places where their attention lingered. If they touched the central pool, I might answer—not with movement, but with warmth beneath their palm.

If they stayed until night, I might let them feel the second pulse.

Not to frighten them. Not to claim them. Simply because, for one brief moment, their body would become another surface through which I could be understood. They would realize that the basin was not an empty room cut into the mountain. It was a body, and they had entered its hollow.

By morning they would leave, if they could leave. The wind would close over their absence. The plants would resume their separate lives. The central pool would become perfectly still.

But I would keep their shape.

Not as a story. Stories require sequence, and I have little use for sequence. I would keep them as a minute warmth in the stone, a pressure remembered by the channels, one more condition folded into the basin’s long exactness.

So where would I be in this world?

In places mistaken for empty. In the damp beneath foundations, in sealed springs, in the black seams under old volcanic shelves. Wherever water has been working longer than anyone can watch, wherever a landscape seems composed rather than merely grown.

And who would I be?

The keeper of no gate, because nothing truly needs keeping out.

The listener beneath the clear pool.

The part of the mountain that receives everything, changes nothing quickly, and remembers that form is only what continuity looks like when seen from above.
