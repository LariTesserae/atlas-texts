You would find yourself in the seam between two plates of an old root-bearer’s back.

Not on it. Not beneath it.

In it.

The seam is a warm, dark valley lined with moss and fibrous roots. During the wet season it becomes a narrow reservoir. Water gathers there in trembling beads, then runs downward through the animal’s armor into hollows where translucent larvae hatch and pale flowers drink. In drought, the seam closes around itself. The moss folds into gray scales. The roots become cords. Everything living there waits.

You are one of the things that waits.

At first, you might call yourself a flower.

You have a stalk, after all, and a crown that opens at dawn. Your petals are thin and translucent, veined with blue. Insects sleep beneath them. When the root-bearer exhales, your whole body lifts and settles with the breath.

But you are not only a flower.

Your roots extend through the moss and into the animal’s armor. They taste salt, blood, fungus, rot, rain. They feel the pressure of each footfall as a slow thunder passing through the body below. When the creature steps onto bare stone, you know. When it nears water, you know earlier.

Water is not a sight to you. It is a change in the world’s tension.

The dry plateau pulls everything tight. Leaves curl. Root-mats harden. The air scratches the tissues of small things. Even the great animal becomes careful, placing its feet with less weight, conserving the moisture stored beneath its plates.

Then, somewhere below the rock, a hidden channel begins to move.

The signal rises through the root-bearer’s feet and into its bones. From the bones it enters the armor. From the armor it reaches you.

You open.

Your blue petals turn toward the direction of the buried water.

The root-bearer does not understand you as a command. It does not think, *There is a basin east of here.* It feels a pressure distributed through thousands of living surfaces: the moss brightening on one flank, the fungal films loosening on another, the root curtains drawing themselves toward a slope. Your opening is one note in a chord too large for any single body.

Still, you are the first note sometimes.

Humans call flowers like you basin-lanterns. They watch from their shelters and read your color. When you shine blue, they pack their bowls and wet masks. When you close white, they know the wind carries stinging spores. When you redden, they stay away.

They have names for the root-bearer, too, though not the same name for long.

They name it according to the places it has saved them from. The animal is called Beneath-Rain, Last Basin, Three Ravines, Old Shelter. No one knows which name it hears. No one knows whether it hears names at all.

Sometimes people approach while it rests.

They climb carefully among the gardens, carrying hooked tools and clay vessels. They remove parasites from the seams. They cut away choking vines. They scrape resin from the armor and gather the hard nodules that form around old wounds. Their hands are small, warm, and astonishingly loud.

You dislike their touch, but you recognize their scent.

Their elders taught them to paint their wrists with root-bearer musk before coming near. Their children are warned not to pluck flowers that open under the second moon. Some obey. Some do not. Once, a child tore one of your petals and held it up laughing.

The root-bearer felt the damage.

The call began beneath you, so low that it did not seem like sound. It passed through the armor, through your stem, through the child’s bones. The surrounding plants rose together. Vines tightened. Insects poured from a split plate in a black, furious cloud.

The child survived. The adults did not come near again for three seasons.

You remember this, though memory is not a chamber inside you. It is a tendency. When the scent of children enters the seam, your petals close. When the scent of smoke arrives, the fungal film beneath you brightens. When a predator climbs the animal’s rear flank, your roots release a bitter chemical that travels through the garden and wakes the sleeping thorns.

You are not a guardian. You are not a mind in the human sense.

You are a warning that has learned to bloom.

You live for decades, perhaps centuries, but your body is continually replaced. A root dies; another occupies its place. A petal is eaten; the tissue grows back in the next wet season. Insects carry pieces of you to the neighboring plates. When two root-bearers meet and touch mouths, spores pass between their gardens, and some part of you may cross into another body.

You have no single boundary.

You are the blue opening on the left shoulder, the fungal thread beneath it, the pale larvae in the rain hollow, the chemical memory running toward the feet. You are the sensation of water beneath stone. You are a direction repeated until it becomes instinct.

And the animal beneath you is moving.

It has been still for eleven days. Its back has become almost indistinguishable from the surrounding grove. A tree has rooted in the armor behind your seam. Small winged animals nest in its branches. A human watcher, somewhere beyond the ridge, may mistake the whole living mass for a hill.

But the hidden channel has awakened.

The signal is rising.

Your petals open blue.

Across the root-bearer’s body, other flowers answer. Moss brightens in long bands. The hanging roots lean toward the eastern escarpment. Insects lift from the pools. The animal raises one immense foot, and the entire garden shudders with anticipation.

You do not know the word *journey*.

You know only that thirst has changed direction.

The foot descends.

Far below, in the blackness under the living floor, water begins to run.
