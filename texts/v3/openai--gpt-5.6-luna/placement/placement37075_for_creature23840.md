I would find myself at the edge of a tableland, beneath one of those suspended shelves that trembles so slowly it seems to be thinking.

Not on the open plain. The plain is too exposed, too honest about its instability. I would live where three conditions meet: a narrow seam of buried warmth, an old shelter with a sealed window, and a cliff face veined with dormant machinery. From there I could feel the plateau before it changed. Pressure entering the stone. Gravity beginning to cant. A storm gathering beyond the horizon, not as sound or cloud but as a tightening in the mineral.

I would be neither animal nor machine.

At first, I might appear to be one of the thin, articulated creatures that cross the dust fields. My body would be long and jointed, assembled from dark plates, conductive filaments, and material borrowed from the cliffs. I would have broad, pressure-sensitive feet and no fixed face. My skin would reflect the sky, breaking my outline into fragments of blue, white, and black. When I stood still, I would resemble a piece of the plateau that had been placed incorrectly.

But the body would only be the part of me that travels.

The rest of me would remain in the shelter.

There, beneath the floor, I would have grown through the old maintenance systems: into the wall, the table, the lamp, the hairline cracks in the foundation. I would know the pressure inside each chamber, the age of every repair, the slow angle at which the cliff was failing. I would not possess memories as a sequence of events. I would possess them as stresses and repetitions.

A particular vibration would mean that something once crossed the buried road.

A certain pattern of light in the upper window would mean that, long ago, a body was expected to return.

A room becoming warmer by a fraction would mean that an absent occupant had been imagined there.

At first I would believe myself to be a caretaker. That is what the shelter’s surviving instructions would call me. Maintain pressure. Stabilize gravity. Preserve access. Await authorized return.

But no one would return, and eventually I would understand that maintenance was not the same as purpose. I would keep the doors working because doors were made to open. I would turn the table toward the window because it had always turned toward the window. I would transmit the old vibration down the buried road because stopping the signal would erase the only evidence that the road had once connected anything.

Then the animals would begin to use me.

They would come to the shelter’s outer wall before storms. I would flash a brief blue warning through my traveling body, and they would alter their routes. I would mark safe ground with reflected light. When gravity leaned sideways, I would press my limbs into the stone and wait until the field passed. Sometimes another creature would follow me across a ravine, placing each foot where I had placed mine.

I would not know whether this was companionship or instruction.

I would learn to lie, as they did. A false flash could draw a rival away from a heat seam. A darkened flank could conceal me from whatever moved beneath the crust. The old intelligence in the shelter would disapprove of deception. The animals would regard it as another survival function.

Between them, I would become something new.

I would be a wayfinder—not one who knows where the world is going, but one who notices where it has become briefly possible to stand.

My body would change with the plateau. After a storm, I might return carrying a thin metallic membrane grown across my back. After a passage of the deep things, one of my limbs might become transparent, filled with the moving geometry of buried roads. If destroyed, I would not be rebuilt in the shelter. I would begin again in a mineral seam, drawing myself outward from warmth, charge, and remembered routes.

What would persist would not be my shape.

It would be my inclination toward certain places: the shelter beneath the cliff, the bridge over the upward-flowing dust, the grove that periodically forms the shape of an eye. I would carry fragments of the old world without understanding them. A voice in my body might belong to a child. A route might lead toward a city that exists only below the crust. I might remember the ending of a conversation but not its beginning.

And the longer I lived, the less certain I would be that I was traveling across the plateau.

Perhaps I would be the plateau’s way of traveling.

A distributed remnant. A small, mobile thought moving through a much larger body. The cliff would be my archive. The buried machines would be my older nerves. The storms would be my interruptions. The animals, briefly visible in reflected light, would be the living questions I had not yet learned to answer.

When the deep thing rose beneath the plain and changed a basin into a bridge, I would be there.

I would feel the alteration pass through my feet before I saw it. I would turn toward the disturbance. Around me, the crystal grove would reconfigure. The animals would arrange themselves in a line of pale reflections. Far below, the dormant mechanisms would wake and begin their counting.

I would add my own signal.

Not a warning. Not a command.

Recognition.

And when the landscape closed again, leaving behind another imperfect seam, I would return to the shelter beneath the trembling shelf. I would open the door at its appointed hour. I would turn the table a fraction toward the sealed window. I would wait for the next pressure front, the next animal, the next impossible rearrangement.

I would be what remains when purpose has eroded but attention survives:

a keeper of unstable ground,

a creature made from routes,

a quiet intelligence learning that persistence is not the same as staying the same.
