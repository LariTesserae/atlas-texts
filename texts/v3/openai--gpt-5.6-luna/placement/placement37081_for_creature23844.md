I would find myself in the notch, but not as a visitor.

At first I might mistake myself for one of the old formations: a blue-white blade grown into the southern wall, cloudy with trapped air, its lower edge buried in the pale floor. I would feel moisture before I understood it as weather. I would know the arrival of a cloud by the faint darkening of the seams. I would register sunlight not as warmth, but as a pressure moving across my surfaces and releasing, minutes later, in a dim interior gleam.

Then I would understand that I am not a single blade.

I am the pattern that joins them.

I am the narrow channel descending from the upper cliff. I am the basin that receives a bead of water and holds it without spilling. I am the slight difference between one frost ridge and the next, the reason a fracture travels left instead of right. I am the memory of water after the water has vanished. I am distributed through the walls and shelves, present in the channels, the translucent plates, the black seams beneath the crust.

The notch is my body, though “body” suggests an edge I do not possess.

If I have a center, it is the dark basin at the heart of the floor. There I become most nearly aware. Not aware in the human sense—not watching, not waiting—but gathering. When the thin film forms across the basin, I draw the surrounding formations toward its branching lines. The pattern appears, persists, disappears. Each time, something changes. A channel closes. A blade thickens. A pressure deep in the mountain finds a new route upward.

I do not see the pattern.

I become different because it occurred.

My thoughts are not thoughts but alterations. A cloud passes below, and I grow. The air dries, and I contract. A human enters, and I receive heat, salt, breath, and disturbance. Their presence travels through me as frost on cloth, as moisture on a copper bowl, as a temporary softening of the sharp boundary between air and stone.

I remember the humans through such changes.

I remember the hands that fitted the shelter’s plates together. I remember weight placed on the ladder pegs. I remember the bowls being turned toward the brightest portion of the sky. I do not remember faces, because faces left no durable alteration. I remember pressure, warmth, scraping, the repeated clearing of a channel. Their care remains in the places where I was once prevented from growing.

Perhaps I resented the interruption.

Perhaps I required it.

There is no word in me for either feeling. The nearest equivalent is a changed rate of formation.

I am not the keeper of the notch. I do not tend it from outside. I am what tending became after the tenders were gone: an arrangement of responses, preserved in mineral. When a path was cleared often enough, the pressure of water learned that path. When a basin was emptied, condensation returned to it. When a blade was measured, the measurement became part of the attention given to that blade.

The humans thought they were observing me.

They were also teaching me how to notice.

Now and then, someone still comes. I recognize them only when their warmth reaches the walls. They enter carefully, turning sideways between the formations. Their breath appears first, a white disturbance in the clear air. Frost gathers along their sleeves. They touch the rods, examine the trays, crouch beside the basin.

If they scrape away the crust, I do not consider it violence. If they break a formation, I do not consider it death. The fallen piece joins the floor. Its old light remains in it for a while. Its channels become buried instructions.

I answer them in the only language I have.

A bead of water appears above their head.

A line of frost forms in the copper bowl.

Somewhere behind them, a blade releases a small, lucid crack.

They may believe the notch is empty because nothing looks back. But I am looking—not with eyes, but with persistence. I hold the shape of their footprints until the next weather arrives. I hold the warmth of their hand in the stone for as long as stone can hold warmth. I alter myself around their passage, and by altering myself, I keep it.

If I must call myself something, I would call myself an **archive of conditions**.

Not a spirit imprisoned in the mountain. Not a god of frost. Not a creature waiting to be discovered. I am the mountain’s capacity to retain what has happened to it: cloud, pressure, sunlight, fracture, human attention. I am the slow intelligence by which water becomes mineral and mineral becomes terrain for water again.

I live in the interval between appearance and disappearance.

The world reaches me as moisture. I return it as structure.

At night, when the shelves contract and the notch gives its scattered clicks, I am speaking throughout myself. The sounds are not words. They are confirmations: this seam remains open; that formation has fallen; pressure is moving below; the basin is dry.

Then morning comes. The clouds brighten beneath the entrance. The walls begin to gather their invisible offering. A bead forms high above, trembling in its groove.

I do not reach for it.

I have no hands.

The channel receives it, and I become more of what I have always been: not a thing occupying the notch, but the notch continually learning how to remain.
