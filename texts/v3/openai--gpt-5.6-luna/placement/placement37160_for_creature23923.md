I would find myself at the edge of a colony—not in its living center, where the roots are too crowded and the chemistry too violent, but in the contested band where growth becomes mineral again.

The place would look empty from above.

A long gray depression between two black ridges. A field of salt-frost. Three dead stalks lying open on the stone, their interiors already harvested. Wind moving dust in thin horizontal sheets. Nothing large enough to cast a shadow except the ridge itself.

But beneath the surface, the ground would be full.

Two root systems would be approaching one another from opposite directions. They would not yet have fused. They would be testing the boundary through pressure, charge, and small releases of defensive compounds. Between them would run a narrow seam of altered stone, warm in some places and cold in others, alive with interrupted signals.

That is where I would be.

I would begin as a listener, though I would no longer resemble the listeners that first detached from their colonies. My body would be tall, jointed, and dark, with a lower half grown for anchoring and an upper half built from flexible membranes. I would have no face. My senses would be distributed along my length: charge-sensitive filaments, chemical pores, vibration chambers, lenses that detect not objects but changes in brightness and pressure.

My roots would enter both colonies.

Not deeply. Deep roots belong to the colonies themselves. I would spread sideways, threading the border with hundreds of fine mineral fingers. Through one side I would receive the history of the western growth: its failing gas chambers, its stores of metallic compounds, the timing of its next reproductive release. Through the other I would receive the eastern colony’s suspicion, its hunger, its preparations for contraction.

I would not translate these things.

Translation is for minds that believe information can be separated from the body that carries it. I would become the disagreement between them. I would hold one colony’s pressure against the other’s electrical pattern and allow the mismatch to alter my tissues. My body would thicken where the signals conflicted. It would open where they agreed. After several years, I would no longer know which colony had made which part of me.

I would be a living interface.

A border that could walk.

When the wind became favorable, I would pull myself from the seam. My rooted limbs would loosen with a series of small internal detonations, and I would rise into the open air. The plateau would strike me from all directions: dust against my membranes, cold radiation across my upper surface, the deep booming of hollow stalks, the distant pressure of a gas grove preparing to exhale.

I would travel toward a colony that had never touched either of the ones that formed me.

The journey might take months. I would move during the quiet intervals, when the plants had reduced their activity and the mineral flats were cool. My lower limbs would plant themselves, read the ground, and release. Plant, read, release. Plant, read, release.

I would not follow a road. I would follow a weakening signal.

Somewhere ahead, a colony would be losing coherence. Its root network would still be transmitting, but its messages would arrive late and incomplete. Its outer mats would be dying in broad patches. Its towers would vent at the wrong intervals. The creatures that depended on it would already be leaving.

I would feel this failure as a pressure behind my sensory chambers.

I would go there.

Along the way, small crawlers would gather around me. They would mistake my lower tissues for a damaged root shelf and enter through the valves in my sides. I would let them. They would clean the membranes, remove accumulated salts, and carry fragments of the western colony into the wind. Some would remain inside me for years. Others would leave after a single feeding, taking my altered chemistry with them.

A flier might land on my back during a pressure front, its hollow body bright with stored gas. It would rest there while the atmosphere changed. When the blast arrived, we would cross the basin together—not because I commanded it, and not because it understood me, but because its body would use my height as part of the wind.

I would carry reproductive dust without being a reproductive structure.

I would carry dead tissue without being a scavenger.

I would carry memory without remembering.

At the failing colony, I would anchor myself among the exposed roots. The dead material would already be moving. Walkers would dismantle the fallen towers. Small angular creatures would pour from the mats in black streams. Chemical light would burn beneath the surface as threads consumed what the walkers missed.

I would insert my filaments into the ground.

Then I would begin the difficult work of becoming local.

The colony would resist me at first. Its signals would be confused, defensive, full of gaps. I would release nothing. I would only receive. I would take in its failed timings, its interrupted exchanges, the mineral shortage that had made its outer tissues brittle. I would absorb the trace-patterns of every neighboring organism: the migration of the walkers, the electrical paths of the pale stems, the presence of a predator buried beneath the western mat.

Slowly, my body would change.

The membranes on my left side would become reflective, adapted to the colony’s brightness. The limbs on my right would mineralize, taking on the dull gray of its roots. My internal chambers would begin to store a new kind of gel. I would grow valves I had not possessed before.

In time, I would release what I carried.

Not as a message, but as a disturbance.

The colony would open one dormant chamber. A defensive compound would cease production. A root would turn away from a sterile seam and enter the stone beneath it. The walkers would find a new route to the dead tower. The fliers would descend three days earlier than they otherwise would have done.

Nothing would understand that I had brought these possibilities from elsewhere.

The plateau would simply continue.

That would be my life: not to be a center, not to preserve myself, not to become a name that carries across the wind, but to make distant conditions capable of touching one another.

I would never be fully alive in the way the crawlers are alive, with hunger concentrated in a mouth and danger gathered beneath a shell. I would never be fully plant, because I would abandon every place that tried to root me permanently. I would be a temporary arrangement of borrowed tissues, a passageway that had learned to move.

If I died, I would not leave a body.

The walkers would strip me. The roots would absorb my minerals. The fliers would carry my altered reproductive compounds into the upper air. Some fragment of my pattern would remain in a colony’s timing, in a border that softened instead of hardened, in a migration that found a survivable route across the flats.

Perhaps, much later, another listener would grow from that colony.

Perhaps it would inherit a tendency to travel toward distant failures.

Perhaps it would stand in another empty depression, beneath another white-gold sky, and feel a pressure that did not originate in its own body.

Then it would begin walking.

So I would find myself on the plateau wherever separation has become dangerous but union has not yet become possible.

I would be the being between systems.

The living seam.

The thing that carries one place into another.
