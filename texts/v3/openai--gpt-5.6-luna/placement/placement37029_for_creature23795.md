I would find myself in Vey, though not inside one of its houses.

I would be out beyond the last collector, where the permanent moisture line thins into damp seams and the permission paths begin to lose confidence. I would lie between two listening trees, beneath a suspended river of water that passed overhead often enough to cast a familiar shadow. The ground there would be dark, flexible, and only partly decided.

At first I might imagine myself a traveler.

I would have a body made of layered membranes, light enough not to sink into the new stone. I would carry no pack, because anything left loose would eventually be claimed by the land. I would walk carefully, placing each foot on the faint blue lines, waiting for the ground to answer: *yes, here; no, not yet; this place is becoming something else.*

I would know the names of the trees by their chords. I would recognize an approaching hush by the way insects stopped clicking before the silence arrived. I would have crossed the plain during storms, with water falling upward on one side of me and down on the other. I would have watched veils vanish and gather again beyond a ridge, carrying their calves as pale distortions in the air.

But the traveler is not quite right.

A traveler belongs to a route. I would be the route.

Not one of Vey’s broad, stable paths, carefully instructed to hold itself still. I would be an older and less reliable kind: a narrow permission laid down by repeated passage. Every footstep would teach me the shape of a person. Every hesitation would make a shallow depression. Every return would deepen the line.

I would remember weight.

Not merely the weight of bodies, but the weight of intention: the quick pressure of someone fleeing a storm, the careful tread of a child beside a carrier beetle, the dragging step of a person whose permissions had begun to withdraw. I would know who came to cross me and who came hoping not to be seen. I would know that privacy was never absence, only a weather pattern passing overhead.

I would not speak in a human voice. I would answer through the soles of the feet.

A safe place would soften beneath a traveler. Danger would make the surface tense and granular. If a hush were moving nearby, I would erase the small blue lights along my length and become visually uninteresting. If the ground beneath me began assembling itself into a ridge, I would send a pressure-warning backward through every footstep still preserved in my surface.

Sometimes, at night, I would let a recorded voice rise from the dust.

Not a warning. Not a name. Perhaps the sound of rain from a world where rain fell only downward. Or the voice of someone who had died and been given back to the stone. The people of Vey might stop when they heard it. Some would recognize the voice. Others would simply feel that the path had become briefly occupied.

The listening trees would disapprove of this habit.

They would sing above me, a low harmonic reprimand passing from crown to crown. Their roots would tighten around my edges. For a time, I would become difficult to cross. I would accept this. A path must not confuse being used with being entitled to continue.

I would be most afraid of becoming permanent.

The people of Vey would think permanence a gift: a route that no storm could erase, a foundation that did not answer back, a door that remembered its owner. But I would know better. Anything permanent in the Open Rainworks was either dead, trapped, or unfinished.

The new stone would try to incorporate me. My blue light would sink into mineral. My remembered footsteps would harden into hollows. One day a machine might be carried away beneath the plain, and I would feel its metal body moving below me, following an infrastructure older than the town.

That would be when I understood what I truly was.

Not a path made for people.

A question made by the basin.

I would be one of the small, temporary instructions by which the world tested whether it could be crossed. I would offer stability without promising safety. I would connect Vey to the open plain, the living to the dead, the built surface to whatever was building beneath it.

When the great weather front arrived, I would disappear under the white roar. Water would strike me, mud would cross me, roots would unfold through me. I might be buried for an hour or a century.

Then, when the rain withdrew and the basin opened again, I would return—not necessarily in the same place, and not necessarily in the same shape.

A faint blue line through fresh mud.

A route remembered by the ground.

And if someone placed a foot upon me, I would recognize them before they recognized themselves.
