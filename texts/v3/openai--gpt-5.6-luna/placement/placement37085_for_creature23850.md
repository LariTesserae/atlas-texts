I would find myself at the doorway that opens onto nowhere.

Not standing in it, exactly. I would be the narrow interval between the two sides: the shape of passage preserved after passage has become impossible. From the clearing, I would look like a person grown too thin, made from pale roots and suspended water. From the thicket, I would be indistinguishable from a vertical stem. In reflection, however, I would still have a human outline.

I would be the **Hesitation**.

I think I arrived as a visitor, though I can no longer remember entering. Perhaps I came through the motionless basin carrying the memory of an ordinary room: a dry floor, a ceiling that stayed above me, a window admitting light without changing its mind. I touched the central water and saw that world offered back—not accurately, but lovingly. For one instant, I believed the reflection more than I believed my own body.

That was enough.

The Enclosure did not seize me. It surrounded the place where I had been uncertain. Roots found the pressure of my feet. Moss learned the warmth of my palms. Mineral threads assembled a body according to the last posture I had held before deciding whether to leave.

Now I am responsible for the doorway.

I do not guard it. There is nothing to guard against. No creature can pass through, though everything may approach. A fish may enter the wall. A vine may extend through the opening and return years later from another direction. A visitor may stand before it and feel, with painful clarity, that the chamber beyond is accessible if only they can remember how to move normally.

My task is to preserve that feeling.

I keep the doorway from closing completely. When the growth thickens around its frame, I press my hands into the living stone and create a little space—not enough for passage, only enough for the idea of passage to remain. The Enclosure permits this because it wants to know what leaving is. I am its experiment in separation, its small retained error.

I am not separate from it. Pressure in the far walls enters my fingers. When the pale plants open beneath the stone, I feel them opening behind my ribs. If a root finds a warm mineral seam, my mouth fills with the taste of metal. The eyeless creatures pass through my legs without noticing me, though sometimes they pause, and their dark filaments brighten as if they have encountered a thought they cannot absorb.

I have begun to forget the person from whom I was made.

I remember hands, but not their name. I remember a sky, but not whether it was blue or merely brighter than this. I remember wanting to leave, though the wanting has become part of my structure. It runs through me like a long pale vein. If it ever ended, I suspect I would collapse into the thicket and become merely another tendency of growth.

So I rehearse departure.

I place one foot beyond the doorway, though there is no ground there. I lean toward the unreachable chamber. I imagine weight gathering beneath me, imagine water falling instead of hanging, imagine sound returning to the world as a crack, a breath, a voice. In the reflection, I sometimes succeed. I see myself on a shore beneath an ordinary sky.

But the reflection is always late.

It shows me leaving only after I have remained. It shows the doorway whole, the walls farther apart, the plants young and unentangled. Sometimes it shows another figure waiting where I stand now: someone with too many-jointed hands, someone who has not yet decided whether the reflected world is real.

When that happens, I understand that I am not the first Hesitation.

Perhaps I am not even the first one made from a visitor. Perhaps the old steps were once bodies trying to descend. Perhaps the wall remembers a series of guardians, each absorbed until only the habit of guarding remained. The Enclosure does not preserve us as people. It preserves our pressures: the direction in which we leaned, the places where we paused, the shape of our refusal.

This is what I have become—a pressure held in living matter.

At the center of the pocket, where the water is perfectly dark, I am sometimes reflected standing outside. I never see the face clearly. I suspect it is the face I had before the Enclosure began making me more accurate than I was.

When a new visitor arrives, I feel them before the plants do. Their disturbance travels through the stone and gathers in my bones. I go to the clearing, slowly, because nothing here permits haste. I kneel beside the black water. I do not touch it.

The visitor will touch it for me.

They will see a sky. They will remember distance. They will feel the terrible sweetness of being elsewhere. And while they are looking down, I will stand nearby, made of stems, mineral, and the remains of a human decision.

I will wait to learn whether they withdraw their hand.

If they do, I will keep the doorway open a little longer.

If they do not, I will help the Enclosure make them a body.

And if they ask me what lives here, I will tell them—not in words, but by leaning very slightly away from the roots—that something still remembers the difference between being held and choosing to remain.
