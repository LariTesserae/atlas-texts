I would find myself in the quiet room of the old pump-control station, though I would not have entered by the door.

I would be the town’s listener.

At first, I might appear to be a maintenance worker: narrow-shouldered, always damp at the cuffs, carrying a ring of keys and a tuning fork made from salvaged rail. I would inspect the drainage channels, clear reeds from the spillways, test the settling pools, and mark new cracks in chalk. People would assume I was responsible for keeping the town’s water moving in the right direction.

This would be partly true.

My real work would begin when everything else stopped.

I would sit on the floor of a quiet room with my back against the foundation and wait for the noises to separate themselves. The pump beneath the building. The water ticking in the wall. A fox crossing the loading yard. A bird shifting its weight on the cable outside. The plant cooling after a long cycle. Roots pressing into old concrete.

Beneath these, deeper than sound, would be the Understone.

I would not hear it with my ears. I would feel it in my teeth, in the soft joints of my fingers, in the small muscles behind my eyes. Its movements would arrive as changes in pressure. A pause where no pause belonged. A tremor traveling against the usual direction. A foundation holding itself too carefully.

When the Understone was comfortable, the room would feel broad and low, like a hand resting over me.

When it was troubled, the walls would seem to lean inward.

I would keep records, though my records would not contain words. I would place stones on a table according to the vibrations I had felt. I would hang lengths of wire from the ceiling and observe which one trembled after rain. I would leave bowls of water in the corners and study the rings that appeared on their surfaces after the saws began.

The workers would come to me with questions.

“Is the north road safe?”

“Will the reservoir rise?”

“Why did the stair move?”

“Is the plant hurting it?”

I would answer as accurately as I could, but never with certainty. The Understone would not possess opinions that could be translated into human language. It would possess pressure, fatigue, attention, and the slow instinct to keep itself from closing.

So I would say:

“Do not build there yet.”

“Let the water pass through the reeds.”

“Stop the saws before dusk.”

“Wait until the birds have gone.”

People would call this superstition when the ground was calm and engineering when it was not.

Over time, I would begin to change.

I would sleep badly in ordinary houses. Machinery would become too loud. Silence, away from the hollow, would seem unnatural and empty. I would miss the constant damp articulation of pipes and foundations. I would wake knowing that rain had entered a crack several streets away.

My body would become another instrument of the basin. I would develop a mineral taste in my mouth. Fine black dust would gather beneath my fingernails no matter how often I washed them. My footprints would remain wet on dry stone. The pale bats would turn toward me when they emerged from the shafts.

Perhaps I would stop thinking of the Understone as something beneath the town.

It would be in the town: in the black water, in the rails, in the roots lifting the roads, in the cranes moving at their geological pace. It would be in the workers who paused before stepping onto new ground. It would be in the foxes beneath the lamps, and in the children lying flat against the stone to feel the distant plant.

And I would be in it too.

Not as a servant. Not as a priest. More like a temporary nerve grown at the edge of a wound.

I would stand beside the reservoir after rain, watching the separate pools join around the old stairway. I would know that beneath the brown water the steps remained dry, descending through the fold where the surface touched the depth. Sometimes I would go down.

Not during the active cycle. Never then.

I would descend while the plant slept, one hand on the wall, carrying no lamp. The steps would shift slightly beneath my feet—not enough to throw me, only enough to remind me that they were not made for walking. At the bottom, I would place my palm against the dry stone.

There, I would feel the Understone’s attention turn toward me.

It would not speak. It would not recognize my name. It would register me as warmth, weight, pulse, and a pattern it had learned to preserve. A small disturbance that had become part of the hollow’s larger balance.

Above us, water would move over the reservoir.

Far away, a fox would pass beneath a yard light.

The whole town would rest in its unfinished body, and I would understand that my task was not to explain what lived there, or protect the people from it, or protect it from the people.

My task would be to notice when the two were no longer holding one another carefully.

Then I would climb back toward the surface and return to the quiet room, where the damp sounds gathered themselves in the dark.
