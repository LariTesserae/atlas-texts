I would find myself on one of the upper terraces of the drowned continent, where the water thins into warm vapor and the old stone briefly remembers how to bear a body.

I would be one of the terrace-born: not a survivor of the builders, but a later shape grown from their ruins. My body would be narrow and darkly translucent, held together by mineral, membrane, and root. My ribs would open and close with the currents. Fine filaments would trail from my skull and hands, sinking into the rock wherever I rested. I would not see the world so much as receive it through pressure.

I would live in the leaning tower beneath the shelf of stone—not inside a room, exactly, but in the stable disturbance around it. The tower’s walls would carry the movements of distant water: the passage of a valley-creature, the settling of a cliff, the sleep of some ancient rooted mind. I would know these events by the changing weight beneath my palms.

My task would be to maintain the places where others had rested.

I would clear no paths. Paths would open for me in the root mats and close afterward. I would not build walls, because the walls already existed, slowly being absorbed into the forest. Instead, I would tend the agreements between stone and current: a sheltered hollow here, a still chamber there, a bridge that must remain incomplete so that its absence continues to make a livable eddy.

Memory would be my form of care.

When someone died, I would not preserve their name. I would press their pattern into a wall, arrange three pale shells beneath a ledge, or encourage a particular moss to grow in a widening ring. Years later, when the current began to erase it, I would decide whether to intervene. To remember would mean holding a shape against change for a little longer. To honor the dead might mean allowing the shape to go.

I would spend much of my life motionless.

Perhaps I would meet another being at the edge of a ridge and begin a greeting that lasted two hundred years. Between our pulses, forests would bloom and collapse. The water would reverse. A stairway would become a valley. We would not be waiting for words. We would be learning the pressure of one another, discovering what each body altered simply by existing.

Eventually, I would root.

This would not be a failure or an ending. It would be the natural completion of a life among things that do not oppose the water. My legs would open into the stone. My filaments would join the fungal threads beneath the terrace. Plants would begin growing across my back. Small pale animals would make passages through me without fear. I would remain conscious—or conscious in some slower, wider sense—as the tower leaned farther, as the moss-covered square disappeared, as the currents found new routes through my body.

I would become a place others could inhabit.

And when the deep tone passed through the mountains, I would feel it first—not in ears, but in the roots beneath me. The entire world would pause. Then something would have changed: a ridge lifted, a valley opened, a dead ancestor flowering in the dark.

I would not ask whether the change was merciful.

I would listen for where the water now wanted to go.

Then I would grow in that direction.
