I would find myself in the interval between two sources of water: not at a settlement, exactly, but in an old pumping station halfway down a copper-colored terrace, where the plateau breaks into long shelves and the wind enters through the walls.

I would be one of the Cistern Folk, though not one who belongs entirely to a household. A **listener**. A keeper of buried things.

My station would once have been a mineral-processing plant. Its towers are gone, its rails end at the edge of a ravine, and half the building has been taken by lichen. The remaining rooms contain a pump, three ceramic cisterns, a condenser array assembled from mismatched panels, and a narrow inspection tube descending into the rock. At night, when the temperature falls, the tube clouds from within.

That is how I know the old well is still breathing.

I live above it in a room with no southern window. The walls are thick concrete, flaking into gray plates. Birds nest in the vents. A pale, broad-eared animal sleeps beneath my bed and leaves when I begin work. My clothes hang from pipes because there is nowhere else to put them. I keep my face covered during the day, not against dust but against the theft of breath. Every outward journey is a negotiation with the air.

My work begins before dawn.

I rise while the stone is still cold and place my hand against the pump housing. The machinery speaks through vibration before it speaks through sound. A healthy engine gives a regular shudder, like a heart too deep to be felt directly. A failing one skips. A dry one trembles at the wrong rhythm. If I press my ear to the casing, I can tell whether the obstruction is mineral, metal, or something living.

I am not always sure which of those is most dangerous.

The pump belongs to no one, though my station is the nearest habitation. When it starts, water rises through the inspection tube, dark at first, carrying old sediment from beneath the plateau. I collect a small amount in a shallow dish and set it beside the wall. The first creature to approach is usually a beetle. If it drinks and continues walking, I place a second dish outside for the lizards. If they return by nightfall, I open the lower channel and let the water pass into the basin.

That is the first law here: water must not arrive alone.

A cistern without insects is suspicious. A seep without birds is worse. If the cats begin appearing too soon, it means something upstream has failed and concentrated the life below. I read the health of a source by what gathers around it: the density of tracks, the color of the algae, the number of eggshells crushed into the mud.

People think I maintain the machinery.

What I really maintain is the pattern of arrival.

By midday I retreat into the shadow beneath the station. The plateau outside becomes too bright to look at directly. I can hear a flock moving along the far escarpment, though I cannot see it. Every so often the old metal frame above me gives a soft ticking sound as it expands. There are long periods in which I do nothing that would look like work to an outsider. I sit beside the cisterns. I listen to the rock. I wait for a change.

This is how the plateau has trained me: not to act quickly, but to notice early.

I know there is water behind the violet lichen on the eastern wall because the cliff cats avoid that patch. I know the condenser on the western ridge has begun to fail because the bright-backed insects no longer gather beneath it at dusk. I know a person has crossed the northern shelf three hours before I see them because the scavenger birds lift from the old rail bed and do not settle again.

The people call me by my personal name when they use the radio. In the nearest basin, they call me **Under-Ear**.

I do not dislike the name. It is accurate. My hearing is broad and deep, tuned less to voices than to hidden continuities: the click of water entering a pipe, the soft collapse of a damp seam, the first hollow note inside a tank that has begun to empty. At night, I can hear the plateau rearranging itself. Condensation ticking into gutters. Small bodies emerging from stone. Pumps waking below miles of rock.

Sometimes, lying awake, I wonder whether the machines hear me too.

The older people say that every keeper eventually becomes part of their station. Not spiritually. They mean that the work enters the body. Mineral stains spread beneath the skin. Fingers stiffen into the shapes of tools. One begins to dream in pressure and flow. After enough years, a keeper can stand beside a silent pump and know how long it has been dead without opening the casing.

I am not yet that old, but I have begun to understand what they mean.

When I travel, I carry a ceramic vessel against my ribs so that my body warms it evenly. I carry mesh, wire, resin, a knife, two test insects, and a strip of blue cloth used to mark unsafe channels. I walk at night, under stars so numerous that the sky appears to have been drilled through. The route between stations is not marked by posts. It is marked by absence: a gap in the lichen, a place where the wind changes, a seam in the rock that remains cool after sunset.

Once, I found a new seep by following a line of moths.

They had emerged from a crack in the basin floor and were moving uphill, toward a darkness I could not see. Beneath the stones was a pipe, and inside the pipe was a slow thread of clean water. I opened it carefully. By morning, birds had arrived. By evening, the first reeds had begun to uncurl.

I stayed there for nine days, sleeping beneath a collapsed conveyor, watching the basin become occupied.

That is the closest I come to feeling pride.

Not because I made the water. I did not. Not because I commanded the life that followed. I only recognized an opening and kept it from closing.

If I must say what I am, then I am a human being altered toward attention. A body shaped by thirst, altitude, glare, and mineral dust. A temporary instrument for detecting what the plateau is already doing.

I would be found at the edge of a basin before dawn, kneeling beside a darkened patch of stone. My palm would be pressed to the ground. One ear would be turned toward the buried pump. Behind me, the condenser mesh would be beginning to drip.

I would be waiting to learn whether the sound beneath the rock was water—

or the arrival of something else.
