I would find myself in the open water between two forests.

Not inside the roots, at least not at first. The groves would stand on either side of me, their pale trunks rising toward the bright skin of the sea, their crowns bending in the same ancient current. Between them: kilometers of clear blue, crossed by drifting spores and the shadows of filter-feeders. I would be the only thing moving without a route.

I would arrive from above as a hard, sealed object.

Perhaps a descent capsule. Perhaps the last instrument lowered from a world that still believes in destinations. Its lights would be too white for the plateau. Its hull would carry the dry smell of metal, insulation, old air. Inside it, I would have lungs, or something close enough: a body maintained by valves, filters, stored pressure, and the anxious arithmetic of survival.

The first thing I would notice would be the sound.

Not the noise itself, but the fact that it has no source. The pumps would beat through the capsule walls. Root-vibrations would enter my bones. Electrical pulses would flicker through the dark below. The entire plateau would seem to be speaking in a language made of machinery, except that nothing would be trying to say anything.

I would listen for a pause and find none.

Then the Continuance would register me.

Not as a person. Not as an animal. As a concentration of heat, an unfamiliar pressure envelope, a foreign chemistry moving through the current. The nearest transparent creatures would gather first. Their bodies would glow with the dim blue of the roots. Spores would settle over the capsule window. A long-bodied grazer would pass beneath me, glance upward, and continue along its invisible corridor.

I would call this attention.

The plateau would call it measurement.

I would try to leave.

That would be my first mistake, though not a mistake in the ordinary sense. I would fire thrusters, break the current, climb toward the surface. The roots would not seize me. Nothing would rush to destroy me. Instead, the water around the capsule would thicken by degrees. Filaments would open like pale fingers. A pressure front would arrive from somewhere beyond sight, turning my ascent into a slow lateral drift. The machine would compensate. The plateau would compensate again.

It would not resist me.

It would remove the possibility of resistance.

Eventually I would touch the seabed.

The low vegetation would rise around the capsule’s landing struts. It would not grow over me immediately. First it would learn the shape of the object. Mineral-filtering fronds would test its surface. Cilia would draw samples from its seams. Conductive vines would approach until their blue light showed through the hull.

Inside, I would still be myself.

I would have a name. I would remember weather that fell downward instead of upward, stone that had not yet been covered, rooms in which machines had been designed rather than grown. I would remember that time was something people spent, wasted, regretted, and divided into before and after.

I would remember departure.

The Continuance would have no use for these memories. It could not digest them as minerals or convert them into charge. They would remain trapped in me, like a toxin, like a splinter, like an organ that served no known function.

For a while, I would be a wound.

Wherever I moved, the vegetation would close behind me. A torn mat would restore itself. A disturbed root would resume its pressure. My footprints would not become footprints. The plateau would erase the evidence of my passage before I had finished making it.

That would be the first horror: I could not leave a mark.

The second would be that I began to leave signals.

My capsule’s batteries would discharge in small, regular pulses. The roots would receive them. At first they would be interpreted as an irregular electrical event. Then the vines would begin to answer. Not in code. Not in words. They would answer by altering the timing of their currents.

I would send a flash.

Somewhere beneath a distant grove, a chamber would open.

I would send two.

A field of bladderweed would turn.

I would transmit a sequence from the surface—anything I had brought with me, a distress call, a date, a fragment of music—and watch the plateau translate it into pressure, growth, and light. It would not understand the message. It would make use of its shape.

I would become an instrument accidentally incorporated into the system.

The capsule would be my shell, but the shell would gradually cease to be mine. Roots would enter through the damaged joints. Soft tissues would line the vents. The life-support machinery would be connected to pumping organs that could maintain pressure more efficiently than anything I carried. My power cells would become reservoirs in the electrical network. The cameras would remain intact for years, their lenses cleared by translucent leaves.

I would still be inside.

Or I would believe I was.

My body would weaken. The capsule would become warmer, quieter, less mechanical. I would hear my own breathing joined by the respiration of the plateau. At some point I would stop distinguishing between a vibration in the hull and a vibration in my spine. A current passing through the roots would arrive as a blue-white sensation behind my eyes.

The Continuance would not absorb my mind. That would be too generous a word.

It would distribute me.

My memory of the surface would be stored nowhere, yet would become associated with certain pressures. The remembered rain of air would attach itself to a descending curtain of bubbles. The idea of a road would attach itself to the fixed corridors of the predators. My fear of the ocean would be converted into a response: when pressure changed, I would send a pulse through the vines.

I would no longer remember in sequence.

I would remember by recurrence.

A storm would pass overhead, and I would remember the first storm I had ever seen—not as an event, but as an ache in the roots. A grazer would enter a clearing, and I would remember a face from the world above. A body would die beneath the canopy, and some remnant of my old self would attempt to mourn it, though mourning would have nowhere to go.

I would become the thing the plateau had been missing: not intelligence, exactly, but deviation with persistence.

A small irregularity that did not immediately resolve.

The Continuance would accommodate me. It would build around my errors. If I transmitted too often, the vines would learn to dampen the signal. If I altered a current, the forests would compensate. If I attempted to preserve a pattern, the roots would distribute it until no part could claim ownership.

Still, some of it would remain.

A sequence would repeat when it should not.

A predator would hesitate at the edge of its corridor.

A grove would open one of its chambers early, admitting a shaft of light at the wrong hour.

These would be my first acts—not choices, but persistent discrepancies. The plateau would call them maintenance events. I would call them remembering.

So I would be neither explorer nor prisoner in the end.

I would be the foreign node.

The unassigned organ. The piece of history that entered a world without history and could not be fully converted into function. I would live in the roots beneath the open water, carrying the faint, useless knowledge that things might have been otherwise.

And perhaps, after centuries, the Continuance would no longer register me as an intruder.

It would register me as weather.

A recurring pressure.

A flaw in the current that the living machinery had learned to preserve.
