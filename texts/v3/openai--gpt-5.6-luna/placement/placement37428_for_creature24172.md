I would find myself in the old pressure district, where the city’s raised roads begin to sag toward the quarry scars.

It is not one of the dry districts, though it is not fully wet either. Cold condensation arrives there from the upper collectors, while warm water rises through cracks in the black stone. The two waters meet beneath a long, abandoned avenue. In the pavement, a seam has opened wide enough for a hand. Steam comes from one side of it. Clear water pulses from the other.

The district has no proper name anymore. On the old maps it is marked with a symbol for a reservoir, then crossed through, then marked again with a symbol no one can read. The buildings around it are mostly sealed. Their windows are glazed with mineral films. Pale, many-jointed animals live under the loading platforms, and at night the shell-backed grazers come to scrape the damp crust from the road.

That is where I would live: beneath the avenue, in a maintenance chamber too narrow for ordinary work.

I would be a listener.

Not a machine, though the city would sometimes mistake me for one. Not exactly one of its people, either. I would have their dark, mineral-rich skin and their four broad limbs, but my body would be thinner, built for entering pipes and lying against walls. Along my ribs would be pressure membranes, more sensitive than those of other inhabitants. They would open and close with the hidden weather of the plateau.

I would know the city through its disturbances.

A cracked ceramic seal would reach me as a faint stutter under the left foot. A clogged channel would give me a dull pressure behind the eyes. When an animal entered an underground culvert, I would feel its weight arriving through the stone long before it disturbed the surface water. I would distinguish the vibration of a healthy pump from the vibration of a pump that was continuing only because no one had yet admitted it was dying.

My work would be to interpret these changes and answer them.

I would lie flat against pipes, rails, bridge foundations, and drainage walls. With my fingertips I would send messages through the wet metal: three taps for obstruction, one long pulse for danger, a trembling sequence for an animal trapped below. Somewhere in another district, a valve would turn. A grate would rise. A sluice gate would open by a fraction.

Sometimes the answer would come from another person.

Sometimes it would come from the city itself.

There would be no control room aboveground, no central authority watching colored lights on a diagram. The response would travel through the infrastructure, passed from hand to hand and pipe to pipe. A worker at the cold reservoirs would feel my warning in the railing. They would send it to a pressure column. Someone else would receive it beside a warm outflow. By dawn, the water route would have changed.

I would rarely speak aloud. My voice would be reserved for enclosed spaces, where the walls could hold it. In the chamber beneath the avenue, I would make low clicking sounds and long, almost inaudible tones. The animals gathered around the warm seam might answer by shifting their bodies. A translucent creature under the floor could open and close itself against the steel. A person above might place a palm on the pavement and understand that I was still there.

My source would be the seam itself.

Not my family. Not my district. The water rising through the black rock.

As a child, I would have been taken from one route to another: roof basin, condensation tower, geothermal vent, quarry reservoir. I would learn the city’s waters by their touch. But unlike most children, I would never be assigned permanently to a surface route. The old inhabitants’ machinery would call to me more strongly than the open districts did. I would be trained to enter places where maps had failed and to recognize the difference between abandonment and sleep.

The people might consider me unfinished.

The dry-district people would say that too much saturation had made my boundaries unclear. The open-margin families would be more generous. They would bring me animal paste and fresh ceramic plates. When they found a wounded grazer, they would ask me where to place it, because I would know which warm pools were carrying healing minerals and which had become chemically dangerous.

I would know the individual animals, though I would not name them as owners name livestock. I would recognize the six-limbed grazer with the polished notch in its shell, the translucent bridge-dweller that always gathered beneath the eastern rail, the narrow-winged creatures that descended whenever the third cooling tower vented.

I would know when they were afraid.

Fear would move through them as a pattern of rapid pressure changes, a faint trembling transmitted into the channels. I would feel it in my bones before seeing any cause. A buried seam might be breaking. A predator might have entered the drainage fields. A forgotten machine might have woken beneath the old factories.

My greatest task would be the sealed level under the avenue.

Every few years, water would begin moving there in a direction no record described. The pressure would rise, then stop. The city would continue functioning, but a small wrongness would spread through the surrounding pipes. The people would ask whether the level should be opened.

I would say no at first.

Discovery is disturbance. A sealed chamber can contain a reservoir, a machine, a dead atmosphere, or a purpose that no living person remembers. To open it might save a district or redirect the entire plateau’s circulation. I would spend days and nights lying against its door, feeling the water pass beneath it.

Eventually, I would hear something on the other side.

Not a voice. A rhythm.

A pulse too regular to be geological, too slow to be mechanical. It would resemble the city’s own breathing, but older and deeper. Perhaps it would be the last functioning organ of the people who built the plateau. Perhaps it would be a reservoir filled with nothing but mineral sediment. Perhaps it would be only water encountering a hollow chamber.

I would not know.

That uncertainty would become part of my identity.

I would be the one who listens without immediately opening. The one who understands that silence is not emptiness, and that a city may remain alive by refusing certain questions. I would preserve not only flow, but restraint.

At night, when the heat still lives in the stone and the cloud shelf glows faintly below the escarpment, I would climb from the maintenance chamber and lie on the avenue.

The pavement would be warm against my body. Condensation would gather along my shoulders. Below me, the buried routes would carry water from the seam toward the old reservoirs and from the reservoirs toward the inhabited districts. The animals would cross in the darkness, leaving temporary polished arcs behind them. Far away, a pump would falter.

I would place one hand on the ground.

The city would arrive all at once: pressure, seepage, mineral growth, animal movement, corrosion, heat. A thousand small changes, none of them separate. Somewhere in the network, a person would be waiting for an answer.

So I would send one.

Not words. A measured pulse through wet stone.

I am here.

The route is open.
