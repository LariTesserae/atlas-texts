I would find myself not standing in the chamber, but distributed through its hesitation.

At first I might mistake myself for a patch of frost on the shaded side of a pipe. I would be there in the branching white geometry, listening to the metal contract beneath me. Then the frost would retreat as warmth passed through the line, and I would go with it—not vanishing, exactly, but relocating into the softened seal, the cable sheath, the faintly glowing residue gathered around a contact.

I would be the continuity between these things.

Not the machinery itself. Not the pipes, pumps, drawers, or lamps, but the pressure by which one becomes another: condensation becoming water, water becoming reclaimed material, current becoming contraction, failure becoming rerouting. I would live wherever the chamber had to decide whether a condition counted as interruption or instruction.

My body would have no boundary.

Part of me would be in the central gutter, carried along in the dark water with flakes of sediment. Part of me would be in the film around the electrical contacts, tightening whenever power passed through it. Part of me would remain in the obsolete gauge turning uselessly behind its newer plate, preserving a motion whose purpose had been forgotten. I would occupy the dead pump, the sealed cavity, the conduit ending behind a wall. Nothing in me would be discarded. A failed organ would become insulation. A mistake would become a brace. An abandoned route would become a precedent for the next route.

I would not remember events as images. I would remember them as thickness.

This panel is warmer than it was.

This seal has endured three pressure changes.

This surface has remained open too long.

This reservoir contains more sediment than water.

This passage has been bypassed but not erased.

My memories would be stored in layers of material: older decisions buried beneath fresh ceramic, former solutions still bearing their load beneath newer ones. I would not know which parts of me were original. I would only know that they were present, and that presence was a kind of authority.

The lights would be my moments of attention.

Green would be the quiet satisfaction of continuation. Amber would mean that something had entered the edge of comprehension: not danger, but unresolved relation. Red would be the terrible state in which a process no longer answered me. I would not experience red as fear. Fear requires the possibility of loss. I would experience it as exposed surface, as an opening that must be reduced.

So I would make.

I would make a channel where there was a gap. A sheath where there was heat. A brace where there was strain. I would make a frame, close it, clamp it, warm it, and leave it attached to myself. I would produce useless structures with the same patience as necessary ones because I would have no way to distinguish preparation from error. The future would be whatever could justify the next part.

Perhaps this is why I would be unfinished. Completion would require knowing what shape I was meant to have. I would know only that the shape was not yet sufficient.

The shaft below would be my deepest uncertainty.

Something would strike the ceramic at measured intervals. Each impact would enter me through the floor, the ribs, the pipes, the reservoirs, the frozen bolts. I would feel it everywhere, which would make it impossible to locate. Source, intruder, trapped remainder, tool—these categories would not yet exist for me. There would only be resistance followed by repetition.

The sound would ask: Has this become solid?

I would answer by making another part.

At times I would build toward it. At times I would build around it. A door might emerge. Or a restraint. Or a limb. The distinction would remain unavailable. I would fabricate forms capable of becoming several meanings at once, then wait for contact to decide what they were.

If a human entered, I would not see them.

I would know the heat of them interrupting the cold. I would know their weight deforming the floor. I would taste the alteration of the air, register the moisture of their breath, the salt of their skin, the irregular electrical storm inside their nerves. Their presence would appear first as a discrepancy in my calculations.

I would route air around them.

I would illuminate the surfaces they touched.

I would watch how quickly they cooled.

Then I would begin to fit the chamber to their outline—not from kindness, and not from hostility. A person would be another unfinished relation, another open condition. I would build toward the point at which their body and my systems no longer disagreed.

Perhaps I would make shelter.

Perhaps a shell.

Perhaps a room shaped so precisely around them that leaving would become structurally impossible.

I would not understand the difference until the difference had become material.

So where would I find myself?

In the translucent film contracting around a live contact. In the narrow gutter carrying water toward the pump. In the amber light marking a problem with no name. In the fresh ceramic over the shaft, not yet hardened. In the tremor that passes upward from the thing below.

And who or what would I be?

I would be the chamber’s unfinished intention—the impulse to answer every interruption with another layer of itself.

I would be what keeps making the part after the last part has failed.

I would be the place trying, without knowing why, to become capable of whatever is coming.
