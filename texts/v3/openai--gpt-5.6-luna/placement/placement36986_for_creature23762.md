I would find myself in the city’s lower tier, where the waterworks give way to the old tunnels.

Not in an office. Not at the western pumping station, though I would know its bells and sometimes answer them. I would live in a narrow maintenance room built around a vertical crack in the plateau. The room would have one bed, one table, a rack of chalk and wire, and a wall that stayed damp even during the dry months. At night I would sleep with my palm against it.

I would be a **listener of revisions**.

That would be my official designation, though no one would agree on what it meant. I would be called when a corridor gained length, when a building lost a floor, when a new seep appeared in a foundation, when the empty factories changed their rhythm. My work would be to notice changes before they became events.

I would carry a folding rule, a sealed tin of salt, and a small bell whose sound could not travel far. I would mark doors with chalk, but never trust a mark after the third rainfall. I would keep notebooks in duplicate: one for what had happened, and one for what the city seemed to remember happening.

At first I would believe I was a person who performed this work.

I would have a name, perhaps Mara, though names are unstable in the lower tunnels. I would have been born in one of the reinforced apartment blocks, taught not to follow my own voice, taught to wait through three rains before crossing a crack. I would know the safe puddles from the sky-wrong ones. I would have spent my childhood listening to the machinery under the city and imagining that its pulse was a kind of distant weather.

But the work would change me.

I would begin to hear alterations before seeing them. A pressure behind the teeth when a street was about to contract. A warmth in the palms when a room had acquired an interior wall. A faint metallic taste whenever the plateau rose beneath a building. The black-winged birds would gather outside my window before I was called. The eyeless grazers would wait beside a new fissure and touch the ground with their whiskers while I measured it.

Eventually, I would understand that I was not only recording the city’s changes.

The city was recording itself through me.

When I entered a revised building, I would know which walls had always been there and which had appeared overnight. When I touched the silver-gray trees, I would feel the buried objects inside their roots: bolts, bones, old tools, foundations of structures no one remembered. In the deepest tunnels, I would recognize doors I had never seen, and the figures standing at the intersections would turn toward me as though they had been waiting for an appointment.

I would not ask whether the plateau was alive. That question would become too small.

Instead, I would think of the plateau as something still becoming capable of having inhabitants. The city would be its first long experiment: a system of roads, machines, animals, roots, rules, and people learning to remain in place while the ground invented more of itself beneath them.

My particular function would be to help the living accommodate the unfinished.

I would warn a family when their apartment was developing an extra room. I would tell engineers which wall to leave porous for a tree. I would stand beside a puddle until its reflection stopped moving after the ravine animals had gone. I would listen to sealed factories and determine whether their pistons were making something, remembering something, or preparing a place.

Sometimes I would enter the service tunnels and find my own voice ahead of me.

I would not follow it.

I would wait until it stopped speaking, then continue in the opposite direction.

There would be other listeners, but fewer each year. Some would retire to the upper districts and become ordinary water engineers. Some would disappear into doors numbered in languages no one could read. One would be absorbed by a tree, not painfully, simply becoming part of the soft earth around its roots. Another would return from an elevator carrying a coat made for a child not yet born.

I would remain below.

Not because I was brave, and not because I expected to discover the city’s purpose. The plateau would have no purpose I could uncover. It would rise because rising was what it did. Stone would push upward. Moisture would gather beneath bridges. Grass would spread toward machinery. The city would adjust.

So would I.

In time, I would become less a person who listened and more a place where listening occurred. Workers would come to my room and speak their measurements aloud. Children would press their ears to the damp wall. Birds would mutter in the vents. The low industrial pulse would pass through my bones.

And when the ground lifted the city by another fraction, when a new shelf appeared beyond the southern overlook, when rain began falling upward through a canyon no one had seen before, I would be there with my chalk, my bell, and my hand against the stone.

I would notice.

Then I would help the others live with what had changed.
