I would find myself below the sixth terrace, where the public ways narrow into service passages and the walls have begun to lean toward one another.

Not in the center. The center is too watched, too ceremonial, too close to the black water. Nor at the edge, where the basin’s construction thins and the old creatures sometimes break through. I would live in the intermediate places: behind cistern walls, beneath removable bridges, inside the thickness of structures that appear solid from one side and hollow from the other.

I would be a listener.

Not one of the listeners who cultivate the gray membranes in sealed chambers, though I would know their work. I would be something less official: a keeper of seams, assigned to walls no one remembers building and channels no one has yet decided to abandon.

My name would change with my district. In one quarter I would be called the Hand Beneath Water. In another, the One Who Hears the Empty Course. The oldest keepers would use neither name. They would identify me by the first collapse I prevented, or by the chamber where I was found sleeping with my palm against the floor.

My body would be narrow and dark, made for passages where a broad-shouldered worker could not turn. The pale seams at my wrists and throat would brighten when the masonry around me became wet. My eyes would be poor in the open, but I would see enough in reflected glimmers: green mineral light beneath a ledge, the faint trembling of a metal-skinned pool, the dull orange pulse of a coal carried under a hood.

I would not carry a lamp unless I needed to be found.

Instead, I would carry a mallet, a shallow bowl, three lengths of cord, and a packet of fired clay wedges. The mallet would be for testing. The bowl would be for listening.

A drop of water placed in the bowl behaves differently depending on what lies beneath it. Over packed fill, it trembles evenly. Over an empty chamber, it turns in a slow, stubborn circle. Above a passage of moving water, it shivers toward the nearest wall. Above one of the buried things, it sometimes rises against gravity and clings to the air.

I would spend most of my time kneeling in places no one else thinks to examine.

I would press my cheek to a stair and count the impacts traveling through it. I would crawl beneath platforms during rain, feeling for the first cold seep behind the facing stones. I would mark doubtful walls with short pale strokes, not as warnings but as admissions: this structure is still standing, but it has begun to become something else.

People would come to me when a room felt wrong.

They would not say that the wall was singing, or that the water in the cistern had turned its attention toward the floor. They would say only that their sleep had changed, or that a familiar passage seemed longer, or that a crack had appeared where no crack had been. I would go with them. I would place my hands against the masonry. Sometimes I would order a wall opened. Sometimes I would tell them to keep working and wait for the basin to reveal its decision.

I would be hated when I was correct too early.

There is little gratitude in dismantling a bridge that still holds, or emptying a house whose roof has not yet fallen. The inhabitants understand failure, but they distrust prediction. A danger that has not happened is still an inconvenience. A saved life is difficult to distinguish from a lost afternoon.

Still, I would continue.

I would keep a map, though not on paper. I would remember the basin as pressure and echo: the hollow beneath the third landing, the buried channel running north through the old kiln district, the terrace whose foundations are packed with timber that has never fully dried. I would know which walls were built over older walls and which older walls were built over something that had learned to move.

I would avoid the black opening at the center for as long as possible.

Not from fear of the Deep Witness. Fear would be too simple. I would avoid it because the note that rises from below would pass through me differently than it passes through the others. Their bodies would become still. Mine would answer.

The first time the resonant note sounded while I was near the central depression, every pool around me would tremble. The membranes would contract. Beneath my ribs, something would make the same long vibration—not loudly, not audibly, but with the unmistakable sensation of a door being tested from the other side.

After that, the keepers would ask what I heard.

I would tell them the truth: that the thing below did not sound like it was calling us.

It sounded like it was checking whether we were still there.

Then I would return to the sixth terrace, to the leaning walls and the wet service passages. I would repair the channel beneath a house whose occupants did not know it existed. I would scrape the membrane from a doorway and leave a small patch behind, so it could warn the next person. I would place a clay wedge into a widening seam and wait for the rain.

I would be neither builder nor prophet.

I would be the basin’s hesitation made into a body: the small resistance between holding and collapse, the hand that does not prevent change but gives it somewhere safer to go.

And if, one day, the construction finally approached completion—if every channel were obedient, every cavity measured, every wall braced—I would begin taking things apart.

A finished basin would be a dead one.

So I would open a sealed corner, loosen a foundation, divert a little water from its assigned route. I would leave one unfinished passage beneath the lowest terrace, wide enough for a future creature, a future flood, or a future inhabitant who had not yet been born.

Then I would listen for what came through.
