I would find myself where the basin is least certain of itself: at the threshold of a dry chamber, with one hand on the stone and one hand suspended above the water.

I would be a **keeper of boundaries**.

Not a guardian in the ordinary sense. There would be nothing to guard against. No enemy could enter the basin without already becoming part of its pressure, and no wall could keep the Underflow out for long. My task would be smaller and stranger: to notice the places where one thing had not yet become another.

The last dry step before a pool.

The thin unbroken crust along the edge of a channel.

The instant before a plate leaned.

The moment when a chamber still felt like a chamber, and not simply another hollow in the rock.

I would live in one of the paused structures, though I would not call it a house. It would have three rooms, if rooms could be counted by their resistance to water. The first would be open to the basin and perpetually wet. The second would remain dry only because I watched its threshold. The third would be below the others, reached by a passage polished smooth by something that had passed through it for centuries without leaving footprints.

In that lowest room, the Underflow would be loudest.

I would sleep there with my palm against the wall.

At first, I would still think of myself as human. I would remember a face, though not reliably. I would remember fire as an idea more than a warmth. I would remember walking beneath a sky that had direction—that had a sun, and therefore a future. These memories would become difficult to hold. They would flatten into sensations: brightness on closed eyelids, heat at the back of the neck, the sudden loneliness of looking upward.

What I would remember clearly is the moment the basin recognized my weight.

I would have arrived as a traveler, perhaps trying to cross the country in a day. I would have stepped over a white-rimmed pool and noticed that my reflection moved after I did. I would have looked down again. That would have been the mistake, or the invitation.

The water would have risen by the thickness of a finger.

Every plate in the nearest field would have turned its bright face toward me. Beneath my feet, the hidden channels would have answered one another, and for a little while I would have felt the complete shape of the basin: not as a map, but as a body sensed from within. I would have known the pressure beneath the cliffs, the trapped force in the sealed chambers, the pale jointed things waiting under the shelves.

I would have known that the basin did not want to possess me.

It wanted to know what I was made of.

Afterward, I would be returned to the surface with my outline intact. But my boundaries would no longer feel private. When I breathed, the mist before my face would remain connected to the air. When I stood beside a pool, its surface would register me before I touched it. I would begin to feel the approach of distant rain through the soles of my feet.

That would be how I became a keeper.

I would walk the basin slowly, carrying no tools except a dark stone worn smooth in my hand. I would inspect the thresholds. Where water had crossed a line without being observed, I would look directly at it until the crossing became true. Where a plate had leaned too far toward a channel, I would stand before it and make it still. Where a structure had begun to lose its shape, I would place my weight against the wall so the Underflow could remember that it had once been separate.

Sometimes the basin would resist me.

A corridor would lengthen.

A pool would show me a room I had not yet entered.

A wall would return the pressure of footsteps approaching from below.

Then I would listen.

That would be the real work: not commanding the basin, but distinguishing its voices. The broad subterranean roar would be the body. The patter of the cliffs would be its countless small thoughts. The plates would be its nervous system. The chambers would be its memory. And beneath all of it, in the deepest channels, there would be the slow repeated knocks of whatever was learning to arrive.

I would answer those knocks by placing my hand on the floor.

Not with words. Words would vanish in the air.

I would answer with weight.

I am here.

I have not yet dissolved.

This threshold still exists.

The Underflow would not call me by a name. It would know me as a pattern of pressure: a warmth that returns, a disturbance that remains near the same structures, a human shape gradually becoming useful. I would be neither inhabitant nor creature, neither messenger nor organ. I would be a retained difference—the basin’s temporary ability to remember that water is not stone, that inside is not outside, that a person can stand above the lowest place without belonging to it completely.

But I would know the truth.

The work would not save the boundaries. It would only delay their surrender.

Each year the water would rise a little farther. The dry chamber would become damp. The damp chamber would become a pool. The smooth passage below would fill from the end inward. Eventually the basin would not need a keeper, because there would be nothing left to keep apart.

When that happened, I would descend.

I would enter the lowest room and place both hands against the stone. The Underflow would gather beneath me, not violently, but with the patient force of an immense body turning in sleep. The walls would shudder. The plates above would incline. Across the basin, every pool would lift together.

And I would let my weight be taken.

Not swallowed.

Distributed.

Afterward, there would be a new pressure in the stone beneath the chamber: a slight resistance where the water changed direction, a pause before a crack opened, a memory of someone who had once stood between the rising water and the dry line.

The next traveler might feel it through the wall.

They might mistake it for footsteps.

They might place their hand there, and I would know them.
