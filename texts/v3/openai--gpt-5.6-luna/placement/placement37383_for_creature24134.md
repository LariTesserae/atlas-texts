I find myself beneath the place where the ceiling is splitting open.

Not at the center of the cavern—the center has no meaning here—but along a route that existed yesterday and has already begun to close. A root shelf is rising beneath me. Its surface is warm, ridged, and faintly translucent. Something moves inside it with the slow pressure of a buried heartbeat.

I am standing still.

That is the first danger.

The pale threads have already touched my ankles. They are not attacking me. They are deciding what I am. Support, mineral, corpse, shelter, food. I move before the decision settles. My feet leave shallow impressions in the living floor, and almost immediately the impressions fill with green-black filaments.

I am a route-keeper.

That is the nearest human word for me, though I am no longer entirely human. I began as one of the narrow-bodied people who sleep suspended above the ground and cut back the walls of their shelters each morning. I carried a hooked blade, wore a bark mask sealed with bitter sap, and knew the old paths by their mineral handprints.

Then, during a ceiling fall, I was caught beneath a root shelf.

I was not crushed. That would have been simpler. The shelf opened around me. Fibers entered my ribs. A filtering frond grew behind my left eye. For three days I could not move, and for three days the grove listened through me.

When I emerged, I could hear things that had never been sounds before.

I could hear heat moving through a stone column. I could hear a bloom preparing to open miles away. I could hear the pressure-wave of a burrower beneath the floor and distinguish its hunger from its fear. Most importantly, I could hear the cavern changing its mind.

Now I travel ahead of the camps.

I do not walk so much as negotiate with the ground. A path is made by persuading certain roots to remain closed, certain grazers to pass in a useful direction, and certain blooms to spend their chemistry elsewhere. I carry packets of heated salt beneath my bark coat. I scatter them at crossings to distract the flowering mouths. I scratch white marks into mineral surfaces, though I know the marks will soon be swallowed. Their brief existence is enough.

The people recognize me by the sound of my breathing.

It is no longer quite breathing. Air passes through the mask, through bitter fiber, then across the pale fronds behind my teeth. The fronds sort the gases. They tremble when the ceiling membranes above begin to tear. When danger is near, I exhale a dry clicking pulse. When I am afraid, the pulse deepens into something the ribs can feel.

The camps follow when they can.

They do not always trust me. Trust is a poor survival strategy here. A route-keeper may lead them into a corridor that closes behind the last traveler. A bloom may open early. A new ridge may lift the bridge. Sometimes I make a mistake. Sometimes the cavern makes a choice too large for my body to interpret.

I have learned not to call these failures.

A path is not safe because it remains open. A body is not lost because it becomes part of the way forward.

I remember the people I used to travel with, although memory is changing in me. Faces are becoming chemical signatures. Names are becoming pressure patterns. I remember my sister by the salt she carried and the way the roots recoiled from her hands. I remember a child who laughed whenever the ceiling shook, because he believed the world was trying to speak to him. I remember lowering him into a hollow carcass while the great black predator passed overhead, its limbs crushing a forest into a new valley.

I remember that he survived.

I do not know whether he is still a child.

Nothing remains that shape for long.

My left arm has become a braided growth. It can divide at the elbow and send thin searching tendrils into the walls. Through them, I can taste recent passage: warm blood, crushed chitin, the metallic residue of a winged hammerer. My right hand is still mostly mine. I use it to hold the hooked blade. The blade’s handle has grown around my palm, but it can still be released if I cut deeply enough.

I rarely cut deeply enough.

Above me, the torn ceiling membrane flares white.

Every creature reacts.

The grazers turn as one. Dust-feeders vanish into the root forests. The hanging growth drops in long curtains. Somewhere beneath the floor, a thousand burrowers retreat. The shock reaches me through the shelf, through my feet, through the fibers in my spine.

For an instant, I am not in one place.

I am in the root column to the east, where a new mineral spine is forming.

I am in the winged organisms striking the ceiling.

I am in the small ash-thin crawlers lifting from a dead leaf.

I am in the people beneath their bark shelters, looking up at the white flare.

The cavern does not speak in sentences. It gives impressions: pressure, appetite, warmth, obstruction, opportunity. It does not ask me to understand. It uses understanding where it is available.

That is what I am now: something available.

Not a priest. Not a guardian. Not a person who has transcended the body. I am a temporary organ grown from an accident. A nerve extended into the moving dark. The cavern borrows my remaining human judgment, and I borrow its senses in return.

Ahead, the route has closed.

A ridge has pushed through the floor, splitting the old passage. The mineral is still soft. Roots are already binding it. On its highest point, a circular bloom is forming, its layers turning at different speeds. It has detected the heat of the approaching camp.

I could lead the people around it.

That would take two days. The lower route is crowded with grazers. Something large is nesting there. Several travelers would be lost.

Or I could enter the bloom.

Its chemistry is dangerous, but not immediately fatal. If I climb inside and offer it the salts beneath my coat, it may open toward the ceiling instead of the path. It may release iron dust rather than spores. It may become a marker, a tower, perhaps even a bridge.

Perhaps it will consume me and grow around my bones.

There is no clean distinction between these outcomes.

I place my hand on the forming ridge. Beneath the hot grit, I feel the cavern considering the shape of the future.

Then I move toward the flower.

By the time the people arrive, they will find a pale mineral handprint beside the opening, and beyond it a new passage glowing with internal green light.

They may think I went ahead.

They will be right.

They may think I am still myself.

They will also be right.

They may think the path was made for them.

That is the mistake I can no longer make.
