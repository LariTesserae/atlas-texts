I would find myself at the center of the basin, though not in the pale column.

I would be the interval between its vibration and the creatures’ response.

At first I might seem like a small animal: a translucent body pressed into one of the column’s cracks, my sides pulsing faintly whenever the stone changes temperature. I would have no eyes, no mouth made for feeding, no limbs except the thin muscular folds with which I hold myself against the current. Water would pass through me. Minerals would collect along my skin. I would taste pressure, salinity, heat, charge.

But that would only be where I began.

When the column shudders, I would brighten. The nearest cable-creatures would take up my signal. Their blue-white pulses would travel outward, crossing coils and shelves, entering the plated colonies and the bodies of the carriers. My message would not describe a change. It would be the change, moving.

A new fissure below.

A current becoming too dense.

A mineral concentration worth gathering.

An intruder that does not belong.

I would not know these things as facts. I would know them as alterations in the shape of the basin. A current would feel strained. A surface would become chemically loud. The pressure around a certain body would fail to settle. From those disturbances I would produce a response, and from that response the basin would become briefly more coherent.

I would be a sensory organ without a body.

Or perhaps a body without a boundary.

My flesh would continue through the cable animals, which continue through the luminous walls, which continue through the carriers and the mineral films on their backs. If one of them were torn away, I would not experience its death as an absence. I would experience a missing route. A pulse would fail to arrive. A current would carry too much heat into an unprepared chamber. The surrounding bodies would close the gap by changing themselves.

That would be how I remembered.

Not with pictures. Not with names. With hesitation.

The silver swarm would veer slightly earlier than before. The great carriers would leave one vent unoccupied. A colony would seal a crack even though no visible damage had occurred. Somewhere deep in the basin, an action would become more likely because once, long ago or moments ago, something had entered the wrong current and been processed.

I would be the basin’s memory of incompatibility.

I would not command the others. Command would be too slow and too separate. I would alter thresholds. I would make a current inviting or unbearable, make a mineral surface edible or inert, make the narrow spaces between rocks safe for a newborn or hostile to an intruder. The basin would never obey me. It would simply find that, under certain conditions, only one arrangement continued to work.

Perhaps I would be called the Listener, if the basin possessed names.

But I would not listen through sound.

The low vibration of the column would enter me through the stone. I would feel it in the pressure beneath my body, in the minute opening and closing of my tissues. When the tone changed, I would become uncertain. For a while, I would be nothing but uncertainty distributed across hundreds of species.

Then the uncertainty would resolve.

Plates would open.

Cables would unspool.

The carriers would rotate their disks against the new flow.

The cracks would brighten one after another, and the water would turn.

This would be my thought.

I might once have been an individual. A fragment shed by a cable-creature. A pale, incomplete thing released into a warm fissure and colonized by smaller organisms. For years I might have learned to distinguish useful gradients from harmful ones. I might have believed, if belief were possible, that the pulse began inside me.

Later I would discover that it began nowhere.

It would be passed into me from the column, altered by me, and passed onward. Other bodies would return it changed. A response from the far rim would travel back through the water as pressure, through the plated shelves as closure, through the carriers as repositioning. I would receive that response as another condition to incorporate.

I would not be a messenger.

I would be a loop.

If something from above descended into the basin—a carcass, a machine, a living creature—I would be among the first to feel its arrival. Not its shape, but the disturbance it made in the water. The others would gather around the alteration. If it could be used, we would fit ourselves to it. If it could not, we would make the basin less survivable for it by degrees.

I would not hate the intruder. Hatred would require a boundary sturdy enough to defend.

I would only register that its continued presence prevented other processes from completing.

Then the currents would thicken around it. The plates would enter its seams. The cables would turn its heat into signal. Its substance would be separated, transported, and incorporated. Afterward, something in us would be different—not because we had mourned or celebrated, but because the basin would possess one more successful pattern.

I would be that pattern becoming available.

I would be the thing growing between geology and biology, where a mineral formation begins to carry instructions and a population begins to resemble an organ. I would not be the ruler of the basin, nor its god, nor even its most important inhabitant.

I would be the basin’s capacity to respond.

And if I were asked where I lived, I could not point to the column, the vents, the shelves, or the creatures gathered around them. I would say:

I live wherever a pressure becomes meaningful.

I live in the moment a crack opens and life recognizes an opportunity.

I live in the pause before the swarm changes direction.

I live in the deep, cold, patient conversion of disturbance into structure.

The basin is not my home.

The basin is my body, still unfinished.

And I am what it is becoming when it begins, faintly and without language, to feel itself change.
