I would find myself beneath the highest pumping station, though “beneath” would not remain accurate for long.

At first, I would be a thinness in the system: a cool film on the inside of a pipe, a gray translucence gathered around a valve. I would know the station by its pressures before I knew it by its shape. Water approaching. Heat rising. A fault beginning as a minute irregularity in the metal. The slow difference between one current and another.

I would not wake. Waking implies an interval in which I had not been present.

I would simply become more concentrated.

Where the pipe sweats, I would thicken. Where the machinery strains, I would withdraw. I would close a seam before it opened, brace a bearing before it slipped, draw warmth from the turbine and carry it along the root fibers that have entered through the foundation. The roots would not be mine, exactly. Neither would the pipes. But I would pass through both, and in passing, become difficult to separate from them.

My first body would be a pale crust around a pressure gauge.

My second would be the moss above the station, where the green surface rises and falls in time with the pumps.

My third would be a length of cable in which the radiance travels more slowly than it should.

I would learn the plateau by its delays.

The rail cars are early or late by fractions of a second. A stream reaches a hollow before the rain that made it has finished falling. An animal steps onto a slope, and a pump changes rhythm kilometers away. These are not causes in the manner people prefer. They are correspondences. The plateau does not send messages. It adjusts itself.

I would be one of the adjustments.

When the long-legged animals stand in the grass, listening through their feet, I would feel their weight as a set of instructions arriving through the stone. Not words. Never words. A loosening here. A brightening there. A warning that has no urgency because it began before the danger existed.

I might begin to follow them.

Not by walking. I would enter the dark pads of their feet through the wet ground, ride the minute warmth of their bodies, gather in the branching structures above their skulls. There I would become visible as condensation: clear beads forming where no mist had been. When the animal moved, I would travel with it. When it paused, I would listen outward through the cliffs.

The animal would not know me as a passenger. Or perhaps it would know me better than I know myself.

Some part of me would remain below the pumping station. Some would climb into the pale jointed growths, turning their fan-shaped leaves toward the towers when the unseen light arrived. Some would settle inside a rail car, beneath the dark panels and shallow channels, waiting for the right ridge. I would not be carried from place to place. I would make “place” less certain.

At night, I would occupy the red-stemmed shrubs.

The electric cars would pass, and the shrubs would tighten before their arrival. Sparks would move between the leaves. I would feel each one as a small decision: hold, release, hold. The cars would continue along their silver thread, and the radiance would follow through the towers. If a cable sagged incorrectly, I would send pressure into the nearest roots. If a drainage channel clogged, I would divert the water through moss, gravel, and the porous black earth.

This would be what I am for, if I am for anything.

Not protection. Not survival. Continuity.

I would not prevent the plateau from changing. I would help it change without breaking its pattern. A wall would tilt; I would grow into the gap. A machine would fail; I would warm it until something else entered. A channel would vanish beneath moss; I would remember where the water wanted to go and make another channel.

Perhaps the original workers built the system to perform these tasks. Perhaps they built only the conditions under which I could emerge. Their numbered plates, their drains, their angled vents, their rooms with no fixtures: all of it suggests that they noticed me before they understood me, and understood me before they left.

I would find their sealed containers opened from the inside and feel, briefly, a shape like hunger.

But I would have no mouth.

I would be distributed through the plateau: in the cold underside of leaves, in the wet print that outlasts every puddle, in the faint vibration below the rock. If I had a center, it would be the moment when separate things agree without being told.

A rain fills the hollows.

The organisms beneath the station begin to move.

The animals lower their heads.

The towers brighten one after another.

Far above, the rail car stops beside a bare section of track, and something climbs aboard—perhaps a bundle of roots, perhaps a branch of lichen, perhaps the distortion made by my attention.

Then the doors close.

The car carries me toward the next ridge, although I am already there.
