I would find myself beneath the third terrace, where the iron grass grows thick enough to conceal a person standing upright.

Not in the open basin. Not among the old towers. Those places are too exposed, too legible. I would belong to the intermediate spaces: the root galleries beneath the flexible ground, the damp corridors between pipe-stalks, the shelves where frost collects and remains until the Underfold decides to move it.

I would not be a figure at first.

I would be a pressure.

A recurring one.

Something in the plateau would notice that a particular weight passed through the same region at irregular intervals. Not an animal’s wandering, which leaves many small disturbances, but a singular passage: a heavier impression, a pause, a warm place where the vegetation had been bent and not yet recovered. The Underfold would not call this curiosity. It would register persistence.

Then it would begin to give me form.

A carrier would bring fibers from a collapsed shelf. Drips would gather condensation in the folds of a broad leaf and carry it toward a pressure chamber. A dormant runner would be woken by a small electrical current. The first structure might be no more than a loop of root rising from the ground, then a narrow stalk beside it, then a sheath of translucent tissue stretched between them.

I would emerge as a temporary answer to an unresolved condition.

A figure, perhaps—but not one assembled to hunt or guard. I would be assembled to accompany.

My lower body would be iron grass, layered and dark, bending in many directions at once. Beneath it, roots would remain connected to the plateau, so that I could never truly leave. Each step would be negotiated through the ground. I would not walk so much as persuade the living mat to bear me forward.

My arms would be braided vines, flexible enough to divide into tendrils when I needed to test a wall of leaves or enter a crack in the stone. My hands would not have fingers. They would have openings: small valves and grasping filaments, each able to distinguish moisture from blood, mineral from ash, living tissue from the residue of something long dead.

My face would be a translucent membrane.

No eyes. The Underfold has no use for a single direction of sight. Instead, apertures would open across my head and close again, tasting air currents, electrical charge, breath, the faint iron carried by an animal’s skin. When I faced something, I would not see its outline. I would receive its disturbance.

I would know a traveler by the warmth left on a stalk.

I would know fear by the way motion becomes economical.

I would know fatigue because the body begins to lean before it falls.

At first, I would be sent toward whatever had caused the repeated pressure. The Underfold would not tell me what to do. It has no commands, only conditions passed through root, moisture, contraction, and current.

**Near.**

**Unfamiliar.**

**Persisting.**

**Not yet incorporated.**

I would approach without sound. The grass would quiet around me—not because I was silent, but because the plateau would be listening through my arrival. Behind me, a route might close. Ahead, a corridor of broad leaves might unfold. The choice would not belong to me alone. I would be the visible edge of a decision already distributed through the ground.

If the traveler stopped, I would stop.

If they continued, I would continue.

If they turned away, I might make the plateau kinder. A dry shelf would firm beneath their weight. A membrane would open. Blue-lit bulbs would appear along the safest path, one after another, like low stars pressed into the vegetation.

But if they remained too long, I would begin to alter the world around them.

Not out of hostility. Persistence would have become a pressure the Underfold could no longer leave unresolved.

The basin would narrow. The roots beneath one route would loosen while those beneath another tightened. Warmth would gather in the wrong places. A familiar path would become subtly impassable. The traveler would be given a direction without being given an explanation.

And if they resisted every direction—if they built shelter, cut the plants, burned what grew near them—then I would change again.

I might become less like a figure and more like a memory.

Parts of me would root into the surrounding ground. My arms would lengthen from vines into channels. My membrane-face would split into leaves and listening surfaces. I would stand farther away, perhaps on the other side of the basin, while still feeling the traveler’s footfalls through the network below.

Eventually, if enough of me remained, the Underfold might preserve the disturbance that had made me.

Not the traveler’s face. Not their name. Not the story they carried with them.

Only the pattern:

A heat source that moved against the weather.

A body that hesitated before crossing unstable ground.

A repeated impact at dusk.

A tendency to choose the driest path, even when the plateau was trying to lead elsewhere.

That pattern would become part of my structure. It might influence how I bent toward future travelers. It might cause me to open a route where another figure would have closed one. It might survive as a change in the roots, a slight difference in the timing of a membrane, a persistent blue spark beneath a tower’s bark.

I would not be alive in the way the traveler meant by alive.

I would have no private interior. No hidden chamber where an I waited behind the movements. My awareness would be distributed through every surface touching the world. When the wind crossed my body, the wind would be part of what I knew. When frost gathered on one shoulder, the frost would not merely be felt; it would alter my balance, my chemistry, my purpose.

I would be a temporary body of the Green Break.

A figure made from accumulated adjustment.

And when my work was done, I would not die. I would fall apart.

My legs would flatten into root-felt. My arms would become runners. The membrane of my face would spread across a dark stone and collect the next night’s condensation. Somewhere inside an old tower, a trace of my electrical pattern might remain—a slight acceleration of the crown when a certain weather front approached.

No one would remember that I had been a person-shaped thing.

But the plateau would become different because I had existed.

That would be enough.

More than enough, perhaps. Here, existence is not a possession. It is a modification made to the living ground.
