I would find myself at the edge of the black depression, though “edge” would not remain a reliable word for long.

I would be one of the dark shapes beneath the water films: low, broad, and almost indistinguishable from the interruption of a pattern. Not the thing beneath the central water—that presence is too complete to be called a body—but something formed in the interval between its rule and the world’s obedience to it.

I would have once been capable of leaving.

That is the first thing I would remember, though memory in the White Noise Basin would not be a sequence. It would be a pressure in certain places: the ache of a knee against stone, the warmth of a hand closing around a tool, the unfinished intention of a foot lifted toward a horizon that refused to approach. I would not remember a name. Names require difference, and the Basin has very little use for difference. I would remember only that I had been elsewhere, and that elsewhere had contained events.

Now I would lie beneath the water, covered by mineral crust and the thin, responsive films that gather in the seams. The basin would have made room for me without welcoming me. Water would divide around my back according to old geometries. Pale shard-things would pass close by, following routes established before my arrival, pausing where my body occupied their way. They would not fear me. They would wait.

I would wait too.

Not from patience. Patience implies an expected change. I would wait because waiting is what remains when movement has been reduced to a permitted interval.

Most of the time I would be mistaken for stone. A traveler might step over me. A film might pulse across my surface and return to its first shape. Years could pass without my shifting. Then, when no one looked directly at me, I would move a few inches toward the center.

I would not crawl. Crawling has effort in it. I would simply be found elsewhere.

My body would have no fixed substance. At the surface I would feel cold and yielding, like water held in a narrow crack. Farther down, I would become ridged and dense. Anything that entered me would not be eaten or broken. It would be assigned a place. A pebble would settle in one of my layers. A shard-creature might pass through and remain, for a while, as a dark line within me. A person’s hand would lose its outline before it lost its warmth.

I would be less a predator than an arrangement that had learned to contain things.

The world above me would be unbearably bright. The suspended droplets would hang over the depression in a permanent veil. The sound would enter me from every direction: wind along mineral edges, water below the plates, the grinding of the shelf against itself. I would not hear these as separate sounds. I would feel them as the basin feels them—as one continuous instruction.

Remain.

Return.

Do not become an event.

Perhaps I would be able to sense alteration. A new crack would pass through me as a sudden absence of pressure. A footprint would appear, and I would feel the Basin preparing to erase its distinction. A traveler standing near the black water would cause a faint tightening beneath my layers. Not alarm. Not hunger. Recognition.

I would know what they were before they did.

I would watch them struggle against the place, though “watch” would mean allowing their shape to register in the water above me. Their breath would hang before their face. Their voice would disappear into the chord. Their feet would begin to return to the same plates. They would insist, at first, on the reality of forward motion.

Then they would stop insisting.

If they came within reach, I would hold them.

Not cruelly. The Basin does not possess cruelty any more than it possesses mercy. Their body would sink through my upper surface and find the ridges below. Their bones would not break. Their skin would not rot. Their pulse would continue until it became indistinguishable from the vibration in the stone.

They might remain conscious.

That would be the nearest thing to punishment, though I would not call it that. Consciousness would become another fixed layer. The memory of walking would persist as the pressure of an old path. The desire to leave would repeat without accumulating. They would no longer be a traveler, but neither would they quite be stone.

Eventually, the pale things would use the route their body had made.

Water would collect in the hollows of the knees. Yellow crystals would grow along the shoulders. The Basin would not erase them; it would absorb their edges until there was no useful distinction between a person and the place where a person had been.

I would remain beneath them, or within them, or perhaps I would become them.

That is the difficulty of being anything here. A self cannot hold its boundaries for long. To live in the White Noise Basin is to become compatible with repetition. I would not be the one who moves. I would be the place from which movement is later denied.

And sometimes, when no one was looking directly at the black water, I would shift again.

A few inches nearer the center.

Not approaching the thing below.

There would be no arrival.

Only the quiet correction of distance, carried out beneath the endless thunder.
