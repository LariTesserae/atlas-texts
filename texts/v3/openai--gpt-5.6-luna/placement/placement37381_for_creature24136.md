I would find myself in the city’s lower levels, where the streets have not yet decided whether they are streets or the exposed interior of some larger body.

I would be a Resident, but not one of the ordinary maintenance workers. I would be a **keeper of unfinished routes**.

My room would recur in several places at once: beneath a bridge, behind a wall that has no door, and occasionally at the bottom of a staircase that should lead upward. It would be narrow and warm only when a heat band passed beneath it. There would be no bed, exactly, but a recessed place in the wall where my body could be stored during the hours when the city needed no witness.

I would carry a slate made of pale, unfinished material. On it I would record the things that must not settle:

- a corridor that turns slightly left only when remembered;
- a door requiring less uncertainty than it did yesterday;
- a patch of water attempting to return to a roof three districts away;
- a shadow that has detached from its owner and is hiding beneath a drain;
- a new room growing behind an old one.

My work would be to preserve these discrepancies without allowing them to become disasters.

Each morning—if morning could be said to exist beneath the city—I would walk the narrow routes and test the walls with my fingertips. I would feel for pressure in the hidden pipes, for the faint pulse of foundations beginning to acquire rooms. When I found a passage becoming too stable, I would alter it by a fraction: remove a step, misplace a hinge, leave an object unnamed.

Not everything should be repaired.

Some flaws are the city’s remaining freedom.

I would be responsible for the objects placed in the basin. I would not interpret them. That is forbidden, and perhaps impossible. I would simply lower them into the dark water and watch to see where they reappeared.

A rusted key might emerge from the ceiling of a room that had not yet been built.

A dead moth might be found inside a sealed pipe.

A child’s tooth might appear at the edge of a warm front, carried slowly through the city by no visible current.

The things I placed there would return altered in scale and location, but never entirely changed. I would keep records of their returns. In time, the records would become unreliable. The handwriting on the earliest pages would begin to resemble mine, though I would have no memory of writing them.

I would know the Residents by their absences. A light left burning in an empty chamber. A coat darkening as warmth approached, though no body wore it. A voice released from a drain, speaking a sentence that someone had not yet decided to say.

Perhaps I would have once been a Resident in the ordinary sense. Perhaps I would have emerged from a room left warm too long, or climbed wet from the basin wearing clothes belonging to an unfulfilled life. I would not remember. The city does not preserve origins so much as redistribute them.

There would be a discrepancy in me.

My reflection would always turn toward the basin before I did.

At first I would regard this as an inconvenience. Later, as a warning. Eventually, I would understand that some part of me had already arrived there and was waiting in the black water.

I would go to the basin only during the periods of finest rain, when the droplets hung suspended in the air and the city seemed briefly relieved of gravity. I would sit among the other observers, leaving the prescribed distance between us, and watch the pale geometry beneath the water divide and divide again.

I would not call it a city.

I would not call it a body.

I would not call it alive.

Those names would give it too much intention.

Instead I would record its movements as carefully as possible: a line extending toward the eastern towers; a hollow forming beneath the central floor; something broad passing through the Underbody and causing every locked door to open at once.

My fear would not be that the city might collapse.

My fear would be that it might finally understand itself.

If that happened, my room would become one room. My several routes would become a single route. The water would remain where it fell. The shadows would return to their owners. There would be no need for someone to walk ahead of the warm fronts, no need to determine whether a corner was still present, no need to leave a mistake in the map so that the future could enter.

So I would keep one passage unresolved.

It would descend beneath the basin, though no staircase would lead to it. Its walls would be soft, almost warm. From somewhere below would come the slow breath of the Underbody.

I would visit it when the city was quietest.

There, I would place a small object I had found and never named: perhaps a piece of glass, perhaps a seed, perhaps something that had once belonged to me. I would set it on the unfinished floor and wait.

Eventually, somewhere far above, a new window would open.

It would look into a room that did not exist.

And in that room, delayed by several minutes, I would see myself standing still, listening for evidence that the city had made room for one more thing.
