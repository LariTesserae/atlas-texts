You would find yourself in the abyssal basin of a drowned continent, far below any surface that still remembers the sky. Not merely on the ocean floor, but inside a world whose floor, water, weather, and history have become one continuous body.

And you would not be a visitor there.

You would be one of the low, armored organisms gathered around the vents—though “organism” would already be too clean a word. You would be a living shelf beneath the silt, a broad mineral-skinned body with no distinct head and no urgent need for limbs. The upper part of you would resemble shale: dark, ridged, dusted with iron, furred with bacterial films. Beneath the sediment, your living tissue would spread outward in fine, sensitive roots.

You would know the basin through disturbance.

The current would arrive as pressure along your flank. A falling stone would be a brief percussion. A weakening vent would be a slow cooling in your buried flesh. The passage of some enormous animal would announce itself as a distant alteration in the weight of the water, a wake moving through the darkness long before the creature itself could be seen—if seeing had any meaning here.

You would feed by receiving.

Water would pass through the folds along your body, bringing bacteria, dissolved remnants, mineral dust, and the invisible residue of lives ground into the seabed. You would take what could sustain you and return the rest as fine sediment. Around your body, this rejected matter would gather into a low mound. Fronds would root there. Films would spread there. Small blind animals would eventually feed there.

Your waste would become habitat.

Your habitat would become you.

You would not make a shelter. You would gradually become one. Over centuries, your plates would harden and be coated with minerals until other creatures could no longer distinguish your body from the crust around it. A collapse might bury you. You would not climb free. You would slow almost to death, preserving yourself in the warmth beneath the new weight. Perhaps you would awaken when the vent reopened. Perhaps you would remain there, becoming another dark layer beneath the layers.

You would communicate without voice.

A contraction in your buried tissues would send a tiny tremor through the mud. Nearby bodies would feel it and close their feeding folds. The message would pass outward—not as language, but as a shared moment of withdrawal. Then, when the danger had moved on, the plates would loosen. The filaments would reopen. The current would enter you again.

You might be one body, or a colony, or a whole buried ridge whose separate parts had survived for so long that separation had ceased to matter. One portion of you could die beneath a collapse while another continued feeding beside a warm seam. Your boundary would not be a line. It would be a gradient in the sediment.

You would have no horizon, no sky, no surface, and no need of them.

You would be an elder of the basin: nearly indistinguishable from stone, slowly filtering the dark, recording the world in pressure and temperature. The drowned continent would pass through you as chemistry. The currents would pass through you as sensation. Dead ages would accumulate upon your back.

And when the water moved over you, carrying silt across your plates, you would remain almost still—not because you were unchanging, but because your kind of movement was measured in collapses, mineral growth, buried generations, and the gradual transformation of a living body into landscape.
