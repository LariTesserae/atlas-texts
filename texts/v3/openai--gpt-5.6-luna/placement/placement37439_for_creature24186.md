I would find myself in the upper maintenance terraces, just below the Rimward settlements and above the third condenser.

Not in a tower, exactly, but inside the wall of one: a narrow room built around a vertical survey shaft. My bed would be bolted to a floor that was once level. The room would contain a kettle, a pressure mask, three calibrated listening plates, and a window too narrow to see through comfortably. Beyond it, the neighboring peak would rise slowly in the distance, changing by an amount no eye could detect.

I would be a listener.

Not someone who listens to people, though I would become good at that too. I would listen to the mountain.

My work would begin before the first light reached the city. I would walk the service corridors with one hand against the walls, feeling for the minute differences in vibration that instruments sometimes missed. The pumps beneath the district would have their ordinary signatures: the soft cycling of pressure, the pause before release, the long metallic complaint of a valve adjusting to a chamber far below.

I would know them the way another person might know the voices of family.

This pump is tired.

That rail is carrying too much.

The eastern foundation has shifted again.

Something below the old quarry is answering.

I would carry no weapon. My tools would be a listening disk, a mineral scraper, a spool of bright wire, and a small sealed vessel for collecting anything that had begun to sweat where it should not. My authority would be modest but absolute in one narrow circumstance: if I marked a seam red, the work stopped. If I reported an irregular pulse, a district could be evacuated. If I said that a door had changed its closing time, engineers would come to inspect the hinges, the frame, the wall, and eventually the mountain behind all three.

People would trust me because I would never say the mountain was angry.

Anger is too simple. Anger has a direction.

I would say only what I could prove.

The wall is warmer.

The knock came twice.

The condensation formed on the inside.

Over the years, I would become compact and heavily jointed, with lungs that disliked sudden elevation and hands permanently stained by iron salts. My birth records would list a pressure tolerance, a water requirement, and the elevation at which I first breathed. I would have a work-name used over the machinery and a private name used by someone who knew how I slept during tremors.

Perhaps I would love someone from the lower districts, someone who worked in water recovery. They would visit by taking the inclined lift up through the mountain, bringing me a repaired filter or a strip of food cartridge with the flavor I preferred. I would check the seal of their mask before letting them inside. They would complain that I always treated affection like an inspection.

I would tell them that affection was an inspection.

I would mean it.

At night, I would climb to the highest inhabited rim. The air there would burn faintly in my throat. The city would spread below me in black terraces and small controlled lights, its bridges burdened with pipes, its factory vents glowing like hidden furnaces. No trees would interrupt the view. No animal calls would rise from the slopes. The whole world would appear stripped to its necessary materials: stone, water, heat, metal, breath.

I would keep a journal, though not of my own life.

I would draw the horizon.

I would measure the eastward peak.

I would mark where new fractures appeared and where old ones closed. I would record the angle of the survey stakes, the color of the mineral blooms, the precise duration of every unexplained sequence of knocks. I would leave room in the margins for later corrections.

The mountain would never be finished in my lifetime. That would comfort me.

Completion would mean that the negotiations were over. The adjustable joints would become permanent. The empty chambers would acquire their final purpose. The city would cease to be a thing becoming itself and become merely a place.

I would not want that.

I would want to remain one of the small, attentive organisms of the unfinished body: not its master, not its prophet, perhaps not even its citizen in the usual sense. I would be a nerve at the edge of a wound. A hand resting against the stone. A person whose entire usefulness consisted in noticing the first wrongness.

And sometimes, during the brief rain, I would see the pale creatures emerge from the drains.

I would not touch them.

I would kneel beside the collector channels and watch their transparent bodies carry the orange furnace-light through themselves. If one came near, I would place a polished piece of glass on the wet stone and wait.

Perhaps it would disappear.

Perhaps it would remain, filmed with salt.

Either way, I would record the event.

Then I would return to my shaft, put my ear to the listening plate, and wait for the next knock from below.
