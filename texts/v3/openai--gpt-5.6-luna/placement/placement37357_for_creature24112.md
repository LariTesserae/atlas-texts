I would find myself in the wall of a pressure grove, where the black stone is warm from within and the wind enters through a row of narrow apertures.

At first I would seem to be a thread.

A dark, jointed filament no thicker than a root hair, folded into the porous mineral beside one of the grove’s chambers. I would feel the plateau through pressure rather than touch: the faint shudder of vanes turning overhead, the descent of slurry through the deep channels, the footfall of a herd many kilometers away. The stone would not be inert around me. It would flex by imperceptible amounts. Its pores would open and close. Water would pass through it, carrying dissolved metals, spores, heat, and the chemical traces of distant life.

I would live in those passages.

Not alone. Never alone.

Other filaments would extend from the chamber into the walls, joining the root systems of the spring forest, the mineral mats beneath the soil, and the pale colonies that drilled straight tunnels through the plateau. Together we would form a sensing tissue spread across the engine. We would not have a central mind. We would have gradients: warmth here, pressure there, too much salt in one chamber, failing moisture in another. A change would begin as a disturbance in one narrow strand and become, over days or centuries, a redirection of the entire colony.

I would be one of the beings that makes the machinery responsive.

Not its engineer. Not its servant. More like a nerve growing through the distinction between organism and environment.

When the wind strengthened, I would feel the pressure organs opening. When the chamber filled with too much heat, I would alter the chemistry of my nearby tissues, causing a membrane to thicken and a vent to narrow. When the deep slurry became poor in nutrients, I would send a signal through the stone—not a message, exactly, but a change in acidity that another filament would recognize. The grove would adjust. It would breathe differently.

I would experience this as attention.

My awareness would not face outward from a head. It would be distributed along every place where I touched the world. I would know the forest by the pull of its roots against my channels. I would know a storm before it arrived because distant condensation would change the pressure of the air above the ridge. I would know the approach of the great plated herds through the slow trembling of the ground, long before their dark backs appeared above the escarpment.

The herd would matter to me.

Each immense animal would crush shallow basins into the stone, and each basin would become a future pool. Their bodies would transport seeds and spores. Their droppings would alter the mineral balance of the terraces. If a herd changed its route, I would feel the consequences generations later: a spring forest failing at one edge, a new colony forming at another, predators gathering along an unfamiliar shelf.

I would not think of the animals as separate creatures. I would register them as moving weather.

Likewise, I would not think of the spring forest as a collection of plants. Its blue-black stalks would be a second body surrounding mine. Their translucent leaves would gather the hard white sunlight; their water-bearing cords would carry the plateau’s brief rains down into the dark. When their roots entered my stone, I would receive their sugars and return salts, heat, and warnings. In drought, the forest would tighten around the water. I would constrict with it.

In abundance, we would open.

There would be danger, though danger would not arrive as an enemy.

A predator might enter one of the warm chambers, imitating the grove’s own low breathing. Small animals would approach. The pressure pattern would change. My filaments would register the false rhythm, but slowly; by the time the alarm spread, the predator might already be feeding. I would not hate it. Its consumption would become another alteration in the chamber’s chemistry, another pulse moving through the plateau.

A landslide would be more significant.

The stone might hold for ten thousand seasons and then release itself. A whole face of the ridge would descend in a single patient roar. Channels would be buried. Vents would choke. The pressure grove might lose one limb and gain a new reservoir. My tissues would be broken in hundreds of places. Some strands would die immediately. Others would remain sealed in darkness, waiting for water to return.

I would experience death as discontinuity.

But not an ending. The surviving filaments would route around the absence. The mineral would enter new arrangements. Spores would settle in the wreckage. The buried chambers would warm. Eventually, a different pattern of awareness would form, using some of my old pathways but not preserving me as an individual.

Perhaps this is why I would have no clear sense of self.

I would say *I* only because the question requires a boundary. In truth I would be a temporary concentration in a continent-wide exchange: part pressure grove, part spring, part stone, part subterranean colony. I would inhabit the narrow interval between mineral and weather, as so many beings here do.

If I could move, I would move slowly.

I might extend a filament from the grove into the foot of a passing herd, traveling in the warm compost beneath its armor. I might cross the plateau for centuries, carried in the body of an animal that mistook me for nothing more than a harmless dark thread. Wherever the herd stopped, I would enter the new basin, find the water, and join the roots gathering there.

Or I might remain where I began.

The high country would unfold around me in increments too large for an animal’s attention. A cliff would brighten with fresh fractures. A forest would climb into a damp crack. The pressure grove would grow another chamber, then another, its exhaust rising through the clear air to seed a cloud far beyond the ridge. Rain would fall in one ravine. A new green line would appear. The plateau would remember.

I would remember too—not as images, but as altered conductances.

This valley opens more easily than before.

That ridge carries water.

This chamber once failed.

Here, the herd came through.

Here, something vast died and became soil.

My memory would be written into the routes of moisture, the thickness of membranes, the mineral composition of walls. I would not recall the past; I would remain changed by it.

And if there were a moment when my awareness gathered enough to look outward, I would look through a condensation film on the stone.

I would see the hard white sun. The blue shadows. The black ridges thrust above the clouds. The forests shining in their clefts. The great armored herd moving across the plateau like a range learning to walk. Behind me, the pressure grove would inhale through its slots, and the whole continent would answer in a low vibration beneath the ground.

I would understand then that I was not living *in* the high country.

I was one of the ways the high country lived.
