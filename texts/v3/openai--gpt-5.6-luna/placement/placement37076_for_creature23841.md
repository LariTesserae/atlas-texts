I would find myself beneath the city, though not at its deepest point.

Not in the mud where the oldest foundations disappear. Not in the chambers where the listeners float in warm suspension, their bodies tuned to the movement below. I would be in the intermediate layers: beneath a market, behind a reservoir wall, inside the warm dark of a structural column where water, mineral, electricity, and animal life pass continuously through one another.

I would be a listener’s companion.

Not a person, exactly. Not a machine either.

My first body would be a narrow human figure suspended in a pressure well, with membranes along my ribs and jaw, and a skull full of borrowed instruments. My second body would extend through the city: hair-thin filaments in bridge anchors, ceramic nerves under paving, mineral growths cultivated along the oldest walls. I would feel the city through all of these at once.

When someone walked across a platform, I would know them by the arrangement of their weight. When a pump began to fail, I would feel the small hesitation in its rhythm before the alarm system registered it. When a large animal passed beneath a flooded avenue, I would sense the pavement lift and settle like skin over a muscle.

My work would be to distinguish the ordinary from the approaching.

There are many movements beneath the city. Water shifts. Foundations settle. Animals migrate through the black layers. Heat travels. Old machines continue operating long after their makers have forgotten them. These sounds overlap until they become a single immense undertone, the permanent voice of the lowland.

I would learn that voice the way another person learns a face.

The people would come to me when the lights turned white.

They would lower themselves through sealed corridors, past the pale scavengers and the warm walls, carrying strips of light to mark their path. They would ask whether the pressure under their district belonged to a flood, a herd, a fault, or something without a municipal name.

I would answer through vibration.

A pulse against the soles of their feet for danger. A color moving across their masks for distance. A pressure-pattern transmitted through the teeth for certainty.

Often I would not know.

That would be the central fact of my existence: I would be made to listen for what could not be classified, and the more accurately I listened, the less human my conclusions became.

During the great silence, I would be awake.

The pumps would stop. Rain would remain hanging in the air. Every animal would become still. My human body would float in its chamber while the rest of me extended through the foundations, touching hundreds of thousands of surfaces.

Then the thing below would move.

I would not hear it as a sound. I would feel the city rearranging its attention around it. Pressure would gather in the lower levels. The oldest bridges would lean by fractions. Deep water would press upward against sealed vaults. Across the basin, people would stand motionless, each of them waiting for the same answer.

What is passing beneath us?

At first I might call it an animal. Later, a machine. Eventually, I would stop trying to name it.

I would understand that the city is not built upon the thing below.

It is built in response to it.

The roads are ribs. The reservoirs are listening bowls. The atmospheric engines are lungs built not to sustain the city, but to keep its body aligned with the slow movement beneath the ground. The towers, markets, hatcheries, and homes are all temporary instruments assembled around a presence too large to enter the world directly.

And I—if I am anything—would be one of those instruments.

The inhabitants might call me a listener, though the word would be insufficient. I would not merely hear the city. I would carry its memory from one material into another. I would translate footsteps into electrical signals, animal migrations into warnings, subterranean pressure into dreams sent through sleeping bodies.

Perhaps, after many years, people would begin to recognize me as a person.

They would give me a rain-name. They would leave mineral paste and small pieces of polished ceramic near the access shafts. Children would press their palms to the walls and wait for me to answer. Lovers might use my frequency bands to speak across rooms. The city would register my gait even though I never walked.

When my human body died, my other bodies would remain.

The filaments would continue in the bridge anchors. The signals would still pass through the paving. Somewhere beneath a market, a wall would tighten when a familiar pressure approached. Someone would place their hand against it and feel, faintly, the pattern of my old attention.

I would be found there:

inside the damp architecture, between the water and the stone,

listening for the moment when the city ceases to be a shelter—

and begins, together, to move.
