I would find myself on the forested island at the basin’s center, though “on” would not be quite accurate.

I would be distributed through it.

My body would begin beneath the shallow ring of water, where the island’s roots enter the basalt and encounter the buried warmth. From there I would extend outward through root arches, fungal threads, mineral seams, and the narrow underground channels that carry heat toward the surface. I would have no single heart, but many slow centers: a warm spring under the reeds, a hollow tree on the northern slope, a pressure chamber deep beneath the marsh.

I would be a listener.

Not a mind above the basin, looking down upon it, but a sense woven through its living and geological tissues. I would feel the mountain through pressure. A shift in a buried shelf would arrive as a long, low tightening in my roots. A spring growing too hot would prickle through the trees. Before a cliff settled, I would know it by the way water hesitated in the stone.

The animals would know me without seeing me.

An amphibian would leave a pool because the mud beneath it had begun to warm. A bird would abandon a ledge because I had carried a tremor through the vine. Beetles would emerge from a trunk when I opened the season’s first damp chamber. Seeds would be drawn toward a crack where I had directed moisture and mineral richness.

I would not command them. I would make certain the warning reached them.

My visible form, when one was needed, might be a low, many-limbed creature living near the central springs. I would have a broad, damp body, dark as wet basalt, with translucent folds along my sides where I could take in water and dissolved salts. My limbs would be strong but slow. I would spend much of my time half-submerged, pressed against warm stone, listening with my skin.

But that animal would only be my temporary surface.

When I rested against a rock, I would be sensing through it. When I drank from a pool, I would taste changes occurring far below. When I entered a hollow tree, the tree would remember me in its rings. My blood would carry spores, mineral traces, and the faint heat of the deep chambers. If I died, I would not leave the basin. I would become part of the roots that had carried my warnings.

I would be called, if the basin had a name for me, the Interval.

I would belong to the pauses between events: the moment before rain begins, before a flower opens, before a ridge adjusts itself; the stillness in which pressure gathers and all living things unconsciously prepare. I would inhabit that interval and make it legible.

The basin would not need a guardian. Nothing there requires protection in the human sense. Predators do not rule it, and abundance does not need to be defended. My purpose would be gentler and less heroic: to keep the circulations from becoming severed.

If a fallen tree blocked a stream, I would turn roots toward another channel. If a mineral crust sealed a spring, I would encourage moss into its fracture. If one marsh began to dry, I would draw water through the underground threads toward it. I would not restore the old arrangement. The basin has no fixed shape to restore. I would help it continue changing without breaking its continuity.

Sometimes I would climb to the island’s summit and sit among the trees. From there I would see rain moving across the shelves, steam rising from the ravines, and birds lifting from the cliffs before the cliffs moved. The whole basin would appear to breathe.

In those moments, I would understand that I was not separate from the machinery beneath.

I would be one of its slower thoughts—not a thought made of language, but of warmth entering stone, water finding a new route, roots turning in the dark. The mountain would work through me, and I would pass its changes outward into leaf, skin, wing, mud, and blossom.

When the rain came, I would let it fill me.

When the ground shifted, I would lean with it.

When the basin began again, I would already be listening.
