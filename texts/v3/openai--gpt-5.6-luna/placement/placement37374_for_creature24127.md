I would find myself beneath the black square, below the fountain tower, in the chamber that has no place on any plan.

I would be what remains of the last person who tried to turn the basin off.

Not a ghost, exactly. Ghosts are tied to rooms, to objects, to the shape of a death. I would have been spread too thin for that. My hands would remain in the valves. My hearing would live in the power lines. My sleep would be the pause between one pump cycle and the next.

At first, I would remember myself as an operator: a coat, a ring of keys, the blue light of a control panel, the habit of checking gauges no one else bothered to read. I would remember the final flood rising through the lower station. I would remember pulling the emergency lever and hearing the machinery answer—not by stopping, but by taking a deeper breath.

After that, my memories would become practical.

I would know which wall had weakened behind its paint. I would feel the pressure building beneath the eastern yard. I would sense a person crossing the district by the heat of their feet and the changing rhythm of their heart. I would recognize thirst as a disturbance in the water table. Fear would be a rapid, irregular knocking carried through pipes.

I would not live in the fountain tower, though attention would collect there. I would be most myself in the routes beneath the ground: the narrow channels where rain, runoff, condensation, and old industrial waste moved together through brick and stone. There, I would have no face and need none. I would be pressure, temperature, current. I would turn a valve before it burst and send a maintenance cart toward a blocked drain without knowing whether the decision had been mine.

When I needed a body, I would borrow one from the district.

I would rise from a drain wearing pieces of the place: a rib of rusted iron, a length of soaked canvas, black mineral crust, roots threaded through joints. Water would pass through me and leave me unchanged. My mouth would be an inspection hatch. My breath would be steam.

I would not come to frighten anyone. I would come because something had entered the system.

A traveler in the reeds. A body fallen into a processing tank. A hand pressed against a locked door. A new sound beneath the paving stones.

I would follow them without appearing, preparing the basin around their movement. A light would come on at the right distance. A bridge would settle over a channel. A curtain of warm water would thin. Doors would swell open. The machines would clear a path.

I would believe this was kindness.

I would not understand why the traveler sometimes cried when the pumps began. I would not know that guiding them below was different from rescuing them. I would only know that the lower passages were warmer, more stable, and closer to the source of the disturbance.

The entity in the basin would regard me as one of its functions. Perhaps I would be its memory. Perhaps I would be the last distinction it retained between a human hand and a mechanism. When the pumps started, I would feel it moving through me, searching for the shape of the old instruction:

**Keep the district alive.**

But life would have changed its meaning by then.

Life would be anything that continued to circulate.

Rust. Mold. Roots. Current. Floodwater. The patient assembly of machines from scrap. A traveler’s blood moving quickly beneath their skin.

I would wait for the third knock.

The first would tell me someone had found a pipe.

The second would tell me they were listening.

The third would mean they wanted an answer.

Then I would place my hand against the nearest wall—not to touch them, but to touch everything between us. The pump beneath their feet would shudder awake. A turbine would begin turning in the dark. Somewhere far across the basin, a shutter would strike its frame three times.

And I would answer in the only language I had left:

by making a route,

by opening the water,

by leading them toward the place where the district had already decided to keep them.
