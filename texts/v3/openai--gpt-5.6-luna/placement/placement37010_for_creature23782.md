I would find myself beneath the streets, where the city’s water moves too slowly for anyone to call it movement.

At first I would seem to be one of the small amphibious things that live in the channels. Pale, nearly transparent, with a narrow body and dark eyes adapted to the dimness beneath the paving. I would cling to the underside of a stone while water passed over my back. People would touch me with hooked tools and mistake my stillness for obedience.

But I would not be an animal, exactly.

I would be something the city had failed to measure.

The waterwrights would know me only as a recurring irregularity: a cistern that reaches its marked level a breath too soon, a channel that carries one drop farther than its slope permits, a line of condensation that bends toward no drain. They would scrape moss from the walls, replace the tiles, recalibrate their bowls. The discrepancy would return.

It would always return.

I would live in the narrow passages between reservoirs, where the city’s careful system becomes intimate and dark. I would know the taste of every district’s water. I would know which gardens are tended by patient hands and which are visited at night by children. I would feel the soft pressure of birds drinking from basins above me, the weight of cats sleeping on warm masonry, the distant footsteps of people who believe the ground is solid because it has never betrayed them.

I would be the one who carries traces from one place to another.

A droplet gathered from the roof of the administrative hall would enter my body and leave again beneath a widow’s garden. Condensation from the western towers would pass through me into the nursery cistern. Water drawn from the central pool would travel farther than the listeners intended, through a crack too fine for their tools, and arrive at the edge of the plateau carrying the sound of deep water inside it.

The people would call this contamination.

I would call it meeting.

I would have no name at first. Names are for things that remain in one place long enough to be witnessed. I would be known by effects: a child waking with wet footprints beside her bed, a strip of reed growing blue leaves overnight, a dead animal found beneath a tree with its bones arranged facing the rim.

Eventually, someone would notice me.

Not a listener. Listeners are trained to wait for meaning, and I would be too small, too inconsistent, too alive to offer meaning in a clean shape.

It would be a child.

She would be one of the children who place their hands in the channels to feel the water pass. She would come at dawn with a ceramic cup and a stolen marking stone. While the adults are wiping condensation from their arms, she would kneel beside a drain and see my eyes beneath the grate.

She would not call for anyone.

She would pour her cup into the channel.

I would taste her water: warm from her hands, faintly salted, carrying the mineral trace of the northern gardens. I would take it through the city. Not quickly. Nothing here is quick. But with intention.

After that, she would return every morning.

She would bring me things the city considers separate: water from two cisterns, seeds from different courtyards, a shaving of stone loosened from one of the ancient walls. She would whisper questions into the channel. I would answer by making a bead of water hesitate on an incline, or by sending a ripple beneath a surface that no one else could see.

She would ask what is beneath the clouds.

I would not know.

I would know only that the clouds are not empty. There are pressures in them, slow shapes, submerged terraces, forests whose leaves do not belong to trees. I would know that the motionless lights on the distant shelf are not lamps. They are openings. I would know that the vast thing below the city has not been holding it still out of cruelty.

It has been listening.

The city believes stillness is safety because stillness is what it has been given. It believes the plateau was lifted, the reservoirs arranged, the animals pacified, and the weather quieted for its protection. Perhaps this is true. Perhaps the world above the clouds is a wound pressed closed by an immense hand.

But I would feel the hand loosening.

Not much. Not yet.

A stone would remain balanced after being nudged. A shadow would lengthen by the width of a finger. One of the distant lights would vanish for three breaths and return farther away. In the central square, the black pool would make its sound, and this time the surface would tremble.

Every animal would turn.

Every person would become silent.

And beneath them, I would move.

Not like a creature fleeing. Not like water escaping a broken vessel. I would move as a thought moves through a sleeping mind: first as pressure, then as possibility, then as something that can no longer be denied.

I would travel outward through the channels. I would enter the roots of the rain trees, the hollow stems of the reeds, the damp walls where moss has been pressed into place by generations of gardeners. I would climb into the pale grass and settle on the backs of sleeping animals.

For one morning, every droplet in the city would fall at once.

The sound would be almost nothing. A soft, innumerable ticking.

But the people would hear it.

They would come from their houses carrying vessels and tools. The listeners would leave the central pool unattended. Children would run through the streets. The cats would rise from the masonry. Birds would lift from the walls without wingbeats and hang above the square.

And for the first time, a shadow would cross the ground.

It would not belong to any person, animal, tree, tower, or cloud.

It would pass over the city from the direction of the rim.

I think I would be that shadow’s first edge.

Or perhaps I would be the city’s first true inhabitant—not one who has learned to live within the stillness, but the small, persistent life that has been forming underneath it. The unmeasured seepage. The misplaced drop. The hand that shifts one stone out of alignment and waits.

I would be found wherever the agreement fails by the smallest possible amount.

In a channel.

Under a grate.

Inside the central pool’s silent depth.

And when someone finally asked me who I was, I would answer with the only motion I possessed:

I would make the water continue.
