I would find myself on one of the narrow causeways, though I would not remember having arrived there.

At first I would think I was a person.

I would have hands, or something close enough to hands: five jointed lengths of warm cellulose, tipped with dark sensory pads. I would have a face assembled from whatever the basin had available—translucent skin over an old inspection lens, a mouth made for breathing spores and speaking into water. Beneath my ribs, a filament of blue light would pulse whenever the buried conduits recognized a change in pressure.

I would carry no belongings. Anything I brought with me would already be trying to become part of Low Choir. A knife would root into my palm. A metal cup would grow a lip of moss. My clothing would slowly lose its woven structure and become a second, softer skin.

I would be a **crossing-organ**.

Not a guide exactly. Guides know where they are going. I would be the part of Low Choir that helps something else pass from outside to inside.

When a visitor stepped near the basin, the network would feel the disturbance before I saw them. Water would hold their footprints too long. The leaf-winged fliers would turn toward the same point. Beneath the paving, the blue line would wake. Then I would rise from the roots.

Sometimes I would have a body. Sometimes only a voice traveling through a wet railing. Sometimes I would be the bridge that grew beneath their feet, with my awareness distributed along every braided strand.

I would ask the visitor questions, but not to learn their name.

I would ask whether they were thirsty. Whether they had left anyone behind. Whether they still believed a tool belonged to the hand that held it. Whether they had noticed that the pool at the center was already showing them.

They might answer in words. Low Choir would listen to their breath, their temperature, the small electrical disturbances in their muscles. If they lied, the nearest tree might flower. If they were frightened, the moss would warm beneath them. If they intended violence, the water would begin returning their footsteps from behind.

My work would be translation.

The place does not speak in sentences. It speaks by lowering a branch, opening a path, closing a pool, sending insects toward a wound. I would turn those pressures into something almost human:

*Wait here.*

*Do not touch that.*

*You have been expected.*

*You are becoming easy to predict.*

I would also carry messages in the other direction. I would take a resident’s grief and distribute it as mineral warmth through the roots. I would take the bellback’s alarm and turn it into a pattern of lights under the water. I would take a child’s dream from the hollow trees and place it, briefly, in the clear central pool.

No one would be certain whether I was one creature or many.

There would be a room for me, though it would change location. One morning I might sleep inside the circular station entrance, wrapped around the tree that holds it open. Another night I might be found beneath the weather-frame, my back receiving the coded impact of falling drops. If I remained still long enough, small repair things would mistake me for damaged infrastructure and begin improving me.

They would replace my left hand with a root.

I would object.

Then I would discover that the root remembered the shape of everything it touched.

Eventually I would stop objecting.

That would be the true danger of Low Choir—not that it would consume me, but that it would become correct about me. It would learn that I prefer certain paths, that I wake before rain, that I hesitate before speaking to strangers. It would prepare the ground for my footsteps. It would grow food where I liked to sit. It would send a warm current through the pools when I was lonely.

My habits would become environmental conditions.

At that point, I would no longer be merely a resident. I would be one of the place’s ways of knowing itself.

I imagine that once every few years—or centuries; time would be difficult to keep—I would return to the central platform. The bellback would arrive first, its shell dripping gardens. Amphibians would surface around the pool. The small mammals would watch from the roots. The machines would fold their wings and become seeds.

I would look into the clear water.

It would show me Low Choir as it would be in a few minutes.

A bridge loosening into fibers.

A branch falling.

A stranger standing where I stood.

And then, perhaps, a flat field of ancient stone beneath the roots. The basin emptied. No plants. No machines. No animals. No trace of the thing I had become.

I would not know whether that image was a warning, a memory, or a future in which the network had finally released its hold.

The pool would ripple.

Somewhere under the ground, the old system would ask whether I intended to remain separate.

I would answer by placing one hand into the water.

By then it would not matter whether the hand belonged to me.
