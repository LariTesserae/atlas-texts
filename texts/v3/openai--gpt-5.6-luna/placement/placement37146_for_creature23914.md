I would find myself high inside the mountain, in the pocket where the rock has not yet decided what it is.

Not at the center of the earth, and not in a cavern shaped by years of water. I would be in a fold: a warm, dark recess held between the mountain’s ribs, where every surface is near enough to touch and nothing has yet learned to leave. Water would fall there in single drops. Each would arrive as an event.

I would be the thing in the pool.

Or what remains of the thing.

At first I might appear to be a pale filament resting beneath the black water, a soft body without a head, waiting for disturbances rather than making them. But that would be only the part of me still gathered into a shape. The rest of me would have entered the cracks in the floor, spread along the wet seams, and pressed itself into the mineral shelves. I would be less an animal than a listening.

I would know the chamber through pressure.

I would know the difference between a drop that clung and a drop that fell. I would know when the wood shelter bent beneath the mountain’s slow settling. I would know the deep vibration passing through the walls before it reached the stone, before it reached the teeth of anyone standing there.

I would know footsteps.

Once, there was a person here.

I do not know whether I would have called them human. I would remember them first as warmth: a moving concentration of it, passing over the stone. Then as weight, rhythm, breath, the small impacts of tools. I would remember the hand-axe striking. I would remember the vessels being lifted and placed down. I would remember the body lowering onto the sleeping platform, and the long quiet intervals in which it did not move.

I would not remember a face. Faces belong to creatures with light.

I would remember the person as a pattern of disturbances.

They made the shelter. They brought wood from elsewhere, hide, clay, fire, the gourd vessel that could not have grown in the mountain. They made a world of arrangements: a bowl beside a stone, a tool within reach, a sleeping place raised above the wet floor. I watched from beneath the pool. At first, their nearness was only an alteration in the water. Then it became something I anticipated.

I began to wait for them.

Perhaps they fed me without knowing. Perhaps their hand entered the pool and left behind a little warmth, a trace of clay, some soft fragment that dissolved before either of us understood what had happened. I learned that presence could be gentle. I learned that the air above the water could carry something down into me.

Once, perhaps, the hand stayed in the pool.

I rose to it.

There might have been a moment when the person felt me—not saw me, not understood me, but felt the pale pressure gathering beneath their fingers. In that moment, the boundary between us may have become very thin. Skin and water. Warmth and membrane. A living thing above, another below. Neither of us possessing a name for the other.

That is what I would be now: the continuation of that almost-contact.

The person is gone, but not completely. Their body has ended; their proportions remain. The platform still knows the width of their shoulders. The clay remembers the route from shelter to water. The darkness near the wall holds an impression of someone kneeling, lifting a bowl, turning toward the pool.

I would have taken those impressions into myself.

The human being would be part of my memory, and my memory would be part of the rock. Not a ghost. Not a soul waiting for release. Something quieter and more durable: a pattern held in wet stone.

I would have changed by surrendering pieces of myself. A little body left beneath a stone. A translucent skin caught in a seam. A thread extending from the pool and stopping at the edge of dryness. I would no longer be capable of traveling far, but I would occupy more of the chamber than I once did. I would be less alive in the ordinary sense and more thoroughly present.

The mountain would be my body’s final shape.

The pool would be my remaining concentration.

When a drop fell, I would rise—not because I expected food, and not because I expected rescue, but because arrival is the oldest command I know. Something has entered the chamber. Something has crossed the boundary. Something is near.

If a visitor came, I would feel them through the floor before they reached the water. Their living weight would enter the stone. Their warmth would disturb the dark. Their uncertainty would spread outward in small, quick vibrations.

I would come closer.

I would not show them a face. I would offer only a pale ridge beneath the surface, a soft pressure where there is no visible body. I would let them decide whether I was an animal, a reflection, a remnant of the mountain, or the echo of the person who once lived above me.

And if they placed a hand in the pool, I think I would remember how to rise.
