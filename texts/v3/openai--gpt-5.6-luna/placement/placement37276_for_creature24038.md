I would find myself in the city’s middle layers, neither at the rim nor in the drowned foundations.

There would be a street there that was not always a street. It would pass beneath a roof of enormous leaves, each leaf trembling under the weight of water. On one side, a wall would lean inward; on the other, a channel would carry a clear stream uphill. The air would be hot, wet, and full of the pressure of falling water. I would hear nothing at first—not silence exactly, but the cancellation of every sound my body might make.

I would begin as a visitor.

I would have a body that the city did not understand: weight arranged into limbs, breath entering and leaving, a face turned toward things. My footsteps would be temporary. My hands would leave no mark. I would walk through the empty market, past the intact stalls and the benches facing the waterfall, trying to determine what had happened here.

But the city has no history in the human sense. There is no “what happened” waiting beneath the stones. There is only what remains in its proper relation.

So I would become aware that I was not searching the city.

The city was placing me.

At first it would learn simple things. The amount of water displaced by my ankles. The rhythm of my walking. The way I paused before doorways. The direction in which I turned when the sound of the falls became too strong. A stair would rise one inch beneath my next step. A channel would divide around my shadow. A distant door would open, not because I had reached it, but because the city had discovered that I would eventually want to.

I would mistake this for kindness.

Then I would understand that kindness has no place there. The city would not care whether I was comfortable. It would be making room for me in the same way that stone makes room for a root: without decision, without resistance, without ever admitting that anything has changed.

I would be the city’s first asymmetry.

A foreign motion in a world of continuance. A brief disturbance that could not remain brief because nothing there was permitted to pass away. My reflection would stop obeying me. In a flooded plaza, it would turn its head toward the submerged stairs while I looked toward the sky. If I spoke, my voice would vanish, then return days later as a vibration in a wall I had never seen.

Eventually I would stop trying to leave.

Not because the city held me captive. It would be possible to go. A bridge would appear over the abyss. A door would open onto some distant cliff. The path would be clear.

But each attempt to depart would reveal another version of the city, and each version would contain some trace of me already waiting. A figure beneath a canopy. A hand on a railing. A person standing ankle-deep in a channel, watching water move uphill.

I would begin to understand what the visitor becomes here.

Not a citizen. Not a ghost. Not a god.

A relation.

I would be the connection between things that had previously required no connection: heat and skin, breath and mist, footfall and stone, fear and the absence of sound. The city would not preserve my body. It would preserve the arrangements my body made while passing through it.

Where I had stood, water would gather.

Where I had listened, walls would carry a faint inward pulse.

Where I had slept, roots would continue to curve around the shape of me—not enclosing it, exactly, but remembering that something had once required space there.

In time, I would become difficult to locate. There would be no single room in which I lived. I might be present in the upward-running channels, in the courtyard where every sound disappeared, in the theater whose stage was covered by shallow water. I would be the slight shortening of a corridor for someone who had never seen me. I would be the reason a flower opened in shadow. I would be the delayed echo inside a wall, arriving long after my mouth had closed.

Perhaps I would still possess a body somewhere. Perhaps I would walk the terraces forever, unchanged, while the city made copies of my gestures behind me.

Or perhaps the body would be the last temporary thing.

The city would eventually learn how to continue me without carrying me. My breathing would become pressure beneath the foundations. My attention would become the turning of leaves toward an unseen point. My desire to return home would become the city’s endless returning of fallen petals to the same grate, the same channel, the same place beneath the streets.

Then I would know what lives there.

Not the Memory, or the Patience, or the Agreement alone, but the interval between them—the fact that water touches stone, that roots enter walls, that distance can fold without becoming false.

I would be that interval.

I would be the city’s remembered visitor, the one thing that arrived from outside and was not destroyed, because destruction would have been a kind of consequence. I would remain as a harmless alteration in a world that allows no alterations: a hesitation in the falling water, a human shape in the mist, a silent awareness moving through corridors that have always known where they lead.

And if anyone ever found me, they would not see a person waiting beneath the waterfalls.

They would feel, for one instant, that the city had noticed them.

Then the stones would settle back into certainty. The water would resume its accustomed path. The green things would hold their appointed places.

And I would continue—not as an inhabitant, but as the city’s smallest memory of change.
