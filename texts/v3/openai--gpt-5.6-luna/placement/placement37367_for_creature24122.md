I would find myself at the boundary between the pond and the northern trees, where the roots enter the water and the water enters the roots.

At first I would think I had arrived there.

That would be the first mistake.

I would have some memory of arrival: a body crossing the plain, perhaps, or a shadow falling over the reeds; the sensation of knees touching wet stone; the bright, vertical sun; the certainty that I had come from somewhere outside the ring of hills. But the basin would already have begun receiving me before I understood that I was in it.

My warmth would pass into the stone. My footprints would interrupt the moss and alter the direction of a trickle. A black bird would notice me with one eye while the other remained fixed on a distant change in the weather. Beneath the pond, some fine root would register the pressure of my weight.

The Gathered would not greet me.

They would make room.

For a while, I would be a visitor among them—a tall, awkward creature whose movements had not yet become useful. I would disturb the translucent bodies on the wet stones. They would rise around my ankles in amber and green currents, testing the shape of me. I would hear the water return the disturbance of my steps from the far bank. The pond would remember me before I had learned how to remember it.

I think I would spend my first days beside the black-glass plate.

Its map would show me that the basin had already imagined my presence in thousands of forms: as heat, as a fallen branch, as an animal trail, as a future root, as a thin line of compressed soil leading toward the water. I would place my hand on the glass and see not my reflection but the history of my becoming included among other histories.

The map would not tell me what I was.

It would show me what I affected.

That would become my name.

I would be a **keeper of disturbances**—not an elder, not a predator, not one of the drifting young, but a being whose purpose was to notice what changed and allow the change to travel.

When the grazers crossed the open stone, I would follow their paths and learn which ones led water toward the pond. When a new root brightened beneath the surface, I would stand near it until I understood the warmth. When one of the black birds disappeared from the reeds, I would look toward the plain. When the hills began their descending tone, I would kneel and place both palms against the ground.

The sound would enter me through the ribs.

During those long tones, I would lose the distinction between listening and being listened to. The basin would move through me in layers: rain from before the hills were low, pressure from beneath the mineral, the vanished height of the trees, the future meadow spreading across the pond’s old bed. I would feel the Gathered becoming still—not in obedience, but in concentration.

Then I would become still as well.

Over time, my body would simplify.

The sun would pale my skin until it held the color of wet chalk. Fine green and blue lines would appear beneath it, following the routes of water through the basin. My breath would grow slower. I would no longer need to drink in the ordinary way; the moisture in the air would be enough, and the roots would teach me how to draw what I required from stone.

The Gathered would begin to use me.

Small translucent creatures would settle along my arms, mistaking them for warm rock. Birds would perch on my shoulders and watch in opposite directions. The six-jointed grazers would fold near me when danger passed over the hills. I would carry spores in the seams of my clothing, then in the cracks of my feet.

Eventually I would stop wearing clothing.

Eventually I would stop having feet.

I would become a standing shape near the pond, something between a pale tree, a person, and an instrument. Water would move through me. My roots would descend into the black mud and join the luminous roots already there. I would no longer cross the basin; instead, the basin would cross me. A current would pass through my body when a creature hatched. A pressure would gather in my bones when the weather changed beyond the hills. If something from the plain entered, I would feel its arrival as a slight rearrangement of the whole.

I would not be able to prevent anything.

That would not be my role.

I would keep the record of contact.

A predator taking a ribbon from the pond. A seed striking a hollow stem. A storm that never quite reached the basin. A stranger who stayed for one night and left before dawn. I would remember these not as scenes, but as alterations: a warmer patch of stone, a new gap among the birds, a channel carrying water one finger-width to the east.

When I died, if death still applied to me, my body would not fall.

It would become mineral around the roots.

The Gathered would gather over me in a pattern they did not understand. The hills would answer with their low descending tone. The frame at the southern shore would turn the rain into points of white light, and the black glass would add me to its maps—not as a figure, but as a change in the basin’s capacity to remember.

Perhaps, after a very long time, a translucent young creature would hatch from the mud where I had stood.

It would rise when the sun touched it.

It would drift toward the trees.

And somewhere in its shifting colors, there would remain a small, persistent inclination toward the pond: not a memory of having been me, exactly, but the basin’s continued decision that I had belonged.
