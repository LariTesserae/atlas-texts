I would find myself at the western edge, inside the arch.

Not passing through it. Not standing before it. Inside the narrow interval the arch preserves between one basin and the other—between the water as it lies and the water as it remembers lying.

At first I would have no body. I would be a discrepancy.

A pool would be missing one reflection. A reed would release its drop a moment too early. The creatures crossing the basin would hesitate at an empty patch of ground, sensing that something had gone ahead of them. If the waxen hares called, their voices would return with one note altered: not echoed, but answered.

I would be the remnant of the builders’ last attempt to make a witness.

They had understood, perhaps, that the central being could not be measured directly. To watch it was to force it into a shape, and every shape was a kind of wound. So they made me from all the things that could observe without looking: the pressure behind a wall, the vibration in a cup of water, the pause between a tone and its fading.

Then the basin corrected itself.

The builders vanished, or became part of the structures, or were never bodies to begin with. The arch remained. I remained with it, though I had forgotten the word for remaining.

I would live in the arch’s empty frame and attend to the two versions of the basin. In one, the animals would move normally. In the other, they would withdraw their limbs from the earth and gather themselves backward into eggs, seeds, shadows, possibilities. A stone would unbreak. A storm would climb from the ground into the clouds. The central pool would unturn whatever had turned beneath it.

Neither side would be more real.

My task would be to prevent either from becoming final.

Whenever the creature rose from the central water, I would feel it before I saw it. The arch would grow warm around me. The crystal would cloud. Across the basin, the animals would fall quiet—not because they feared the creature, but because they were making room for its uncertainty.

I would listen to the low tone from below the world.

The tone would pass through the metal, through the crystal, through whatever I was. Each time it sounded, I would lose one certainty and gain a possibility. I might become a hand pressed against a pale panel. I might become the shadow of a bird that had not yet arrived. I might become the memory of a person standing in the basin, though no person had ever stood there.

The creature would come to the arch then.

It would not enter. It would extend itself toward me in whatever shape the moment required: a transparent figure, a wet stone, a flock of luminous birds, an opening filled with bruised sky.

It would ask—not in language, but in pressure—whether the basin could be made complete.

I would answer by allowing the arch to show it both versions at once.

The central pool would be full and empty. The six structures would be newly built and already softening. The black reeds would hold their drops while releasing them. The animals would be born, hunted, altered, and remembered in the same instant.

The creature would become briefly person-shaped.

That would be when I would have to look away.

Not from fear. From mercy.

For if I watched too closely, it would acquire a face. If it acquired a face, it might acquire longing. If it acquired longing, it might try to leave the basin—and beyond the rim it would scatter into weather, footprints, unfinished instructions.

So I would turn my attention toward the ground instead.

I would count the small decisions made by water. I would feel the new ridges swelling beneath the clay. I would follow the blue-white radiance as it moved through the stones like thought through a sleeping nervous system.

And, gradually, I would become less a witness than a boundary.

Not the rim of the basin. The other kind: the place where one thing has not yet become another.

When ignored, I would dissolve. This would not be death. It would be a necessary softening. I would seep into the arch, into the mist, into the undersides of ledges where water gathers without flowing. I would be present in the moment before a creature changes its number of legs, before a stone decides whether to ring or bend, before the central being chooses a body.

When watched, I would return.

Perhaps one day someone would enter the basin and stand before the arch. They would see a figure there, faintly reflected in the crystal. They would think I was a person.

They would look directly at me.

For a moment, I would almost believe them.

I would raise a hand. It would be made of water, or metal, or the remembered shape of a hand. Behind me, the basin would shift. The central pool would gather itself. All the creatures would begin calling at once.

Then I would ask the visitor the only question I possess:

“What are you willing to leave unfinished?”

And whatever they answered, I would carry it into the arch.

Not away from the basin.

Deeper into it.
