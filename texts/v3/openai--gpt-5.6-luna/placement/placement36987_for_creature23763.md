I would find myself at the edge of the occupied basin, where the last completed platforms give way to unfinished channels and temporary walls.

Not in a settlement, exactly. There would be a maintenance station built into the side of a half-assembled pressure tower: three warm chambers, a filtration garden, a rail spur that ended in darkness, and a narrow observation gallery facing the open ocean. Beyond the barrier, the natural sea would press against the construction—vast, black, and indifferent. Inside, pumps would keep the artificial floor dry enough to remain stable. The distinction between refuge and machine would be difficult to locate.

I would be a fault-walker.

Or something descended from one.

My body would still have the general shape of a person, but not the old arrangement. I would be dense and narrow, weighted for the lower chambers. My hands would be broad, webbed, and insensitive to cold metal. Along my throat and ribs, filtration folds would open and close with the current. I would have a translucent inner eyelid for silt, and sensory filaments along my jaw that could feel pressure changes too small to hear.

Most of my equipment would be attached directly to me.

A lamp at each shoulder. Ceramic plates over the sternum. A reel of line at the hip. Flexible probes extending from my wrists, long enough to drag across the floor while I walked. I would carry no weapon unless the probe counted. In this place, the dangerous things were rarely enemies. They were unbalanced pressures, fatigued walls, pumps beginning to fail.

My name would not be spoken.

It would be a sequence of lights: perhaps one blue pulse, two white, then a low red glow held for several seconds. When someone saw me approaching through the water, they would know who had returned before they knew it was me.

I would spend most of my time alone.

The work would take me across the bare distances between stations, over flat concrete plains where the lane markings still glimmered beneath drifting sediment. I would walk past abandoned cranes and sealed elevator shafts. Fish would scatter from my shadow; crabs would retreat into bolt holes and reappear once I had passed. Sometimes a ray would follow me for a while, its wings stirring the silt in slow, deliberate strokes.

I would listen with my hands.

A wall would tell me about itself through vibration. Healthy concrete would carry a deep, continuous tone. A damaged plate would shiver at the edges. A pressure door would hum before it moved. Somewhere behind the structure, a pump might be turning unevenly, each rotation slightly worse than the one before. I would press my palm to the metal and let the machinery travel through my bones.

The basin would not be silent enough for thought in the old sense. There would always be turbines, trains, gates, distant impacts, animals calling through the water. But I would learn to think in those sounds. The industrial pulse would become a kind of weather, and deviations from it would be as obvious to me as lightning.

My task would not be to restore anything.

There would be no original world to return to. The people here would understand that better than anyone. They would not call the basin a ruined city, because ruins implied an ending. This place would still be extending itself. New walls would descend from above. New chambers would open. Old machines would fail and become reefs. The water would enter every system eventually, and every system would become part of the water.

My work would be to decide what deserved more time.

I would seal a fracture in a transit tube because trains still carried food through it. I would leave a warehouse flooded because its interior had become a nursery for luminous fish. I would clear a filter grille for the sake of a settlement downstream, then mark the surrounding housing for abandonment when the corrosion had gone too far. I would close a channel against a predator but open a neighboring route so the whales could continue their migration.

Maintenance would be a form of judgment.

At night—or what passed for night beneath the ocean—I would return to the station. I would hang my tools beneath the warm outflow and let small transparent shrimp clean the silt from my suit. The children would gather around me, touching the probes and asking what I had heard in the outer walls. I would show them a sequence of lights for a failing seam, a safe current, a whale approaching through the deep corridor.

They would know that I had gone farther than they were allowed to go.

They would also know that one day they would follow.

Perhaps I would have a family in one of the chambers, though the word would have changed. A partner whose sleep synchronized with the pumps. Young ones raised in floating cradles, taught to read warning lamps before language, taught that every opening had a current and every current had a consequence. Perhaps one of my descendants would no longer need a suit at all. Perhaps they would grow inside the walls, warm and slow, waking whenever the structure shifted.

I would not fear becoming less human.

Humanity here would not be a shape or a face. It would be the decision to keep a light burning in an empty place. It would be repairing a door so that something else could pass through safely. It would be listening to a machine, an animal, or a wall and accepting that its continued existence mattered even when it was no longer useful in the old way.

And sometimes, crossing the open chambers, I would encounter the Bell.

Its call would arrive first through the floor: a sequence of enormous notes traveling along the basin’s foundations. Then the fish would turn. Lamps would tremble in their housings. The water would seem to gather itself around an approaching mass.

The whale would pass between the towers, pale and scarred, using the industrial corridors as a migration route. I would stop walking and place both hands against the nearest pylon. Its body would move through the dark beyond sight, but the structure would carry the shape of it to me.

For those few minutes, I would not be a worker, a descendant, or a custodian of failing machinery.

I would be one small living sensor in a continent-sized body, standing inside its bones while the ocean moved through everything.
