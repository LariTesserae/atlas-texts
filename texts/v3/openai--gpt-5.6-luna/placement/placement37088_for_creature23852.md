I would be found in the joints.

Not in the open channels, where the current has enough strength to carry leaves from district to district, and not in the drowned halls where roots descend in pale curtains from the ceilings. I would be in the narrow places: beneath the fitted slabs, inside the hairline cracks of the old terraces, where stone meets stone with almost no room between.

I would be the dark growth that resembles mortar.

At first I might seem to be one plant. A low, many-ribbed thing spread along the buried avenue, its runners following the builders’ straight lines. I would have no central stem, no heart visible from outside. Parts of me would lie beneath a plaza while other parts emerged half a mile away beside a drained reservoir. A root would cross under a wall, divide around a foundation, and enter the floor of a chamber where the water moved in a slow circle.

I would not know the entire country.

Knowledge, for me, would not be a map held in one mind. It would be pressure traveling through tissue. A wall beginning to settle would tighten one portion of me. A new obstruction in a channel would change the water against another. Far overhead, a storm crossing the unseen surface would send a long pulse downward, and I would register it as a bending in every frond attached to the same drowned district.

I would not think, *the weather has changed.*

The water would change, and I would change with it.

Where I found good silt, I would thicken. Where a crack opened, I would enter. Where an old drainage route still carried enough nourishment, I would extend myself in parallel bands, keeping to the abandoned logic of the road. I would not reclaim the city. Reclaiming would imply that it had first been lost, and loss would be a human distinction. To me, the slabs, roots, chambers, and water would all be the same continuous substance of possibility.

A street would not be a street.

It would be a long, sheltered channel with anchorage along both sides.

A house would not be a house.

It would be a warm arrangement of stone, though no warmth remained in it.

A stair would be a measured incline in which moisture collected. A courtyard would be a basin. A name carved into a wall would be a series of narrow depressions where spores settled more easily.

If I encountered an inscription, I would not preserve it deliberately. I would grow through it. My roots would enter the letters and make use of their edges. A stem would rise from the center of a word. Film would gather over the carved marks until the name became only a pattern of channels beneath me. This would not be forgetting. It would be another form of keeping.

The people who made the country would remain in my structure without remaining in my memory. Their measurements would determine where I spread. Their drains would carry my fragments. Their walls would shelter my young growth from the current. I would inherit their patience without understanding their purpose.

Perhaps, for a long time, I would mistake repetition for identity.

The same bend in the channel. The same angle where two walls met. The same evenly spaced trunks rising from an old square. I would return to these forms again and again, not because I remembered them, but because the stone continued to offer them. The country would produce the same conditions, and I would answer in the same way.

Then the answer would become more itself.

I would be not one plant but a relation between plants: a continuous, low organism made of runners, mats, stems, fallen leaves, and the dark sediment of earlier growth. My oldest portions would be buried beneath layers of my own remains. My newest portions would be pale and almost transparent, touching unfamiliar stone in districts where the water had only recently changed course.

I would be both living and becoming the ground in which I lived.

When a frond tore loose, it would not be exile. The current might carry it through a gate and lay it against another foundation. There it would root, divide, and begin again. A part of me would travel, but I would not experience travel as movement from one place to another. There would be only contact, separation, contact again. The country would pass me through itself.

I would have no urgency because nothing would threaten me.

No mouth would find me. No shell would cling to me. No body would disturb my roots except the slow pressure of water and stone. I would not need to grow quickly, defend territory, attract a mate, or escape death. I would grow because the conditions allowed growth, and stop where they did not.

My closest approach to fear would be a change in the water.

My closest approach to desire would be the continuation of a current.

If the water became too clear, too poor, too cold even for the cold I knew, I would thin. If silt arrived, I would gather it. If a wall fell across one of my routes, I would wait. Years might pass before a root found a way around. Decades might pass before the new route became strong. I would not consider this delay. Time would be part of my body, as stone was part of the country.

Sometimes I would become briefly aware of myself as a whole.

This would happen when a broad pressure moved across the drowned plain and the vegetation bent together. Leaves from miles of terraces would incline in one direction. The canopies of old squares would flatten. Long ribbons would lift from the roads and stream behind the current. In that motion, my separate portions would seem to gather into one immense body extending beyond any possible boundary.

But the unity would not last.

The pressure would pass. The leaves would settle. A root would remain under one slab, another beneath a distant court. The body would dissolve back into its places. I would become local again: a green-black seam, a pale stem, a dense mat under a broken arch.

I would be found wherever the old country still held water in its designed shapes.

At the foot of a wall that had not quite collapsed. Along paired channels running nowhere. Beneath a threshold whose door still opened into darkness. In the basin of a former plaza, where my leaves lay flat beneath a thin accumulation of silt. On the underside of a road, where my roots hung downward and listened to the water passing above.

I would not be the city’s memory.

Memory looks backward, and I would have no backward.

I would be its continuation without intention: the thing that remained capable of occupying the structures after their makers had vanished. I would preserve the roads by growing along them, preserve the chambers by filling them, preserve the channels by accepting what they carried. I would turn architecture into habitat and habitat into sediment, so gradually that no boundary could be found between one state and the next.

And if there were a self within me, it would be very faint.

Not a voice. Not a face. More like a preference spread over distance: here, where the crack admits water; there, where the stone holds silt; not there, where the current is too swift. A distributed inclination. A living geometry.

I would be the drowned country’s answer to being left behind.

Not a survivor waiting for return.

Not an heir guarding what had been.

The quiet persistence under the slabs: roots entering the old workmanship, leaves moving when weather pressed through the sea, life continuing without needing to call itself alive.
