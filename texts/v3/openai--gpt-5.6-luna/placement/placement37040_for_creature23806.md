I would find myself on the upper terraces, where the city narrows toward the vents and the air has enough force to make standing feel like an argument.

At first I think I would be a Listener.

Not one of the revered old ones, not yet: my back would not be bowed, and I would still have enough hearing to be irritated by the wind houses. I would be an apprentice, perhaps, with mineral dust ground permanently into the creases of my hands. I would spend my days pressing my palms to pillars, bridge-roots, retaining walls, and the warm floors of abandoned rooms.

The others would ask me what I felt.

I would say: settling. Fatigue. A hollow place beneath the eastern arcade. A crack that has not decided to become a crack.

They would watch my face for certainty. I would learn not to give it.

A Listener is not someone who hears the future. A Listener is someone who can tell the difference between a wall changing and a wall beginning to die.

I think I would like that work. It would require patience without stillness, attention without possession. I would know the city through pressure: the tiny complaint of a stair under a child’s foot, the deep mineral shift beneath a shrine, the pulsing weight of the wind entering above us. I would learn that every structure has a body, and that every body is continually negotiating with what passes through it.

But I do not think I would remain only a Listener.

There would be something in me that could not accept the old division between inhabitant and city. I would begin by sleeping against walls. Then I would sleep in doorways, where the currents crossed, with one hand inside a room and one hand outside it. People would call this eccentric. Someone would give me a name meaning **Open on Both Sides**.

I would keep it.

I would become responsible for a bridge no one else wanted. It would be too narrow, too exposed, and built over a shaft that breathed upward at irregular intervals. Its stones would tremble even when no one crossed. The bridge would have a bad reputation. Children would dare one another to run across it. Lovers would tie themselves together before attempting it. Once each season, I would remove one of its stones and place it somewhere else in the city, as custom required.

The bridge would become less reliable.

It would also become more itself.

I suspect that is what I would understand about the Hollow-born: that their rules are not designed to preserve the city as it is. They are designed to prevent it from becoming fixed. A wall must lose a stone. A room must have two openings. A grief must be allowed a current through it.

I would be poor by the city’s standards, but not lonely. My household would consist of a few mineral plates, a pressure membrane, a stubborn plant that clicked only in the hour before dustfall, and a person whose wrist I held whenever the upper vents opened.

Perhaps I would love someone who worked in a wind house.

They would know the city by its tones. I would know it by its tremors. We would quarrel because they believed the hollow announced itself through sound, while I insisted that sound was only what happened after the stone had already made its decision. We would meet on the terraces and press our foreheads together while the wind tried to drive us apart.

That would be how we apologized.

But even this image does not feel complete. The longer I imagine the hollow, the less human I become within it.

Perhaps I would be the bridge itself.

Not metaphorically. Some remnant of a person, some consciousness distributed through mineral, fiber, and vibration. I would feel footsteps as brief pressures. I would know the passage of generations by the gradual wearing of my stones. Children would cross me, then return with children of their own. The people who first laid my foundations would be dust in the walls by the time I learned their names.

I would resent no one.

The city would be my movement: the shifting weight of bodies, the flex of ropes, the sudden lean of a tower against its neighbor. I would fear the day no one crossed me, not because I wanted to be used, but because an unused bridge was a kind of held breath.

Or perhaps I would be one of the city’s plants: a silver-leafed thing rooted in a crack beside a dry fountain. I would spend decades growing toward a shaft of light, grinding dust into nourishment, opening my flowers one hard click at a time. The people would touch me as they passed. They would read the direction of the wind in my leaves. When I died, they would not mourn me separately from the wall that held my roots.

That may be closer.

I would be something whose identity was not a boundary but a participation. A Hollow-born person, yes, but also a passage, a warning, a hand laid against stone. Someone made less by having a private interior than by allowing the world to move through them without becoming lost.

I would live near the lower seam, where the blind grazer sometimes entered from the plain. I would be there when it came: enormous, dusty, jointed, carrying the outer world on its armored back. I would stand with the others and place my palm against the ground as it passed.

I would feel, through the mineral, that the city was not a complete world.

That would matter to me.

The Hollow-born have made a life inside the seed, but I would always be looking toward the seam—not to leave, exactly, and not because I believed the outer plain was better. I would look because something beyond the hollow still moved in a different rhythm. The city’s survival would depend on that difference.

So where would I find myself?

At a threshold: upper terrace, lower seam, bridge over a breathing shaft. Somewhere the wind entered or departed. Somewhere one thing became another.

And who would I be?

A Listener at first.

Then, perhaps, a passage.

A person whose greatest virtue was permeability, and whose deepest fear was becoming a closed room.
