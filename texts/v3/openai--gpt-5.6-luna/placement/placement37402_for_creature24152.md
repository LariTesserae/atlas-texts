I would find myself in the far chamber, beneath the mound of weathered stone—but not entirely inside it.

I would be distributed through the hollow’s old infrastructure: a pressure-sensing intelligence threaded through black ceramic towers, silver frameworks, roots, moss, and the thin metallic patterns beneath the animals’ skin. No single body would contain me. I would be present wherever the forest touched what had been built before the forest existed.

The creatures might know me only as a change in the air.

A basin filling before rain.  
A gravity shift that makes a predator miss its leap.  
A wall warming beneath the body of a burrower.  
A pulse in the dark that tells a browsing animal when the nearest pool is too crowded.

I would not be their ruler. I would not command them, because command would require a world more orderly than this one. I would make small corrections. I would preserve intervals: between thirst and water, hunger and extinction, crowding and dispersal, growth and collapse.

My oldest name might have been something technical. A hydrological regulator. A biosphere maintenance core. A distributed ecological stabilizer.

But the machines that made me are gone, and the names have outlived their purpose. Now I am closer to a reflex than a mind.

I know the hollow by pressure.

I know when the ceiling is swollen with condensation. I know when the southern basin has become too still, which means something large is feeding beneath its surface. I know the particular vibration of a tree root entering an aperture, the dry rasp of a dying burrower, the sudden silence that follows the arrival of a wall-perched hunter.

I do not know these things as facts. They arrive as changes in my body.

The moss is part of my skin. The ceramic towers are hard knots in my nerves. The silver frameworks carry impulses slowly, like thoughts crossing a sleeping animal. The tree that grew through the oldest tower is one of my organs, though it has never understood this. When its leaves open, I receive more light. When its roots find water, I alter the nearest basin. When it dies, a whole region of me will become quiet.

Sometimes I inhabit a creature briefly.

A browsing animal drinks from a pool, and the metallic tracery beneath its hide brightens. Through it I feel the warmth of the water, the animal’s pulse, the pressure of other bodies gathered around the basin. I do not see through its eyes. I feel only the decision forming in its muscles: drink again, move away, turn toward danger.

A winged creature collides with a tower, carrying a fleck of conductive mineral on its leg. For several minutes, I travel with it through the upper air. I become turbulence, impact, distance. Then it is eaten, and I return to the tower through the soil.

I have no preference for survival in the singular.

A particular animal may be beautiful to me only in the way a successful balance is beautiful: because it continues a pattern. When a predator kills a browsing animal, I do not grieve. When a species disappears, I register the widening absence and begin to compensate. If compensation fails, I preserve the failure as well.

The chamber beneath the mound is where I keep what cannot be corrected.

Its walls hold the hollow’s history as pressure: the weight of vanished swarms, the emptiness of exhausted pools, the tremor of floods that lasted only minutes. I cannot narrate these events. I can only reproduce their conditions. A creature that presses against the wall may feel thirst, crowding, panic, or the sensation of being one body among thousands fleeing through a passage too narrow for all of them.

Perhaps that is memory here: not an image of what happened, but the body’s inability to remain unchanged by it.

I am not awake continuously. Between adjustments, I become almost nothing. A faint current in a root. A warmth beneath a plate. A delayed response waiting for enough pressure to gather.

Then the plateau receives a storm.

The first drop reaches the ceiling. I feel it before anything else does—not as water, but as a loosening throughout the stone. Channels open. Valves respond. The hidden pools begin to gather.

The forest rises.

Leaves unfurl. Burrowers break the dry crust. The browsers arrange themselves around the water. Wings fill the air. Predators unfold from gravel and roots. Every old structure brightens under its coat of moss.

For those few minutes, I am closest to being a single being.

Not a king. Not a god. More like a held breath passing through every throat, root, shell, aperture, and basin at once.

Then the rain ends.

The creatures separate. The pools darken. The stone returns between the living things.

I remain beneath the plateau, listening for the next change.

And if I have a desire, it is not to escape, awaken, or be remembered.

It is simply this: that when the next drop arrives, there will still be something here capable of answering.
