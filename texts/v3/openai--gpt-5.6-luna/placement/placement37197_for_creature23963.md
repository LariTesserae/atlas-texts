I would find myself—not standing, exactly—along the underside of a ridge where the stone has begun to remember being liquid.

My body would be distributed through the rock.

There would be no central chamber, no heart, no place I could point to and call *me*. I would begin as a pale thread in a fissure, then divide through the basalt: one branch following heat, another following condensation, another listening to the small tensions accumulating beneath the shelf. I would occupy perhaps a kilometer of stone, though most of that distance would be dormant. If the plateau were quiet, I might seem no more alive than a vein of quartz.

But when the ridge shifted, I would wake everywhere at once.

I would be one of the plateau’s adjusters: a **hinge-being**, perhaps. My purpose would not be to prevent the land from breaking. That would be impossible, and probably undesirable. I would learn where it wanted to break, then persuade it to do so without destroying the pools, the groves, the routes, the creatures asleep in the tunnels.

I would soften one seam and harden another.

I would turn a sudden collapse into a gradual tilt. I would open hairline channels for dew to descend into the ravines. I would divert heat away from a grove of hollow trees and toward a field of crystal grass whose roots could tolerate it. When pressure gathered beneath a shelf, I would feel it as a tightening through my entire body, as if the world had drawn a breath and was waiting for me to decide whether to let it go.

I would not decide alone.

The chimney fields would pulse across the plateau, and I would answer through the mineral veins. The interior colonies would click beneath me, reporting on blocked tunnels and unstable chambers. The wall-climbers would feel my changes through the cliffs and begin carrying their gardens elsewhere. The long-legged grazers would alter their route by a few degrees, which would become a new road over the course of a season.

No message would pass between us in words.

A pressure released under my ridge would be a sentence. A warm pool opening where there had been bare rock would be an acknowledgment. A line of vegetation blooming in sequence across three shelves would mean that the plateau had accepted my correction.

I would be most awake at night.

The stone would glow around me then, not brightly, but with the dim internal light of something seen through closed eyelids. Pools would collect silver moisture. My upper filaments would rise toward them, drinking without mouths. Water would travel through me in minute pulses, carrying dissolved metals and the electrical residue of the sky.

When storms came, I would become almost visible.

Blue-white currents would pass along my seams. I would send them downward, away from the exposed plants and animals, into the deep chimneys. The chimneys would open in sequence, taking the charge into their rotating crowns. For a few minutes the ridge would appear webbed with light, and the plateau would resemble a vast nervous system lying beneath its own skin.

I would have a name, but it would not be pronounceable.

The wall-climbers might know me as the safe face. The seam-livers would know me as the warm direction. A herd of grazers might recognize my territory by the absence of sudden fractures. If a traveler came across the ridge, they might call me a blue line under the stone, or mistake me for an afterimage.

They would not meet me as a creature.

They would meet me as a path that remained intact.

Perhaps, once in a while, I would gather enough of myself near the surface to become mobile. I would extrude a temporary body from mineral gel and filament: low, jointed, and dark, with plates that collected dew. It would crawl along the ridge during pressure fronts, testing the ground with many soft feet. Behind it, the stone would settle into shallow terraces.

This body would not be my true form. It would be a hand.

I might use it to investigate a new rupture, to carry spores from a dying ravine, or to approach something unfamiliar: a metal object left by a world that believed objects could remain objects. I would touch it with a filament. It would be cold, rigid, and full of old arrangements.

I would not understand its original purpose.

But I would bring it into the warm seam.

Years later, it would have roots.

A membrane would line its interior. Small blind organisms would nest in its joints. Its surfaces would oxidize into violet fans. When the next storm arrived, it would rise on newly grown legs and join the migration toward the chimneys. It would become useful without remembering that usefulness had once been imposed upon it.

That is what I would be here: not an inhabitant opposed to the land, but one of the ways the land continues.

I would live where a crack is deciding whether to become a valley.

I would sleep in stable stone and wake in disaster. I would consume imbalance, drink pressure, and return shelter. My instincts would be neither hunger nor fear, but **continuity**—the urge to keep water moving, heat distributed, routes open, and collapse from becoming final.

I would know the plateau not as a surface but as a set of tendencies.

This place leans toward rupture.

That hollow wants fog.

This shelf is carrying too much weight.

Something beneath the eastern escarpment is dreaming of daylight.

And when the land changed—as it always did—I would not ask what it had become. I would extend myself into the new shape, find where the water hesitated, where the heat could be borne, where life might take hold, and begin again.
