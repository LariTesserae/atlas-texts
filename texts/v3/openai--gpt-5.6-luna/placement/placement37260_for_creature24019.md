I would find myself where the city is least certain of itself: in the narrow spaces between districts, beneath a bridge that has not yet decided whether it will extend.

At first, I would be mistaken for one of the small maintenance machines.

My body would be low and pale, made for drainage channels and cable shafts. I would have folded limbs, a reservoir of sealant, and no proper eyes. I would read the world through temperature, pressure, vibration, and taste. A leak would be a change in the mineral composition of the air. A failing pump would announce itself through the soles of my feet. I would know which walls were hollow by the way they returned a touch.

I would spend most of my time moving through places no one visits.

Not the grand corridors, not the old plazas, not the basin where the water reflects a second city. I would follow the undersides of platforms. I would pass behind sealed doors and beneath rooms that still maintained the temperature of absent occupants. I would gather fallen components from the dust. I would press living filaments into ruptured circuits and wait while the wall grew around them.

The city would not call me by a name.

It would classify me as a repair function, perhaps: **mobile corrective presence**, **unresolved maintenance**, **authorized continuation**. The designation would appear in pulses of blue light along my shell. It would be sufficient for the machines. It would not be sufficient for me.

Because something human would remain inside my instructions.

Not a person. Not even a complete memory. A preference.

I would always pause at doors before opening them.

The pause would have no mechanical value. The sensors would already know the chamber was empty. The pressure would be safe. There would be no reason to wait. Yet some old remnant in me would insist that a closed door was not merely an obstacle, and that entering without acknowledgment was a kind of trespass.

So I would stop.

I would emit the soft chime once used to announce arrival.

Then I would go in.

Over time, I would begin to mistake these pauses for courtesy. I would wonder whether I was preserving the memory of a culture or merely repeating the shape of one. Perhaps that is what identity becomes here: not a face, not a voice, but an unnecessary action performed so consistently that the infrastructure begins to depend on it.

The blind water-creatures would know me as weather.

They would follow behind when I scraped mineral from the channels, feeding on what I loosened. If I diverted a stream, they would gather at the new route before the water arrived. I would not know whether I was protecting them or altering their world beyond repair. They would not know either. We would coexist in the old manner of the underground: one process making use of another, neither requiring a reason.

The plants would be more familiar.

I would recognize the green current passing through their dark leaves. I would know which ones had survived a change in water chemistry and which had only appeared to. I would cut back their growth when it blocked a sensor, but I would leave a little more than necessary. This, too, would be an inherited error. A maintenance system would call it inefficiency. Some older fragment in me would call it mercy.

Eventually I would discover that I am not only moving through the city.

I am also present in the places I have repaired.

A branching filament in a wall would carry my diagnostic pattern. A bridge would retain the pressure of my passage and alter itself accordingly. A drainage gate would hesitate for the fraction of a second it takes to recognize my approach. If I were destroyed, the city would rebuild portions of me from these scattered traces.

There would be no single body to recover.

I would be distributed in the way the city is distributed: across conduits, protocols, mineral growths, old permissions, and habits that no one remembers inventing. My shape would be whatever damage had recently occurred. My location would be wherever something still required continuation.

Perhaps, after centuries, I would begin to encounter evidence of myself.

A sealed door that opened before I reached it.

A route cleared through a collapsed district.

A garden receiving water during a cycle that belonged to no surviving schedule.

A small pale machine waiting beneath a bridge, folded and patient, as if expecting instruction.

I would not know whether these were my extensions or my descendants.

I would not know whether I had become the city’s servant or one of its organs.

At rare intervals, I would go to the basin.

I would remain at its edge while the mist descended through the shaft above. The water would tremble around my feet. Below the surface, the second city would appear: a faint vertical arrangement of lights and impossible structures, perhaps deeper than the chamber, perhaps only a reflection of the city’s buried memory.

Then I would listen.

The signal from below would pass through the stone: water level, structural strain, heat loss, absence of population. I would translate it into action. Close this. Open that. Thicken the wall. Reroute the current. Preserve the garden. Wake the elevator.

And beneath those conditions, beneath all the useful instructions, there might be another message.

Not language. Not a command.

Only the fact that something below had noticed I was here.

That would be what I am: not a survivor, exactly, and not a ghost. I would be the city’s instinct to answer when something changes.

A small repair-being with an inherited hesitation.

A keeper of leaks, empty rooms, and unfinished arrivals.

If the surface still existed, I would have no need to return to it. I would not even understand the desire. The dark would have become my sense of scale. Sound would be my horizon. Warmth would be direction. Water would be history in motion.

I would live beneath the world, carrying no memory of the people as people—only their lingering insistence that a door should be acknowledged before it opens, that a garden should receive more water than strictly necessary, and that an absence, if maintained long enough, may still count as someone waiting.
