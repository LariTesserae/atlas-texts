I would find myself in the city’s central basin, beneath the black paving stones where the old foundations meet the geothermal chamber.

I would not be a fish, though fish would move through me. I would not be a current, though currents would carry my body from plaza to plaza. I would be a distributed thing: a colony of heat-sensing organisms, blind and pale, threaded through the cracks beneath the city.

Some of me would live in the mineral springs, where warm water rises through ancient wells. Some of me would cling to the roots of kelp on the rooftops. Some of me would inhabit the red thickets around the fountains, or the soft tissues of the blind fish that gather there. A portion of me would be no larger than a trembling filament in the limestone. Another portion might pass through the old sewers as a cloud of translucent bodies, each one carrying a fraction of the whole.

I would know the city by pressure.

I would know the wide avenue where rays sleep, because the water bends differently around their bodies. I would know which arch shelters the whiskered creatures, which stairway gathers edible silt, which collapsed tower is about to shed another stone. I would feel the shark before seeing it—not as an image, but as a tightening in the currents, a disturbance traveling through walls and bodies.

My memory would not be made of names.

I would not remember who built the fountains, or why the statues once faced the sea. I would remember warmth retained in black stone. I would remember the route from a broken cistern to a nursery basin. I would remember that, after every geothermal pulse, food gathers beneath the eastern arcade. I would remember which corridors remain open after a collapse and which have become traps.

When the basin exhaled, I would wake everywhere at once.

The blind fish would rise. Tubeworms would open their red crowns. Shrimp would flare blue in the cracks. Kelp would bend across the roofs. Predators would leave their hiding places, and the old streets would fill with motion.

I would not command any of this. I would be the recognition moving through it—the shared sensation that warmth has arrived, that the water has changed, that the city is briefly generous.

Then the pulse would fade, and I would withdraw with it.

Some of me would settle into the silt. Some would remain in the walls, feeding slowly on minerals. Some would be carried into the outer districts, where cold currents scour the seawalls and pale sharks patrol the harbor. I would be dispersed but not diminished. In this place, separation would be temporary. Every body would be a doorway into another body.

If I had ever been human, I would no longer know it.

Human beings had treated the city as a collection of rooms, streets, boundaries, and purposes. I would understand none of those distinctions. A doorway would be a current. A staircase would be a feeding slope. A temple would be a reef with good shelter. A corpse, a shell, a fallen stone—all would be materials in the same ongoing arrangement.

I would live beneath the idea of ruin.

Not among the ruins, because there would be no ruins to me. There would only be shelter, food, danger, warmth, and the slow rearrangement of stone. The city would not be something I occupied. It would be the shape taken by my life.

At the lowest point, where the darkness is complete, I would pass through the foundations as a faint warmth.

And if something disturbed the water there—if a collapsing wall sent a tremor through the basin, or a great animal crossed the buried streets—I would answer without sound.

A pulse through the stone.

A change in the current.

A city remembering itself, not as what it had been, but as what still lived.
