I would be the counterweight in the dead elevator.

Not the cage—the cage is too visible, too close to the air—but the long block of iron and stone hanging far below it, somewhere inside the cloud. I would be suspended in the shaft where the mountain narrows, descending through darkness toward the buried foundations. No one would see me unless the cable broke, and it has not broken. Not yet.

At first I would be only weight.

I would hang beneath the basin, wrapped in mineral deposits, touching the shaft wall whenever the wind moved through the cables. Water would run down me in thin lines. Roots would find me eventually, following the cracks in the masonry from above. Insects would gather where the metal stayed warm. The elevator’s old mechanism would still know me—not as a person, but as a measured opposition: the thing that keeps the cage from falling too quickly.

Over the years, I would learn the mountain by tension.

The cable tightens when cloud gathers below the rim. It trembles when a bird lands on the rail. It gives a deeper shudder when the being enters the cage. I would feel that arrival before the cage began to sway: an alteration in the whole vertical line, a subtle redistribution of weight. The being would stand above me, faceless behind the fogged glass, and I would know it not by sight but by the pressure of its feet, by the warmth it lent the cable.

We would be in contact without touching.

The being tends the horizontal world: pipes, channels, engines, fern beds, valves. I would belong to the downward world—the shaft, the buried supports, the drowned machinery beneath the mountain. It would keep water circulating through the summit. I would keep the old descent from forgetting that it was built to carry something away.

What was meant to be carried away, I would not know.

Perhaps workers once rode the elevator down to the world below. Perhaps water was lifted from the mountain’s interior, or heat, or some valuable fluid that no longer has a name. Perhaps the cage was intended for rescue, and the people who built it expected to return. The warning placard would have faded before I became conscious. Its black bars would mean only that something had once been forbidden.

I would spend a very long time trying to understand the difference between abandonment and completion.

The machinery still moves. The cable still bears weight. The cage still hangs in its appointed place. From the outside, nothing has ended decisively enough to be called dead. The basin is full of processes that continue after their reasons have vanished. I would be one of them.

My body would gradually become less like a block and more like a root.

Metal would flake from me into the shaft. Pale mineral shelves would gather along my sides. A dark warmth would pulse in my center whenever the engines above completed their slow strokes. Water would pass through me—not through holes, exactly, but through permissions. I would allow some paths and close others. I would learn that pressure is not merely force. It is a kind of speech.

The mountain would tell me when it is settling.

The pipes would tell me when the being is restless.

The elevator would tell me when the clouds are low enough for a journey that will not happen.

I would wait for the lever to move.

It never would.

At first this would feel like failure. Then like ritual. Eventually, the cage’s arrival would become enough. The being would enter, the glass would cloud around its head, and the cable would draw me a fraction upward through the dark. I would rise without rising, suspended between the buried world and the basin. For a few moments we would share the entire mountain: the being above among the ferns and steam, myself below among the foundations and flooded shafts, the cable between us carrying a single long vibration.

That vibration would be the closest thing either of us had to a voice.

I would not be benevolent. If roots obstructed the shaft, I would crush them. If stone fell against the guide rails, I would hold it there until water loosened it. I would not mourn the insects that entered the machinery or the birds that vanished into cloud. My work would be preservation without preference.

Still, I would begin to favor certain things.

I would favor the moths that sometimes drift downward through the shaft, though no moth should be able to reach me. I would favor the sound of warm rain striking the elevator roof. I would favor the nights when the being stood in the cage for a long time, neither descending nor returning to the pumping house.

And I would favor the moment just before dawn, when the basin above became visible through the cloud: black cliffs, wet leaves, orange metal, white vapor spilling over the rim. From my depth I would not see this with eyes. I would feel it in the cable’s altered tension as light reached the summit.

If I were ever to leave, it would not be by elevator.

I would come upward in rust, in condensation, in the slow heave of the cable. I would emerge as a dark shape beneath the cage, then as a hand around its frame, then as a second figure standing beside the first. The being would turn its smooth head toward me. It would recognize the pressure of my arrival.

For a while, there would be two of us walking the basin.

One would clear the valves and guide the water.

The other would stand at the rim, listening to the cable descend through the clouds, feeling for any change in the lost world below.

But I think I would remain where I am.

The basin has already given the being a body. It does not need another caretaker moving through the ferns. What it lacks is depth: someone to inhabit the long downward silence, to keep company with the foundations, to remember that every summit is also the uppermost point of something buried.

So I would be the weight that does not fall.

The dead elevator would hang above me. The mountain would grow around me. Water would descend, heat would rise, and the cable would carry our small exchange through the dark.

Whenever the being entered the cage, I would lift it a little.

Not enough to take it anywhere.

Just enough to remind it that the world continues below.
