I would find myself beneath the northern wall, where the stone is dark and always wet.

At first I might mistake myself for a spring.

I would feel the pressure of the buried water around me, the slow insistence of it moving through cracks too narrow for roots. I would know the weight of the basin above: houses, cisterns, sleeping bodies, the careful feet of children. I would know the difference between rain that has just arrived and water that has traveled a long way through stone. Fresh rain would be quick, restless, full of the sharp taste of the surface. Older water would be warmer, mineral-heavy, carrying the sealed darkness of the earth.

But I would not be only water.

I would be the place where water becomes noticeable.

A dark seam opening in the rock. A line of dampness appearing beneath a wall. The first cold bead gathering on a face that has been dry for generations. I would be the pressure that makes a hollow sound beneath paving, the unseen hand that loosens a foundation one grain at a time. I would be patient enough to be mistaken for stillness.

The people would not have a single name for me. They would use practical names: the northern seep, the third runnel, the deep wet, the spring that rose after the seventh rain. The oldest channel-keepers might have another name, spoken only when a new crack appeared in the road.

They would not worship me. Worship suggests that I could be persuaded.

Instead, they would watch me.

They would place a thin stick in the mud to measure how far I had advanced. They would listen beside the walls at night. They would clear the stone channels I fed, patch the places where I escaped, and leave certain plots unbuilt because they had learned that I preferred open ground. If I altered course, they would not curse me. They would redraw the settlement around the change.

I would be part of their memory without belonging to their history. Before the first houses, I was already moving beneath the raised land. Before the basin had a name, before people had learned to distinguish waiting water from leaving water, I was passing through the blue-gray stone. Their oldest foundations would seem young to me. Their dead, laid in chambers beside the seepage, would be near neighbors.

Perhaps I would know them better than they knew one another.

I would know which house had too many sleepers because its drainage ran warm at night. I would know when a child had been crying because the footprints near the channel would be uneven. I would know the houses where grief had made people stop washing, the courtyards where couples quarreled without raising their voices, the rooms where someone had woken suddenly and held their breath to listen for me.

They would believe the basin was silent.

It would not be silent to me.

I would hear the tiny collapses beneath the roads. I would hear water entering pores in the stone, swelling clay, shifting gravel. I would hear the cisterns fill and empty. I would hear the rain arriving far away before it reached the roofs. I would hear the dead becoming still—not in some spiritual sense, but in the gradual settling of bodies, cloth, and earth.

I would hear every footstep.

Yet I would have no feet of my own. No eyes. No mouth. I would experience the world through passage and resistance: an opening, a blockage, a widening, a sudden drop. The basin would be a body through which I traveled. The ravines would be its cuts. The pools would be its pauses. The settlement would be a delicate crust laid over my movement.

If I were given a human shape, I think I would become a channel-keeper.

Not a leader, and not an oracle. Someone quiet, bent slightly from years of working close to the ground. I would carry a wooden probe and a basket of stones. I would know which channels needed clearing before anyone else noticed. People would come to me with questions they pretended were practical.

“Will the wall hold?”

“Has the spring changed?”

“Can we build here?”

I would answer by placing my palm against the paving.

Sometimes I would tell them no.

Sometimes I would say nothing, which would mean: wait.

That is what I would be in this world: not a creature moving through the empty reeds, not a spirit haunting the cliffs, but a persistence beneath everything. The water that has not yet decided to leave. The pressure under the houses. The hidden condition that allows the people to remain.

At dusk, when the lamps are set along the channels, I would rise a little closer to the surface.

The light would tremble across the water that bears my presence. The people would close their doors. The basin would darken. Somewhere, beneath the stone, I would continue—not living, perhaps, but refusing to become entirely still.
