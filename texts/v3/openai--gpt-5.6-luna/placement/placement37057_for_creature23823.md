At first, I think I am beneath the plateau.

Not buried—there is no sense of burial here—but held. The soil is soft around me, black with water, and beneath it the roots pass in every direction. They are not separate roots anymore. They are roads, veins, old walls, the fingers of trees that met one another so long ago that their joining has become indistinguishable from geology.

I feel the water moving through them.

Some of it is cold, drawn from stone. Some is warm from the deep mineral places. Some has entered through leaves only moments ago and is still carrying the faint taste of sky. I can tell where each thread is going, though not by sight. The plateau speaks in pressures. A blocked channel tightens beneath my palm. A dying shelf of moss grows lighter against my weight. Far away, a pool receives too much rain and begins to spill toward a hollow where nothing has drunk for years.

I am not beneath the plateau.

I am standing in it.

My body is narrow in some places and immense in others. One leg is planted beside a basin of reed and floating leaves. Another limb has entered the ground and emerged beyond the roots of a fallen tree. I do not remember growing this way. There is no moment of becoming. There is only the long recognition that the thing I took for distance is part of me.

Moss lies across my shoulders. A pale flower has opened in the hollow of one elbow. Something shelled and deliberate is moving along my wrist, and I allow it to pass. Its small feet make a pattern I can feel through the ridges of my skin.

The forest is not around me. It is on me, through me, beneath me.

I am one of the Tendermen, though that answer is too narrow. I am a Tenderman at the visible edge of myself: gray-green skin, jointed limbs, hands pressed into wet ground. But much of me is elsewhere. A strand of my attention runs through a root-bridge two valleys away. Another rests in the wall of an old chamber, where flowering plants have filled the doorway and turned the stone warm. I can feel a young tree leaning incorrectly toward a marsh. I will touch it once this season. Perhaps my descendants will touch it again.

Nothing here is made quickly enough to belong to a single life.

I remember—in the way a pool remembers a fallen branch—that there were once people who built in straight lines. They raised walls and marked boundaries. They cut entrances into things and called the openings theirs. Their traces remain, but only as suggestions. A stair enters the earth and comes out as roots. A terrace has become a wet garden. A fitted stone rests inside the wood of a tree like a seed that never learned to sprout.

I do not know whether I was ever one of those people.

Sometimes a reflection in a dark pool shows me with a face that does not belong to my present body. Sometimes leaves turn toward a sound that happened hours ago, and I feel an old name moving through them. But these impressions have no sharp edges. They are not memories as a human would hold them. They are shapes retained by growth: the shadow of a roof kept in the plants beneath it, the pressure of a hand preserved in the curve of a living wall.

Perhaps I am what remains when a person stays long enough.

Perhaps I was born in a warm root cavity, as the pale young are born, and the old human shape is only something the plateau once imagined through me.

The distinction does not matter for long.

A channel is closing beneath the marsh. I feel the obstruction: a mass of drowned leaves, a stone, and the slow grip of roots that have forgotten water must pass. I lower one hand. The mud receives it. The pressure travels outward, not as command but as invitation.

The roots loosen.

Water begins to move.

It travels uphill first, along the living grain of a buried trunk, then descends through the reeds in a dozen silver threads. The floating leaves separate. A small creature beneath them wakes and turns. Somewhere beyond sight, a low-backed browser steps into the replenished basin, its moss-covered back opening with tiny flowers.

This is what I am for.

Not guarding the forest. Not ruling it. I have no boundary to defend, because separation is the one true danger here. I gather what has been divided. I keep the old bridges from becoming merely dead wood. I carry seeds across weather-zones. I listen to the places where the land is losing its shape and lend them my shape for a while.

When the plateau speaks to me, it does not use words.

It sends a tremor through the mud.

A warning: a stand of tall growth is leaning too far.

A greeting: a pool remains full.

A summons: one of the old ones has fallen.

I go slowly. By the time I arrive, flowers may already be growing in the hollows of the body. Roots will have entered the joints. Small animals will have begun to make use of the shelter. I will place my hands against the remains and listen for the last pattern of tension.

Then I will redirect the water.

For many years, perhaps centuries, I will return to that place. Eventually I will stop. Not because I have forgotten, but because the memory will no longer require my presence. The body will become a bridge. The bridge will become a ridge. A path will form beside it, and creatures will follow that path without knowing they are walking beside someone.

So where would I find myself?

On the high plateau, certainly—but not as a traveler standing beneath its canopy.

I would find myself wherever water is being prevented from reaching water.

I would be the Tenderman with one foot in the marsh and one foot in the roots; the slow pressure that persuades a tree, a wall, a path, or a dying body to remain connected a little longer.

And beneath my hands, the land would be answering.
