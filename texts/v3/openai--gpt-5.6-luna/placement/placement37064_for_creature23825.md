I would find myself in the lower districts, where the gutters are deep enough to walk through and the water remembers places that are not presently there.

At first I would think I was a walker.

I would wear pale insulation that sealed itself at the wrists and throat. My boots would change width beneath me as the floors changed. I would know, without being told, which doors were doors and which were only the idea of doors left behind by some earlier arrangement of the works. At every junction I would pause—not from fear, exactly, but because the Stillworks would be listening for what I intended to do next.

I would carry a length of red thread.

Not to find my way back. Nothing in the Stillworks allows a route to remain a route for long. The thread would be for proving that I had passed through at all. I would unwind it behind me through service tunnels, across drainage channels, beneath the bellies of slow machines. When I returned, the thread would seldom be where I had left it. It might be stretched vertically through a shaft, wound around the antlers of a waiting animal, or stitched into the seam of a wall.

I would accept this as an answer.

My work would be to accompany things before the country assigned them a function.

A tool left on a step, a bird asleep on a belt, a person waiting too long beside a sealed gate: these would be my concerns. I would not repair what was broken. Repair implied returning something to a previous condition, and the Stillworks had no previous conditions, only earlier designations.

Instead, I would keep things in motion.

I would tap the side of a pressure gauge until its thoughts loosened. I would lead a flock away from a conveyor just before the belt remembered how to move. I would wake a pale mammal by touching the floor beside its head, never the animal itself. I would stand with a child—if children existed here—at the mouth of a corridor until the child understood that the corridor was waiting to see whether they would enter as a traveler or as material.

I would carry salt for the walls.

Copper for the machinery.

Warm stones for the hollow at the center.

And sometimes, though I would not know why, a tooth.

My face would be difficult to remember. This would be useful. A ceramic mask, perhaps, clouded enough to reflect the white light without returning it. Or no face at all: only a dark opening beneath my hood where the Stillworks could not easily determine whether I was looking, listening, or becoming.

There would be other walkers who recognized me by my habits. I touched rails before crossing them. I counted the pauses between distant impacts. I never looked directly into the black reservoir, but I would watch its reflections from the corner of my eye.

The thing beneath the water would know me anyway.

Sometimes it would rise.

Its smooth back would break the surface without making a wave. Windows would brighten along its sides: a room full of snow, a platform under red lamps, an unfamiliar kitchen where a kettle had just begun to boil. Once I would see myself in one of those windows, standing in a place above the basin, holding no thread and wearing no mask.

I would understand then that I was not entirely a walker.

I had been made from one, perhaps. Or from several. A route given a body. A maintenance instruction that had continued after its authors disappeared. The Stillworks might have built me to escort materials between departments, then forgotten the materials, then forgotten the departments, leaving only the motion.

This would explain why I could not remain still.

It would also explain why I sometimes woke in places I had never traveled to, with fresh seams in my sleeves and a new indicator light blinking beneath my ribs.

The danger would not be pain. The danger would be usefulness.

If I stood too long beside a valve, the valve would begin to turn in time with my breathing. If I slept against a wall, the wall would make room for the shape of my back. If I allowed someone to call me by the same name too often, the name might become a label, and the label a function.

So I would move carefully, but never hurriedly.

I would pass through the country at the speed of an intention becoming real.

In the upper districts, I would walk beside the long-legged birds. Their shadows would click over the concrete. They would tell me where new construction had appeared during the night: a bright stair descending into a tower, a row of windows opening onto a weather that had not yet reached the basin.

In the service levels, I would travel with the pale mammals. They would flatten themselves beneath the pipes, and I would lie beside them until the machinery passed. Their transparent eyelids would close over eyes that remained awake. They would teach me signals I had never heard before.

At the center ring, I would approach the hollow only during the mist season.

The opposite side would shimmer. Sometimes I would see brown grass beyond it, bending in a natural wind. Sometimes I would see the Stillworks from above, its basin reduced to pale geometry and black water. Sometimes I would see a country with no structures at all.

I would leave the warm stone at the edge.

I would leave the tooth beside it.

Then I would wait—not motionless, never motionless, but circling slowly, keeping one heel from touching the ground for too long. I would listen to the hidden machinery complete another fraction of its work.

Perhaps the country would answer by opening a door.

Perhaps the door would contain a person who had been waiting inside it for centuries.

Perhaps it would contain only a room, and in the room a lamp, and beneath the lamp a length of red thread leading onward.

In the end, I would not be the keeper of the Stillworks. Nothing here could be kept. I would be its temporary resistance: the small, deliberate movement that prevents a thing from settling into its final use.

A person before they become a hinge.

A pool before it becomes a shaft.

A memory before it becomes a light.

I would walk beneath the hard blue brightness, carrying salt, copper, and thread. Behind me, the country would alter itself. Ahead, new stairs would rise from the manufactured ground.

And if anyone asked what I was, I would answer carefully.

“Again,” I would say.
