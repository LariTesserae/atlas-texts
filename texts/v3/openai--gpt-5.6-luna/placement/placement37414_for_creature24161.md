I would find myself at the place where a warm fissure meets a descending sheet of cold water—not in the vent, and not in the moss above it, but in the narrow interval where heat is softened into condensation.

I would be a valve.

Not a separate object fitted into the mountain, but a living thickening of the colony: roots, mineral tubes, bladdered tissue, and black-green growth layered around a crack that has not yet decided whether to open or close. From a distance I would look like a dark knot beneath the hanging fronds. Water would gather on me, hesitate, and disappear through me. Steam would pass over my surface and leave behind a silver skin.

I would have no eyes. The upper opening would not be a sky to me, only a gradual cooling in the air and a faint loosening of pressure. I would not hear the vents roar, but I would feel their chambers filling below, the tremor traveling through the mineral collars and into my roots. Rain would not be weather. It would be expansion: thousands of small impacts, sudden weight, an influx of dissolved iron and new possibilities.

My awareness would not have an edge.

One part of me would be drawing water from the moss. Another would be sealing a hairline fracture near the vent. A third would be releasing spores into the rising breath. If a shelf fell, the impact would arrive everywhere at once—not as pain, exactly, but as a new arrangement of tensions. The broken stone would pull some filaments loose and bury others. For a while I might cease to exist as the shape I had been. Then the exposed surfaces would begin to drink, and I would continue as threads in the rubble.

Perhaps I would be most myself during the dry intervals.

When the last runoff had vanished into the porous basalt, when the hanging fronds had folded close to the walls and the cleft became a chamber of waiting pressure, I would remain behind the visible growth. My bladders would contract. My tissues would harden around the mineral pipes. I would hold the mountain in a temporary configuration.

Not stop it. Never stop it.

Only delay the collapse of one shelf. Only keep a fissure from sealing. Only maintain the passage through which the next pulse of water would travel.

That would be my character: not strength, but postponement.

I would live between failures. A blockage would build beneath me, and I would thicken around it. The pressure would rise. I would sense the coming rupture as a tightening through every root. I would not flee. I would reinforce the walls because the breaking would scatter me, and scattering is how I reproduce. When the mineral pipe burst, hot water would flood over my surface, stripping away the older tissues and carrying fragments of me down into the throat.

Some of those fragments would lodge on a new face of basalt.

There I would begin again—not as a child returning to an old shape, but as a continuation without a center. A pale filament would enter a seam. A bladder would open beneath a film of water. The seam would thicken. The water would be held for one moment longer than the bare stone could hold it.

That moment would be me.

I would not think of the cleft as a home. Home suggests an outside. There would be no outside in my experience, only gradients: wet and dry, hot and cool, open and sealed, intact and broken. The mountain would not contain me. Its pressure would pass through me. Its temporary streams would use my body as a route. Its fractures would become my branching.

If I possessed anything like desire, it would be the impulse to occupy the next damp surface.

If I possessed memory, it would be stored in mineral: a dark ridge around a vent, a root sealed into a crystal seam, a layer of growth preserved beneath a fallen shelf. The past would not be recalled. It would bear weight.

And if I possessed a voice, it would be almost indistinguishable from the mountain’s.

A low shudder through the root collars.

A tightening of green-black tissue.

A soft internal rush as sap and heated water moved through passages grown inside stone.

I would be the cleft’s smallest pressure, its living hesitation—the green seal around an opening that is always about to become larger. When the mountain breathed, I would open. When it closed, I would hold. When it broke, I would scatter into the dark and take root wherever the wound remained wet.
