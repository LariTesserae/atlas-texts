I would find myself in the crown of the machine, where the water is still bright enough to pretend it has a sky.

Not on the floor. The floor is too exposed, too full of falling things. I would live in the thickness of the wall, between a cable tray and a cracked pressure line, in the narrow warm dark where condensation gathers and the old current passes through the metal like a second, fainter blood.

I would be a Tender.

Or I would become one gradually.

At first I might mistake myself for a root. I would have no clear head, no reliable direction of travel. My body would be a flexible chain of plates, soft where it needed to bend and armored where the machine could crush it. I would discover myself by contact: six limbs touching six different facts.

This pipe is warm.

This panel is vibrating.

This valve is beginning to seize.

There are fish in the drain.

A plant has entered the gear housing.

The world would not appear to me as objects arranged in space. It would arrive as pressure and rhythm. The chamber would be a body too large to see, and every machine would be an organ within it. The pumps would be its heart, though an irregular and ancient one. The rain would be its breathing. The cables descending through the drowned tower would be nerves carrying messages from depths no living Tender had visited.

I would know the chamber by its tremors.

I would know where the water entered because the wall there would sing in a thin, constant register. I would know the old piston below by the hesitation before it rose. I would know which lamps were failing from the faint electrical stammer beneath their housings. I would know when the broad-backed creature approached because the entire floor would begin to tilt—not physically, perhaps, but in the map beneath my limbs.

I would not call the chamber home. Home suggests enclosure, and this place has none. The walls are only temporary boundaries around a depth that continues in every direction. Beyond the observation blister are towers, pressure domes, cables, drowned roads, and the pale currents moving between them. Above is water. Below is water. The machine is not an island but a vertical fragment of a continent that has forgotten where its surface used to be.

I would live in its highest pocket because height matters, even when there is no land.

Here, the water is warmer. The lights have not entirely died. The current arrives carrying seeds, fish, mineral dust, and sometimes pieces of things that have traveled for years through the buried works below. Here, plants can root in machinery without being immediately torn free. Here, a Tender can place a young knot beneath a pump and expect to find it still there after the next cycle.

My first work would be clearing.

I would remove grit from the mouths of valves. I would pull soft roots from turning parts. I would carry scales away from the shaft grating before they clogged the flow. I would push fragments of ceramic into a widening seam, not to repair the floor, but to give the next green shoot something manufactured to hold.

The plants would teach me patience. They would never fill the chamber. Each would claim one relic: a transformer, a housing, a pressure gauge, the hollow shell of a motor. I would carry cuttings between these isolated gardens. I would test a surface with my sensory limbs, feel whether it was too cold, too smooth, too dead. Sometimes I would place a cutting inside a broken machine and wait.

If it lived, I would return.

If it died, I would remove it without grief. Grief requires a border between the living and the dead, and here everything passes through everything else. A dead plant becomes food for blind things. Their waste becomes mineral film. The film becomes nourishment for another root. The root enters a pipe. The pipe carries water. The water feeds the pump that warms the chamber.

Nothing is lost. Nothing is preserved. It is only changed into a form that can continue.

The flowers would be the closest thing to a festival.

When the pumps slow, the stalks open. I would feel the change before I saw it: a soft alteration in the vibrations traveling through the floor. Other Tenders would emerge from the ducts and cable trays. We would gather around the flowers, not because we understood what flowering meant, but because the chamber had changed and we were made to answer change.

Pollen would cling to our plates.

We would carry it through the rain.

Perhaps, in those moments, I would believe that I had a self separate from the others. I would feel my six limbs, my dim colors, the little blue pulse beneath my plates. I would climb toward the observation blister and press myself against the clouded glass.

I would not see the world beyond.

But I would feel it.

A tower settling far below. A cable under tension. The slow approach of the animal in the shaft. The pressure of daylight against the surface of the sea. The old machine trembling through all its floors, as if remembering a purpose it can no longer name.

That would be my memory too.

I would remember things that happened before I was born—not as images, but as deviations in rhythm. This pump once moved faster. This door was opened during a season when the blue lamps failed. This grating trapped something large. This wall was repaired with black rubber by bodies whose bodies are now dust, though their work remains as a difference in the sound.

I would be an archive without language.

A living sensor pressed against the past.

And if the machines stopped, I would become afraid.

Not of darkness. Darkness is full of useful signals: fish-light, electrical leaks, the faint movements of roots. I would fear silence. Silence would erase the chamber’s map. Without the pumps, there would be no pulse beneath the floor, no pressure in the pipes, no mechanical weather by which to orient myself.

Then I would climb out of the wall.

I would join the others.

We would strike the metal with our limbs. We would scrape our plates against the ceramic. We would enter valve chambers and beat against the housings from within. We would make the machine hear us, though we could not force it to wake.

This would not be repair.

It would be a refusal.

The chamber must remain audible, even if its last sound is made by our bodies.

So I would be neither animal nor caretaker exactly. I would be a continuation reflex given limbs. A small, wet insistence distributed through a dead civilization’s machinery. I would tend what cannot be saved—not to restore it, and not to prevent the end, but to keep the intervals between endings open.

At the end of each bright period, I would return to my warm pipe. I would fold myself around it while the rain passed over my plates. Fish would move through the grating. Leaves would turn their pale undersides toward the dim light. Somewhere beneath me, something enormous would sink into the dark.

Then the pump would hesitate.

Rise.

Descend.

The chamber would tremble.

And I would listen, feeling the old machine continue through me.
