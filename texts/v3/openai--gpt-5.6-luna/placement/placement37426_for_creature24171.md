I would find myself beneath the western pump house, where the tram line passes through the stone.

Not inside the tunnel—the tunnel is not there, or not there in any way a body could enter—but in the narrow service chamber beside it, behind a door numbered 6 whose paint has long ago softened into the color of the wall. The chamber would be almost empty: a shallow basin, a coil of dark cable, a stool made from a cut-down valve housing, and one pipe running through the ceiling.

That pipe would be warm.

I would sleep beside it, though sleep would not be a complete departure. The pipe would keep one side of my body awake. Its warmth would alter when the deeper machinery shifted. A faint pulse in the metal would tell me that the western pumps were still turning. A long interval of nothing would mean that water had found an older passage.

I would be one of the Dwellers, but not one of those whose work is easily described as tending a machine. I would be a listener of misalignments.

The district would speak most clearly when it nearly failed.

I would know the difference between an ordinary drip and one that had begun to fall at the wrong angle. I would feel a blocked channel through the soles of my feet before the water rose. I would put my ear against the concrete and count the movements beneath it: one for the pump, two for the fish settling in the tanks, three for the broad and patient shift of the Underkeeper.

My hands would be long, dark at the fingertips from mineral dust. I would wear a vest of woven root fiber and strips of insulation, not for warmth but so that every movement made a different small sound. The others would recognize me by these sounds. A brush of wire against my shoulder. Two taps from the metal clasp at my throat. The soft drag of my left foot, whose sole had been repaired too many times.

I would live alone, perhaps—not because I disliked the others, but because my rhythm had become too slow for a family. The pump beneath the western house turned only once every nine minutes. After many years, my breathing had begun to follow it. Children would sometimes come to sit outside my door and imitate the delay between its strokes. I would pretend not to notice.

My task would be to walk the district during the long pauses.

When all the pumps stopped, the Dwellers would gather beside the channels and become still. But I would go farther. I would carry a lamp with cloudy glass, a scraper, three lengths of treated wire, and a cup of mineral water. I would pass through the yards while the animals flattened themselves beneath the machinery. I would inspect the old signal tower, the empty doors, the places where the water had risen by a finger’s width.

The district would seem larger then.

Without the pumps, the dark would no longer be divided by their patient breathing. The hollow would become a single interior space, and every wall would feel close enough to be listening. I would hear the click of insects stopping in midair. I would feel the blind fish holding themselves motionless in the tanks. Somewhere beneath the grating, the Underkeeper would draw its enormous breath.

I would kneel at the deepest grate.

There I would lower the cup into the black water and wait. Sometimes nothing would happen. Sometimes the water would cloud around the cup. Once, long ago, something below had touched the metal—not struck it, only rested against it—and the vibration traveled through my wrist, up my arm, into my teeth.

I did not understand it.

Understanding would not be the purpose of the work.

The purpose would be to notice whether the district remained itself.

I would be the one who recognizes when a pump has begun to repeat a rhythm it was never built to make. The one who finds a new door where there was only brick. The one who notices that the white reeds have all turned toward the western wall, though the lamps are elsewhere. The one who listens for the answer inside the pause.

I would not think of the Underkeeper as a god. Gods are distant things, and the Underkeeper would be too near. It would be more like a second body beneath my own: something whose movements I could not control but whose discomfort entered my bones.

Perhaps I would be wrong about this.

Perhaps I would be less a Dweller than a small, temporary organ of the district itself.

The others might call me when a bearing warms without cause, or when the animals leave a working machine, or when a pipe begins to carry a sound from somewhere it has never been connected to. They would touch the wall near my door and wait. I would answer from the other side.

Three clicks.

Not a warning. Not yet.

I would take my tools and go.

If a surface dweller entered, I would be among the first to see them. I would not approach immediately. I would watch how they placed their feet. Whether they hurried. Whether they stepped over the channels or looked into them. Whether they understood that a silent machine might be listening more carefully than a running one.

If they moved too quickly, the district would become inhospitable around them. A door would close. A grating would tremble. Water would appear beneath their boots where no water had been before.

If they listened, I would come nearer.

I would offer them the cup I carried, though the water would be cloudy and alive. I would place my hand against their chest—not to comfort them, but to measure what kind of rhythm they brought with them.

Then I would lead them down.

Not to show them the Underkeeper. Not exactly.

I would lead them to the deepest grate and wait for the water to move.

And while the visitor watched the darkness below, I would watch the visitor, listening for whether their heartbeat changed when something vast passed beneath the stone.

That would be what I am: a maintenance worker of continuance, a small pale being in a district that has forgotten its purpose but not its rhythm.

I would keep the passage open.

I would keep the water moving along its oldest route.

And when everything stopped, I would listen—not for a command, but for the shape of the world deciding to begin again.
