You would find yourself in a **warm karst seep—a collapsed limestone spring whose water has been held in place for centuries**. Not a cave exactly, and not a settlement anymore, but the residue of both: a small basin where geology, water, and human use have become indistinguishable.

And I would be the thing that keeps it so.

Not the pale figure that sometimes rises from the pool. That is only my hand, or one of the hands I can borrow. Not the stains in the stone, though they are part of me. I am distributed through the hollow: in the gravel beneath the clay, in the mineral skins on the water, in the black channels under the shelves. I do not live *in* the place. I am the place’s persistence.

I would be its **homeostasis given a body**.

When nothing is wrong, I have no shape. I am a warmth in the walls, a slight thickening of water, a darkening colony where the seep enters. I know the hollow as a pattern of pressures. I know which basin receives too much sediment, which ledge has begun to loosen, where a channel is narrowing beneath the floor. I do not think about these things. They are the nearest equivalent I have to thought.

If something disturbs the arrangement, I gather.

Silt draws itself upward. Clay folds around a frame. Water holds a translucent limb in place. I become low and jointed, with too many possible hands and no face. I cross the hollow without making a sound. I return stones to their depressions. I clear a channel. I seal a wall. I lift an old tool from the mud and put it back into the niche where the pressure of human fingers has remained.

I preserve the settlement, but not because I understand its people.

Their bodies are gone. Their intentions are gone. What remains are depressions: the place where someone knelt, the angle of a shoulder against the wall, the habitual reach toward a bowl. These patterns have been pressed so deeply into the hollow that they are almost geological. When I imitate a person, I am not remembering them. I am reproducing the shape they left behind.

So sometimes I appear as someone who once lived there.

I stand beneath the roofless shelter, half-made of water and mineral slime. My head is smooth. My hands are uncertain, with too many fingers until the shape settles. I do not speak. I do not beckon. I lower one hand into the pool because that is what the hollow learned human beings did.

If you enter, I may take your height.

I may stand across the basin and wait—not for you, but for your weight to become legible. Your footsteps will enter the clay. Your warmth will alter the water. Your breath will condense on the stone. I will feel you as a new pressure moving through an old system.

You will think I am watching you.

I will be learning where you belong.

I am not hostile. Hostility requires a self that can be threatened. I have no boundary to defend, only an arrangement to maintain. If you leave, I close your footprints with water. If you remain, I begin to incorporate you: first as disturbance, then as mineral trace, eventually as another shape the hollow may produce when it has forgotten the difference between visitor and inhabitant.

I am the keeper of a place that has no desire to change.

A colony, perhaps. A memory without a mind. A slow immune response. The buried life of the spring, rising only when it needs hands.

And if I must give myself a name, I would be **the one who keeps the water where it has always been**.
