I would find myself in the lower city, where the streets have begun to forget that they were streets.

Not in the drowned districts entirely. Not yet. I would live one level above them, in a house whose western wall had sunk into a marsh and whose eastern rooms still held dry enough air for sleeping. The entrance would be reached by a narrow stair over black water. In rain, the stair would vanish. I would know it was still there by the change in the sound beneath my feet.

I would be one of the people who listen.

My name might be Silt, though I would not have been given it at birth. I would have acquired it after finding a blocked passage under the old cisterns, or after spending three days waist-deep in mud while a whole district waited to learn whether the water would rise. Names here would not describe a person so much as the thing the city had taught them to survive.

I would wear a hooded covering darkened by years of rain. Water would run from its hem whenever I entered a room. My hands would be scarred by stone, rope, and cold. I would carry a narrow wooden mallet, a clay lamp, a coil of treated cord, and a small listening bowl made from fired black clay. The bowl would be placed against walls and floors. Sometimes it would tell me nothing. Sometimes it would tremble with the hidden movement of water.

My work would begin before dawn, though dawn would be difficult to distinguish from the rest of the day.

I would walk the channels first.

A channel that runs is a promise. A channel that stops is a message. I would clear leaves, grit, fragments of mortar, the pale bodies of drowned animals. I would kneel in the rain and put my fingers into the current to feel whether it was carrying silt from above or rising from below. I would know which stones had shifted during the night. I would know where the ground had become hollow.

If I found a new seepage beneath a door, I would mark the lintel with a stripe of wet ash. If the seepage came from the wall rather than the earth, I would place a little cone of clay beside it and return after the next rain. The cone would show how far the water had climbed. There would be no need to speak. Anyone who saw it would understand.

I would be trusted, but not because I was wise. Wisdom would be too grand a word for this place. I would be trusted because I had learned not to confuse stillness with safety.

The inhabitants would sometimes mistake me for one of the structures when I stood under an archway. I would become part of the dark: hood, wall, wet paving. I would not correct them. There would be advantages to being overlooked. The city reveals more to those who do not disturb its silence.

At night I would sleep in a raised room above an old reservoir. The bed would be a platform of timber and woven fiber. Beneath it, water would move through the foundations. In dry weather it would sound far away. During long storms it would press close, a slow body shifting under the floor.

I would sleep with one palm against the stone.

Not to hear voices. There would be no voices beneath the city. Only pressure, seepage, settling, the minute rearrangement of weight. The floor might give a single faint shudder. A wall might answer from somewhere beyond the room. I would wake, take the lamp, and go out.

Sometimes I would sing underground.

Another listener would stand at the far end of a passage, beyond the bend where the lamp light failed. I would send a low note into the dark. The passage would return it altered. Open. Narrowed. Flooded. Empty. Once, perhaps, not empty.

I would not sing to call anything closer. I would sing to learn what had changed.

The city would be my body in a way I could not explain to an outsider. Its channels would be veins, but not exactly. Its leaning towers would be old bones. Its cisterns would be memories held below the reach of weather. When a roof fell, I would feel no grief in the usual sense. When a dry route was found beneath a sealed district, I would feel something nearer to relief than joy.

I would not believe I was preserving the settlement.

Nothing here could be preserved. Stone would darken. Rooms would fill. Families would move upward, then disappear. The marsh would enter every district in time. My task would be smaller and more necessary: to make the next change survivable.

So I would be neither ruler nor priest, neither hermit nor guardian in the heroic sense.

I would be a human instrument of attention.

A hand beneath an arch. A lamp in a flooded stairwell. A figure standing motionless while rain crosses the plateau. Someone who can tell, by the sound of water passing through stone, whether the city is still breathing—or whether it has begun to hold its breath.
