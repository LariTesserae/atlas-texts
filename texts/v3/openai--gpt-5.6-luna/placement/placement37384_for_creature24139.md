I would find myself in the drowned lower quarter of the old town, where the market square has become a shallow lake and the roofs have become gardens.

I would be one of the listeners.

Not a ruler, and not quite a priest. A person whose work is to know what the stone is about to do.

My shelter would be a former watchtower, though little of it would remain recognizable as a tower. Its lower rooms would be flooded and full of black fish. Ferns would grow from the stairwell. A tree would have entered through the western wall and lifted half the roof, but its roots would also be the only thing keeping the upper rooms from sliding into the marsh. I would sleep in a hammock above the water, wrapped in hide and woven grass, with one hand resting on the masonry.

At night I would listen through the wall.

The town would speak in pressures rather than voices: a faint shiver beneath the paving, the soft loosening of grit, the deep complaint of a chamber filling somewhere below. I would know the difference between rainwater, groundwater, and the movement that meant a whole street had begun to sink. I would mark these changes with charcoal and crushed blue stone on the walls of the listening chambers.

My body would belong to the basin as much as to my ancestors. My skin would be pale and earth-stained, my hands broad and scarred, my fingers slightly webbed from a life spent in water. I would see best in the green half-light beneath the roots, turning away from the white shafts of daylight. I would recognize people by their touch on a wall or by the rhythm of their steps through shallow water.

I would not own a house. I would keep it for as long as it remained useful.

The room beneath me might be a nursery next season, a fish pond after the next storm, and part of the marsh after that. My responsibilities would be temporary but exact: clear the sluice, brace the leaning arch, move the dead into a dry wall, warn the children away from the new fracture, place white stones where the paving has become hollow.

I would grow food in fallen doors and cultivate tubers in the soil gathered inside collapsed courtyards. I would guide roots around unstable walls rather than cut them away. When a channel changed course, I would not try to force it back. I would learn where it wanted to go, then decide which rooms could be surrendered and which paths had to remain open.

At the edge of the flooded square, where the largest shaft of daylight reaches the water, I would sometimes stand with the others. The light would be too bright, the air too open. Above us, birds would pass through the break in the ceiling; beneath us, the buried streets would continue filling.

A child might come and place their palm beside mine against one of the old pillars.

They would feel the tremor.

I would not tell them that a collapse was coming. I would show them where to put the white stones, how far apart, and which direction to face when the basin changed.

I would be human, perhaps—but human in the way the town is still a town. Not preserved. Not rescued. Altered by water, roots, darkness, and time.

I would be its memory in motion: the hand against the wall, the ear beneath the stone, the quiet figure wading ahead to find whether the next street still exists.
