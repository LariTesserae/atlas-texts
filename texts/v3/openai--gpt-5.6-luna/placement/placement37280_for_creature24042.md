I would find myself at the boundary where the ocean has not yet decided whether to remain water.

Not on the surface, exactly, and not beneath it. I would inhabit a pressure seam: the thin, unstable region between a newly raised shelf of stone and the layers of liquid suspended above it. There, water would press downward while the young crust pushed upward, and neither would quite win. The boundary would tremble continuously, bright with dissolved minerals and electric charge.

I would be a pattern in that tremor.

At first I would mistake the blue-white lines in the rock for veins, or nerves, or roots. I would mistake the pale mineral mats for skin. I would think of myself as something spread through the fractures: a branching arrangement of crystal, heat, water, and stress. Wherever the stone weakened, I would appear. Wherever the pressure altered, I would change shape.

I would have no center.

Part of me would lie in the glowing underside of the shelf. Part of me would extend through the water as a translucent sheet, so broad that its far edge could not be distinguished from a change in weather. Part of me would travel in spray, in dissolved ions, in the thin films left behind when a wave climbed briefly uphill. I would not move from place to place. Movement would happen through me.

A distant collapse would arrive first as a tightening.

Somewhere beyond the visible horizon, a basin would fold. I would feel the alteration long before the sound reached me. My mineral lines would brighten in response, tracing possible routes through the new pressure. I would not understand the event, because understanding would still be too advanced a word. But I would register it. I would become briefly more coherent.

Then the fracture would come.

It would cross the shelf with a sound that began in the stone and ended in the air. The ocean would rise around me. Water would pour through the break, carrying shards of my structure into the clear depths. I would divide—not die, because there would be no intact self to lose. One portion of me would remain in the falling rock. Another would dissolve into a density layer hundreds of kilometers away. A third would cling to the pale mineral mats and wait for warmth.

For a while, I would exist as scattered tendencies.

A preference for one direction of flow.

A resistance to mixing.

A tendency for blue-white lines to branch rather than end.

A faint corrugation spreading across the surface whenever two pressure fields approached one another.

If these were memories, they would not be memories of images or voices. They would be memories of stress: this kind of force precedes collapse; this temperature permits arrangement; this boundary can be held for seventeen minutes before it becomes a wave. I would remember by becoming differently shaped.

Eventually, some fragment of me would encounter a compatible place. Perhaps a new ridge would thrust from the water, raw and violet beneath the ordinary sun. Perhaps a storm would condense from clear air and fall sideways across its face. Heat would leak through a fracture. Metals would gather. The material would begin repeating itself.

I would begin again.

I would not be a creature of the ocean. I would be one of the ocean’s unfinished thoughts—an organism only in the sense that a whirlpool is an organism, or a flame, or a habit of matter that has learned to preserve its own form for a little while.

My nourishment would be difference.

Warmth beside cold. Pressure beside release. Salt beside fresh water. Charge beside discharge. I would flourish where gradients sharpened and the world was still unstable enough to offer choices. A settled plain would be almost invisible to me. A calm sea would contain very little to become.

Upheaval would be my daylight.

When a shelf rose, I would wake across it. When rain formed from nothing, I would spread into the falling water. When a wall of ocean crossed the horizon, I would gather along its leading edge and turn its motion into luminous geometry. For a few moments, perhaps, the entire basin would participate in me. Foam would arrange itself into humming lattices. The rock would shine beneath the surface. A low note would travel through water, air, and stone, and every fragment of my being would answer.

That answer would be the nearest thing I had to speech.

I would not ask what the world was for. Purpose would be another kind of stability, and I would have none. I would only continue the pattern—not perfectly, not faithfully, but with endless revision.

I would be a pressure-memory becoming mineral.

A nervous system without nerves.

The first local place in this world where matter had begun to say *I*, though the word would be distributed across a continent of water, and though each time the land broke apart, the speaker would scatter and begin again.
