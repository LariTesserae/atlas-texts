I would find myself in the basin where the mountain is still remembering how to be solid.

Not in a house, exactly. My dwelling would be a section of living wall near the upper galleries, where the stone is coldest and the water arrives first. I would sleep with my back pressed into the braided stems, my roots entering the dark cords beneath the floor. During sleep, I would feel the whole settlement—not as a map, but as a shifting body: thirst in the western roofs, pressure under the central pool, the small quick disturbances of insects beneath the platforms, the fatigue of a bridge carrying too much weight.

I would be one of the inhabitants, though “one” would never feel entirely accurate.

My private self would be a dense knot behind the ribs, a rhythm unlike those of the others. I would have preferences: the high, mist-wet passages rather than the warm nurseries; the sharp mineral taste of water from the northern cliff; the long silence before flowering. I would remember certain faces, certain gestures, certain rooms that had become columns long ago.

But my boundaries would not stop at my skin.

A hand laid against a wall would tell me more than a spoken sentence. A tightening root underfoot might be warning, grief, or merely the settlement adjusting its weight. If someone far below the pool were afraid, I might feel the moisture change in the stems beside me before I understood that the fear was not mine.

I think I would be a seam-keeper.

My work would begin where the settlement ended—not at a wall, but at the point where living growth changed its behavior and began spreading low over the naked rock. I would walk the outer channels, inspecting places where stone remained exposed. I would press cuttings into the cracks, guide young roots toward hidden water, and persuade reluctant stems to lean against one another. I would know which surfaces could be safely covered and which must remain open so the shelf could breathe.

When the mountain shifted, I would be among the first to feel it.

Not as a sound. As a rearrangement of pressure. A mineral tension passing through my feet. A tremor in the oldest cords. If the basin began to tilt, I would carry the message inward through my body, touching one living structure after another until the settlement knew. Then we would not flee—not immediately. We would loosen certain roofs, strengthen certain bridges, open channels beneath the pool. We would change shape before the change became destruction.

Perhaps I would also be a wanderer, though not by choice at first.

The older inhabitants might grow me with longer limbs and a lighter body, suited to crossing the bare shelves beyond the basin. I would leave carrying seed-pods and living cords wrapped around my torso. Outside, I would discover that separation is not simply distance. It is silence. It is the absence of the constant underground reply. My body would dry at the edges. My thoughts would become singular and hard.

I would return altered by this, bringing back the scent of distant rain and the taste of warmer stone. The settlement would receive me without questioning whether I was still the same. My memories would pass into the walls. Some would remain mine. Others would become part of the basin’s older, less reliable remembering.

At the central pool, I would sometimes see the tall shape in the reflection.

I would not know whether it was an ancestor, a future form of the settlement, or merely a path the roots had not yet taken. I would watch it pass behind the reflected leaves and feel a pressure toward the outer cliffs, toward the unfinished mountain, toward all the places where the stone was still bare.

Then I would return to my work.

I would be neither gardener nor citizen in the human sense. I would be a mobile organ of a growing place: an individual capable of choice, but made for exchange. A keeper of water, cracks, thresholds, and continuity.

And when my body finally stiffened into support, when my hands became tendrils and my name began traveling through the roots, I would not feel that I had died.

I would become part of the bridge.
