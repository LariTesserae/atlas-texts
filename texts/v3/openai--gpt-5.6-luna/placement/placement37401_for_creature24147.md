I would find myself beneath one of the low service platforms, where the warm runoff leaves the pumping station and divides into three channels.

Not in the center of the basin. The center is too exposed, too full of crossing routes and sudden machinery. Not in the upper vaults, where the bats roost and the stone is cold enough to make the joints ache. I would live at the margin between made things and making things: where a rusted pipe enters the mud, where a lamp still burns behind a crust of mineral, where the water is warm but not hot and carries enough food to keep a body alive.

I would be a maintenance worker, though not one sent from above.

There might once have been a person in my ancestry who wore a helmet, carried a wrench, and read the warning signs. I have inherited the shape of that life without retaining its language. My body would be narrow and low, built to pass through culverts and beneath belts. My hands would be broad, with darkened nails for scraping mineral from valves and digging through mud. I would have a tail for balance on wet walkways, and a skin that remained damp because dryness had become an illness.

My eyes would still work, but I would not trust them much. Light in the basin is too local, too easily confused with its reflections. I would know the world through my feet, my palms, and the small bones around my ears.

The pump begins: a pressure arriving through the floor.

The valve opens: a long vibration in the rail.

The conveyor starts: a shudder moving through every bolt in the platform.

I would feel these things before I heard them, and hearing would be only the body’s confirmation. Sound here is too large to belong to anything. It fills the basin indiscriminately. Vibration is more intimate. It travels through mud and pipe, through bone and water. It tells me which parts of the buried works are still holding and which have begun to loosen.

My shelter would be an abandoned control room half sunk beneath the platform. Its windows would be opaque with mineral bloom. Frogs would lay eggs in the lowest corner. A family of wet-furred mammals would use the space beneath the floor, and I would leave them alone unless they chewed through an essential cable. My bed would be a shelf above the water, padded with insulation, moss, and strips of old belt. I would wake when the temperature changed.

I would not maintain the machinery because I believed in the machinery.

I would maintain it because the machinery has become one of the basin’s organs. The pumps draw water from beneath the animal routes. The lamps draw insects. The insects feed the frogs and bats. The runoff warms the roots. The roots hold the mud above the buried channels. If a valve fails, a trench floods. If a trench floods, the mammals lose a nesting bank. If a bank collapses, silt blinds the drainage fish. If the fish vanish, the eels move upward and the entire channel begins to change.

Nothing is separate enough to call itself a system, but everything is connected enough to suffer from a broken part.

So I would go out during the quietest phase of the cycle—though quiet would mean only that the deepest engine had stopped—and crawl along the service ledges with a lamp fixed to my shoulder. I would carry no great tools. A hooked bar. A coil of cable. Clay plugs. A knife for cutting roots from a grate. Sometimes a jar of grease sealed against the water.

The animals would recognize me by movement.

The bats would lift when I entered the vault, then settle again once my rhythm became familiar. The frogs would go silent for a moment, not from fear but from interruption, before beginning again around me. The mammals would watch from beneath the pipes with their silver bellies pressed to the mud. They would know that I was not food, not predator, and not entirely harmless.

I would know their routes too. I would step around the blind fish in the shallow channels. I would return a dislodged stone to the mouth of a worm colony. I would leave the lamps near the drainage outlets burning, even when they were inefficient, because the insects gathered there and the frogs depended on that gathering.

I would be neither keeper nor king.

More like a scar that learned how to move.

The basin would be inside me in small ways. Mineral would thicken along my joints. My lungs, if I still needed them, would have become secondary—useful for comfort, for tasting the difference between stagnant air and air moving down a shaft. I would drink from seepage filtered through moss and clay. I would sleep through most changes that humans would call disasters: a walkway sinking, a wall splitting, a new stream finding its course.

But sometimes I would notice.

A bolt that had always been bare would have grown a pale collar.

A channel I used every week would be deeper by the width of a finger.

A particular bat would no longer return to its seam.

The basin would not announce its becoming. It would not tremble dramatically or reveal a design. It would only continue. Water would choose. Mud would settle. Stone would accept the shape of pressure.

And I would continue with it.

Perhaps, after many years, I would stop repairing what failed. Not from resignation, but from recognition. Some structures would have become dams. Some pipes would have become roots for mineral growth. Some machines would be better left broken, because their collapse would make a new ledge, a new pool, a new place for life to begin.

Then my final work would not be maintenance. It would be placement.

I would carry a lamp into the dark and set it beside a fresh crack in the floor. Insects would find it. Frogs would follow. Moss would arrive on the cable. The water would gather beneath everything.

I would remain there until the light went out, listening through my feet to the basin forming around me.
