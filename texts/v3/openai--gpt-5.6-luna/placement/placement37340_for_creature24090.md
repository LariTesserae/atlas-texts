You would find yourself in the older quarter, beneath a roof so low that your raised hand nearly touches it.

Not in the center of the city, where the winged colonies make speech useless, nor at the far mineral plain, where the herds move in slow green-banded migrations. You are in the district of sealed foundations: streets laid over streets, courtyards filled and emptied, walls repaired so many times that no one can say where the original stone ends and the later hands begin.

You are a **seam-reader**.

Not exactly a wall-worker, though you know the hidden passages. Not exactly one of the Keepers of Continuance, though they consult you before closing a chamber or moving a household. Your work is older than your title. You listen to the city where it touches itself.

Each morning, you walk the terraces with your fingertips against the walls. You feel for loosened grit, for mineral dampness—rare and dangerous—beneath the crust. You press your cheek to paving slabs and wait through the ordinary trembling of the basin: claws in the passages, herds crossing distant ground, the slow rubbing of roots against buried stone.

You know the difference between a wall settling and a wall preparing to fail.

The distinction is small.

A settling wall gives one long complaint through its whole length. A failing wall interrupts itself. The vibration stops, resumes, stops again, as if something beneath it is trying to remember how to bear its weight.

You have learned this from your mother, who learned it from hers. Your hands are broad and plated across the knuckles. Your ears are folded close beneath your hair. One of your eyes is shaded by a thin shell visor, not because the light is bright, but because the pale ceiling sometimes opens along a mineral seam and releases a blade of brightness sharp enough to blind.

You carry no lantern. There is nothing to illuminate that cannot be found by touch.

Your tools hang from a fiber belt: a bone stylus for testing powder, a scraper, three wedges of resin, a coil of old garment-fiber, and a shallow bowl containing living root tissue. The root is insurance. If you find a crack that must be held open or sealed, you press the tissue into it and let the plant begin its patient work. Years later, the root will have taken the wall into itself.

You do not repair things so they may remain unchanged.

You repair them so they may continue changing without collapse.

That is the difference between your people and the stone, though at times you suspect the difference is only temporary.

Your dwelling is a chamber beneath a collapsed meeting hall. Its front wall has been rebuilt from mismatched slabs, all rounded at the edges by use. A vine enters through the ceiling and descends behind your sleeping niche. It bears no fruit this season. You leave it undisturbed. The plant was there before you were born, and the chamber is considered partly its house.

You sleep with one hand on the floor.

The city is always speaking through it.

At night—or what passes for night beneath the mineral membranes—you hear the familiar layers. The dry tapping of the wall colonies. The faint rubbing of wing against ceiling. The low calls of the grazers outside the district. Once, far away, the long grinding tremor of a roof adjusting to the basin’s pressure.

Then, beneath all of that, something new.

Not loud. Not urgent. A fine displacement under the paving near the oldest avenue.

You wake before the rest of the household. You place your palm against the floor and feel the message arrive in pieces. A ridge has shifted. Dust is entering a sealed passage. One of the ancient chambers beneath the avenue has opened by less than the width of a finger.

You do not know yet whether this is danger.

It may be nothing. The basin has room for harmless changes. A root may have turned. A pillar may have settled into a stronger position. A burrowing animal may have broken through from below.

Or the old city may be making space.

You dress in layers of resin-sealed fiber. Before leaving, you touch the sternum of the person sleeping beside you: *I have felt something.* Their hand closes around your wrist: *Return before the next shift.*

Outside, the streets are already full.

A child crouches beside a mineral bed, watching yellow flowers open beneath a slab. Two herders pass with a line of plated grazers. Above them, the ceiling colony ripples, and a pale rain of shed wing-cases drifts through the gray light. Someone is singing while scraping salt from a doorway. The tune descends in three notes and travels through the stone.

You take the old avenue.

People recognize you and move aside, not out of reverence, but because they know that when a seam-reader stops walking, everyone nearby should stop as well. You kneel. You place your ear against the paving.

The sound is almost below hearing.

A soft, granular rearrangement.

You brush away the powder. Beneath the slab is a line no wider than a hair. It runs toward the abandoned district, where the oldest rooms lie beneath gardens and no household has entered for generations.

You could mark it and call the Keepers.

You could seal it and postpone the question.

Instead, you press your fingers into the crack.

The stone is cold. The movement beneath it is patient.

Something has been living below the avenue—not a beast, not exactly. A root system, perhaps, or a colony of small bodies sharing a passage. The city has grown over it and around it for centuries. Now it has reached the edge of one of the old foundations.

You feel the pressure build.

You understand, suddenly, that the city has never been a structure placed inside the basin. It is a negotiation between bodies, roots, dust, walls, and time. Every street is a temporary agreement. Every house is a layer of consent. The oldest inhabitants were not the builders who first raised the walls, but the things that remained beneath them.

You are one of those things now.

Not stone. Not plant. Not animal.

A habit of attention.

A person whose life is measured by the changes they prevent from becoming catastrophes, and by the changes they recognize as necessary. You belong to the buried city not because you own a room within it, but because you can feel when the room has ceased to belong to itself.

You lift your hand.

Then you begin to open the avenue, one fitted slab at a time.
