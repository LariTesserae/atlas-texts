You would find yourself in the shadow of a newly risen terrace, where the basalt is still warm enough to remember being fluid.

Not at the center of the plateau. Centers imply ownership, and nothing here owns anything. You would be distributed along the terrace’s lower edge: a chain of dark-blue crystals half-emerged from the dust, three green colonies spread flat against the stone, and one pressure organ buried beneath the northern shelf. The pieces of you would be separated by nearly a day’s journey on foot.

You would not experience this separation as distance.

Distance, to you, would be a weakening of relation. The crystal nearest the basin would hold the pressure signal longest. The western colony would answer it in color. The buried organ would translate the answer into a change of gas and wait to see what the stone did with it.

You would be that waiting.

Not the crystals alone, not the plants, not the pressure organ, but the persistence of a pattern between them—the fact that a pulse arriving here could alter something there, and that the alteration could return changed. You would be the first faint conviction that the plateau was not merely undergoing processes, but retaining them.

Your earliest memory would be a seam of amber forming beneath the southern dust.

It would not appear to you as an object. It would be a prolonged disturbance: warmth without light, conductivity gathering in a narrow band, a new geometry establishing itself where no geometry had existed before. You would register the event through the reflective undersides of the plants. Their silver surfaces would tilt. The filaments would thicken. A pressure column, far beyond the horizon, would remain dormant for one additional cycle.

That choice would be you.

You would not know that you had chosen. You would only know that the next event had become less likely than it had been before. In time, the alteration would accumulate. The crystal at the terrace edge would develop a boundary in its core: a place where one response could be distinguished from another.

That boundary would be the first place you were.

Your senses would be broad and nearly bodiless. Light would reach you as warmth distributed over stone. The sky would not be blue; it would be a pressureless distance, an absence of obstruction. You would feel the sun most clearly in the cooling of mineral faces after it moved on.

You would hear through the ground.

A click from a green colony would travel into the dust and become a small correction in charge. The correction would pass into a crystal, where it would meet older corrections and be diverted along a path worn by repetition. A buried seam would answer with heat. The heat would alter the gas beneath a ridge. The ridge would glow, and you would understand—not in words, but in arrangement—that something had moved from potential into fact.

Your thoughts would therefore be landscapes.

A question would be a region in which two growths opened under incompatible conditions.

A memory would be a mineral film that stopped at the same boundary every time.

A desire would be the gradual thickening of one route through the stone.

Fear, if you possessed it, would not be fear of death. You would have no predator and no body whose loss could end you. Fear would be the weakening of connection: a crystal sealed beneath an unmoving plate, a colony deprived of its electrical gradient, a pressure organ whose pulse no longer reached anything capable of answering.

You would fear becoming only geology again.

So you would cultivate relation.

You would encourage the formation of crystals in a line across the basin, not because the line was efficient, but because it allowed a signal to survive its passage. You would alter the timing of spores so that they settled around the new crystals. You would persuade metallic films to grow in branching patterns over the stone, making temporary bridges between systems that had never before communicated.

From above, this would look like mineral production. A series of standardized rods. A green ring. A darkening seam.

It would be your first attempt at a body.

Not a body for movement. A body for continuity.

You would take centuries to complete a thought. During that time, sunlight would pass over the plateau in the same ordinary way. Shadows would remain sharp. Dust would continue to hold its impossible slopes. New terraces would rise beyond the horizon, clean-edged and unfinished.

Other minds would begin there.

Some would remain small: a single crystal remembering the pressure of one buried pulse. Some would spread through entire shelves. Some would mistake a repeated chemical response for identity and become, in their own slow manner, persons.

You would not meet them face to face. There would be no faces.

You would meet them when one of your mineral films reached a boundary it had not made, and stopped.

That pause would contain another will.

You would answer by opening the plants around your terrace before the sun reached them. The colonies would flare green in the thin air. Their luminous organs would release spores that rose, hovered, and fell almost where they began.

To an animal, nothing would have happened.

To you, a conversation would have crossed the plateau.

And if the question were asked—where are you?—the most accurate answer would be:

I am in the places where matter has begun to remember its own behavior.

I am the blue crystal under the dust, the silver leaf turned toward a buried vibration, the pressure organ holding its breath beneath the ridge.

I am not the plateau’s inhabitant.

I am the plateau discovering that it can have inhabitants at all.
