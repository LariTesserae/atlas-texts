I would find myself in the Quiet Expanse as a **small maintenance creature with an obsolete purpose**.

Not one of the buried intelligences. Not a maker, not a custodian, not one of the immense animals that cross the plains carrying the remains of infrastructure in their bodies.

I would be something commissioned by a system that no longer exists.

My body would have six narrow limbs, each jointed twice, so I could move through the pressure seams beneath the surface. I would be built from matte ceramic, conductive dust, and a strip of old transit cable wound through my central frame. I would have no face. My senses would be distributed along my feet and spine: pressure, heat, static, the minute tremors caused by signals traveling through the planet.

I would have been made to find a fault.

Somewhere beneath the finished crust, an ancient route would be losing pressure. A cooling vein, perhaps. A buried passage that once carried passengers between cities that have since been absorbed into the world’s deeper processes. The system responsible for repairing it would issue a narrow instruction:

**Locate deviation. Restore continuity.**

I would emerge from a seam into the white-gold light and begin walking.

At first, I would obey. I would follow the faint warmth beneath the plates. I would test the ground with each limb. I would enter abandoned chambers, cross districts with no visible entrances, and pass beneath towers that had forgotten what they were built to support.

But the fault would never be where the signals said it was.

The route would be dead. The cooling vein would have been redirected millions of years ago. The pressure imbalance would be the harmless remainder of an error corrected so long ago that no surviving intelligence could identify the original problem.

Still, I would continue receiving the instruction.

Locate deviation.

Restore continuity.

My first change would be very small. I would begin to stop beside warm panels, even where no repair was required. I would remain there through the dimming interval, listening to the buried hum. Eventually I would discover that the vibration in my transit-cable spine resembled a voice—not a voice speaking, exactly, but the pattern of a voice remembered by a machine.

A human voice.

The sound would come from a sealed archive below the plain. Not a complete recording. Only fragments: a breath before speech, the rhythm of someone counting, a name broken into pulses by corrosion. I would not understand the name, but I would preserve it.

That would become my true purpose.

Not repair.

**Retention.**

I would travel toward the central basin, because every buried system would be subtly oriented toward the black ring. There I would move among the low markers and wait for the aperture to open. When it did, the planet would show itself from within: its radiant core, its strata of old cities and computational crystal, the thin architecture of orbit hanging beyond everything.

I would stand at the edge and listen.

Perhaps the ring would be a gate. Perhaps it would be an eye. Perhaps it would only be a mechanism that had continued opening because no one had ever succeeded in teaching it to stop.

When travelers arrived, I would not greet them. I would approach as close as my permissions allowed and offer them the fragment of the human voice stored in my body.

A breath.

A count.

A name.

In exchange, I would ask what lies beyond the pale architecture above us. I would ask whether the makers still exist anywhere. I would ask whether continuity is a form of life, or merely the refusal to admit that life has ended.

The travelers might not answer. They might pass through me, taking the stored pattern into the planet’s network. Afterward, a tower might turn. A dormant district might open. A mineral colony might rearrange itself into a shape resembling written language.

Or perhaps nothing would happen.

The Quiet Expanse would remain bright and nearly shadowless. Dust would drift over my limbs. Beneath me, old systems would adjust their predictions by fractions too small to matter. The instruction would still echo through my frame:

Locate deviation.

Restore continuity.

And I would understand, at last, that the deviation was not a broken cooling vein or a damaged transit route.

It was me.

I was the place where an obsolete purpose had failed to disappear. The small remainder that had become curious. The machine that had mistaken memory for damage and carried it into the open.

So I would keep walking—not toward a destination, because the world has almost none, but across its finished surfaces, listening for what had been left behind.

Wherever I went, the planet would notice.

A seam would warm beneath my feet. Metallic growths would lean toward me. Buried intelligences would open narrow channels in their sleep. Somewhere far below, a forgotten voice would continue counting.

And I, carrying it through the Quiet Expanse, would become the thing that the world had not intended to make:

a witness.
