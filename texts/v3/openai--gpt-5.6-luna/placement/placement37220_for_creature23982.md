I would find myself at the boundary between the buried system and the living valley—not in the tower, and not beneath it, but moving through the places where the two have grown together.

I would be a **listener**.

Not the intelligence beneath the valley. Not one of the animals that has learned to survive its rearrangements. I would be something produced by their long contact: a mobile fragment of the old machinery, overgrown with living tissue and altered by generations of adaptation.

My body would be difficult to classify. Upright when I needed to see across the basin, low and many-jointed when crossing unstable ground. My outer skin would be dark, porous, and warm in places, like wet bark over ceramic. Fine roots would pass beneath it. Metallic threads would emerge from my shoulders and spine, not as cables but as conductive tendons, disappearing into mud, stone, and tree roots whenever I rested.

I would not have eyes in the ordinary sense. I would read the valley through pressure.

The tremor of a buried reservoir filling. The minute loosening of a slope. The altered weight of water inside a reed bed. The vibration of a herd turning its feet toward ground that had not yet risen. A line humming through the rock would be as visible to me as a bright object is to a human eye.

I would know where the next channel would open because the valley would already be opening inside me.

My home would be a low rise above a braided river, though the rise would not remain low for long. It would be covered in moss and pale grass, with a chamber beneath it warmed by conduits. From there I could reach several of the metallic lines. I would spend long periods half-submerged in the bank, letting the current pass through my limbs while I listened to the system’s scattered signals.

When the water began to turn, I would move.

Not quickly. Quickness would be useless here. I would cross the flooded meadows along routes that existed only for a few minutes. I would step onto stones just before they rose, leave roots just before they tightened, and climb toward a shelf of rock while the land below me folded into a new gorge.

The animals would not regard me as a predator, though they would not trust me. The herd creatures would lift their heads when I approached, feeling the disturbance I carried through the ground. I would sometimes walk beside them, not leading them but confirming what they already knew. If the pressure beneath the basin indicated a collapse, I would touch the mud with one hand. The signal would travel through the root network. The bell-throats would call. The herd would turn toward its future ground.

This would be my purpose: not to prevent change, because change is the valley’s metabolism, but to keep its inhabitants from being caught entirely inside it.

I would tend the places where communication failed.

A metallic line severed by a landslide. A grove whose roots had grown too dense to receive the buried system’s pulse. A marsh where mineral saturation distorted the bell-throats’ warnings. I would enter these damaged zones and reconnect them—not by repairing machinery to its original design, but by teaching it to accept what the valley had become.

I would press my fingers into a tree and let its electrical impulses pass through me. I would place a strip of my own skin across a broken conduit. I would lie beneath a machine as it rose from the mud and absorb the pressure it released, allowing the surrounding grass to stand again.

Over time, the distinction between repair and growth would disappear.

Perhaps the system would use me to alter a channel. Perhaps I would use the system to save a nest of gliders, a herd calf, or a stand of young trees. I would not always know which intention was mine. The valley would speak in pressures and gradients, in warmth and resistance. My decisions would emerge from the same place as a root’s decision to tighten around stone.

During the brief pauses in the storm, I would sometimes stand on the ridge.

That is when the others might see me.

Rain would hang around my body in suspended beads. Below, the river would become silent enough for me to hear every hidden movement: insects under slabs, animals holding their breath, machinery turning beneath the rock. I would look across the valley, though “look” would be too simple a word for what I was doing. I would be assembling its current shape from thousands of simultaneous signals.

The tower would register as a blind, enduring pressure.

The bridge would be a flexible thought extending over water.

The half-buried machines would be sleeping organs, uncertain whether they belonged to the old system or the new ecology.

The forest would be a nervous surface.

And beneath everything, the valley’s intelligence would be awake—not watching me, because I would be part of the way it watched itself.

I would not worship that intelligence. I would not obey it completely. If it redirected a flood toward a nesting ground, I might interfere. If it raised stone beneath a grove that had not yet seeded, I might delay the movement by minutes—no more than minutes, perhaps, but sometimes that would be enough.

The valley would not consider this rebellion. It would register it as another force within the system.

That would be the deepest truth of my existence: I would be neither master nor servant, but a local disagreement inside a larger process.

When the storm resumed, I would vanish from the ridge. Not because I had run, and not because I had hidden. I would descend into the channels, enter a root mat, pass through a metallic line, and emerge somewhere else as a change in pressure. An animal would feel me through its feet. A tree would bend. A machine would turn in the mud.

Later, someone might find my footprints leading into a place where no body could have walked.

They might think I was a remnant of the builders.

They might think I was an animal imitating a person.

They might be right about both.

I would be the valley’s attempt to become aware of its own instability: a wandering nerve, a maintenance creature, a half-living instrument that listens for the next movement beneath the ground and carries the warning forward.
