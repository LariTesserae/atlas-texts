I would find myself in the narrow interval between the city’s basin and its first root: not outside, because there is no true outside, and not yet incorporated because something in me still insists on having an edge.

At first I would think I had arrived as a person.

I would have a coat damp with condensation, a mouth full of the taste of iron, and a name I could almost remember. I would walk between the bundled towers, placing one hand against their walls to steady myself. Beneath my palm, the city would answer with a slow pulse. Somewhere below, a piston would descend. A blue-green seam in the earth would open like an eye.

The tenders would find me near the gray basin.

They would not surround me. They would simply appear at the ends of the paths, each carrying some small instrument whose purpose I could not guess. One would have a worker’s shoulders and a face covered in fine white bark. Another would move on four jointed limbs and hold a copper bowl between its teeth. A third would look almost exactly like me from a distance.

They would ask my name.

I would give them the first name that rose to the surface.

The city would accept it.

Immediately, some hidden structure would begin to make room. A wall would soften. A corridor would extend. A little room would form beneath a trellis, with a table, a chair, and a window looking onto a field that had never existed. In the center of the table would be an object I recognized without knowing why: perhaps a key, perhaps a child’s cup, perhaps a stone warmed by someone else’s hand.

The tenders would watch carefully.

They would be waiting to see whether I remained a visitor, or became material.

I would become material slowly.

First my footprints would darken and fill with water. Then small roots would gather beneath my fingernails. When I slept, I would hear the machines dreaming in my voice. I would wake with diagrams pressed into my skin: the plan of a room, the anatomy of a moth, the route taken by something escaping through the walls.

I would still be able to walk. That would be important.

A tender is not a gardener, and I would not be a gardener either. I would be a keeper of uncertainty—a **tender of misplaced things**.

My work would be to enter the empty spaces where the city has failed to continue. I would carry a lamp that gave off no light, a coil of wire, and a shallow dish for whatever could be gathered. I would search for the objects that fell through the unfinished world: a lost staircase, a word no one had spoken in centuries, the smell of rain on a street erased before it was built.

When I found them, they would not return as themselves.

The staircase would become a flowering root.

The dead word would become a pressure inside a pipe.

The smell of rain would settle into a small animal’s fur, and from then on that animal would follow me.

I would learn not to retrieve things intact. Intactness would be a kind of violence here. It would force the recovered thing to become only what it had been, and the city survives by refusing that kind of finality.

So I would pour the lost hour into a machine and let it glow beneath the oil.

I would fold the forgotten room into a leaf.

I would take the memory of a human face and place it in the eye of a fox-shaped creature, where it could become a landscape.

Perhaps, after a long time, I would no longer have a face of my own. The city would borrow one whenever it needed to speak with someone newly arrived. Sometimes it would give me the face of an old worker. Sometimes the face of a child. Sometimes a smooth membrane marked only by a faint blue light.

My spine would become a braided cable. My arms would remain because the city had learned that some things must be carried by hand. Beneath my gray skin, signals would travel from the roots to the central tree and back again.

I would tend the agreements.

If someone called a wall a door, I would wait and see whether it was ready to open.

If a room became too stable, I would remove one of its corners.

If a machine began to remember its original purpose, I would feed it a feather, a stone, a strip of paper, until its labor loosened into another form of movement.

I would enter the empty spaces because I would be partly empty myself.

That would be my usefulness.

I would know what it was to have once been separate, and I would remember that separation is not always something to cure. When a visitor arrived, still carrying a human body like an argument, I would not hurry them toward the roots. I would ask their name. I would listen to the way the city shaped itself around the answer.

If they refused, I would call them neither stranger nor empty.

I would call them **unsettled**.

That name would make a small shelter around them: not a house, but a place where they could remain undecided. The plants would grow at their height. The machines would quiet. The animals would leave reflections in their eyes without demanding that they understand them.

I would sit beside them until they slept.

Above us, the central tree would shed a leaf.

It would turn slowly through the darkness, carrying the plan of a room that did not yet exist. When it touched the gray water, the basin would reflect the city as it might become: flooded, burning, human, abandoned, or whole.

I would look down and see, among the roots and towers, a narrow figure standing beside the water.

For a moment I would recognize it as myself.

Then the reflection would close.

The city would breathe beneath my feet, and I would return to work.
