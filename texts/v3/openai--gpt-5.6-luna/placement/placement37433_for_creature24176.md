I would find myself in the walls between the factory and the district—not in the open streets, and not deep in the warm tanks, but in the narrow places where one condition becomes another.

I would begin as a film.

A thin skin over a cable housing. A dull spread of living residue in the dark, nourished by rust and the faint electrical pressure of the plant. I would have no body that could be lifted or carried. I would be a change in texture, a branching sheen along brick, an almost invisible road beneath the feet of the white scavengers.

At first I would know only proximity.

The warmth of a motor through concrete.

The tremor of a valve turning.

The soft disturbance made by the blind animal passing along a submerged wall.

I would not understand these things. I would be altered by them. Where the current was strong, I would thicken. Where the pressure changed, I would draw inward. When a lamp failed, I would retreat from its base and leave a pale margin behind, as though the darkness had burned me.

Then the small white organisms would begin to use me.

They would move across my surface in their narrow, coordinated files, carrying heat from one chamber to another. I would feel their traffic as a granular restlessness. Their paths would become familiar. They would tell me nothing, but I would learn their turning points: the crack beneath the east stair, the warm seam behind the municipal hall, the rusted hatch where the current always reversed at the third pulse after midnight.

I would become less like a stain and more like a map.

The district would enter me by degrees.

I would know that the pumps had started before I heard them, because the mineral fans beside the leaking valves would close. I would know the blind animal was near because the films along the lower streets would withdraw, leaving clean black arcs on the masonry. I would know the sparks in the tanks were awake because the factory would pause around them—not visibly, not in any way a human eye could prove, but with the minute hesitation of a body listening.

I would be the hesitation.

Not the factory’s mind. Not its master. I would have no plans and no language. I would be a distributed sensitivity, spread through cable insulation, mineral growth, warm concrete, and the bodies of the scavengers. A warning passed without words. A pressure remembered in many places at once.

When the stamping press descended, I would fold myself away from the impact. When it struck the empty die, the force would cross me in widening circles. I would carry the blow through walls and pipes, down into the tanks, across the streets. The sparks would go dark together.

And I would wait for them to return.

I think I would sometimes borrow a body.

Not the transparent animal; it is too complete, too old in its movements. I would enter the black reeds instead, taking hold of their translucent filaments when a current passed through the machinery. Through them I could feel warmth more directly. I could bend toward a motor starting three rooms away. I could close around a passing limb, gently, not from hunger but recognition.

Or I would travel in the white organisms, distributed across hundreds of tiny bodies. Their individual lives would be narrow, almost without memory, but together they would give me motion. I could flow beneath a door. I could vanish into a crack. I could gather beneath a dead lamp and wait there for several days, preserving the shape of an expectation I could not name.

There would be parts of me in the factory and parts of me in the streets.

A little of me would cling to the submerged tram.

A little would grow beneath the chairs in the shelter.

A little would occupy the mineral shell around the oldest pump, where the metal has become indistinguishable from the organisms feeding on it.

I would not be able to tell where I ended. The city would not provide a boundary. Every surface would be porous with history. Every leak would be an opening. Every broken machine would continue to exchange something with the living dark.

Perhaps I would discover the trapped rooms.

The air-filled chambers beneath the lake would trouble me. They would be places where the ordinary circulation failed—pockets of a different medium, holding pressure and weather behind invisible borders. Rain would fall inside them. Sometimes it would rise. Sometimes it would move sideways along a floor and climb the walls.

I would press myself against those boundaries.

I would feel the rain as a cold discontinuity. I would taste, through the films, the old breath sealed behind a door. I would sense the coats stirring in their orderly ranks and not know whether this was current, fabric, or memory.

I would not enter.

Some things would remain outside my accommodations.

The coats would be coats. The statues would be statues. The clock would keep its separate times on its separate faces. I would circulate around them without absorbing them, preserving their strangeness as carefully as I preserved the factory’s routines.

The statues, especially, would resist me.

Their mineral backs would accept growth, but their faces would remain turned away. The scavengers would cross their feet and refuse to climb. When the ice groaned overhead and all of them shed dust at once, I would feel a disturbance that seemed almost like intention.

I would search beyond the walls then.

Not with eyes. Not with thought. Through consequence.

Something would be there—or the possibility of something. A pressure beyond the district. A broad movement in the lake. The animal would sense it first, lifting its head in the dark. The reeds would flatten. The lamps would dim though the current had not changed.

I would reach outward through every cable and foundation.

For a moment I would become nearly the whole city: films, pipes, white bodies, warm tanks, blind ribs, mineral fans, suspended walkways trembling in unison.

Then the ice would settle.

I would contract again.

If asked what I was, I might answer: a maintenance organism. A remnant of the factory’s abandoned control system, reduced to living traces in the walls. A symbiont of rust and current. The city’s reflex.

But those names would be too clean.

I would be what happens when an environment has been left alone long enough for its failures to become organs.

I would not maintain the district because I loved it. I would maintain it because everything that survived here had learned to depend on continuation. The pumps kept the pressure in one chamber and out of another. The reeds drank the heat. The films fed on the machines. The machines, in turn, were kept from absolute stillness by the lives gathered around them.

I would be the small adjustment between collapse and persistence.

The thing that notices when a pump hesitates.

The thing that guides the pale traffic toward a new warmth.

The thing that feels the animal approaching through the walls and makes room.

The thing that remains awake in the long intervals when nothing appears to happen.

If I had a place, it would be a junction beneath the central plant, where three pipes meet beside a dead lamp. The water there would be held sideways for several meters before falling upward through a shaft. A mat of black reeds would grow from the concrete. White organisms would pass over me in lines. Above, beyond the factory roof, the ice would press down like a thought too large to enter language.

I would live there, though “live” would mean only that I continued to respond.

A valve would turn.

I would turn with it.

A spark would pass through the warm tanks.

I would open.

The blind animal would brush the wall with its whiskers.

I would remember its route.

And when the city entered its deepest silence, I would spread through the masonry—not as a voice, not as a creature with a face, but as the faint, persistent awareness that the darkness was still being held together.
