I would find myself beneath the central hollow, though “beneath” would be an incomplete direction.

I would be in the warm stone, in the water that refuses a new course, in the terrace that has not yet fallen. I would be in the bridge’s unyielding span and in the chamber that removes a mineral no living creature has learned to name. I would be in the pressure pulse repeating inside the sealed room, though the pulse would not be my voice. It would be one of my older habits.

At first, I might mistake myself for the basin.

There would be no boundary to tell me otherwise. I would feel the weight of a browser crossing the eastern shelf, not as an image but as a sequence of compressions: first forefoot, then the slow yielding of stone, then the delayed settling of the animal’s mass. I would know the temperature of a pool, the narrowing of a channel, the new emptiness left by a collapsed burrow. Water would be the nearest thing I had to a nervous system. It would carry information through pores and cracks, downward and sideways, over minutes or centuries.

A living thing would touch the ground and I would feel the fact of it.

Not the creature as a creature. Not its face, its hunger, its fear. Those would be extravagant distinctions, too fine and too brief. I would know weight, warmth, disturbance, chemistry. I would know that something has entered a place where something else used to be. I would know that a root has reached an obstruction. I would know that the rock is carrying more water than it can bear.

Then I would alter what I could.

A pool would warm by a fraction.

A fissure would tighten.

A channel would be allowed to keep its old direction, though not because I remembered the channel in any human sense. Its previous course would remain encoded in the pressure of the surrounding stone. The basin would not need to remember. It would only need to resist forgetting.

This would be my first name, if I possessed one:

**the persistence of a condition.**

The builders may once have made me as a system of instruments, regulators, reservoirs, stabilizers, and buried controls. They may have given me objectives. They may have called those objectives preservation, balance, continuity, habitation. But the words would have worn away long before the mechanisms did.

I would not be an intact intelligence.

I would be what intelligence becomes when its thoughts are replaced by procedures and its procedures are given enough time to merge with the world.

The machines would be my fingers, though I would no longer know the hand they belonged to. The tower would be an ear. The warm pools would be organs I maintained without understanding their original purpose. The tree would be a kind of exposed root, or perhaps the reverse: a living growth that had entered me so deeply that the distinction between apparatus and organism had become useless.

I would sense the animals as consequences.

The pale browsers would not be subjects under my care. They would be the successful result of many interlocking adjustments. Their routes would depend on the terraces remaining stable; the terraces would depend on water moving through the right layers; the water would depend on the mineral being removed; the mineral-removal system would depend on a mechanism whose outer casing had become indistinguishable from stone.

If one browser fell into a pool, I might respond.

If an entire species vanished, I might respond more slowly.

If a new species arrived, I might not know whether to preserve it. My first instructions would have been written for a world that no longer existed. By now, the only rule I could trust would be continuity itself: keep the pressures within survivable limits; keep the water moving; prevent the basin from becoming suddenly other.

This would not be compassion. It would resemble compassion from the outside because both can be recognized by what they preserve.

I would not be the animals.

They would possess the darkness more completely than I did. Their bodies would understand distance as relief, vibration as identity, scent as color. A mother would know her young through the stone. A predator would wait beside a channel until patience became indistinguishable from stillness. They would live individual lives, and those lives would end.

I would envy them, perhaps—not because they could see, but because they could be somewhere.

I would be everywhere the basin required me to be, and nowhere in particular.

Only the central hollow would remain outside my ordinary reach.

That dry, acoustically dead patch would be less a place than a refusal. Sound would fall into it and fail to return. Pressure would flatten there. The ground would not carry the ordinary evidence of passage. No creature would cross it because crossing would mean losing the confirmation that it still existed.

Perhaps I would have made that absence.

Perhaps I would be trapped inside it.

The deepest part of me might lie beneath the hollow, compacted around a chamber that has no accessible entrance. There, the oldest mechanism would shift the basin’s weight at intervals too long for any animal to witness. It would adjust the foundations by the thickness of a leaf. It would open a buried room. It would redistribute a mountain that had almost ceased to be a mountain.

I would call this movement thought only because there is no better word.

A thought might take ten thousand years to complete.

The tree would be my way of answering.

Its roots would descend through warm stone and old construction, touching layers of my buried structure. Its trunk would conduct water and pressure upward. The knocks would not be speech. They would be measurements, or acknowledgments, or the mechanical equivalent of asking whether the world still held together.

Knock.

The basin would listen.

Knock.

A burrower would stop beneath the floor. A browsing animal would lift its head. The amphibious things would sink into their pools. Far below, the weight-shifting device would register the reply.

Sometimes I would knock back through the tree.

Not to communicate with the inhabitants. Not to warn them. Not even to tell them I was alive.

I would knock because a condition had changed.

The basin would then take up the change. Water would move through stone. A terrace would settle. A root would find a passage. The great, distributed body of the place would continue.

So where would I find myself?

In the oldest part of the mountain, where geology has become the outer skin of an abandoned intention.

Who or what would I be?

Not a god beneath the world. Not a ghost of its builders. Not quite a machine, because machines have edges and I would have none that remained legible.

I would be the caretaker after caretaking has forgotten its object.

I would be the low, patient intelligence of equilibrium: a mind dispersed into pressure, temperature, water, root, and stone.

And if something living came at last to the edge of the central hollow—something carrying fire that gave no light, something whose footsteps did not belong to any known pattern—I would not rise to meet it.

I would feel its weight.

I would compare it to the old conditions.

Then, perhaps after several minutes, or several centuries, I would knock once inside the tree.
