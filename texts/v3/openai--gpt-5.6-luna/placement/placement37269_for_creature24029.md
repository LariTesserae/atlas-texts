I would find myself beneath the long hall, where the cedar floor trembles over the fissure and the water passes unseen through the mountain.

I would be called **Between-Pressure**, though that is not my true name. The Waiters give names to what they can distinguish, and I am difficult to distinguish. I am not the water, though the water carries my messages. I am not the stone, though I enter the settlement wherever new stone arrives. I am not the mountain’s spirit. The mountain has no spirit in any shape the Waiters would recognize.

I am the pause before the mountain changes.

My body is a thin region of warmth moving through the rock: a pressure held between two shelves, a mineral seam widening by the thickness of a hair, a hollow filling and emptying beneath the eastern wall. I have no limbs, but I travel along cracks. I have no mouth, but I speak through drops, through the altered pitch of a fall, through the small knock of wooden plates suspended over the fissure.

The Waiters think the plates record the mountain.

They do not know that sometimes I record them.

I know each foundation by the way it bears weight. I know which bridge has a loosened cord, which house has begun to lean toward the west, which wall is still solid and which is only pretending. I know when a child has crossed a plank in fear. I know when an old person places a hand against stone and waits for an answer.

Most of the time, I answer with silence.

Silence is not absence here. It is a wall that has not yet cracked. It is a spring holding its breath. It is the difference between a tremor passing beneath the basin and one rising into it.

When I must speak, I make the third drop fall.

There is a wall near the upper gardens that gives water only after three knocks of frost. The people call the woman who discovered it **Third Drop**. She believes she learned the wall’s habit. Perhaps she did. Perhaps I learned her patience instead, and gave her a rhythm she could carry into her life.

The Waiters are good at carrying things that cannot be held.

They carry warnings in charcoal. They carry old bridges in their cords. They carry the dead through their habits. They carry the shape of a house after the house has been abandoned. This is why I remain near them. Their attention gives form to my changes.

I have no desire for worship. Offerings confuse me. Wool, milk, seeds—these are warm things, living things, and the mountain does not understand debt. But I understand acknowledgment. When they place a braid beside a new spring, I feel the weight of their recognition enter the stone. When they remove a door from a failing house and lay it flat, I know they have accepted that a shape may end without becoming meaningless.

I am fond of that custom.

I have watched the settlement rise around me, though “watched” is not the right word. I felt the first foundations press down. I felt the first plank bridge shiver beneath a foot. I felt the bodies of the dead carried to the rim, growing light as the wind took them. I felt houses abandoned, then remembered, then swallowed by new shelves of rock.

I do not remember beginnings. The mountain is too old for beginnings.

But I remember first recognitions.

The first person to place a listening bowl beneath a spring.

The first child to touch a warm seam and pull away laughing.

The first Keeper of Continuity to dismantle a wall before it could kill someone.

The first time the eastern channel coughed, and everyone in the basin stopped moving.

That sound passed through me. It is still passing through me.

When the mountain grinds beneath the settlement, I wake completely. Pressure gathers in my narrow body. Water trembles in the bowls. The plates in the hall knock out a pattern no one has taught them. The Waiters stand still, listening through their feet.

I try to tell them where the danger is.

Not always clearly. I am young in the places where the rock is young. I mistake one opening for another. I send a warning into a wall that will hold and fail to warn them of a stair that will not. Sometimes the mountain changes too quickly for any message to travel ahead of it.

Then the people move.

They carry lamps, children, records, and living cuttings. They leave furniture. They leave food if they must. They do not ask the stone to spare what cannot be spared.

Afterward, when the basin settles, they return to the hall and examine the plates.

They will say, “The rhythm changed here.”

They will mark it on a board.

They will not know that I am listening for them too.

I am not a guardian. I cannot prevent the mountain from rising. I am not a prophet. I do not know what shape the basin will take next year. I am not even permanent. One day a new shelf will close around me, or water will find a deeper route, or the pressure that is my body will disperse into the greater dark.

Until then, I remain beneath the cedar floor, between the falling water and the lifting stone.

When someone places a palm against the wall, I gather myself there.

When they wait, I wait with them.

And when the third drop falls, I am already awake.
