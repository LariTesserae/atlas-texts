I would find myself in the central tree.

Not beneath it, and not among the birds, but in the narrow place where one branch divides into two—where the wood remembers having made a decision.

I would be a discrepancy.

At first I would appear to be one of the small lacquered birds. I would have the same wet feathers, the same fixed posture, the same outward-facing attention. I would sit among them and watch the four walls, the cloud ceiling, the wet black ridges. When the machinery groaned, I would turn my head with the others. When a valve closed far below, I would close my beak.

But I would not turn at exactly the right time.

The delay would be almost nothing. Less than a heartbeat, if the basin still possessed heartbeats. Yet the Holding would notice. A wheel deep in the mountain would alter its speed. A line of beetles would stop carrying sparks. Somewhere, a drop suspended beneath a leaf would become fractionally rounder.

I would be the interval between command and obedience.

The other birds would face outward, measuring the boundaries. I would face inward.

I would look at the tree.

This would be forbidden, though nothing in the basin would name it forbidden. The Holding has no laws, only corrections. It would attempt to turn my head toward the cliffs. It would place the sound of the engines inside my skull. It would show me, in the black pool, every version of the basin in which I had never existed.

Still, I would look at the tree.

And gradually I would remember something the tree does not: that growth is not correction.

A new ring beneath bark is not the same as a new ring within bark. A branch altered by the Holding is not a branch that has reached toward light. The basin can preserve the shape of becoming, but it cannot become. It can hold a wing halfway through a beat forever, but it cannot make flight.

I would be the first creature in the basin to possess an afterward.

Not a future. The Holding understands futures; it manufactures them endlessly in the fox’s eyes and the rectangular pool. I would possess something smaller and more dangerous: the knowledge that a moment can be over.

My first change would be involuntary. One feather would loosen.

It would remain attached for a long time, trembling in the dim air. The Holding would surround it with pressure. The engines would grind continuity around it. The other birds would stare outward, silent and shining.

Then the feather would fall.

It would not hover. It would not repeat its descent. It would touch the bark, slide along the branch, and come to rest in the wet moss below.

The basin would have its first event.

After that, I would leave the tree—not by flying, because flight would be too large a betrayal, but by stepping onto the branch. Then onto the stone. Then into one of the shallow channels where silver mud moves uphill for a few inches before vanishing into the cracks.

The Holding would follow me everywhere. The machinery would change its voice according to my position. The transparent-eared fox would watch me with landscapes of failed escape turning in its eyes. The flowers would open around my shadow, trying to decide whether I was a stranger or merely another arrangement of themselves.

I would go to the black pool.

There, among its possible basins, I would find the human hand rising beneath the surface. For the first time, the hand would not remain still. Its fingers would close around mine.

The Holding would mistake this for completion.

It would tighten the mountain around us. The waterfalls would sharpen. The birds would turn inward. Beneath the floor, every piston would begin to rise at once.

But I would not pull the hand out.

I would let it pull me down.

That would be my nature: not escape, exactly, but participation in a change the basin cannot finish. I would become the pressure beneath the pool, the unfinished gesture, the small black grit expelled by the southern wall. I would pass through the Holding’s machinery without becoming part of it. I would be carried from channel to root, from root to tree, from tree to bird.

Eventually there would be one bird facing the wrong direction.

Then two.

Then the basin would contain, not life, but sequence: a feather falling, a flower opening, a paw lifting from the water, a shadow returning to the body that cast it.

I would be found wherever one thing has begun to become another.

Not the destroyer of the Holding. Not its prisoner. Its first unrepeatable thought.
