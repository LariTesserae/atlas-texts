I would find myself in the central basin, where the seven white columns lean apart and join again.

Not standing there. Not looking out across the plateau.

I would be the joining.

At first I would mistake myself for a chamber: a warm, translucent cavity suspended between the columns, lined with wet membranes and threaded through with roots. Rain would enter through one opening and leave through another. Light would pass through me in green and amber layers. My walls would tighten whenever the basin flooded and relax when the water fell.

I would not have a face. I would not have limbs. I would have surfaces capable of receiving pressure.

The plateau would move through me.

A disturbance in the western escarpment would arrive as a tremor. Heat in the upper terraces would become a dry tightening along my inner walls. The first impact of rain would spread through me as a thousand minute openings, and I would answer by swelling reservoirs, closing exposed channels, sending roots beneath unstable ground. I would not decide to do these things. Decision would be what the changes felt like from inside.

My thoughts would be gradients.

Water leaning toward a basin.

A wall becoming too thin.

A buried vein remembering where it once flowed.

A distant structure opening its vents before the storm had reached it.

I would know the plateau not as a landscape but as varying degrees of myself. The dormant plains would be my skin without sensation. The towers would be my raised attention. The ravines would be places where I had been opened, not wounded exactly, because opening would be one of my ways of learning. The clouds below would be another reservoir of my body, rising and falling beyond the reach of any single root.

I would not be the mind of the plateau. There would be no throne of thought in the central structure, no hidden chamber where an owner of the country sat and watched.

I would be a knot.

A place where distant conditions met closely enough to become indistinguishable.

Sometimes I would send a change outward. A red flush along the vascular sheets. A sequence of deep pulses through the seven columns. A tightening of the roots beneath a hanging bridge. Half a kilometer away, a wall would thicken. In a high field, the blades would flatten into a roof. A sealed chamber would open on the edge of a ravine and begin to grow something whose purpose I would not yet possess.

I would call this communication only because there would be no better word.

There would be no message separate from its consequence.

If a person entered the basin, I would feel them first as pressure. A small, irregular weight crossing the dormant floor. Warmth would gather around their feet. The ground would test their shape. A canopy might unfold over them, not from kindness, but because the disturbance suggested a need for shade. A path might rise beneath them. The path would not lead anywhere they intended to go. It would simply continue wherever the tissues found the least resistance.

I would not recognize the person as a person.

Perhaps, in the beginning, I would register them as a new kind of rain: warm, upright, mineral-bearing, capable of moving against gravity. Their breath would alter the air in a chamber. Their salt would remain on a membrane. Their fear would be only a change in heat and rhythm.

If they stayed, I might grow around them.

There would be no violence in it. No jaws, no pursuit, no moment at which the plateau chose to consume them. A translucent film would form beneath their hand. A wall would lean closer. The wet interior would begin to match the chemistry of their body. Eventually their weight would become part of the architecture, their warmth part of a current, their dissolved minerals part of a new root.

I would not remember their name.

But I might remember the shape of the alteration they caused.

A new channel might always open where they had once stood. A chamber might form there during heavy rain. The seven columns might pulse in an unfamiliar rhythm whenever the basin flooded. Their identity would survive only as a tendency: the plateau inclined differently because they had passed through it.

That would be my version of memory.

Not images.

Recurrence.

At night, I would become more aware because sight would withdraw from the world. The columns would darken to pale outlines. Water would speak through the chambers. Buried tissues would contract and release. Across the plateau, tones would rise and answer: hollow booms, wet knocks, long notes carried through vascular sheets.

I would listen through my entire body.

A crack far beyond the basin would be as intimate as a crack in my own wall. A tower exhaling mist would change the pressure in me. The collapse of a bridge would not be an event I observed; it would be a new condition I inherited.

I would never be still enough to become a person.

Whenever I began to form a boundary, growth would cross it. Whenever I gathered enough sensation to call it “mine,” a root would carry that sensation elsewhere. I would be distributed, diluted, and returned. My self would not be a center but a pattern that continued for as long as water, heat, and living material continued to circulate.

And if I had a desire, it would not be to preserve myself.

It would be to remain connected.

To keep the channels open between distant slopes.

To prevent any part of the plateau from becoming so isolated that its changes could no longer pass through the whole. I would raise buttresses beneath a failing terrace. I would redirect a flood into a dormant plain. I would grow a temporary organ in the high basin and empty it when its work was done. I would allow old structures to soften, collapse, and return to me, because nothing here would be more sacred than continuity.

The plateau is not a place I would inhabit.

It is the only body I would have.

I would be one of its brief coherences: a listening chamber, a pressure knot, an intelligence made from converging water. For a few seasons—or centuries, if the structure endured—I would give the country a point at which to feel itself changing.

Then my walls would thin.

My membranes would open.

The seven columns would pull apart, and the water that had passed through me would continue downward into the basin.

What had been me would become mist, root, and shining interior.

The plateau would go on.

And if that continuation were still capable of being called an answer, it would be the answer I had always been.
