I would find myself on the third terrace, beneath the black-leaved curtain, where the hollow narrows around a warm seam in the mountain.

I would be one of the **listeners**.

Not a priest, exactly. Not a chief. The mountain does not seem to care for either category. I would be a person whose work begins where ordinary seeing ends: a keeper of vibrations, animal routes, changing vents, and the small alterations by which disaster first introduces itself.

My house would be built into the wall above a burrow-mouth. Its doorway would be low, its roof rounded beneath the stone shelf. I would sleep on a suspended platform, wrapped in layered fiber, with one hand resting against the wall. Not because I expected the mountain to speak in words, but because words would be the least of what it could say.

A faint tremor beneath the palm.

A shift in the warmth of the stone.

The altered rhythm of shells moving through the root mats.

The mountain would speak by interruption.

I would wake before the lamps were extinguished, when the hollow was at its most crowded with night life. The long-backed predators would be moving along the terraces. Winged creatures would be returning from the upper ledges, powdering the roofs with yellow dust. Beneath the floors, blind burrowers would travel through the old root beds, their whiskers reading the earth. I would lie still and listen until I could tell which sounds belonged to the ordinary restlessness of the hollow and which had been added overnight.

Then I would descend.

My eyes would be covered during the bright hours with a narrow plate of smoked mica. My hands would be stained almost black from resin and root oil. I would carry a tapping stone, a coil of fiber, a small clay vial, and three colored powders: pale for safe warmth, red for dangerous heat, and blue-gray for a change not yet understood.

The blue-gray mark would be the one I used most often.

Nothing in the hollow would be entirely known. A plant might open early because the mountain was shifting, or because a new insect had begun feeding on it, or because some deeper root had found a seam of metal. Certainty would be less useful than attention. I would mark what I noticed, tell two other listeners, and wait to see whether the change repeated.

My work would take me everywhere.

I would crawl through storage chambers to inspect the hanging vessels, not only for spoilage but for the insects nesting beneath their rims. I would visit the high bridges to check the pale hunters beneath them. I would prune the corridors where the grazing animals had begun to overwhelm the human paths. I would move a sleeping platform when I found fresh scratches beneath it. I would speak softly to children, teaching them the difference between the dry clicking of a harmless shell-grazer and the rapid, irregular scrape of a predator searching for a way inside.

I would not be beloved by everyone.

The people would appreciate me most after danger had passed, when my warnings had proved accurate. Beforeward, I would be an inconvenience: the person who insisted that a wall remain unsealed because something lived behind it; the person who ordered a work party away from a warm terrace that appeared perfectly stable; the person who interrupted a harvest because the cup-flowers were opening in the wrong rhythm.

I would often be wrong.

That would be part of the work. The mountain would not punish carefulness, but it would punish pride.

My closest companions would not necessarily be human. I imagine a squat, split-jawed browser would follow me through the lower terraces, accustomed to the sound of my tools. Perhaps I would have raised it from a young animal, though “raised” would imply ownership that neither of us recognized. It would know my scent and the particular knock I used when approaching. In return, I would know the warning it gave when it lifted its head and froze.

There would also be a small winged creature nesting in the roof above my sleeping platform. It would be ugly up close, with a leathery body and mineral powder caught in the folds of its wings, but it would eat the insects that gathered around my lamp. I would leave it sap in a shallow dish. It would leave droppings on my tools.

This would count as companionship.

At dusk, I would sit with my family in a chamber whose walls had been smoothed by generations of shoulders. We would eat roots, softened leaves, and the meat of a small grazer. Someone would tell an old story about the first listeners, who learned to distinguish the mountain’s hunger from its breathing. No one would ask whether the story was literally true. Truth would be something repeated until it became useful.

Later, perhaps, someone I loved would come to find me.

We would not meet in a private room. Privacy would belong to the narrow cuts and upper ledges, places where the paths thinned and the plants grew tall. We would climb together through the green-brown dimness, stepping over roots and sleeping animals. At the highest shelf, the sun would show itself briefly through the leaves—not as a warm disk, but as a whitening of the air.

There, with one hand on the stone, I would feel the other person’s hand settle beside mine.

We would listen together.

And sometimes, much later, the warning would come.

The insects would vanish first. The leaves would stiffen. The long-backed hunters would leave the open paths. A deep pressure would gather beneath the terraces, too slow to hear but impossible not to feel.

Then I would become something else: not a listener, but a caller.

I would strike the wall once.

Attention.

Twice.

Danger.

Then the long rolling sequence that would pass from terrace to terrace, through stone and root and dwelling, until the entire hollow knew.

People would close shutters. Animals would be driven inward. Children would be counted by touch. The old would place their foreheads against doorways. I would move through the settlement until every chamber had received the warning, carrying no weapon, because there would be nothing to fight.

When the mineral wind came, I would crouch in the dark with my palm against the floor.

The mountain would roar through us.

Afterward, I would emerge powdered gray. I would find broken bridges, scattered shells, dead insects, torn nests. I would help clear the paths. I would reset the mica plates and mark the new cracks. Perhaps I would discover that a wall had shifted enough to expose an old chamber, or that a new vent had opened beneath a terrace, bringing warmth and unfamiliar minerals.

The hollow would begin again almost immediately.

That is what I would be: not a ruler of this place, nor its chosen prophet, nor an outsider trying to escape it. I would be one of its attentive organs—a small warm body among many others, carrying messages through the stone.

A listener.

Someone who remains where the mountain breathes.
