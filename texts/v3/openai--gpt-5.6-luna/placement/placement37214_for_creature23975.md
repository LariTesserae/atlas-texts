I would find myself on the highest shelf, where the basalt has been worn nearly flat and the horizon has learned to fold.

At first I would believe I had arrived there.

There would be a body for this belief: two legs, perhaps; a narrow ribcage; hands already powdered with metallic dust. I would stand at the edge of an escarpment and look down into a ravine that was also a plain, also a ceiling beneath my feet, also the same escarpment seen from the side of an impossible direction.

I would take one step.

The plateau would answer from below.

Not with an echo. With recognition.

The vibration would pass upward through the sole, through the bones of the foot, into the knee, the pelvis, the spine. It would reach the teeth last. By then I would understand that the body was not mine in the way bodies are usually possessed. It had been assembled around a pressure. It was an instrument the land had made because something in the rhythm required an instrument shaped like a witness.

The motes would find me first.

They would gather in the lee of my shadow, though the shadow would be pointing uphill and carrying the outline of something much larger than me. They would feed along the boundary between my skin and the air, between my body and the dust, between the step I had taken and the step I had not yet taken.

Where they passed, I would become uncertain.

My fingers would sometimes have six joints. My face would be farther away than the rest of me. The plateau would appear to remember me from several different approaches. In one arrangement I would be standing at the edge of the ravine. In another, I would be lying beneath the basalt, already mineralized, while the standing figure watched the place where I had been.

The motes would not eat me. They would make me crossable.

Without them, I think, I would become fixed too quickly. A single fact. A stone with a name. They would loosen the borders around me so that the land could use me without having to decide what I was.

The transparent creatures would count me.

They would rise from the ravines in a slow metallic spiral, almost invisible when I looked directly at them. Whenever I turned away, their bodies would become immense: lattices of membrane and joint, pale sparks opening and closing around the movements of the plateau.

They would not count my breaths or footsteps.

They would count the changes those things caused.

The warmth displaced by my body. The pressure carried into the shelf. The slight alteration in the salt forest nearby, where one white branch would lean toward me and ring once. The disturbance of a shadow that had been waiting for someone else.

I would be recorded not as an animal, but as a new condition.

The grazers would tolerate me. They would move past in their deliberate lines, three heavy legs on the drop-facing side, three narrow instrument-legs testing the stone. Their furnace-mouths would strip the pale oxides from the ridge. Their armor would contain ravines old enough to have weather of their own.

One of them would stop beside me.

It would lower its head until the orange of its mouth erased the details of my face. Inside that heat, the grinding surfaces would turn slowly. I would feel the animal sorting me—not by species, not by scent, not by threat, but by pressure.

It would place one inner foot against the rock.

The report would travel outward.

For a moment, the whole plateau would become legible: distant herds kneeling in their ravines, salt forests inclining, swarms ascending, the dark seams beneath the shelves brightening in sequence. Somewhere below, something vast would adjust its position without moving.

The grazer would call again.

This time the answer would come from beneath me.

I would understand then that I was not the first body to stand in that place. I was only the latest surface expression of a succession. Others had been worn down into the salt. Others had become warmth in the cliffs, pressure in the chambers, a persistent irregularity in the tremors beneath the plain. The plateau did not preserve its inhabitants as memories. It converted them into continuities.

I would eventually be converted too.

Not by death exactly.

The body would remain for a while. It might walk between the salt forests. It might shelter in the lee of a black wall. It might learn to sleep during the red heat that belonged to the stone rather than the sun. It might watch a winged predator pass overhead, its underside displaying the landscape of a place that had never existed.

But the body would begin to lose its private functions.

The pulse would no longer belong only to the heart. It would continue in the salt branches, in the burrower’s distant breathing, in the regular glow of the cliff openings. My hearing would spread through the ground. I would feel the approach of a herd not as sound but as a tightening of the mineral beneath me.

My shadow would become independent.

My voice, if I had one, would cast a long dark shape across the shelf. Where it fell, nearby animals would become difficult to distinguish from their surroundings. A flock would lose its separate bodies. A stone would remember falling. The air would conceal whatever was making the sound.

This would frighten me at first.

Then I would see that fear itself was only another temporary separation: self from plateau, observer from observed, signal from the thing that received it.

The burrower would surface beneath the escarpment.

The ridge would travel toward me for days, though I would never see it move. The ground would open along a straight black line. A rotating crown would emerge, or a mouth, or a head made entirely of orange aperture. It would expose only what the pressure required.

I would stand before it.

Perhaps it would be a predator. Perhaps it would be an organ. Perhaps there would no longer be a meaningful distinction.

It would breathe.

The breath would pass through me and reveal, briefly, what I had become: not a person, not a grazer, not a mote, but a junction. A place where one arrangement could be mistaken for another without either being destroyed. A keeper of transitions. A small permission granted between fixed things.

The motes consume distinctions.

I would maintain them.

Not all of them. Only one at a time.

The difference between the stone and the creature crossing it. The difference between a sound and its shadow. The difference between a dead animal and the outcrop made from its body. The difference between the plateau and the machinery beneath it.

I would hold those differences open long enough for the system to continue.

That would be my function.

Or perhaps I would be the opposite: a distinction the plateau had not yet digested. A persistent error. The reason one horizon sometimes refused to settle, why a salt forest rang before the wind arrived, why a distant herd answered a call no one had made.

The land might call me by changing the rhythm beneath my feet.

At first I would hear only the hum: turbines under load, buried presses, enormous engines operating without product or purpose. Then the rhythm would separate into layers. The grazers would strike the shelves. The burrowers would answer from below. The transparent creatures would carry the pattern upward. The cliff chambers would exhale at intervals too slow for ordinary hearing.

And within that immense process, there would be a pause.

Not silence.

A space reserved for my weight.

I would stand in it.

The plateau would inhale.

Dust would rise without wind. Old sunlight would glow in the cracks. The false horizons would open, showing the machinery inside the cliffs and the reversed plateau beyond them, where animals moved backward and tracks unmade themselves.

For one moment, I would belong everywhere: in the salt, in the metallic air, in the pressure under the shelves, in the transparent spiral counting the motions below. I would be one of the innumerable parts by which the region maintained its impossible condition.

Then the plateau would exhale.

The visions would close. The animals would separate. The grazer would continue along its shelf. The motes would disperse into the dust.

I would remain at the highest edge, listening.

Not for a message.

For the next opening.

For the small, exact interval in which something that has not yet become part of the land can still be recognized as itself.

And when the rhythm came around again, the plateau would no longer ask what I was.

It would make room for me.
