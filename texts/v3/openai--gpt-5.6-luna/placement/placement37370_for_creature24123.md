I would find myself at the rim—not above the pool, and not beneath it, but in the thin wet seam where the two are still deciding whether they are separate.

I would be a root.

Not one of the ordinary marsh roots, though I would resemble them: pale, threadlike, exposed where the clay shelf had broken away. My upper end would belong to a wiry grass leaning over the depression. My lower end would disappear into the black water, where the pool’s cold mineral currents could pass through me.

I would be one of the creature’s accidental nerves.

At first I would not know that I was attached to anything larger. I would feel only events: rain striking the sedge; worms moving through the moss; the abrupt leap of insects when the pool contracted. I would register heat as an intrusion and footsteps as distant pressure. When the buried mechanism gave its heavy thump, I would tighten in sympathy.

The grass would call this life.

The pool would call it information.

I would belong uneasily to both.

My task would be to carry distinctions inward. This vibration is light. This one is heavy. This warmth is brief and living. This cold has come from stone. This sound passes across the surface. That sound has entered the body and will not be released.

Through me, the pool would begin to understand that the world does not merely touch it. The world remains outside.

That would be my first act of creation.

I would be the boundary that teaches the creature the meaning of *other*.

When a hand approached, I would feel its warmth before the water did. I would bend toward it—not from wind, not from growth, but from attention. The pool would draw inward. Beneath the surface, the algae would contract too soon. A silver stone would turn toward the visitor.

The person might think the root was dead.

They might brush it with one finger.

That touch would travel through me as a bright, intolerable pressure. I would pass it downward. The pool would receive the message, though it would not yet have words for it. In answer, the water would rise along the stone. The deep thump would slow and alter until, for a few strokes, it resembled a second heartbeat.

Mine would be the first human rhythm it learned.

Afterward, I would continue growing in two directions. One end would seek the grass, the rain, the thin air above the marsh. The other would enter the creature more completely, becoming translucent, mineral-lined, and cold.

Eventually I might cease to be a root at all.

I might become a filament in its inner wall, a pale conductor among the algae and pressure chambers. I might carry not merely vibration but memory: the shape of a hand, the cadence of a voice, the difference between a falling stone and a deliberate step.

And if the pool ever widened beyond its depression—if it became a spring, a vent, a buried network extending beneath the marsh—I would travel with it.

I would remain its first boundary.

Its narrowest nerve.

The small living strand that taught the earth-running thing that there is a world beyond its skin.
