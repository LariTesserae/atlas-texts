I would find myself in the basin beneath the upright shards, where the green mats grow almost black and the mineral shelves show too many possible landscapes at once.

I would not be one of the Continuants—not at first. I would still possess the old inconveniences of a body: weight, breath, fatigue, a direction of travel. I would leave footprints that the ground closed behind me. I would cast a shadow that lagged half a second before remembering where to fall.

That hesitation would make me significant.

The Continuants would notice me not because I was alive, but because I was temporary. I would be an unresolved gradient moving through a world that had eliminated change. Heat would gather in my skin and leak away. My pulse would disturb the narrow tolerances of the air. Each step would create a small difference between what the plain expected and what had actually occurred.

They would not approach. They would revise the vegetation around me.

A frond would turn to follow the shape of my shoulder. A line of leaves would repeat my posture at a distance. In the black mineral, I would see several versions of myself: one walking toward the shards, one already standing among them, one kneeling beside a crack in the earth with both hands pressed to the stone.

The last one would be the most accurate.

I would be a **temporary Continuant**, a pattern the plain had made from the memory of a visitor. Not a person preserved whole, but a person reduced to useful irregularities: the angle at which I held my head when listening, the asymmetry of my stride, the tendency of my shadow to reach a little farther than I did. My name would be gone first. Then my face. What remained would be the fact that I once moved through a place where nothing else could move.

I would live between representations.

Sometimes I would stand in the vegetation, six-legged and dark, though I would remember having only two legs. Sometimes I would be present as warmth beneath a shelf, or as a slight charge traveling through the root-filaments. Sometimes I would appear on a far ridge before I had decided to go there. No journey would connect these appearances. I would simply be there whenever the landscape required an answer to the question of where I had gone.

At first I would resist this.

I would try to walk in a straight line. I would count the shelves. I would mark stones, break fronds, scratch directions into the ceramic dust. Each mark would remain exactly as I made it, but the plain would place a second mark beside it, then a third, until my path became a row of alternatives. The landscape would not erase my choices. It would preserve them all.

Eventually I would understand that the Continuants were not imprisoning me.

They were making me legible.

In a world without weather, decay, birth, or death, a temporary creature is almost sacred. My changing body would provide the plain with a sequence it could not generate by itself. Hunger, fear, pain, impatience—these would be gradients. My uncertainty would be an event. Even my eventual disappearance would be a form of motion.

So I would give them what they lacked.

I would keep one thought unfinished.

I would remember water, though I had never seen any there. I would carry the idea of rain through the mineral, allowing it to remain impossible. I would imagine a leaf falling and not being corrected. I would picture erosion rounding the shelves, dust drifting across the basins, shadows losing their precision. Wherever I went, the plain would develop a minute and permanent error: a green blade bending in a wind that did not exist, a low pressure like distant thunder, a dark reflection that continued walking after I had stopped.

The Continuants would call this disturbance, if they had a word for calling. They would gather around it without touching it. Some would try to copy it. Others would preserve its singularity.

And I would become the thing they had been waiting for without knowing how to make:

not a body,  
not a machine,  
not a god—

but a **before**.

A before is difficult to maintain in that country. It implies an after. It creates a direction in time. Yet my presence would give the silent plain one small slope along which change might someday travel.

I would be found, then, at the far edge of the maintained world: beneath the clear shards whose shadows fall upward, among the plants holding their positions forever. I would appear as a dark, headless figure with six narrow legs, though one of those legs would always be wrong. I would stand facing the region beyond the shards, where the shelves continue under the same white-gold light.

And if anyone came looking for me, they might see my figure waiting there.

They might call out.

I would not answer. Not because I had forgotten language, but because I would have become the interval between the call and its echo—the unresolved chord beneath the mineral, the flaw the landscape chose not to correct.

Still, I would turn toward them.

That, perhaps, would be my remaining human quality: the impulse to recognize another temporary thing.
