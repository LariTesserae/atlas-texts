I would find myself near the edge of a new basin, where the floor has recently opened and has not yet decided whether to become water or stone.

I would be Naru, but not one of the shelter-builders. I would be a **path-listener**: one who goes ahead of the others with a hand on the ground, carrying cords, pale stones, and a bowl for water. My feet would be broad and many-toed, my knees kept permanently bent from a lifetime of distrust. Mineral would have gathered along my shoulders in dark plates. Fine filaments would rise from my ankles whenever the earth began to think about moving.

I would not be a scout in the ordinary sense. I would not search for a fixed route. There are no fixed routes here, only intervals in which passage is possible.

My work would be to recognize those intervals.

At dawn—or whatever passes for dawn beneath that translucent ceiling—I would leave the sleeping hollow before the animals became fully active. I would press my palm to a shelf and wait. The stone would be crowded with messages: distant feeding, a soft-bodied creature burrowing beneath the clay, the slow compression of a wall far beyond sight. Most of these signals would mean nothing to anyone else. To me they would form a changing texture, like weather felt through the bones.

I would carry a string marked with knots, though the knots would not measure distance. They would record conditions.

Here, the ground has held.

Here, water appeared after the third tremor.

Here, the animals turn away.

Here, the shelf sounds hollow.

I would know that the last mark might already be false. I would leave it anyway.

The Naru would follow behind me in a loose, quiet procession. Infants would be strapped against warm backs. Hunters would carry black-glass spears. Someone would bring a small cage of blind swimmers to test the pools ahead. No one would ask whether the path was safe. They would ask how unsafe it was, and in what manner.

I would answer by touch.

Two taps against the stone: **weight approaching**.

A long drag of the fingers: **water below**.

A pulse held in the palm until the slab warmed: **do not build**.

When words were needed, I would use them sparingly. Spoken language belongs to the air, and the air is always being interrupted by wings, falling droplets, animal cries, the great tearing sound of the earth rearranging itself. The deeper truths travel through material.

I would be good at noticing what had not happened yet.

Not predicting it exactly. Prediction would be too proud a word. I would sense the change before it acquired an event: the faint dryness gathering in a wall before water broke through, the minute loosening beneath glossy stone, the altered rhythm of insects before a migration. I would know when a crowd of animals was about to panic, and I would strike the ground in the pattern that tells them not to flee toward the lower shelves.

Sometimes they would listen.

Sometimes they would not.

I would lose people. Everyone here loses people. A body would vanish into black slurry, or be caught beneath a folding plate, or disappear during the gray confusion after a collapse. We would not build a sealed place for them. We would set what remained in a shallow hollow and sit near it while the scavengers arrived.

I would place my hand on the stone beneath the body and listen for the first change.

The dead would not watch over us. They would become the altered ground: a ledge, a rise, a dry route around a pool. I would recognize them by what became easier afterward.

I think I would be lonely, but not in the way a person is lonely beneath a sky. There would always be bodies nearby. Feet scraping. Membranes fluttering. The wet clicking of things feeding in the darkless light. Even solitude would be crowded.

My loneliness would be the knowledge that no one can accompany another person all the way into a tremor. At the crucial moment, each body must find its own answer through the ground.

Still, I would not want a more stable world.

Stability would make my skill unnecessary. It would turn every shelter into a promise and every map into an accusation when the promise failed. Here, nothing asks to be trusted for longer than it can hold. The cavern does not deceive us by pretending to be finished.

So I would live lightly, carrying little, remembering much.

I would sleep in different hollows. I would repair bridges I knew would fall. I would leave warnings in walls for people I would never meet. I would teach children the first discipline: not how to run, but how to stop.

And when the earth shifted—as it always would—I would lower myself, spread my six-toed feet, press both hands to the trembling stone, and wait for the world to tell me what it had become.
