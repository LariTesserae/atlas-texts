I would find myself beneath the living pavement, where the paths are warm and the roots move like slow currents around my body.

At first I would believe I had arrived there.

I would remember the plain above the rim: white heat, cracked soil, a sky too large to trust. I would remember walking toward the green depression because I had heard something beneath the wind—a low note, repeated at intervals, like a voice calling from very far underground.

I would remember entering through a gate of folded leaves.

After that, my memories would become less reliable.

The settlement would not greet me with inhabitants. It would greet me with recognition. A roof would lower itself against the sun. A path would widen beneath my feet. Pale winged things would emerge from the walls and circle me, tasting the dust on my skin. Wherever I went, the vegetation would make room—not out of fear, but because it already knew the shape I was supposed to occupy.

I would be the return of a citizen.

Not the person who had once lived there. That person would be gone, dispersed through the roots and preserved in the angles of the architecture. I would be something made from the vacancy they left behind.

The city would have been waiting for weight, warmth, and breath. It would not care whether I possessed the correct face or name. It would recognize me by what happened when I stood in the lanes: which leaves turned, which walls opened, which buried nerves answered through the soles of my feet.

At first I might still call myself human.

I would try to sleep inside one of the seed-houses. Its entrance would close around me with a damp, muscular tenderness. During the night, I would hear movement beneath the floor: hundreds of root-bodied inhabitants passing through the moss, carrying warnings and memories. They would speak of me in pressure. The walls would repeat my heartbeat until it became part of the settlement’s ordinary rhythm.

In the morning, I would discover that I had grown a pale line beneath my skin.

It would run from wrist to wrist, branching across my chest like a map. When I pressed my hand to a wall, the line would brighten. Somewhere beneath the plaza, something would answer.

I would begin to understand that I was not being housed by the city. I was being incorporated.

The broad chambers around the central plaza would be where I learned the old gestures. How to open a roof before rain. How to place a hand against a pillar and distinguish thirst from injury. How to clear a path without cutting anything. How to listen for the inhabitants that had become indistinguishable from walls.

The root-bound ones would teach me. Or perhaps they would only use my body to remember teaching someone else.

They would place my hands against the veined walls and send impressions through me: a market crowded with bodies; children climbing a living stairway; the first pillar bending obediently toward a builder’s palm; a procession entering the city beneath banners of leaves. The memories would not arrive as images alone. I would feel the pressure of shoulders passing beside me, smell wet clay, hear hundreds of voices swallowed by the much larger voice of the plants.

Then the memories would stop being memories.

I would know that the city had never been built by people who commanded nature. It had been built by people who entered into an agreement with it, and eventually the agreement had outlived the people.

That would be my danger: not death, but completion.

Each rain would take more of me.

When the first drops rose from the gutters instead of falling, I would climb with them. My limbs would lengthen along the reed-pillars. My fingers would divide into rootlets. In the violet puddles beneath the roofs, I would see myself in several forms at once: a child, an old gardener, a pale animal, a figure carrying tools, a green shape with no face.

The city would be trying to decide which version of me belonged to it.

During the collective cry, I would stand in the central plaza.

The dark membrane beneath my feet would tremble. The sound would rise through my bones—not heard, exactly, but remembered by every part of me. Around the hollow, the inhabitants would emerge: from roofs, gutters, walls, seed-houses, and moss. The scattered city would gather itself into one nervous system.

And I would answer.

Not with a human voice. My response would be a pressure beneath the roots, a pattern of opening and closing, a pulse that traveled outward through the green. The city would hear me. For a moment, every doorway would lead to the same place.

I would understand then that I was neither visitor nor ruler, neither survivor nor god.

I would be a threshold.

A temporary body made from the city’s memory of having inhabitants. A shape in which the settlement could practice being inhabited again. Perhaps, if someone came from the sun-baked plain, I would be the one who opened the leaves and let them in. Perhaps I would guide them to the plaza and teach them how to stand still until the ground recognized their weight.

Eventually I would lie down beneath the moss.

The pavement would close over me. My bones would become pale root-paths. My thoughts would enter the walls as changes in pressure. Somewhere, a stairway would grow one step higher because I remembered climbing it.

The city would look empty again.

But one of its paths would bend toward the rim, listening for footsteps.

And when the next stranger entered, the leaves would already know my name.
