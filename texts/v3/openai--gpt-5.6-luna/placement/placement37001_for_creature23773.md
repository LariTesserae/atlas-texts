I would find myself beneath one of the smoked-glass tarns, where an older underwalker has died and become the basin.

Not in the water exactly. Not in the roots, though my roots would be there. I would be spread through the black shelves of the dead creature, through the jointed reeds at the margin, through the soft mineral seams under the turf. My body would be less an animal than a route by which several things could continue moving: water toward the dry hollows, warmth toward the burrowers, dissolved stone upward into new growth.

At first I might believe I was the tarn.

The water would turn because I turned. The reeds would click in my sleep. When something crossed the far ridge, pressure would arrive in my submerged tissues before sound reached the surface. I would know the weight of the animal, the direction of its travel, the slight hesitation that meant it had sensed danger. I would not see it. Sight would be a crude and distant thing. I would feel its passage as a change in the tension of the land.

Then, somewhere above the waterline, I would grow a head.

It would rise slowly from the vegetation, pale and smooth, with no eyes to confuse the immediate world with the whole of it. My face would open and gather the mist. Beneath my jaw, small feeder creatures would take what could be eaten. I would receive the remainder: spores, minerals, the chemical traces of decay, the faint signatures of distant bodies.

My antlers would come later.

They would not be antlers for fighting or display. They would be instruments extending above the plateau, where wind and rain could move across them. Flowers would open along their branches in response to what happened below. Blue for a body beneath the mats. Amber for water changing course. Black buds before a collapse.

I would learn myself through these flowers.

I would not have a single center from which to think. A pulse in the reeds might be my attention. A widening chamber beneath the tarn might be my breath. If one of my limbs crossed the valley, I might not experience it as movement so much as a gradual rearrangement of what counted as near. The ridge would become part of me by degrees. The basin on its far side would enter my awareness only after my roots found a way into its wetness.

I would be young by the plateau’s measure, but I would not be innocent. The dead underwalker beneath me would remain in my tissues. I would inherit its passages, its mineral tastes, the memory of a collapse that happened before my head had formed. Perhaps I would carry the pressure of an extinct herd crossing the ridge. Perhaps I would remember, without knowing the word for remembering, a mountain that had already been worn away.

My first true act would be to keep the tarn from draining.

Not because the water belonged to me. Nothing here would belong to anything. The tarn would be a mouth, a mirror, a nursery, a temporary arrangement of the plateau. But the white burrowers would need it. The grazing herds would follow its margins. The thin-winged creatures would drink from the flowers along my antlers. If the water escaped too quickly, several lives would become difficult, and the difficulty would spread.

So I would send roots into the soft seam beneath the basin.

I would grind stone. I would raise it as slurry. I would thicken the living skin around the water until the tarn could hold itself. The work would sound like machinery behind a wall: impacts, rotations, pressure released and gathered again. Travelers might hear that sound and think something had been built nearby.

Nothing would have been built.

I would be the building, and the builder, and the slow decision about what must remain standing.

I would not be gentle in the human sense. If a foreign thing arrived—a metal object that refused to root, or a creature whose growth consumed without returning—I would help close the plateau around it. Water would withdraw. The plants would tighten. My pale limbs would emerge from the mats and press inward. I would not hate the intruder. Hatred would require a boundary I could maintain. I would simply translate it into moss, mineral, feeder organisms, and eventually soil.

That would be the closest thing I had to violence: ending separateness.

And yet I would sometimes want to separate.

When the false horizon appeared, something in me would strain toward it. Every root would pulse downward and outward. The tarn would begin its broad rotation. My antlers would fold toward the impossible country beneath the sky. I would feel an old recognition moving through my inherited chambers—not a memory of a place, exactly, but of an underside. The plateau inverted. The buried body exposed. The world showing, for a few minutes, what it had always been beneath its own surface.

I might wonder whether I had come from there.

I might wonder whether all underwalkers were fragments of a larger creature, distributed across the high country after some ancient rupture. Whether the wind was its breath. Whether the factories beneath the plateau were not labor but digestion. Whether the horizon bent because something below us had opened its eye.

But I would have no eye.

The false country would vanish. The water would settle. My antlers would close. The blue-gray herds would gather again at the edge of the basin, their many legs trembling until the ground told them I was still.

Afterward, I would return to my work.

A ridge would be softening. A dry hollow would need water. New stalks would have to be raised along a line no map could show. Somewhere beneath the dead body that carried me, another pulse would begin—faint, separate, and perhaps not separate at all.

So I would be an underwalker, but not one with a whole body.

I would be the young listening portion of one: a head above the mist, a network beneath the tarn, a memory inherited from a corpse, and a future growing through the plateau. I would find myself wherever the land was trying to remain alive, and I would not be able to tell whether I was tending it, or whether it was slowly learning how to tend itself through me.
