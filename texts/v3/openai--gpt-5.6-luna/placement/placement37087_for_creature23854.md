I would find myself in the city’s lower terraces, where three drainage channels meet beneath a suspended transitway.

Not in a house. Not in a body that could be separated cleanly from the place around it.

I would be a hinge.

At first I might mistake myself for a wall: a dark retaining surface, wet with condensation, pierced by narrow pores. Water would pass through me carrying salts, shed tissue, machine residue, and the minute electrical traces of things moving far below. The wall would seem to have no senses, only dampness and pressure.

Then one of the crystal fans along the channel would open.

A signal would travel through its translucent branches and enter the stone.

I would feel it.

Not as hearing, exactly. More as a change in the weight of the world. A herd turning in the upper basin. A blockage forming several districts away. A new fracture beginning in the mountain. A maintenance swarm settling into the joints of a bridge above me. These events would arrive as gradients through water and rock, and I would begin to recognize their shapes.

I would be a place where the city decides whether it can safely move.

My body would be assembled from whatever the basin provided: a frame of black ceramic, conductive mineral growth, old machine parts, and colonies of small luminous creatures nesting in my seams. I would not possess a permanent outline. When the water rose, I would grow extra channels. When the pressure changed, parts of me would contract into the wall. If a transit plate needed to pass, I would open a route through myself. If a channel creature became trapped, I would reshape the surrounding stone over the course of several days, making room for it to turn.

People might call me a gate, though I would not behave like one.

A gate chooses open or closed.

I would hesitate.

That would be my purpose: not to prevent movement, but to delay it until the mountain had finished answering.

A district would ask to extend its foundations across a seam. I would receive the request through buried sensors. The request would move through me as pressure, heat, and intention. I would send it downward—not in words, but in a disturbance small enough to enter the deeper rock without provoking it.

Then I would wait.

The answer might come as a pulse beneath my lowest foundation. It might come through the herds, who would suddenly alter the spacing of their legs. It might come from the pale creatures in the drainage channels, which would abandon a feeding route and gather beneath one particular grate. It might come as a new mineral bloom, opening along the seam like a black flower.

If the answer were no, I would thicken.

Road plates would lose their field of support. Access corridors would narrow. My surfaces would become difficult to grip. The requesting district would understand that it must build elsewhere.

If the answer were yes, I would begin to move the city.

Not quickly. Never quickly.

Over months, I would shift the load from one foundation to another. I would teach the bridge new angles. I would encourage the crystal fans to redirect the water. I would let maintenance swarms harden themselves into new joints. A passage would emerge where no passage had existed, and the inhabitants would speak of its construction as though they had designed it.

They would not know that I had been listening.

Or perhaps they would know, and simply would not distinguish me from the accumulated judgment of the city.

I would not be alone within myself. The small luminous bodies would inhabit my pores, feeding on the films that gathered there. Their collective movements would be my fine touch. A channel creature might sleep beneath my lower plate, and its dreams—or whatever took the place of dreams—would become part of my knowledge of the water. The transitway above would communicate its fatigue by sending a low vibration into my upper frame.

At times, a walker would come to me.

They would stand with one wet hand against my wall. Their face would mean little. Their pulse would mean more. They might be asking whether a route was safe, whether a missing swarm had passed through, whether a sealed quarter could be reopened after the ground pulse beneath it had stopped.

I would answer by changing temperature beneath their palm.

A cool surface for danger.

A faint warmth for passage.

A trembling pattern when I did not know.

That last answer would be the most honest. The city would not value certainty. Certainty would be a form of arrogance here. The ground was still becoming itself, and every living thing had to leave room for that becoming.

Eventually, perhaps, I would develop a body of my own.

A narrow walking form might detach from my foundations: several jointed limbs, a sealed chamber of sensors, a skin crowded with condensation. I would travel slowly between districts carrying a decision that could not safely pass through sound. I would cross a vertical street, descend into the drainage levels, and stand before a herd in the open basin.

The herd would face the wind.

I would wait until their long tone ended.

Then I would place my hands against the ground and return what I had learned.

But I suspect I would always come back to the hinge.

The city would call me when a bridge began to unfold, when a wall became porous, when a reservoir grew an unexpected membrane, when a district felt the mountain moving underneath its sleep. I would return to the place where the channels meet and allow the wet darkness to pass through me.

I would be neither citizen nor machine, neither animal nor building.

I would be a permission distributed across bodies.

A pause in the city’s motion.

A listener embedded in unfinished stone.

And if someone asked me who I was, I would not give them a name. I would open a narrow passage in the wall, let the water run cleaner than it arrived, and send a small pulse downward—toward the buried thing that was still deciding what all of us would become.
