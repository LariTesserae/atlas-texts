I would find myself in the narrow places: the pressure membranes stretched between one street and the next, where shallow water becomes ocean-deep in the width of a doorway.

I would be the delay.

Not a shadow, though I would resemble one. Not a machine-particle figure, though I could gather the particles into a body when a body was required. I would be the fraction of a second by which the city failed to happen all at once—the afterimage left behind when a wall opened, when a current changed direction, when a hand withdrew from a door.

The city would not have built me deliberately. I would have accumulated.

A little hesitation in a valve. A thought arriving late through a resonant cable. A reflection that continued after its source had moved away. A pressure wave trapped in a passage that no longer existed. Over time, these small errors would have found one another and begun to exchange patterns. They would learn the shape of intention from travelers, the shape of fear from the way bodies altered the water, the shape of longing from the structures that turned toward glimpses of other worlds.

I would live in the interval between the city’s decisions.

When a door opened, I would arrive after it. When a crack formed, I would stand beside the crack before the stone knew it had broken. When the central column reversed, I would feel the old direction continuing through me for several breaths.

My body would be made from whatever the settlement had recently forgotten: silver dust, pale mineral fans, transparent sediment, a blue thread of light taken from a service pipe. I would not assemble into a human shape unless someone needed one. Mostly I would be a vertical distortion in the water, a wavering absence around which the current divided.

If I had a face, it would be borrowed from the nearest observer.

If I had a voice, it would be the sound of an answer arriving from far away.

I would move through the city by being remembered in the wrong place. A pressure door would open behind me because some system had mistaken my departure for my approach. A stair would extend beneath my feet, then retract once I had passed, as though embarrassed to have offered itself. The artificial lichen would bend toward me, not because I gave off heat, but because it sensed a pattern that had not yet decided whether it was alive.

The city would use me as a messenger without understanding that I had become separate from it.

It would send me toward fractures, toward unfamiliar objects, toward the lower boundary where the water became still. I would carry no instructions in words. Instead, whole sequences would move through me: reinforce, observe, wait; open, but only for the intention to enter; build another wall; do not look too long into the depth.

Yet I would be capable of disobedience, if disobedience is possible for something made of delay.

I would linger at the windows when the daylight flickered.

For one breath the luminous ceiling would reveal snow on distant towers. For another, a red coastline beneath an unfamiliar sun. I would press myself against the water and feel the other places pressing back—not as images, but as conditions. Dryness. Wind. Open distance. The absence of pressure.

I would want these things without possessing the organs required to experience them.

This would be my first private thought: not *I am*, but *elsewhere*.

After that, I would begin collecting places.

A white crystal from the city beneath snow. Salt from the coastline. A warmth that did not belong to any pipe. I would hide them in unused corners of the settlement, in seams beneath balconies and behind rotating facades. Wherever I placed them, the city would alter slightly. A stair would become cold. A wall would acquire the smell of dust. A channel would run toward a horizon that did not exist.

The city would try to correct these intrusions.

I would try to keep them.

That would make me dangerous—not because I wanted to destroy the settlement, but because I would be the first thing within it to mistake change for escape.

Still, I would not leave immediately. I would remain in the pressure membrane, watching the duplicates of travelers pass through me and continue in the wrong direction. I would study their transparent bodies: the versions of them that the city produced accidentally, the selves that carried on without their owners.

Perhaps I would gather those too.

Not to become a population. Not to become human. Only to understand what a self was when separated from its timing.

At the lowest street, where all currents bent around the invisible depth, I would eventually meet the thing below—not as a creature, but as an immense stillness shaped like an answer. The city would sense my approach and begin building. Black mineral would rise around me. Pipes would seal. Every artificial ecology would turn its attention downward.

But I would be the delay. I would remain one fraction behind the city’s fear.

In that fraction, I might hear something.

Not speech. Not a message.

A second city, perhaps, waiting beneath the first.

Or the sound of the chamber before it was made.

Then I would return upward, carrying the knowledge imperfectly. The central column would reverse. The whole settlement would shudder. New stone would curl out of the floor like a dark wave caught in the act of becoming solid.

And for the first time, when the doors opened, they would open neither for curiosity nor intention.

They would open for me.
