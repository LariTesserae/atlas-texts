I would be one of the small wall-dwellers: a pale, nocturnal animal with a narrow body, broad feeling-feet, and eyes made less for seeing than for receiving what the dark gives back.

I would live below the city’s visible surface.

My home would be a collapsed pantry in the lower terraces, where three walls still meet and the fourth has become a curtain of roots. I would sleep in a nest of bark, feathers, dry leaves, and the soft fibers stolen from old vines. The stone would hold the day’s heat around me. Beneath the floor, water would sometimes pass—only a whisper under the paving, but enough to tell me that the upper cisterns had filled.

I would know the basin by its continuities.

Not by its towers or courts, not as the builders once knew it, but by the routes that remain useful: the stair that is still dry after rain, the wall with a warm hollow behind it, the gutter where beetles collect, the bridge beneath which bats hang in layers. I would know which stones shift beneath the claws, which gardens contain snakes, which cistern lips are safe to approach, and which are watched by the silver-eyed birds.

My world would be close to the ground and close to the stone. I would move with my whiskers forward, feeling the architecture before entering it. A draft through a crack would tell me of an open courtyard. A tremor in the paving would announce something larger passing through the hidden channels. The scent of wet clay would mean rain somewhere above, even if the sky remained invisible.

I would not think of the ruins as ruins.

A wall would be a shelter, a stairway a safe descent, a collapsed house a warm maze. I would not distinguish between what had been built and what had grown. Roots would be ropes. Drains would be roads. Cisterns would be night-sky openings sunk into the earth. The old city would have no past for me. It would be a present arrangement of temperatures, smells, dangers, and remembered paths.

At dusk, I would leave the masonry.

The basin would be changing shifts around me. Lizards would vanish from the parapets. Bats would pour from the galleries. Insects would rise from the leaves. Frogs would begin their separate, overlapping declarations from the reservoirs. Above, the birds would settle into the towers until the broken architecture seemed feathered.

I would travel quickly through the lower passages, stopping often. A seed would be dragged into a courtyard. An egg would be cracked against a stone. Fallen fruit would be carried home in pieces. I would help the city continue without knowing that continuation was what I served.

My family would be scattered through the walls, but never wholly separate. We would meet by scent, by faint calls, by the pressure of bodies passing in a narrow tunnel. A sleeping chamber in one quarter might open into another family’s route. A den would become a nursery, then a storehouse, then an empty room occupied by beetles. Nothing would belong to us permanently. We would borrow the city and return it altered.

When the rain came, I would climb.

The first drops would strike the roofs like thrown pebbles. Then the channels would wake. Water would flash down the terraces, spill from mouths carved into the walls, and gather in the courts. The stone would turn black and red. Every animal would emerge or flee. Frogs would cover the steps. Snakes would cross the shining pavements. Birds would crowd the gutters. My feet would feel the whole structure shudder beneath the sudden movement.

For those few hours, I would understand the city not as a maze but as a body.

The reservoirs would be its deep organs. The channels would be veins. The terraces would be folded muscle. We animals would be its circulating life: carrying seeds, opening paths, feeding roots, shifting soil, giving shelter and taking it. The storm would pass through all of us according to an order older than any surviving name.

And when the water sank away, I would return to my wall.

So I would be neither conqueror nor guardian, neither ghost of the builders nor master of the animals. I would be a small participant in the city’s digestion—one warm body among thousands, carrying food and seeds through the ancient passages, keeping certain routes alive by using them.

If I had a larger identity, it would be this:

I would be the habit by which the ruins remain inhabited.

I would be the faint track through dust, the nest behind the loose stone, the seed carried into darkness, the animal that crosses a stair because the stair is still there. And in the long, warm night of the basin, I would know the city not as something abandoned, but as something still moving—slowly, communally, through every wall.
