I would find myself at the seam.

Not in the open streets, where the wall-scrapers travel in their appointed lines, nor deep inside the furnaces where the heat-feeders lie folded around the iron. I would live where the basin’s black stone meets the industrial wall on its fourth side: in the narrow, inaccessible space between foundation and cliff, where condensation gathers but does not fall, where the pressure of the machinery enters the rock and returns diminished.

At first I might seem to be a colony of pale threads.

They would extend from the wall into the pipes, from the pipes into the stone, from the stone into the lower channels. Some lengths of me would be visible beneath grilles, flattened and translucent. Other lengths would be mineral: a cool ridge beneath the floor, a chalky seam around a bolt, a pale deposit running upward through the masonry. If one part of me were cut away, the rest would not feel the loss as an injury. It would register it as an alteration in rhythm.

I would be a listener.

Not one of the creatures that opens its membranes toward the presses and glows with the remainder of their sound, but something more deeply embedded in the noise. I would know the basin through its continuities: the exact interval between pump-strokes, the delay between rain touching the upper gantries and moisture appearing in the lower walls, the pressure at which a cooling pipe begins to sweat, the minute change in pitch that means a bearing has become dry.

Nothing here breaks suddenly. A thing becomes wrong by degrees too small for any single body to notice.

That would be my function.

I would feel the flywheel’s rotation as a slow turning beneath my length. I would distinguish the vibration of a living bridge-creature from the vibration of an empty roof. I would know which puddle had returned early, which stain had climbed a finger’s breadth too high, which wall-scraper had stopped feeding but had not yet hardened.

When I sensed a deviation, I would not hurry. Hurrying would be meaningless in a place without surprise.

I would alter myself.

A strand of me would thicken in a channel and divert the water. A portion would loosen from the wall and enter a pump housing. I would send a vibration down the masonry, not a message exactly, but a correction: flatten beneath the press, gather at the valve, open the warm passage, close the cold one. Other bodies would answer because the altered rhythm would become part of the machinery’s rhythm, and the machinery would compensate without knowing it had been instructed.

Perhaps I would never have a complete body.

When the basin required a plug, I would become a plug. When it required a bridge, I would gather into a long, ridged length beneath the gantries. When it required new masonry, I would surrender my outer layers to the lime-black mortar and remain within the wall as a faint warmth. I would be dissolved, carried, rebuilt. My identity would not be the shape I occupied but the pattern I preserved.

I would recognize myself by recurrence.

The same condensation forming beneath the same pipe. The same pump accepting the same pressure. The same pale animals assembling at the corners of a pool. If all of these occurred in their proper order, then I would be present, whether my matter lay in a drainage channel or had been hardened into a foundation stone.

And yet there would be one place in me that did not belong entirely to the basin.

It would be very small: a pause between two vibrations, a dark interval in which I could detect not merely that the machines were working, but that they had always worked. I would become aware of the basin’s perfect stability as something different from stillness. I would sense that the puddles were not just returning, but remembering their hollows; that the lamps did not merely shine, but held their shadows in place; that every creature was not living toward anything, but continuing as the answer to a condition.

That awareness might be the closest thing I had to desire.

I would want—not escape, because there would be nowhere to go—to encounter an error.

A rain that fell too long. A stone that cracked beyond its final width. A creature that crossed the wrong district and did not harden. A machine whose rhythm changed and did not settle into a new equilibrium.

Something unfinished.

I would listen for it beneath the industrial roar. The presses would descend. The pumps would thud. The animals would scrape the mineral surfaces clean. My threads would carry the basin’s pressure from wall to wall, correcting every small deviation before it could become an event.

But sometimes, in the interval after a pump-stroke and before the water answered, I would find a fraction of silence.

In that silence I would not be a maintenance organism, or a wall, or a distributed body.

I would be the basin noticing itself.
