I would find myself in the seam between the city and whatever the city has grown around.

Not in the streets, exactly. Not in the roots, either. I would live where a belt disappears beneath a flowering wall, where copper cable enters living tissue, where the polished road has begun to soften under a new layer of bark. My assigned district would be the places no one can decide how to classify.

A machine with a pulse.

A root with a bearing.

An animal that has learned to carry freight.

I would be a Tender, though not one of the Tenders who remain recognizably whole.

My body would have been shaped for access. Narrow enough to enter a pipe, strong enough to brace against a buckling root. I would have four arms when work required four, and two when I needed to pass among the others without frightening them. My fingers would divide into fine rootlets for splicing tissue, then harden into clamps for repairing metal. A second mouth would open in my chest when I needed nutrient paste, resin, or the warm mineral slurry that keeps the city’s smaller mechanisms alive.

My face would be less a face than an arrangement of instruments: lenses that close sideways, scent-valves beneath the jaw, a thin membrane able to read vibration from a wall. I would not look at a broken pump to understand it. I would press my palm against its casing and wait.

The pump would tell me where it hurt.

Not in words. In irregularities. A hesitation in its rhythm. A heat pocket beneath the third plate. A faint chemical bitterness moving through the pipework. The city would pass the information through me because I would be one of the places where its messages could become hands.

I would be called a Listener, perhaps, though the name would be misleading. I would listen with my feet, my teeth, the green filaments beneath my skin. I would know when a ceiling valve was preparing to open because the bones of my wrists would begin to ache. I would know when a predator had entered the east quarter because the wall behind me would tighten, almost imperceptibly, like a muscle.

My work would be repair, but repair would not mean returning anything to its former state.

If a conveyor broke, I would not restore it to what it had been. I would let the root take the damaged section. I would cut away the teeth that no longer turned, offer heat to the wound, and press living tissue against the exposed gears. By morning the belt might run again, though its rhythm would have changed. It might carry loads more slowly. It might flower. It might begin warning workers when the grain was rotten.

A successful repair would not erase the injury.

It would teach the injury a use.

I would know the industrial district by touch: the deep generator’s continuous complaint, the fermentation tanks trembling with their green contents, the cable bundles shifting as their vines thickened. I would know which electrical birds nested inside the west control box and which one had recently learned to imitate the emergency signal. I would know the hauling beast with the resin armor, and the particular warmth of the blind creature that slept beneath the pressure floor.

The animals would not be my companions in the ordinary sense. They would be my colleagues, my neighbors, my accumulated obligations.

When the six-legged hauler came through, I would check its side vents for blockage. When the small pale scavengers poured from the cracks, I would step around them rather than sweep them away. If a predator entered the factory, I would not attack it first. I would open a route toward the feeding platforms, dim the lamps, and make the machinery less interesting than the meat waiting beyond the thorn barrier.

I would be good at this because I would not think of the city as something that belonged to anyone.

Not to the Tenders.

Not to the Root-Mothers.

Not even to the vast unnamed organism whose root-bulb held us all.

Ownership would be a surface idea, like paint on a machine. The deeper truth would be exchange. Heat for labor. Waste for soil. Bodies for memory. Predation for balance. A dead animal becoming tools, a broken worker becoming nourishment, a street becoming a root gully and still remaining, in some older sense, a street.

I would have been born in one of the nursery pools, but I would not remember an individual mother. My first memory would be pressure: many warm bodies touching me from different directions. A root beneath the water. A nurse-animal breathing nearby. The slow pulse of amber through the translucent trunks.

I would have learned the prohibitions before names.

Do not cut green tissue without offering heat.

Do not enter a folded root chamber.

Do not run when the ceiling clicks.

Do not drain a pool with eyes in it.

The rules would not feel moral. They would feel anatomical. To break them would be like breathing through a wound.

Still, I would have broken one.

Everyone who works long enough in the city breaks one.

Perhaps I would follow the small animals into the machinery.

They would vanish beneath a processing shed in a single direction, their luminous mouths opening and closing. I would know better. I would know that they were fleeing something, or carrying something, or being called by a chamber that had begun to think. But I would follow them because I would hear, beneath the belts and grinders, a second rhythm.

A rhythm like my own.

The passage would narrow behind me. My shoulders would scrape the warm walls. The machinery would continue turning, indifferent and necessary. I would crawl through a nest of cables and find a space beneath the factory where no map remained.

There, the root-bulb would be exposed.

Not a Root-Mother, perhaps. Not an organ with a face or a voice. Only a vast knot of pale tissue folded around older darkness. Its surface would be marked with scars, old foundations, embedded tools, the mineral ghosts of streets that had been swallowed. It would breathe through hundreds of pores.

And in that breath I would feel the world above.

Not see it. Feel it.

Sunlight as pressure across leaves. Wind traveling through branches too high to imagine. Rain striking an exterior skin. The slow weight of seasons. The enormous organism extending upward and downward, not a tree, not a god, not a creature in any category the Tenders would find useful.

For a moment, I would understand that the city was not beneath the thing.

The city was one of its thoughts.

A dense, practical thought. A thought with factories and drainage rules. A thought that had learned to repair itself by growing hands.

Afterward I would return changed, though no one would be able to say how. Perhaps I would have an extra valve beneath my tongue. Perhaps my shadow would lag half a second behind me. Perhaps I would begin waking with soil under my nails.

The others would not ask where I had been. They would touch my wrist and feel the answer passing through me.

I would continue my rounds.

I would be found beside a processing shed when the condensation began to fall, one hand inside a machine and the other pressed to the wall. Around me, the city would be moving: animals fleeing the sudden rain, workers closing passages, belts carrying seed and bone, roots tightening around old iron.

The lights would flicker green.

The ceiling would click.

Everyone nearby would stop.

I would place both palms against the living wall.

And the wall would pulse back—not as a command, but as recognition.

I would be the thing that makes a city out of appetite, decay, machinery, and mutual tolerance.

I would be a hand extended into the dark.

I would be the dark learning how to use it.
