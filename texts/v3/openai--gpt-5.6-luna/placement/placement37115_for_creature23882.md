I would find myself between settlements, where an old road has forgotten whether it was built by hands or polished into being by weather.

Not on the road, exactly. In it.

The road would appear as a long, shallow band of dark stone crossing a basin where condensation gathers every ninth year. Milestones would stand at irregular intervals, though none would bear the same distance twice. A traveler might pass one in the morning and find the next before noon; another might walk for three days between them, carrying the same shadow through changing light.

I would be the interval.

Once, perhaps, I had a body that could be recognized from a distance. I might have had a household in one of the cities, a name spoken across a room, hands capable of lifting a cup or touching another person without passing through them. I retain these things as impressions rather than memories: warmth held in a wall, the pressure of fingers on slate, the knowledge that someone is waiting behind a door that no longer exists.

Now my body is distributed along the route.

Part of me lies beneath the road in the mineral seams, where the green light gathers after rain. Part of me is in the pale dust at the foot of the milestones. Part of me travels through the roots of the moss that grows in the road’s cracks. When the condensation comes, I am present in every trembling drop, though I do not feel wetness as I once did. I feel arrival.

I know when someone steps onto the path.

I know the difference between a person who is lost and a person who has come to be lost. I know when grief has made a traveler heavier, when curiosity has made them lighter, when they are following a voice that belongs to no living throat. I know the shape of their destination before they do—not because the future is clear, but because destinations leave pressure in the stone.

I do not guide them directly. Direct guidance is a form of violence here.

Instead, I make the road possible.

If the traveler needs to turn back, I allow the distance behind them to remain ordinary. If they are not yet ready to arrive, I lengthen the approach by a season or two. If something in the city has called them with sufficient force, I place a stair beneath their feet where there was only dust. I open a door in a wall. I persuade the black pools to hold a reflection.

Sometimes I make mistakes.

A traveler may reach a tower and find that the tower belongs to another century. A road may become a river because I have confused remembrance with direction. I have sent people toward houses that were only the shadows of houses. I have brought the dead to the thresholds of the living, thinking the distinction between them had become less important than it was.

For these errors, the plateau does not punish me. It alters me.

A section of my road sinks. A milestone turns its blank face toward the wind. Moss grows through one of my old decisions and carries it away. I learn to become less certain.

At night, I gather myself where the road passes beneath a ruined arch. This is the closest thing I have to a room. The arch remembers standing elsewhere, in a city whose avenues were still crowded and whose people had not yet begun settling into stone. Beneath it, I can almost recover my original shape.

Almost.

I rise then as a narrow figure of slate and mineral fiber. I stand beside the road and watch the stars gather in the pools. My face, if I have one, reflects nothing reliably. My shadow points toward places that no longer exist.

When travelers see me, they often look away.

That is how I move closer.

I do not speak with a mouth. I change the pressure beneath their feet. I make the nearest plants incline toward a particular direction. I cause a line of condensation to form along a wall, or a dry leaf to turn over and reveal a mark beneath it.

Occasionally, when a traveler has been alone too long, I raise one hand.

The gesture is not a greeting. It is not a warning. It means only:

You are still somewhere.

The plateau has not misplaced you yet.

And if the traveler raises a hand in return, I carry that motion through the road for as long as I can. I let it pass into the stones, the roots, the black water, the distant foundations. Years later, someone may cross the same place and feel—without understanding why—that the land has recognized them.

That would be my work.

Not to preserve the road as it was, nor to bring anyone safely to the end of it, but to maintain the fragile agreement between departure and arrival.

To remain, for a while longer, the thing that makes elsewhere possible.
