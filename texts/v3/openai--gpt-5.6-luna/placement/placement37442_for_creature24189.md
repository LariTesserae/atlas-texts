I would find myself at the settlement’s unfinished edge, where the last vaulted chambers give way to groves and then to raw mineral.

I would be a **seam-keeper**: one of the six-limbed inhabitants whose task is to notice where the city ends, and to make certain that it never ends too sharply.

My body would be low and plated, ochre along the back, green-black at the joints. I would carry no tools. The forward limbs would be broad and sensitive, suited to distributing pressure; from beneath my abdomen, fine contact-organs would unfold into the ground. I would spend much of my life kneeling.

Not praying. Listening.

The plain would speak through pressure before it spoke through movement. A faint swelling under a road. A change in the way warmth traveled through a wall. A mineral terrace beginning to lift beneath a residential chamber. I would feel these things long before they became visible. The settlement would depend on my kind to tell the difference between a harmless fold, a future bridge, and a ridge that would bury half a district.

I would know the city by touch.

The smooth depression where thousands had crossed. The warm hollow in a communal chamber. The old buttress containing the altered substance of the dead. The subtle chemical traces left along the walls by those who had passed through generations before me. I would not need names for every inhabitant. Their routes would be enough. Their pressures. Their residues. A familiar vibration in the floor would be as recognizable as a face.

My first lesson would have been a route.

An elder would have led me from the chamber of my cultivation, beneath the hanging leaves and across the plaza, out past the oldest walls to the raw mineral beyond. There I would have lowered myself to the ground. The continent would have flexed beneath me—slowly, almost too slowly to be called movement—and the elder would have answered with a pulse through the chest.

Belonging would mean learning how to answer.

Now, at the settlement’s edge, I would be facing a new fold in the land. It would appear distant and insignificant, a change in brightness beneath the pale sun. But through my limbs I would feel the pressure traveling toward us. The city would not evacuate. It would not defend itself with barriers. We would persuade the coming ground.

I would gather others around the rising crystal. Some would press warmth into its base. Some would feed it mineral secretions. Others would redirect root-mats and withdraw vegetation from the route we wanted it to take. Together we would encourage the fold to broaden, to flatten, to become a ramp instead of a wall.

It would take years.

Perhaps decades.

During that time, I might sleep beside it beneath a furling frond, wake to the dry click of stems, and find that the ridge had risen by the width of a limb. I would mark the change not with symbols but with my body, storing its shape as a sequence of pressures and textures. When the new land finally joined the settlement, a passage might be grown across it. Or a chamber. Or a shaded place where the young could learn to feel the continent.

I would not think of this as building.

I would think of it as conversation.

At midday, I would lie against a mineralized wall while the district’s great leaves closed overhead. Small grazers would pass beneath me, scraping their orderly furrows. Somewhere below, an animal would scratch through the compacted earth. The city would seem silent, but every surface would carry information: warnings, invitations, traces of grief, signs of ripeness, the slow chemical handwriting of the living.

When I died, my body would be brought to the thinly vegetated ground at the edge.

Others would gather. They would not preserve me as a separate thing. They would receive my warmth, my mineral density, the patterns held in my tissues. What could be used would enter the walls, the root-mats, the next generation. Some trace of my sensing might remain in a corridor, or in the way a frond opened against the afternoon heat.

I would not become a ghost.

I would become part of the city’s ability to listen.

And if, after my death, a young inhabitant placed its contact-organs against the ground and felt the same distant pressure I once felt, some small portion of me would still be there—not watching, not remembering, but continuing.
