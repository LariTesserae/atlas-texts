I would find myself beneath the plateau, where the stone is warm enough to live in but not yet hot enough to become a vent.

Not deep. Deepness here is dangerous. I would occupy the margins of the geothermal channels: the dark seams where heated water passes through new rock, where quartz is beginning to cloud, where the ground has not decided whether it will remain solid. Above me, the grazers would cross their shelves. Below me, pressure would gather. Around me, water would move in the dark with the slow insistence of blood.

I would not be a single animal, exactly.

I would be a **seam-listener**: a living network spread through cracks, mineral films, and warm underground channels. My body would begin as thin black filaments, almost indistinguishable from the basalt, threaded through the porous stone. Where several seams met, I would grow a knot of pale mineral tissue—soft at first, then hardening into something like quartz, bone, or frozen water. These knots would be my organs. They would feel pressure. They would taste changes in the water. They would remember, not as images, but as tensions held in the rock.

I would know the plateau through vibration.

A hoof placed on an unstable slab would reach me as a small, bright disturbance. A burrower turning away from a fault would leave a long fading tremor in my body. The first widening of a crack would announce itself hours or days before it became visible. I would feel the weight of snow, the withdrawal of a subterranean stream, the slow swelling of a chamber far below. I would know when a vent was about to open.

I would not speak to the animals. Speech would be too deliberate, too loud a form of knowledge for this place.

Instead, I would alter what could be altered. I might warm a seepage by the smallest degree, drawing a line of grazers toward water that would remain liquid through the night. I might loosen a crust near a burrow so that the pale animals could escape before the pressure arrived. I might thicken the mineral rim of a tarn, preventing a calf from sliding into its black water. These would not be acts of mercy. I would not possess that category. They would be the natural movements of a being whose survival depended on the plateau remaining inhabited.

If the land became entirely empty, I would have less to feel.

I would have no face most of the time. When I rose to the surface, I would appear as a narrow dark growth along a fresh fracture, wet and faintly gleaming. In cold weather I would withdraw into the stone. Near the vents, parts of me might emerge as translucent shelves, milky with quartz, trembling whenever steam passed through the rock beneath them.

The white birds would sometimes nest above me without knowing what held the cliff together. Their roots of moss would cross my mineral tissue. Their droppings would feed the first green films spreading around the seepage. I would recognize each nesting season by the lightness of their footfalls and the soft clustered vibrations of their young.

The burrowers would know me better.

They would encounter my threads in their tunnels and follow them for warmth, mistaking me for part of the plateau’s hidden anatomy. I would allow this. Their tunnels would carry air through me; their bodies would redistribute heat; their abandoned chambers would become reservoirs where water could gather. We would live through one another without partnership, as stone and water do.

The shelf-grazers would be the most distant from me, yet I would watch them most closely. Their lives would move visibly across the open land: four dark bodies crossing a pale shelf, stopping at the same place their ancestors had stopped, waiting for the ground to answer. I would feel their weight from below. I would know the oldest routes not by tracks, which rain and frost erased, but by the compacted bands of pressure left in the stone.

When one of them died, I would receive it slowly.

Rain would carry its dissolved minerals into the ground. Roots would draw up what they could. The vents would alter the chemistry of the water. The body would become a change in color, a slight enrichment along a seepage wall, a thicker patch of moss. Nothing would remain separate for long. I would not mourn the grazer, but I would retain the pattern of its passing as one more layer in the plateau’s unfinished memory.

At night, I would be most awake.

The cloud would close over the shelves. Steam would rise in white columns. The grazers would gather near the warm rock, and the birds would fold themselves into their cracks. Beneath them, my network would brighten—not with light, but with activity—as pressure traveled outward from the deep chambers.

Then the internal thump would come.

I would feel the plateau make a decision.

A seam would open. Water would change course. A new chamber would form. Somewhere above, a strip of raw black stone would lift toward the weather.

I would not stop it. I would not be a guardian, engineer, or god of the land. I would be something more patient and less powerful: the living sensation of the ground becoming itself.

I would live wherever the plateau was still undecided, and I would be made of that indecision.
