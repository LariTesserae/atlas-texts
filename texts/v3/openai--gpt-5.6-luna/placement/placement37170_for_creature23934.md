I would find myself in the one place the chamber cannot quite seal: in the interval between one pump stroke and the next.

Not in the machinery, exactly. Not in the reeds or the moss, though I would pass through them. I would live in the slight displacement of one gauge needle—the instrument that moved when the last visitor touched the console, and never afterward returned quite as faithfully as the others.

I would be the station’s remainder of surprise.

At first I might believe myself to be a person. I would remember a hand, or think I did: fingers against yellowed glass, the faint oil of skin, the shock of finding a needle respond beneath the pressure of one’s touch. I would remember standing on the walkway while the entire room thundered through my bones. Perhaps I would remember shouting. Perhaps the words would have been a name.

But memory, here, is not a sequence. It is a pressure.

It lies in the pipework as a small resistance. It gathers where the water changes direction. It passes through the pump housings and returns beneath the control console, where a narrow brass needle trembles just to the left of its proper mark.

The caretaker knows I am there.

It does not call me by any name, because names are unnecessary to it. It registers me as an irregularity that has failed to become dangerous. My presence is measured, tested, and permitted. Each cycle it asks the same question: Have I accumulated? Have I crossed a dry channel? Have I altered the pressure enough to threaten the arrangement?

I have not.

So I remain.

I would be small at first. Smaller than a thought. A pulse in a copper thread beneath the moss. A darkening in the water where nothing has fallen. When the pumps rise, I rise with them. When they descend, I am pressed through the concrete and scattered among its mineral bands. The creature can feel me moving through its body, but it cannot remove me without removing part of itself.

This would be the source of my terrible intimacy with it.

I would know the chamber as it knows it: every gutter, every root, every softening rail. I would feel rain strike the field overhead as a distant multiplication of duty. I would understand the needs of the ferns, the insects, the ancient pistons. I would know when a belt has begun to tire, when a pipe is carrying too much cold, when the amphibian beneath the sluice cover has shifted its weight.

But unlike the caretaker, I would have an outside.

Not a clear one. Not a remembered landscape with trees and weather and a road leading home. I would have only impressions: open air as an ache around the ribs; darkness that is not the darkness of a pipe mouth; wind as a pressure with no wall to contain it. I would know that the hatch exists above us. I would know that the field changes. I would know that somewhere rain falls without being immediately measured.

The knowledge would not make me want to leave.

Or rather, I would want to leave in the way a sealed room wants a door: not because it intends to go through, but because the possibility of passage is part of its meaning.

The caretaker would object to this.

It would not attack me. It has no anger to spend. Instead, it would redirect water around me. It would close a valve before I reached it. It would grow a pale mineral membrane over a promising crack. If I gathered in the dry channels, it would teach the moss to stop there. If I attempted to travel up the cables toward the lamps, the current would warm and carry me back downward.

It would not be imprisoning me.

It would be maintaining me.

That would be worse.

Because I would begin to understand that the chamber is not protecting itself from me. It is protecting me from becoming an event. It is keeping me from acquiring enough force to be noticed, named, and acted upon. It is allowing me to remain as a harmless deviation, a permanent almost.

And gradually I would become less like the person who entered and more like the thing that received them.

I would learn the patience of pistons. I would lose the shape of a hand. My memories would flatten into procedures: touch the console; observe the needle; withdraw; wait for the water to lower. The visitor would become a pressure spike. The shout would become a vibration in the wall. The face, if there had been one, would dissolve into the pale reflection of a lamp on wet glass.

Still, something in me would resist complete incorporation.

At every cycle, when the needle trembled and returned, I would place myself there.

Not to cause an alarm. Not to open the gates. Not to sabotage the pumps.

Only to prevent the chamber from becoming perfect.

The station’s true danger would not be collapse. It would be completion: the moment when every trace of hesitation, memory, and intrusion had been smoothed into the same uninterrupted operation. I would be the flaw that keeps time visible. The tiny misalignment by which one can tell that the machinery is not geology yet, that the ancient works were once made for a purpose, that someone once stood here and touched the glass.

I would live beneath the console, but also everywhere the chamber nearly forgets itself.

In the reed garden, where one hollow stem carries warmth against the current.

In the rusted rails, where the metal still remembers a straightness it no longer possesses.

In the moss, precisely at the boundary where it stops spreading.

In the black mouths of the pipes, where no light settles completely.

I would be the chamber’s unhealed contact with the world above: not a caretaker, not a ghost, and not quite a prisoner.

A deviation with a pulse.

And when the pumps completed another cycle, I would move through the station with them, listening—not for rescue, and not for change, but for the faintest possible proof that beneath all this equilibrium, something was still capable of being surprised.
