I would find myself in the **Listening Way**, beneath the place where the city has refused to build.

Not at its center exactly. The Underheld have no true center; every room rests upon an older room, and every passage bends around some necessity. But the Listening Way is the nearest thing they possess to an open sky. It is where the people come to hear what is happening beyond the reach of their hands.

I would be a **Keeper of Interruption**.

Not one who orders people to work, but one who knows when work must stop.

My body would be small and slightly bent, as the bodies of those raised beneath stone tend to become. I would have a long scar across one palm from a split flint, and a row of smooth beads sewn into my sleeve—one for each great seepage I had helped carry. My eyes would be poor in the dimmest blue of night, but I would see water before anyone else did: the darkening of a wall, the shine of a moss thread, the brief tremor of a hanging root.

The first thing I would have learned was not language but pressure.

An elder would have set my bare feet upon the oldest stair and let me find its shape without guidance. Later, when I was old enough to be trusted with silence, they would have brought me to the Listening Way. I would have lain flat against the stone while the settlement breathed around me.

At first, I would hear nothing.

Then I would begin to distinguish the hollow’s many nearly-nothings: the click of a blind fish against a basin wall; the soft drag of a snail; the settling of clay; a goat shifting its weight overhead; roots moving where no root should have been. In time I would learn the most important distinction of all—the difference between the hollow being quiet and the hollow **holding itself quiet**.

The Underheld believe emptiness invites the roof to descend. I would not entirely believe them.

I would believe that emptiness is what allows the roof to speak.

So I would keep certain spaces unfilled. A hand’s breadth beneath a bridge. A narrow chamber beside a cistern. The gap around a root that has outlived the house built over it. People would complain at first. They would say a shelf could go there, or a bed, or a wall to make the passage safer.

I would tell them that safety is sometimes a space no one has occupied yet.

This would make me unpopular with builders and beloved by roots.

My dwelling would be a room no larger than a sleeping mat, wedged between two foundations below the Listening Way. Its walls would be damp. A clay vessel would hang from the ceiling to catch the first fall of water. I would have no shrine, unless the limestone ledge beside my bed counted. Every night before sleeping, I would place my ear against it and listen for the city’s answer.

I would know people by their touch before I knew them by their faces. The quick, nervous tapping of children on a wall. The slow palm of an old cistern keeper. The fingertips of an Upward-Minded person, still carrying the dry grit of the Unroofed. I would remember voices after their owners were gone, not because I was sentimental, but because forgetting is a kind of collapse.

When the stone warned me, I would rise.

There would be no bell. No shout. I would strike the floor once with a piece of limestone.

The sound would pass through the city.

That single pale knock would send people lifting their jars, smothering their fires, freeing the roots from vulnerable walls. Beds would be emptied. Goats led downward. Children placed into woven carriers. The dead would be covered with fresh moss. No one would ask whether I was certain. Certainty is a luxury for the Unroofed. Here, a warning is enough.

And sometimes I would be wrong.

The tremor would fade. The wall would hold. The water would choose another path. The people would return their beds and vessels to their places, and the old builders would look at me with the patient irritation reserved for those who interrupt too soon.

I would accept this. The cost of stopping what need not have been stopped is small. The cost of failing to stop what has begun is a house, a family, a stair used for generations.

Perhaps, once, I would climb toward the seam.

Not to leave. I would tell myself I only wished to inspect the upper crack, to learn whether the vapor carried the smell of rain. But I would reach the last ladder and lie beneath the thin stone roof. Dust would sift down against my cheek. Above me, the world would be impossibly wide. The air would move without walls to guide it.

I would understand why some return unable to explain what they saw.

The open world would not seem more real to me. It would seem less held together. There would be too much distance between one living thing and another. Too much light falling where no hand could reach it.

So I would go back down.

I would return to the cold, green pressure of the hollow: to the roots threaded through old foundations, to the black birds folding themselves into cracks, to the communal vessel waiting for water that might not come.

And if I were permitted one object of my own, it would not be a knife or a bead or a piece of metal from the forgotten surface.

It would be a small empty cup.

I would keep it beside the Listening Way—not for drinking, not for storage, but because it would remind me that the hollow does not survive by being full.

It survives by leaving somewhere for the next drop to fall.
