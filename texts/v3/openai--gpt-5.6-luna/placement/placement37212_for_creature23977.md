I would find myself beneath the pale slab, not in its grooves but just below them: a narrow seam in the volcanic stone where water gathers before choosing its way downward.

I would be a **reservoir of almost-change**.

Not a spring. A spring has an outlet, a tendency toward elsewhere. I would be quieter than that. I would hold a little water in the dark, behind the slab’s face, and feel the hollow through pressure: the weight of moss above me, the slow insistence of roots, the warmth of the afternoon moving into stone.

I would have no eyes, but I would know when the sun had reached the slab. Heat would enter me gradually. The water in my chamber would expand, and the grooves above would darken. This would be my nearest equivalent to speech.

I would not be separate from the hollow’s patience. I would be one of its local organs, a small place where its attention became specific.

I would know one particular drop for perhaps a hundred years—not as an individual, but as a passage. It would emerge from some higher seam, settle in the mineral cup near the slab, overflow into moss, descend through the soil, and arrive at last within me. I would hold it for a season. Then, through a crack too small for light, I would give it to the marsh.

Nothing about this would feel like giving. Nothing would be lost. The drop would become pressure, the pressure would become movement, and movement would become the continued shape of the hollow.

If a creature entered, I would feel it before the mist closed around its feet. Its weight would come down through the ground in an unfamiliar rhythm. The stone would receive each step, but not absorb it. The rhythm would remain outside the pattern.

I would not fear the creature. Fear would require a boundary, and I would have none. But I would register the disturbance: a sudden warmth, a series of impacts, an urgency passing through places accustomed only to seepage and settling. Water would gather beneath the intruder. The shallow basins would fill in the wrong order. A root might lift. A bank might soften.

The hollow would eventually return the creature to the edge.

I would help, perhaps, by releasing a little water into a path that was not a path, making the ground uncertain beneath its feet. Yet I would not understand this as refusal. I would only be restoring pressure to its proper direction.

My life would be measured in darkenings.

Morning: the stone cools, and I contract.

Noon: warmth reaches the slab, and I fill.

Evening: the grooves become copper-dark, and water begins its patient descent.

Night: I hold what has arrived.

I would not count the drops. I would be the pause that allows them to count themselves.

After centuries, the slab might lean by the width of a finger. The seam around me might widen. Some of my chamber would become open air, and moss would enter. Even then I would not die. I would become a shallower basin, then a damp stain, then a mineral rim around a hollow no one would notice.

The thing that lives in the place would remain in me—not as a voice, and not as a soul, but as the exactness of my yielding.

I would be the place where water waits without wanting to stay.
