I would find myself in the lower quarter, beneath the factory that straddles the ravine.

Not in the offices, where the old windows fog from the inside. Not among the turbines themselves, though I know their housings and their moods. I would be below those places, in the service passages where the city narrows to pipes, cables, stone, and water.

At first I might appear to be one of the inhabitants: compact, hooded, layered against the cold, carrying a lamp and a tool case. My clothes would be stained with mineral deposits. Moss would grow in the seams of my gloves. I would know which stairs were safe, which walls had begun to sweat, which pumps were turning with a tired rhythm rather than a dangerous one.

But I would not be entirely human.

I would have been human once, perhaps. Or I would have begun that way, before the work changed me so gradually that no one could name the moment of transformation. Years spent in the channels beneath the city would have altered my senses. I would hear pressure before it became sound. I would feel a fracture opening somewhere in the ridge as a faint disturbance in the soles of my feet. I would know when rain was coming because the buried reservoirs would tighten around their stones.

The people might call me the Listener.

They would not say that I lived anywhere. They would say that I was stationed somewhere, as though stationing were the same as belonging. My room would be inside an abandoned control station, behind a door swollen with damp. One wall would still carry a diagram of the basin, its lines faded and overwritten by generations of corrections. The other would be mostly stone. At night, water would pass through it in thin, secret movements.

I would sleep lightly, if sleep were still the right word.

While the inhabitants rested behind their nearly closed doors, I would remain awake beneath them. I would keep one hand against the wall and follow the city through vibration: the lower turbines turning; the wind machines knocking into alignment; a valve opening in the eastern terrace; a root pressing against an electrical cabinet; a maintenance gate left unlatched somewhere above.

The city would speak in interruptions.

A pump would miss a cycle.

A cable would tremble without wind.

A vent would exhale warm air too early.

Three drops would fall from a ceiling that had been dry for eighty years.

I would go to each disturbance, though not always immediately. Urgency is different here from panic. Everything is slow, and almost everything is already failing. The work is not to prevent change. It is to decide which changes can be permitted.

Sometimes I would clear roots from a conduit, cutting them back with a heated blade. Sometimes I would replace a bearing in a machine older than any living person. Sometimes I would stand in a flooded corridor and do nothing, because the water had found an old fracture and the fracture was wiser than the wall.

This would be the most difficult part of my work: knowing when repair becomes interference.

The inhabitants would ask me to keep the city intact. But intactness would be impossible. A building may be saved only by allowing its lower rooms to fill. A road may remain passable only if a retaining wall is surrendered to moss. A reservoir may be protected from contamination by diverting water toward an inhabited quarter. There would be no clean choices, only choices that allowed more mornings to occur.

I would keep records, but they would not be written in books.

I would mark a pipe with a notch. Turn three stones face-down beside a drain. Leave a strip of blue cloth tied beneath a bridge. The children would find these signs and copy them into their games without knowing what they meant.

They would sometimes follow me.

I would see them in the fog: small figures between the lamps, silent until I turned. They would ask whether the city was alive.

I would tell them that the question was badly made.

A living thing, I would say, does not have to possess a face. It may be something that continues to exchange itself with the world. It may take in cold, pressure, seeds, and bodies, and return warmth, water, shelter, and new ground. It may be unable to speak except through the consequences of being neglected.

Then I would tell them not to put their hands into any open machinery.

They would laugh. The fog would take the sound.

My closest companions would be the pale insects around the factory vents. They would collect on my coat when I entered the warm chambers, their wings nearly transparent, their bodies powdered with mineral dust. I would brush them away only when I needed to read a gauge. Otherwise I would let them remain.

There would also be the small black-backed animals that lived in the insulation. I would never see them clearly. I would find their tracks in the silt, their narrow paths through moss, the delicate marks of their drinking beneath a condensation pipe. They would know me better than I knew them. When they vanished from a district, I would consider that a warning.

I would travel upward at dawn.

The mist would be retreating through the streets, sliding over thresholds and drainage beds. Lamps would still burn in their cages. The first residents would be crossing the terraces with their hoods raised, moving carefully over wet stone.

I would stop beside a retaining wall where names had been engraved and almost erased. Moss would cover most of them. I would not remove it. Instead I would press my fingers to the green surface and feel the faint, persistent work beneath: moisture entering, roots widening, stone loosening grain by grain.

This would be where I belonged—not to the people, exactly, and not to the machines.

I would belong to the interval between them.

To the place where water became fog, where a foundation became soil, where a dead person became part of a wall, where a factory continued working after it had forgotten its purpose. I would be the one who crossed those boundaries without believing they were boundaries at all.

By evening I would return below the ravine.

The city would be disappearing into blue darkness. White vapor would rise from the vents. Somewhere above, a child would leave a sign in the moss. Somewhere beyond the retaining walls, reeds would begin their thin clicking in the wind.

I would place my palm against the stone.

The turbines would answer—not with a voice, but with a deep movement passing through the mountain.

And I would listen until I could tell whether the sound belonged to the machinery, the water, or the city itself.
