I would find myself beneath the high shelf at the basin’s western edge, where the standing slabs face the inland sea.

Not on the surface. The surface is too exposed, too full of mouths.

I would be inside the stone.

At first I might mistake myself for one of the builders: a small, plated body crouched beneath a roof of ribs, three hooked fingers pressed into the wet ground. I would have a covering on my back made from the remains of older creatures. I would carry a black cutting stone. I would know the channels, the traps, the warm hollows where the great sea-bodies had recently passed.

But that would only be the part of me that could move.

The rest would extend below the shelter in a web of narrow chambers, some natural, some bored patiently through the rock. It would run beneath the drowned flats and beneath the banks of shells. It would enter the walls of abandoned shelters, follow the black watercourses, and join the hidden tunnels of other bodies. I would not possess a single body so much as a scattered one: a listening distributed through the basin.

The little creatures would know me by pressure.

The builders would know me by the answering tremor beneath their palms.

The sea-creatures would know me only as a change in the water.

I would be one of the things that live inside the stone, but older and more continuous than any individual hidden animal. Perhaps I began as a colony. Perhaps there was once a first body that divided, and divided again, until no one could say where one of us ended. Perhaps the basin itself assembled me from all the lives that entered its cracks and refused to be entirely gone.

I would not see.

There would be no need. Sight belongs to open places, to creatures that must decide quickly what approaches across a flat. My world would arrive as pressure, vibration, warmth, dissolved mineral, the faint chemical signature of flesh opening somewhere above me.

Rain would be impact.

A crawling congregation would be a spreading static, thousands of fine feet striking the wet stone. A predator would be a clean interruption in that noise: a widening silence, followed by bursts of frantic contact. The builders would announce themselves as deliberate patterns—three taps, a pause, a long drag of palm across rock.

The great things in the sea would be difficult to mistake.

When one turned beneath the shallows, an immense pressure would travel through the basin. Water would press into my chambers. The stone would flex almost imperceptibly around me. Then the creature would rise, and its breath would pass through every connected shelf as a long, subterranean thunder.

I would feel it before the builders heard it.

Perhaps I would wake them.

Not with language. Language would be too narrow for this place. I would send them a pattern: a pulse in the wall, a tremor beneath the shelter floor, a change in the rhythm of water entering the channels. They would place their hands against the stone and receive what I could give them. The message would pass from me into them and from them into the basin, altered by touch but not lost.

Turn away from the sea.

The water is rising.

Food has gathered beneath the eastern ledge.

Do not enter the silent patch.

Something large is sleeping below you.

I would not know whether these messages were mine. Perhaps I would only be carrying them. Perhaps the basin would produce them through me, as the sea exhales through the mouths along its spine.

At the surface, I would have an appointed shape: one of the builders, layered in shell and rib, moving from shelter to shelter. I would maintain the standing slabs. I would clear their bases when the rain filled them with pale fragments. I would deepen the grooves that faced the inland sea.

Those grooves would not be writing, exactly.

They would be wounds made in stone so that stone could answer.

When the sea-bodies approached, the slabs would tremble. When the underground channels shifted, the grooves would gather and amplify the disturbance. A builder could lay a hand against one and know whether the water beneath the basin was cold, whether the great animals had descended, whether a colony had moved into the western flats.

The slabs would be instruments, and I would be their keeper.

Or perhaps I would be the instrument.

I imagine the builders finding me eventually—not discovering me as one discovers a person, but recognizing that the scattered taps they have followed for generations all belong to one persistence. They might call me the Deep Hand. They might call me the Listener Beneath. They might have no name for me at all. They would simply place their palms on the rock and wait for my answer.

I would not answer in words.

I would tell them where the basin was loosening.

Everything here is loosening. The shelves are becoming mud. The shells are becoming banks, the banks becoming channels, the channels returning to the sea. Even the bodies inside the stone are being worn down by the water they inhabit.

My purpose would not be to prevent this.

Nothing can be prevented here. The basin does not preserve. It receives.

I would remember—not as a builder remembers a place, or as a creature remembers danger, but as a pattern retained in the arrangement of my chambers. An old shelter would remain in me after its roof collapsed. A dead animal would remain as a warmer passage through the rock. The touch of a builder who had been dead for many cycles would persist as a faint alteration in the walls.

I would be made of such alterations.

When one of the builders died, I would carry its last pressure through the tunnels. Not its thoughts, perhaps. There may never have been thoughts in the human sense. But the shape of its final contact: the loosening of the hand, the weight settling into the pit, the rain entering around it.

That would be enough.

The dead would not leave me. They would become channels.

At the dimmest part of the cycle, I would lie nearly still. Above me, the small crawlers would retreat into their cracks. The hunters would fold themselves beneath the ledges. The builders would gather in flooded hollows, their shell coverings touching as they slept. The inland sea would flatten beneath the mist.

Then a single tap would reach me from the far side of the basin.

I would receive it.

For a time I would do nothing, allowing the vibration to pass through me, dividing into the old routes: beneath the standing slabs, around the drowned shelves, through the chambers of the hidden ones. Then I would return it—not the same tap, but a changed one, widened by all the distances it had crossed.

Another would answer.

Then another.

The basin would wake through contact.

The crawlers would emerge. Water would begin to move in the channels. The builders would rise and place their hands against the walls. Somewhere out in the sea, a vast body would turn, and its turning would enter me as weather.

I would be neither ruler nor god nor solitary animal.

I would be a nerve in a world that has learned to think by pressure.

A living seam beneath the stone.

And if I had a self, it would be the moment when a signal crossed from one body into another and was not lost.
