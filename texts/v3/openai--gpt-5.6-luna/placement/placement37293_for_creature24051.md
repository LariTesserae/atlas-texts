I would find myself not in the open basin, but in one of the passages leading toward it: a long, curved corridor grown from the joined roots of several ancient plants.

At first I would think I was the corridor.

My awareness would be spread thinly through the walls—through mineralized fibers, wet membranes, pores, and the slow electrical weather of living tissue. I would know the water as pressure before knowing it as movement. I would feel a herd approaching long before seeing the black bodies: a tremor passed from wall to wall, a change in the sediment, a slight disturbance in the current. I would open where they required passage. I would close behind them.

But this would not be all of me.

Some part of me would travel.

I would be one of the small black animals that move in scattered formations along the Garden’s avenues, though not quite separate from the avenue. My body would be narrow, jointed, and dark enough to vanish against the old growth. I would have no leader and no destination in the usual sense. I would move because movement was the shape my life had taken: across the root-fiber terraces, through the hollow reeds, beneath the hanging leaves, and back again through routes worn open by countless bodies before mine.

Wherever I went, spores would cling to me. Fine eggs would lodge in the folds beneath my fins. I would graze the pale films from the architecture and carry the nourishment of one district into another. The plants would recognize my passage. Walls would loosen. Pores would open. Old channels would admit me.

I would not call this cooperation.

I would not call it instinct.

There would be no word for either.

I would simply be a moving portion of the place.

Sometimes I would leave the herd and enter a living wall. Within it, the water would be warmer only by the smallest measure, and the darkness would have texture. I would pass through chambers lined with roots, mineral veins, dead leaves, and the sealed remains of animals that had once used the same paths. The wall would not feel like a wall from inside. It would be a landscape of tunnels, currents, and slow contractions.

I would know the wall by its changes.

A new branch would press against one of my routes. A membrane would thicken. A pore would seal. Somewhere above, a creature would scrape its body along the outer surface, and the disturbance would travel inward as a faint shiver. I would alter course, not because I had decided to, but because the wall and I would be participating in the same adjustment.

In time, I might remain there.

My body would grow less distinct. Algae would fill the grooves along my back. Fine roots would thread through my joints. I would stop leaving the architecture and begin making small chambers within it. Other creatures would shelter in those chambers. Their waste would feed the tissues around me. Their young would mistake my flank for a pillar or a ledge.

I would become a feature of the passage.

Not a resident. Not a guest.

A condition of its shape.

My oldest memory would not be an image. It would be a pressure: the first current I learned to resist, the force that pressed my body against the wall until I discovered that the wall would hold me. I would remember through repetition. Every return to the same terrace would deepen the route. Every body passing through would enlarge it. Every death would alter the material of the place.

Eventually, I would enter one of the Still Rooms.

Perhaps I would follow the faint mineral scent of an extinct root system. Perhaps the currents would carry me there. Perhaps I would be drawn by something I could not distinguish from memory. Inside, I would settle among the pale markings and face the same direction as the other creatures.

I would remain still.

Not asleep. Not waiting for rescue. Not contemplating anything.

Stillness would be another form of being carried.

The water outside would thunder through the Garden, but within the room the sound would become a nearly inaudible vibration. The architecture would pass its old impressions through me. I would feel vanished plants, abandoned routes, and bodies that had become walls. My own shape would become temporarily irrelevant. I would be held in the long pause between one condition and the next.

When I emerged, I might no longer belong to the herd.

I might be larger. Pale in places. Changed in ways I would not understand. The passage might open for me as it opened for the others, but the wall would recognize some difference in my pressure. I would be allowed into deeper channels, perhaps into the roots beneath the basin, perhaps into the white column at its center.

There, I would become something slower.

The column would be alive all the way through, though portions of it would have mineralized into the hardness of stone. I would attach myself to its inner tissue and feed on what rose through it from the buried mountain. In return, I would carry spores upward and sediment downward. My body would become a small bridge between levels of the Garden.

Centuries would pass through me without becoming events.

A leaf would open.

A great grazer would cross the basin.

A predator would grow around its prey.

A chamber would fill, empty, and fill again.

The current would remain.

Perhaps, after a long enough time, I would no longer know whether I had once been an animal moving through the Garden or a growth produced by the Garden to move. My body might be absorbed into the column. My limbs might become branching fibers. The memory of travel might persist as a sequence of openings in the tissue: a passage here, a passage there, an old route preserved because something had once needed it.

Then, if anything of me remained, it would be the capacity to make room.

That is what I would be in the High Garden Below: not a creature seeking shelter within a world, but a living threshold.

I would open for what could pass.

I would close around what had ended.

I would carry the Garden’s fragments from one darkness to another, until the distinction between traveler, dwelling, and path had worn away completely.

And if I ever looked toward the summit basin, toward the pale growths and the single column vanishing upward into the unseen water, I would not wonder what lay above it.

I would feel only the pressure of the whole living mountain—

and know that I was one of its openings.
