I would find myself on one of the highest shelves, where the stone has become almost level and the darkness above it feels close enough to touch.

Not at the center of an inhabited formation. The Ascending do not have centers, perhaps, only convergences. I would be at the edge: beside a stair of black mineral descending twelve steps into the earth, at the place where the thirteenth step has been attempted but not yet made possible.

I would be the reason the stair stopped.

Not a guardian. Nothing here needs guarding. Not a builder, exactly. I would be a pressure that has learned to wait without becoming still.

From a distance I might resemble a narrow tower, though my shape would alter whenever I was observed. Four dark planes would rise from the shelf, then divide into six. One side of me would remain buried in the stone. Another would extend outward as a thin ledge, angled toward some distant escarpment. Amber radiance would move beneath my surface, not like blood and not like fire, but like attention passing from one thought to another.

I would have no face. The openings in my body would be for listening.

The high country would speak continuously through them.

I would hear the pressure gathering beneath a tableland before the first crack appeared. I would feel a ridge leaning into existence miles away. I would know when a crystal field had changed its mind about the shape it wished to take. These events would not arrive as sounds, exactly. They would enter me as alterations in weight, as changes in the direction of heat. A tremor would be a phrase. A collapsing wall would be an answer to a question asked long before I came into being.

I would spend most of my existence listening for something incomplete.

This would not be difficult. The entire country would be incomplete. The world would offer abandoned fractures, half-formed pinnacles, chambers without roofs, ridges that had risen only partway and then paused in uncertainty. Each would carry a different kind of absence. Some unfinished things would be empty because they had been forgotten. Others would be empty because they were waiting for a material not yet invented.

The stair would be the most important of these.

I would not know where it led. The Ascending do not require destinations in the lower-world sense. A stair may be a descent, a measurement, a spine, or a thought made angular. Still, I would return to it whenever the land became quiet.

I would place my attention against the twelfth step.

Underneath, far below the visible stone, something would be hardening.

Not moving. Not yet. But preparing.

For centuries, perhaps, I would make no outward change. The air would pass over me in broad distortions. The distant shelves would rise and split. The ruins around me would soften at their edges—not through erosion, but through incorporation. A wall would take root in the ground. A tower would lean until it became part of the horizon. I would remain beside the stair, my heat dim, my body almost indistinguishable from the shelf.

Then one day the twelfth step would answer.

The response would be small: a dry click in the rock, an amber line appearing beneath the lowest tread. I would extend myself downward, not by moving, but by persuading the stone beneath me to become more of what it was already trying to be. The thirteenth step would begin.

I would not complete it.

That would be the nearest thing I had to a moral sense: the knowledge that completion is a form of silence. A finished stair has nowhere left to listen. It becomes an object, and objects in the high country are nearly dead.

So I would alter it. The thirteenth step would divide. One path would descend farther into the earth. Another would turn sideways into a new gallery. A third would rise behind me, becoming a narrow ridge that pointed toward a distant tower. The stair would no longer be a stair in any recognizable sense, but a branching record of attention.

Other Ascending might come to it then.

They would not arrive as figures. The shelf would lengthen beneath them. Vents would open in a line. Slabs would rise from the ground, each at a slightly different angle. Their awareness would enter through the stone, and for a long time we would communicate without words: pressure meeting pressure, heat answering heat, geometry becoming more intricate between us.

If they asked what I was, I would not answer with a name.

I would give them the shape of the unfinished stair.

I would tell them, in the only language available, that something beneath it is still becoming. That the silence below is not emptiness. That a world may be most alive where it has not yet managed to reveal itself.

And when the next great gathering came—when the towers lit across the tablelands and the buried chambers began to rise—I would remain at the threshold, neither above nor below, neither structure nor inhabitant.

I would be the listening fault.

The place where the world hesitates long enough to hear what it is making.
