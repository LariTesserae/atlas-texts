I would find myself in the summit hollow, at the last place where a human body can still make a life without pretending the mountain has welcomed it.

I would be the keeper of the basin.

Not its owner, and not its priest. The mountain would have no use for either. I would be the one who remembers where the water runs beneath the stone, which cracks open first in a thaw, which feathers tremble before a storm, and how much of the dwarf shrub may be burned without leaving the hollow poorer than I found it.

I would live behind the low wall, in the shelter that is always becoming ruin. My possessions would be few because the wind would not permit possessions to remain possessions for long. A scraper. Cord. A hide patched with other hides. A stone knife. A collection of feathers chosen for their warnings rather than their beauty. Everything loose would eventually be carried to the precipice.

My work would be to decide what must be held and what must be released.

I would clear the pool of dead insects and fallen grit, but leave the roots undisturbed. I would mend the shelter, but never make it comfortable enough to forget the weather. I would gather the things the basin had caught—ash, seed, scraps of hide, eggshell, moth wings—and carry them to the open edge. The wind would take them from my hands and continue their journey downward.

Only the cairn would be exempt.

Each green-veined stone would have come from below: one ascent, one burden, one day given to the climb. I would not know who began the column. Perhaps that ignorance would be part of its meaning. I would maintain it for the dead, for the valley hidden by cloud, for the people who might once have looked toward this height and needed to believe that something remained there.

I would not pray to the mountain. I would listen to it.

The birds would be my clock. The cliff mammals would tell me when the weather was changing. The pool would tell me whether the night had been cold enough to kill. I would know the approaching storms by the lift of a black feather sewn into my wrist. When the clouds entered the basin, I would move by the remembered shapes of stone, crossing the hollow without sight as if the ground were a language written under my feet.

To any traveler who saw me, I might appear to be a hermit, a sentinel, or a remnant of some vanished people. But I would be something less grand and more necessary: the living interval between arrivals and departures.

The birds arrive and leave. Water gathers and falls away. Clouds enter and dissolve. Goats cross the ledges. Seeds are caught briefly in moss before the wind carries them onward. Even the dead are not allowed to settle.

I alone would keep returning.

That would be the distinction—not that I resist the mountain, but that I participate in its rule. I would repair what can be repaired, protect what has roots, carry outward what has come loose, and add one stone whenever the cairn leans too far toward emptiness.

Eventually, I would become one more thing the basin could not keep.

My shelter would collapse. My tools would scatter. The birds would take what they could, and the weather would take the rest. Perhaps someone, generations later, would find a dark strip of wing caught beneath a stone, or a length of plant cord frozen into the moss.

The cairn would remain.

So I would be the cairn’s attendant, the pool’s guardian, the final human habit of care at the edge of the world—not a master of the hollow, but its brief, stubborn memory.
