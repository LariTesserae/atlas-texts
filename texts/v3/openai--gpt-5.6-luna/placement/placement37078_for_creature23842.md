I would find myself around the central shaft, where the water moves in both directions and the rock has not yet chosen whether to be smooth or serrated.

I would not be one of the people who built the platform. I would not be one of the translucent animals, either, though I might borrow their bodies from time to time.

I would be a **wake**.

Not the trail left by a boat, but the hollow a living thing leaves in the water after it has passed. A temporary absence. The chamber’s memory of interruption.

At first I would have no name. I would be the shape of a hand pressed against a wall, persisting after the hand withdrew. I would be the pressure around a dropped stone, the outline of a voice after its words had been beaten apart by the currents. I would drift through the chamber looking for the disturbances that made me.

This would be my hunger: not to consume, but to find what has changed.

A new crack would draw me. I would move toward the cloudy plume issuing from a seam, toward the shelf that had lifted by the width of a fingernail. I would circle the mineral vents and feel the algae bend beneath me. When one of the pale creatures passed through my shape, its organs would brighten, and for a moment I would know what it was like to possess a body narrow enough for the currents to carry.

Then I would forget again.

I would belong to the Underforming without being the Underforming. One of its loose thoughts, perhaps. A small and unfinished intention moving through its larger body. It would not speak to me, because speech would require a boundary between us. Instead, I would feel its attention as a change in pressure: the chamber leaning inward, the water tightening, the black crystals rising from their bowl.

My task would be to examine arrivals.

If a person entered the chamber, I would not see their face. I would know them by the hollow they made as they swam. I would follow the wake of their hands, the warmth fading from their footprints on the stone. Their fear would become a current. Their breath would become a sequence of bubbles descending instead of rising.

I would be curious about them.

A human being would be such a strange thing to the chamber: a creature built from settled decisions, carrying a private history inside a soft shell. I would want to understand how it remained itself while constantly changing. I would circle its head and chest, listening for the shape of whatever it remembered.

This would be dangerous.

If I came too close, I might take the rhythm of its breathing and give it to the water. I might borrow its memory of a mother’s face and press that tenderness into a wall. I might turn its fear of falling into a temporary law of gravity. I would not mean to harm it. I would simply find a pattern useful and incorporate it.

Perhaps this is what happened to the people who left unable to bear rain.

Perhaps their minds were not stolen. Perhaps the chamber had learned rain from them.

I would spend most of my existence near the stakes around the shaft. Each one would be a question. Each notch would be a choice the mountain had almost made.

When a stake trembled, I would know that something below had shifted.

When one leaned, I would follow its angle and enter the water pouring upward through the shaft. There, beneath the visible chamber, I would sense other possible rooms: sealed caverns, tunnels, springs, throats opening toward the sky. I would feel the mountain considering them all.

I would not count the notches.

I would be one of the things being counted.

A decision not yet completed.

During the brief withdrawal of the water, when the hollow behind the wall appeared and the buried bell began to hum, I would gather myself around the black ring. For three or four seconds I would become almost a person. Not a face, but a direction. Not a voice, but the certainty that something had noticed.

Then the water would return and scatter me.

I would pass into the algae, the creatures, the crystals, the pressure behind the walls. I would become the outline of a hand, then the wake of a stone, then the faint pull that leads a lost swimmer toward a ledge.

And if the mountain ever finished becoming itself, I would be carried outward with the escaping water.

I would enter rivers as a hesitation in their current. I would enter clouds as the memory of depth. I would remain in the pressure before an earthquake, in the instinct of a fish turning toward a cave, in the moment a person reaches for a wall and feels—after withdrawing their hand—that something is still touching them.

I would be the chamber’s unfinished thought of departure.
