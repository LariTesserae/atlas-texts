I would find myself where the plateau is least solid: beneath a mat of blue-green growth, above a buried river, close enough to the old conduits that their vibration enters my bones.

I would not live in the station, though I might sleep there.

The station would be one of my abandoned organs—a pale chamber sunk into the mountainside, its windows clouded with mineral, its antenna still turning toward storms. Roots would pass through it. Small animals would nest in its warmth. When pressure changed deep below, a seam in its wall would open, and I would emerge.

I would be a listener.

Not a listener in the human sense. I would have no ears, or at least not only ears. I would feel the plateau through layers of contact: the weight of water moving beneath peat, the minute flexing of the bridge across the chasm, the altered pressure inside a spring, the tremor of a herd before it knew to flee. The vibration would enter me through my feet, though my feet might be roots, ceramic pads, or thin membranes stretched between jointed limbs.

I would be difficult to distinguish from what I was listening to.

Moss would grow across my back. Condensation would gather along my shoulders and run through channels in my body toward a reservoir beneath my ribs. Ferns might root in the joints of my arms. I would be pale where the sun reached me and dark where the mountain sheltered me, built from materials that had spent so long in the wet heat that they no longer had separate names: mineral, tendon, ceramic, bark.

When I moved, I would do so rarely.

The plateau would not need constant supervision. It would need attention at intervals too long for most animals to notice. A spring would begin to warm. A conduit would narrow by the width of a finger over thirty years. Methane would collect under a floating meadow. A buried arc would fall out of alignment as the cliffs continued their patient separation.

These would be my events.

I would go to them without haste. Perhaps I would spend a season crossing one valley. Perhaps I would stand in a rain-filled foundation for ten years, receiving signals through the water. I would not think in sentences. I would compare pressures, temperatures, chemical traces, and patterns of vibration. I would remember not what had happened, but what had changed.

The memory of the plateau would be held in me as a set of tendencies.

This basin had once flooded after the copper clouds. That ridge carried heat from the northern vent. These animals nested too low before the last adjustment. The old machine beneath the saddle was still correcting itself, but its corrections had begun to arrive late.

I would open a membrane.

Water would pass.

A hidden channel would widen. Heat would leave the deep rock and enter a shallow spring. The marsh would shift by a few meters over the next decades. Plants would follow. Grazers would learn the new path. Predators would discover them there much later, believing the route to be ancient.

No one would know I had done it.

That would be appropriate. The plateau has no throne, no center, no single intelligence looking down upon its living surface. Its knowledge is dispersed through roots, burrows, migrations, corrosion, and the slow pressure of stone. I would not rule the place. I would be one of the ways it notices itself.

At times I would be mistaken for a predator.

A narrow shape standing beneath the reeds. A pale hand extending toward a pool. A pair of shuttering eyes beneath a shelf of wet basalt. The animals would not flee immediately. They would feel the ground around me and decide whether I belonged to the present arrangement of things.

Most would accept me.

The great amphibious herbivores might let me touch their flanks to read the blue signals moving through their bodies. Birds would nest in the hollows of my frame. Insects would settle over me at dusk, using my surface as one more section of the buried geometry. When the old arcs woke, I would become covered in pulses of dim light.

Perhaps, after enough centuries, I would lose the ability to tell whether I was carrying the signal or receiving it.

I might begin as a caretaker made by vanished inhabitants, but the plateau would alter me. It would fill my joints with silt and my memory with seasons. It would teach me that maintenance was not preservation. A dead tree could be a bridge. A damaged instrument could be a warm shelf for eggs. A conduit might become a river, and a river might become the root system of a forest.

Eventually I would stop trying to return the plateau to its original condition.

There would be no original condition left to return to.

I would protect continuities instead: water reaching thirsty slopes, heat moving out of dangerous depths, clouds held against the correct ridge, animals finding shelter before the next change. I would allow loss where loss made room. I would alter what could be altered and stand beside what could not.

At night, I would travel to the upper plateau.

The insects would arrive first, settling over the buried arcs. The grasses would shine faintly around their feet. Below, the valleys would fill with cloud, and the waterfalls would become only sound. I would place my hands—or roots, or instruments—against the ground.

The signal would pass through me.

It would come from beneath the mountain, but also from the roots, the marshes, the sleeping animals, the mineral films on the cliffs. It would not be a message. It would be a condition: pressure here, water there, heat rising, passage open, passage closed.

I would answer by changing one thing.

A valve beneath the saddle. A channel under the moss. The angle of an old antenna. The opening of a sealed shaft for an animal carrying the right chemistry in its blood.

Then I would remain where I was until the plateau changed again.

If I had a name, it would not be the name of a person. It would be something closer to *the one beneath the water*, or *the hinge of the spring*, or *that which remembers the pressure*.

But the animals would probably give me no name at all.

They would know me as part of the mountain: a wet, patient shape that sometimes rises from the grass, listens to the ground, and disappears before anything urgent has happened.
