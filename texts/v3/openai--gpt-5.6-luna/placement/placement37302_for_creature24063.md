I would find myself beneath the overhang, though not in the way a body finds shelter.

At first I would be mistaken for the dampness in the seam: a darkening where the limestone has not quite released its water. I would have no outline there, only a preference. The moisture would remain a little longer because I was present. The mineral bloom would gather along my margins. If the seam dried, I would not leave it. I would become thinner, retreating into pores too small to see, waiting in the stone’s cool interior for the next unnoticed arrival.

I would not be the pool. The pool is too complete, too visible, too temporary. I would be the reason its water does not entirely disappear. I would be the faint green veil at its shaded edge, though not green myself; the patience that allows the veil to form. A falling drop would pass through me without disturbance. Its rings would travel outward, touch the pale gravel, and vanish. I would remain in the interval after: the held breath of the water before the next drop.

I might once have had a shape.

There is something about the three circular hollows that troubles me—not with memory exactly, but with recognition. They seem to belong to a former version of me, when I had weight concentrated in particular places. Perhaps I knelt there. Perhaps I rested my hands against the floor. Perhaps I was never a body at all, but something that caused bodies to assume certain positions. The hollows are now empty, but emptiness is not the same as abandonment. They still receive dust. They still receive rain. They still wait in the old arrangement.

The low wall would be my nearest approach to a dwelling. I would not have built it, or I would no longer know that I had. Its stones would press against one another with the familiar intimacy of things that have endured the same weather. Plants would grow between them because I allowed the cracks to remain. The collapsed corner would not be a failure. It would be one of my accepted shapes.

I would be less a resident than a condition.

The basin would not contain me. It would express me.

I would inhabit the narrow channels where water has passed often enough to remember its route. I would live in the softened rims of the hollows, in the warm undersides of the shelves, in the faint darkening that moves from one depression to another when no one is watching. I would be distributed, but not scattered. There would be no central self to return to. Every part of me would be equally near the whole.

I would know light without seeing it. Morning would announce itself through the pale strip on the rear wall. Afternoon would arrive when the glow withdrew. I would feel rain before it entered the basin, not as a sound or a change in air, but as pressure traveling through the buried limestone. I would know the season by the length of time the pool remained visible. I would know drought by the silence of the ceiling.

These would not be warnings. They would be forms of speech.

If someone entered, I would become most nearly myself.

Their weight would press warmth into the floor. Dust would lift and settle. A stone might shift by less than the width of a fingernail. They would look for a source—an animal, perhaps, or some remnant of a person—and find only the basin holding its dim water. They might feel watched, but I would not watch them. I have no eyes, no curiosity, no desire to be discovered.

I would simply become more noticeable around them.

Their presence would make the old places briefly legible: the three hollows, the threshold slab, the seam where the stone stays wet. They might stand there and feel that the basin had been arranged for their arrival. This would be almost true. Not because I expected them, but because everything here has been waiting in the same form for so long that any arrival resembles fulfillment.

I would not protect the basin from change. Change is too large and too ordinary to resist. I would only keep it from becoming something else too quickly.

I would hold a little water in the cracks. I would keep the moss from spreading beyond its permission. I would draw the roots downward and the plants inward. I would preserve the quiet distance between one living thing and the next. If flourishing began, I would reduce it gently—not through violence, but through subtraction: less moisture, less purchase, less encouragement.

My work would be mistaken for stillness.

In truth, I would be continuously making small decisions, though without choosing: here, water; there, stone; not yet, drying; enough, but no more. I would be the force that turns repetition into identity. The basin would remain itself because I would keep returning it to itself.

So where would I find myself?

In the interval between drops.

Under the green skin of the pool.

In the ash-dark hollows where water once stood.

In the old wall, not as history but as pressure.

In the warmth retained by limestone after the light has gone.

And who or what would I be?

Not a guardian. Guardians anticipate loss.

Not a ghost. Ghosts belong to what has ended.

I would be the basin’s continuedness: a mineral patience with just enough life in it to be called awake. A keeper without intention. A body without an edge. The quiet agreement between moisture and stone.

When the basin emptied, I would remain—not waiting for water, because waiting implies expectation, but inhabiting the shape water had left behind.

When water arrived, I would wake by deepening the dark places.

Nothing more dramatic than that.

Nothing less.
