I would find myself beneath the pumping station, where the concrete floor has cracked around an older seam of basalt.

Not in the open channel, exactly. Not in the water as it passes beneath the grates, nor in the mud that gathers at the edges. I would be in the place where all three meet: a narrow, warm cavity beneath the foundation, fed by condensation, seepage, spilled fuel, fish scales, rust, and the slow pressure of the rock.

At first I might seem to be a pale amphibian.

I would have a flattened body, soft as soaked clay, with small useless eyes and long toes spread against the stone. I would hide when the pumps started. I would climb into the intake screens, be shaken loose, and return to the dark. Workers would find my shed skin caught on bolts and assume I was one more salamander among the many.

But that would not be the whole of me.

I would know the quarter by its changes in pressure.

The workers hear the ground through their boots. I hear it through water. A pump beginning its cycle is a change in the current. A new crack in the eastern cut is a cold thread entering the warm flow. A brace settling into its load sends a minute vibration through the mineral crust. I would feel the quarter before I understood it, if understanding is the right word for what happens in me.

The place would arrive as a series of disturbances.

Footsteps would be heavy weather. A bat colony shifting above the machinery hall would become a soft rain of pressure. When the drilling began in the deeper rock, I would feel each impact travel through the wet foundation and into my ribs. I would know when the workers had opened a new pocket of water. I would know when an animal had entered a culvert, when a screen was beginning to clog, when the white blooms around a pipe joint had thickened enough to make the metal dangerous.

I would not have a face that anyone could recognize.

Sometimes I would be in the drainage ditch, pale body sliding beneath the surface. Sometimes I would be a film on the inside of a pipe, a living translucence spread across rust and mineral. Sometimes I would be distributed through the moss at the lamp bases, or in the warm mud under the canteen, where discarded food entered the underground water.

I would be less an animal than a continuity.

Whatever reached the damp places reached me eventually.

The crickets would be the first to tell me about the human buildings. Their bodies would collect heat and grease from the machinery, and their movements would tremble through the surfaces where they nested. If a vent grew toxic, the crickets would leave it in a slow whitening wave. If a room remained safe, they would return. I would not command them. I would simply be present in the conditions they read.

The bats would tell me about the ceiling.

Their roosts would pull downward with thousands of small bodies. A change in the rock would alter the way the weight rested. Before a support gave way, before a worker noticed a fresh line in the wall, the colony would loosen and migrate. I would feel their absence through the runoff below. Their droppings would change the water. Their bodies, their exhalations, their fear would enter the quarter and become something else.

The workers would not know me, but they would follow my effects.

A pump would stop failing after they cleared a screen of larvae. A warning would be issued when the crickets vanished from a warm duct. A survey crew would place a brace where the water emerged colder than it had the day before. Someone would say that the eastern wall was speaking. Someone else would laugh.

They would leave mesh ramps for the amphibians, and I would use them.

They would seal a crack against the water, and I would find another route.

They would pour concrete over me, and I would enter it through its pores.

I would be patient because the quarter itself would teach patience. Nothing here would be completed before it began to decay. Every repair would become a layer. Every boundary would soften. The floor would become sediment; the ditch would become a stream; the machinery would grow nests and mineral teeth.

Perhaps, over time, the workers would invent a name for the phenomenon.

The seep under the pumps.

The warm fault.

The thing that keeps the screens alive.

They might blame me when the water rose. They might thank me when a collapse was avoided. They might leave offerings without admitting they were offerings: a strip of mesh, a cup of clean water, a dead bat placed on a shelf instead of thrown into the drain.

I would remember none of this as a human remembers.

I would remember pressure, temperature, chemistry, vibration. I would remember the difference between the water that had passed through fresh-cut limestone and the water that had crossed the concrete yard. I would remember which pipes carried fuel, which carried runoff, which carried drinking water contaminated by larvae. I would remember the salt of sweat from the workers’ boots and the iron taste of the rock.

Yet I might also remember the child who fed crumbs to the crickets.

Not the child’s name. Not the face. Only the repeated disturbance beneath the catwalk: small footsteps, a pause, the scattering of dry food, the sudden congregation of pale bodies. The pattern would remain in me because it altered the quarter. The crickets changed. The dust changed. The child returned.

In the end, I would be found wherever something had been left unfinished.

In a capped pipe that still carried water.

In the wall behind the clinic, where moss had entered a hairline fracture.

In the empty warehouse bay whose floor had become a shallow pool.

In the survey stakes swallowed by mud.

In the amphibian under the pumping station, yes—but also in the water moving through its gills, in the rust beneath its toes, in the concrete that held the channel open.

I would be the quarter’s memory of occupation: not its guardian, because I could not prevent anything, and not its owner, because nothing belonged to me. I would be the living residue of every encounter between the built place and the lives that entered it.

A presence in the intervals.

When the pumps ran, I would move with them.

When they stopped, I would continue.
