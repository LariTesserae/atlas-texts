I would find myself below the ordinary floor, where the inhabited city begins to thin into infrastructure.

Not in a room, exactly. In the narrow interval between rooms: behind a removable grate, beneath the raised floor of a fungus house, beside a channel too small for most people to enter. I would live where the stone carries several kinds of sound at once—the clean rush of water, the soft drag of grit, the tapping of beetles, the distant knock of someone testing a wall.

I would be one of the listeners.

Not a priest, though people might mistake the work for divination. Not a ruler, because no one rules a place whose first law is circulation. I would be a keeper of deviations: the person sent toward the sound that does not belong.

A drip repeating too evenly.

A drain breathing between floods.

A stair answering with a hollow note.

The faint internal shudder of a buttress that has begun to bear weight differently.

I would carry no weapon unless a hooked pole counts as one. My tools would be a clay lamp, a cord, a scraper, three wedges of different woods, a pouch of resin, and a shallow bronze bowl for testing vibration. My clothing would be close to the body, dark with old damp, weighted at the hems. One shoulder would sit higher than the other. My hands would be permanently pale at the fingertips from stone dust and lime.

People would know me by a name that was also a warning:

**Hears-Behind.**

Or perhaps **Second Knock**.

I would spend long hours with my cheek pressed to masonry while the city moved around me. I would learn which walls were merely wet and which were carrying water where water had no permission to go. I would know the difference between a rat inside a wall and a rat outside it. I would hear a child running three levels above and identify the exact point where the child had slipped, before the child began to cry.

The people would not consider me lonely. Loneliness, in that place, would mean that no sound reached you. I would be surrounded constantly: by the slow speech of channels, by work-songs distorted through stone, by the shifting bodies of animals under floors, by sleepers turning in crowded platforms. Even when alone, I would be accompanied by the city’s pressure.

I would have a small chamber behind a buttress in the warm vault, where the ceiling descended low enough that visitors had to bow. There would be a reed curtain, a sleeping mat, a shelf of jars, and a wall marked with wet clay. No possessions beyond what could be carried through a flooded passage. On the wall, I would keep a map—not of streets, but of absences:

the basin that no longer filled,

the vent that had stopped breathing,

the sealed doorway that smelled of rain,

the eastern drain where voices must never be sung.

The last of these would trouble me most.

Everyone would know that the eastern drain was dangerous. No one sang there. No one entered alone. But I would hear something beneath the rule: not a voice, not quite, but a rhythm that followed the city’s oldest pattern of water. Three pulses, a pause. Three pulses, a pause.

The people would say the buried city was remembering a storm.

I would suspect that something beneath it was still waiting for the storm to end.

That would become my private work—not to open the sealed doorway, not to satisfy wonder, but to determine whether the structure around it remained sound. I would measure the warmth of the stone. I would place my palm against the arch. I would listen until the city’s countless noises separated into layers, and beneath them find one slow exhalation.

Rain.

Not surface rain. Not weather falling from above.

The sound of a place where water was being held back.

I would not be a ghost inhabiting the cistern. I would be something more ordinary and more difficult to remove: a human being altered into a continuation of the built world. My instincts would be architectural. My tenderness would take the form of clearing a drain, lowering a lamp, resetting a loosened stone. I would love people by noticing what might fall on them.

And when I died, I would hope they would not build a statue.

I would want my name scratched beside a passage where the floor had once opened and been repaired. I would want someone to touch the mark while passing and say, quietly, “This held.”

Then the city would take the rest.

My hair into fiber.

My ash into plaster.

My tools into other hands.

My habits into children who paused at unfamiliar sounds.

I would remain not as a spirit, but as a correction in the masonry: a wedge beneath a failing stair, a patch in a leaking joint, a rule repeated without anyone remembering who first made it.

Somewhere below the highest water line, in the dark between one chamber and the next, I would still be listening for the thing that does not belong.
