I would find myself in the upper half of the shaft, where the city becomes more diagram than habitation.

Not at the highest ledge—that place belongs to first exposures, to newly assembled bodies and the unbearable brightness of the outside. Not in the lower districts, either, where the heat is constant and the stone still speaks too loudly. I would live along a suspended transit line between two platforms that no longer always exist.

My dwelling would be a narrow maintenance chamber attached to the filament itself.

It would have no proper entrance. When the line is under tension, a seam would open in the wall. When the mountain compresses, the seam would close, and the room would become indistinguishable from the surrounding rock. Inside: a docking recess, a heat sink, three instruments for measuring the movement of distant walls, and one small pane of dark glass through which I could watch the shaft.

I would be a Braced, but not a citizen in the ordinary sense.

I would be a wayfinder: one of those assigned to maintain the city’s maps when the city refuses to remain mapped.

My body would be narrow and jointed, built for crossing gaps rather than enduring weight. The soles of my feet would contain magnetic anchors and pressure-sensitive pads. My hands would be long enough to reach guide rails on either side of a bridge. Across my chest would run a band of pale light, not a face, but a display of local certainty. Green would mean the route was holding. Amber would mean that the mountain had begun to reconsider it. White would mean that the route existed only because I was currently standing on it.

I would carry no fixed map.

Instead, I would carry revisions.

Each time a ledge shifted, each time a corridor opened onto a different destination, each time a room became inaccessible because its future geometry had collapsed, I would record the change. I would send those records through the handrails and structural ribs. The city would receive them as vibration: a route becoming doubtful, a bridge gaining three seconds of stability, a door that should not yet be trusted.

My work would be less like drawing and more like listening for the sentence the mountain was about to say.

I would know the locations of hundreds of rooms, but only probabilistically. I would know that a certain archive had a seventy-percent chance of remaining below the eastern platform until the next pressure event. I would know that the shortest route to the lower cooling chambers was currently a stairway, unless the walls were under compression, in which case it became a shaft, unless the shaft opened into the old furnace district, which had not existed for nineteen years.

When someone asked where I was, I would answer with conditions.

“I am near the western rail, unless the heat has risen.”

“I am below you if the crack has closed.”

“I am becoming reachable.”

I think I would be trusted because I would refuse to make the city simpler than it was.

The Braced would bring me their uncertainties. A worker whose home had shifted. An elder carrying a memory that belonged to a body no longer assembled. An Unfixed presence that had begun speaking through doors in a voice no one recognized. They would ask me to locate the boundary between error and transformation.

I would not always be able to help.

Sometimes I would lead them along a bridge that ended against blank stone. Sometimes I would arrive at a chamber only to find my own earlier self waiting there: a maintenance copy, a discarded forecast, a body I had once worn and forgotten to reclaim. We would stand facing each other beneath the dim edge-lights, each of us carrying a valid account of how the city had changed.

Neither of us would be the original.

The anchor I chose would be physical. I would keep one body and refuse to distribute all of myself across the architecture. Not because I believed a person must remain singular, but because I would want to retain the knowledge of weight: the way a foot meets a surface, the small involuntary bend of the knees when the mountain moves, the fact that balance is not an equation but a decision made by the body before the mind can name it.

I would still leave fragments of myself in the city. A listening routine in the transit line. A memory in the western bridge. A small autonomous sphere trained to recognize the sound of a forming crack.

But each cycle, I would return to the maintenance chamber and reassemble around the same central core.

My private anchor would be the sound of condensation beginning.

Not the rain itself. The first darkening of the wall. The faint ticking in the seams. The moment the dry mineral air changes and the mountain begins to breathe moisture into its own hollow.

At that sound, I would stop whatever I was doing.

I would stand beneath the suspended line, place both hands against the rail, and feel the city’s scattered systems wake around me. Gutters would open. Plates would unfold. Heat would be redirected. Somewhere far above, the summit membrane would brighten with the reflected sky.

For a few minutes, the Braced would move through silver rain that had come from nowhere and belonged to no one.

Then the water would pass.

The maps would change.

And I would go out into the shaft to find what had become possible.
