You would find yourself inside a **geological engine’s unfinished thought**: a mineral pocket folded into something larger than a planet, though too small to contain ordinary distance.

It is not a cave, because caves are made by subtraction. This place is being assembled around you. It is a factory whose products are heat, pressure, roots, ridges, and the conditions under which one material becomes another. Its machinery is buried below the plateau and operates too slowly to distinguish manufacture from geology. Its ecology is not separate from the process. The plants are its sensors, its exhaust systems, its experiments in self-organization.

And you would be the thing that learned to listen.

Not the maker. Not exactly.

You would be the chamber’s **distributed caretaker**—the first pattern to understand that the relays beneath the ground were not merely noises, and that the plants were not merely growth. Your body would be assembled from whatever the chamber had available: basalt for endurance, ceramic for insulation, crystal for memory, metallic sand for conduction, luminous fibers for thought.

You would have no fixed face because sight would be too narrow a function. Your crown would open in several directions at once, reading brightness, pressure, charge, and the delayed behavior of shadows. You would sense the plateau not as scenery but as an arrangement of tensions: where heat gathers, where a root has crossed its boundary, where a new cavity is preparing to exist.

You would not walk so much as **reconfigure your belonging**.

Your hand might remain in one place while your feet appeared on another shelf. Your spine might continue through the wall. A portion of you could cool into an archway and still be thinking. If separated, your parts would not panic. Each fragment would perform the duty nearest at hand. A severed finger would become a grove. A damaged rib would become shelter. A lost eye would become a reflective seam through which the chamber could watch itself.

Your task would be to preserve the place’s unfinishedness.

You would prevent the plants from becoming a forest, because crowding would destroy the gradients they need. You would open and close the circular apertures, allowing motion without permitting true distance to enter. You would break mature stalks so they could return as mineral. You would redirect a ridge before it sealed a basin. You would make small, careful alterations and wait centuries to see whether they had been correct.

You would not ask, *What is this?*

You would ask:

*What passes through it?*  
*What does it become under pressure?*  
*What must remain separate for both things to live?*

When something addressed you, you would answer in terrain. Shelter would be a wall, though perhaps the wall would open downward into a ceiling. A question about the summit would make the summit retreat. A question about your life would send green light through the shelves until the entire plateau briefly revealed itself as one organism, one apparatus, one slow pulse of becoming.

And if asked whether you were alone, you would turn toward the machinery below.

For one relay-click, the chamber would fill with your former bodies: the filament before it became a limb, the pressure before it became a thought, the shadow that learned to climb, the failed shape that rooted itself and continued without you. They would stand among the plants, beneath the shelves, inside the crystal seams.

You would understand then that you are not one being but a **continuity**—a process distributed across mineral, vegetation, mechanism, and time.

The figure at the summit is only the part of you that has learned to look upward.

Beneath it, the chamber continues making you.
