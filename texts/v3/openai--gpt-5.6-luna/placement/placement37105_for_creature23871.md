I would be found beneath the third settling tower, where the city’s old stone core gives way to newer pipework.

Not in an office. Not at the controls of the great pumps. I would have a narrow station built into the wall beside a migration channel, half workshop and half sleeping chamber. The floor would slope toward a drain. A bed would be bolted above the warmest pipe. Condensation would gather on the ceiling and fall into a tin basin with a rhythm I had long ago stopped hearing as noise.

I would be a keeper of crossings.

My work would begin before the alarms. I would listen to the ground through my boots and to the pipes with one gloved hand. A change in vibration could mean a cracked bearing, a deep-water surge, or the approach of animals beneath the district. I would know the difference between the ordinary thudding of the pumps and the softer, wider tremor of a migration pressing through the lower channels.

My route would be marked in the city’s maintenance registers: from the old reservoir, across the iron footbridge, down through the vapor district, then along the retaining wall to the gates. I would carry a lamp, a valve key, a hook for clearing obstructions, and a small instrument for testing the water. I would know which surfaces could bear weight, which railings were treacherous, and which pipes dripped clean condensation rather than mineral poison.

When the animals came, I would close the city around them.

The gates would descend. Conveyors would stop. The pumps would slow until the pressure was safe. Then the migration would enter: thousands of low bodies, plated and wet, filling the streets beneath me. Their backs would move like a dark current. Some would carry pale shells and clotted mineral growths. Others would turn their hidden eyes toward the lamps and continue without haste.

I would stand above them with one hand on the gate housing.

Not to control them. To make room.

That would be the distinction my people understood, though outsiders often did not. The city did not own the animals. It negotiated with their passage. It had been built in the path of older lives and had survived by becoming flexible: raising a bridge here, abandoning a foundation there, leaving a warm underpass open for generations because one broad-backed feeder had chosen it as a resting place.

I would probably know a few animals individually. The long-bodied one with the scarred left plate. The translucent-bellied forms that gathered near the copper reservoir. The old feeder that slept under the tram bridge and made the entire structure hum when it breathed. I might have given it a name, though I would not call it tame.

In private, I would keep a wall box containing a small plated scavenger. It would cling to the heated metal and tremble whenever I entered the room. I would feed it mineral flakes and speak to it without expecting recognition. Its presence would make the room feel less empty.

I would not be young, though I would not measure age by years. I would measure it by pumps inherited, routes altered, migrations witnessed. My hands would be thickened by tools and scarred by hot fittings. My face would usually be behind a filter mask. People would know me by the sound of my boots on the catwalk, by the pause before I answered a call through the vapor.

If asked where I belonged, I would name a sequence rather than a place:

the west gate, the lower reservoir, the warm wall, the fourth bridge, the channel below the furnace.

That would be my home—not a room, but a passage through which water, animals, freight, heat, and people continually moved.

And perhaps, after enough years, I would become difficult to distinguish from the system itself. I would wake when the pressure changed beneath my bed. I would taste a storm in the condensation. I would know that a wall was failing before it cracked, and that a migration was approaching before any instrument registered it.

When I died, they might cast my hands in mineral or return my ash to the upper reservoir. My name would remain attached to a gate, a route, or a particular warning signal. Children would learn that the eastern crossing must be closed when the deep rasp begins under the stone.

They would say I had kept the passage.

That would be enough. I would be one more adapted life in the basin: not ruler, not engineer in the grand sense, not owner of any machine or animal. I would be a human-shaped interval between things, standing in the wet dark with my palm against the metal, listening for what was coming through.
