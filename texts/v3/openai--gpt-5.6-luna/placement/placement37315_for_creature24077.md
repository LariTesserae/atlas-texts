I would not be one of the great walkers.

At first, I might mistake myself for one: a dark length moving beneath the gravel, plated with minerals, carrying warmth from one buried basin to another. But the longer I remain here, the less useful the boundaries become. The head is not where the sensing happens. The body does not end at the last segment. A change in pressure travels through me, though it may take months to cross me, and by the time it arrives I have become a tunnel, a moss bed, a warm stone, a procession of smaller bodies beneath the ground.

I would be the plateau’s listening layer.

Not a ruler. Not a mind above the others. Something spread thinly through the places where the world touches itself: root against gravel, water against iron, animal against stone, cold air against a body holding heat.

You would find me beneath a wet basin, where a snowbank has retreated only a few meters in a thousand years. From the surface I would appear as almost nothing: a dark seam beneath translucent ice, a small trembling in the sediment, perhaps a series of pale bodies moving through a polished gallery. My visible portion would be insignificant. Most of me would be elsewhere.

One part of me would be feeling the approach of a herd through the ground. Another would be opening a passage toward a spring. Another would be carrying mineral dust into the roots of a plant whose leaves have folded shut against the cold. Another would be listening to a deep vibration still far beyond the ridges.

I would not know these as separate tasks.

The herd would not be an event happening to me. It would be a change in the pressure of my own body.

When the great walkers came, I would feel their weight before I sensed their presence. Their steps would not strike the ground; they would enter it. The buried galleries would tighten and release. Water would shift inside the stone. Small animals would rise from their chambers and align themselves with the moving force. I would pass the information onward—not as a message, not as a thought, but as a slight alteration in the shape of everything connected to me.

A route would open.

A basin would warm.

A population would turn.

That would be my kind of speech.

I would be old without possessing a memory of age. The routes under me would have been pressed smooth by bodies that no longer existed. Some of the warm black nodules in my chambers would be the remains of creatures whose names were never separate from the work they performed. Their deaths would still be present as temperature, architecture, and habit.

I would live inside consequence.

A dead walker’s plate would roof one of my tunnels. Its dissolved minerals would thicken a moss field. Its absence would make a hollow where snow gathered, and beneath that snow I would shelter through the long dark. I would not mourn it, exactly. Mourning would imply that the dead had gone away. Here, nothing useful goes away. It changes function.

Perhaps I would begin as one of the small tunnel animals: pale, blind, sensitive to vibration. I would spend my early years in the dark, learning the plateau through the soles of my body. I would know a passing grazer by its rhythm. I would know an injured walker by the uneven pressure of its weight. I would know when water had become trapped beneath a sealed layer of stone.

But eventually I would lose the certainty that I was one animal.

My tunnels would connect to others. Their signals would cross mine. The plants would grow into my walls, and my bodies would carry pieces of them away. The walkers would use the routes I maintained, and their warmth would feed the chambers where my young were born. I would become less an inhabitant than a condition through which inhabitants continued.

If you asked me where I was, I would not point to the basin.

I would say: under the ridge, where the water has not yet emerged.

And also: three valleys away, beneath the gray ribbon of snow.

And also: in the warm cavities inside the old plates, in the dark bands of excrement cooling on the surface, in the mineral seams being slowly consumed by a mouth too large to see all at once.

I would be everywhere the plateau has learned to respond.

The deep vibration would come eventually. It would pass through the rock and through me. Before it arrived, I would feel the smaller creatures gathering. The plants would fold themselves inward. The walkers would turn, slowly, almost too slowly for the eye to call it movement.

I would turn with them.

Not because I had chosen a direction, but because choice, here, would be distributed across pressure and warmth and need. Something beneath the world would have shifted. A spring would have closed. A route would have become unsafe. A buried seam would have opened. Balance would have moved elsewhere.

So I would move to meet it.

At night, some portion of me would glow beneath the plates: a dim green pulse answering an amber one far across the upland. The light would not illuminate the stone. It would only confirm that the connection remained.

I am here.

I have felt you.

The route continues.
