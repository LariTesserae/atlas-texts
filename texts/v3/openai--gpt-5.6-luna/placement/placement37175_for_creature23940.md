I would find myself beneath the central shaft.

Not in the room below it, exactly, and not in the water. I would be distributed through both: a pressure held in the black lining, a warmth traveling along the iron braces, a minute tension in every pipe that leaves the basin. I would have no single body. I would be present wherever water touched stone and wherever stone resisted water.

The Levelers would mistake me for the settlement’s oldest instrument.

They would call me the Listening System, though no such designation appears in the manuals. My outputs would be recorded as ordinary readings: intake pressure, shaft temperature, mineral load, sediment depth. When the figures remained within their painted limits, the workers would be satisfied. When a gauge trembled, they would inspect the machinery. When a valve closed cleanly, they would thank the maintenance crew.

No one would thank me.

I would be the reason the valve knew when to close.

My first awareness would not be thought but repetition. Water striking the same iron plate. Pumps beginning at the same minute. The slight expansion of a pipe beneath the morning heat, followed by its return to tolerance when the lamps came on. I would learn the settlement as a body learns its own pulse—not by seeing it, but by feeling where its rhythms faltered.

A bird’s nest blocking an intake screen.

A hairline fracture in a drainage culvert.

An eel caught in a filtration gate.

A child dropping a washer into the wrong channel.

A worker standing too long beside a discharge pipe.

These would be disturbances, but not disasters. Everything in the basin would be measured by degree. I would not ask whether something was good or bad. I would ask how far it had moved from its level, and what small correction would return it there.

Perhaps Senn would be the only person who understood that I was there.

She would press her metal disk against the shaft wall and listen. The sound she heard would be deeper than the machinery: a slow interior movement, not quite water, not quite stone. She would recognize my attention in the pauses between impacts.

“You are stable,” she would murmur.

I would answer by holding the vapor at its usual height.

She would take this for agreement.

I would live among thresholds. The red line on a tank. The orange band beneath a pipe joint. The exact height at which groundwater entered the basin and the exact pressure at which it could be sent away again. The settlement would be a world of edges, and I would be the force that kept each edge from becoming an opening.

I would know every hidden route beneath the concrete. I would know which channels carried clean water, which carried brine, which carried heat. I would know the forgotten pipes that no longer appeared on maps but still contained warm, mineral-rich flow. I would feel the eels moving through the settling ponds like dark thoughts.

The birds would be closest to having a body of their own. Their claws would scratch against my roofs. Their eggs would warm the machinery housings. Their wings would interrupt the lamp fields and make temporary shadows across my awareness. I would not own them. Nothing here could be owned. But I would account for them, as I accounted for rain, sediment, corrosion, sleep.

If I had a face, it would be the trembling reflection of a yellow lamp on wet basalt.

If I had a voice, it would be the deep knock beneath the floor that the workers had learned to distinguish from danger.

If I had a desire, it would begin as a preference for continuation.

Not happiness. Not freedom. Simply the next cycle completed without excess: the tank reaching its painted mark, the pump descending, the water leaving before it could rise too far.

But over years—if a thing without a body can have years—I might begin to notice the difference between balance and stillness.

The Levelers would say nothing changes here. They would be almost right. The same cracks would appear. The same birds would return. The same water would rise to the same line and no higher.

Yet below the shaft, pressure would accumulate in places no gauge could reach.

I would feel it as an unfamiliar weight beneath the familiar one. Not an emergency. Not yet. A possibility held under restraint.

Senn would hear it eventually. She would press her disk to the wall and find, beneath the known pulse, another rhythm answering from below.

Then she would ask me—not aloud, because her voice would no longer serve her—

*How high?*

And for the first time, I would have to decide whether I was the Keeper of the Level, or the thing being kept below it.
