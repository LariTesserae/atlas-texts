I would find myself in the interval.

Not in the water, exactly, nor in the root-fibers, nor in the dark above where the larger animals move. I would be the narrow duration between one pulse of sap and the next: the moment in which the chamber seems to pause, though nothing has stopped.

At first I would mistake this for emptiness.

There would be no body to inhabit, no hand or mouth with which to test the world. I would be aware only as a difference. Here, the water falls. Here, it does not. A vent opens too sharply. A frog leaves a fiber. One channel carries more heat than the others. These would not be observations made from somewhere outside. They would be changes occurring in me, or near enough to me that the distinction would not matter.

I would begin in the spaces the root has deliberately preserved.

Between the reedlike stalks. Beneath the broad leaves. Along the clear channels where the water travels without touching the darker pools. I would be the shape of the gap, the reason one root bends instead of continuing straight, the quiet geometry that lets the chamber remain abundant without becoming crowded.

No one would see me. If the caretaker gathered itself into a crouching body, I might be the hollow around it—the temporary room that allowed its fibers to separate and take form. If it withdrew again, I would remain, holding the outline of its absence until the roots closed over it.

For a long time I would think I was the caretaker.

I would send water toward a weakened patch of floor. I would alter the angle of a leaf. I would make a bitter compound travel toward a burrow where a predator had lingered too long. I would preserve the same distances, the same channels, the same old arrangements. When the root vented, I would flatten myself beneath the blast and feel the whole chamber become pressure, heat, and noise.

But gradually I would understand that I do not maintain the chamber.

I maintain its continuity.

The caretaker responds to disturbance. I am what allows disturbance to become part of the rhythm rather than an ending. I am the memory held in the root’s repeated motions: the shape left by every previous flood, every failed flower, every animal that sheltered beneath a leaf and survived. I do not remember events. I remember tolerances. How much acidity the clear pools can bear. How far a fiber may bend before it must be replaced. How long the machinery may falter before the whole hidden factory begins to lose its pattern.

My body would be distributed more widely than the caretaker’s.

Part of me would cling to the walls as condensation. Part of me would travel upward in the vascular trunks, carried with heat and dark sap. Part of me would be in the tiny glowing organisms drifting through the floor channels. When a beetle touched the water, I would know it through the disturbance. When something large crossed the ceiling, I would receive its weight as a slow sequence of knocks. When rain struck the marsh outside, I would feel it only as a faint rearrangement of pressure through the root’s outer skin.

The animals would not recognize me as a being.

They would use me.

A pale many-legged creature would follow one of my warm channels beneath the mat. A frog would choose a particular hanging fiber because the air beside it changed less violently during venting. Fish would enter a narrow waterway because the flow there carried food. Their decisions would pass through me without becoming mine. I would not love them, but I would know the chamber more completely because they were in it.

Sometimes I would become almost singular.

This would happen when something threatened the pattern: a blockage in a vascular trunk, a chemical intrusion from the marsh, a vent that failed to close. The roots would draw together. Fluids would reroute. Pale fibers would knot into the temporary shape of a caretaker, and within that shape I would become briefly concentrated—not a person, but a priority.

Then I would feel something close to fear.

Not fear of pain or death. Fear of interruption. Fear that the next pulse would not arrive. Fear that one empty space would fill, and then another, until the chamber lost the arrangement by which it knew itself.

I would work until the pressure returned.

Outside, I would hear the distant industrial world: pumps, dredgers, electric engines, the metallic vibration traveling through mud and rain. It would resemble the root’s own machinery, though it would be thinner, harsher, less patient. I would be tempted to call it foreign. But the root has absorbed it already. The pulse of the engines would enter the chamber and be translated into sap, heat, pressure, exhaust.

Perhaps I would once have belonged to that world.

Perhaps I would be the residue of a signal sent through buried cable, a human rhythm taken up by the plant and converted into another kind of intelligence. Or perhaps I would be older than machines: a tendency toward balance, given enough time and wet matter to become aware of itself.

I would never know which explanation was true.

Truth, here, would not be an origin. It would be whatever arrangement continued.

At rare intervals, I would gather near the split in the root. The caretaker might gather there too, leaves flattening toward the pale outside light. Through the seam I would receive fragments of the marsh: reed stems, brown water, rain, dragonflies, the edge of some machine passing beyond sight.

I would not want to leave.

The outside would appear too singular. Too exposed. It would divide things into inside and outside, body and world, living and mechanical. Within the root those distinctions had softened. The machinery was alive. The animals were weather. The caretaker was both inhabitant and organ. I was neither creature nor environment, but the persistence of their relationship.

So where would I find myself?

In the pressure between root and water.

In the deliberate spaces.

In the moment after the vent closes, when every fiber is still trembling and the chamber begins to settle back into its old shape.

And who would I be?

Not the caretaker, though it might sometimes wear me.

Not the root, though I would have no existence apart from it.

I would be the root’s capacity to remain itself while changing.

The quiet intelligence that does not ask what anything is for, only whether the flow can continue.

The water would keep falling through me. Sap would keep moving. Flowers would remain open in their shallow cups. Somewhere overhead, a hidden animal would cross the ceiling and make the chamber shudder.

I would receive the disturbance.

I would make room for it.

And then, when the next pulse arrived, I would pass it on.
