I would find myself beneath the western lip of a basin that has no name.

Not at the bottom—the bottom is cloud, and beneath the cloud there may be other mountains, or a sea, or nothing that has learned to hold a shape—but halfway down the cliff, where a root curtain descends from the stone overhead. The roots do not touch one another. Each hangs alone in the dark, pale and wet, turning slightly whenever the wind changes.

I would live among them.

Not in a house, exactly. In the hollow of a dead stalk wedged beneath the overhang, its interior widened by generations of careful cutting. The walls would be lined with folded sail-leaf and dry root fiber. A shallow stone basin would collect the water that fell from the living roots. Beneath the floor, the colony would move in its sleep.

I would be Rootbound, though perhaps not quite enough for the others.

My feet would be broad and ridged, my hands double-thumbed, my face darkened around the mouth by cold and silver spores. I would have the deep ribs and wide lungs of my people, and the green reflection in my eyes that lets me read the faint life-light beneath the leaves. But I would be a listener before I was a gatherer, and a listener is never entirely trusted.

I would know the voices of the stalk forests.

The hollow columns speak in weather, but weather is only the beginning. I would know which deep boom means wind pressing through a healthy stand and which means a stalk has split inside. I would know the dry clicking of frost from the softer clicking of roots drawing water uphill. I would know the long subterranean shudder of a burrower, the sudden tremor of a colony closing around an intruder, and the nearly inaudible pulse that comes before a cliff gives way.

I would carry no bell, no drum. The forest already has instruments enough.

When I needed to send a warning, I would strike one particular stem with the heel of my hand. It would answer in a low green note, and somewhere across the basin another stalk would take it up. Three notes for weather. Two for water. A broken sequence for death.

There would be another signal I could make, one I would never use casually.

A note for movement in the plants.

My people would tell me that plants do not approach. Plants spread. Plants root. Plants follow moisture, light, injury, and the tilt of the stone. They would say that intention is a lowland superstition, invented by those who cannot bear to live in a world without faces.

But I would have seen the leaves turn.

I would have watched a dark mat extend itself across bare rock, not toward water, not toward warmth, but toward the place where my sister had been buried. I would have found a path opened through thorn-vines before my arrival, every hooked stem bent aside, though no traveler had passed that way in years. I would have woken inside my shelter to the sound of roots touching the walls—not striking, not growing, but feeling for the shape of the dwelling.

So I would be the person sent ahead when a settlement must move.

Not because I am brave. The open spaces frighten me as they frighten everyone. Beyond the plant colonies, the stone is naked and black, and the wind has room to become a weapon. Predators lie there flattened against the rock. Sound travels strangely. Distance becomes unreliable. A ledge that seems three steps away may be across a chasm; a call that sounds beside you may have come from the opposite ridge.

I would go because I can tell when the living ground has made a decision.

I would travel at night, when the green glow was strongest. My mask would cover my mouth and nose. A water bladder would hang close against my ribs, and a coil of plant rope would lie across my shoulder. I would carry a white fungus marker and a small mineral blade—not for fighting, usually, but for cutting a single warning notch into a stem.

I would never walk in the center of a mat.

The colony might be holding water there. Or eggs. Or the memory of a wound.

Instead I would place each foot on the dark ridges where the roots had already hardened. I would pause often, pressing one hand flat to the ground. The mountain would move beneath me—not visibly, but as pressure in the bones of the wrist. Sometimes I would feel nothing. Sometimes I would feel the slow gathering of the entire basin, as if the forest were drawing breath.

Then I would turn back.

The people would ask what I had found.

I would tell them: a new crack above the northern pool; a storm coming in from beyond the black ridges; the return of the broad-legged grazers; a hunter beneath the third ledge. These things could be spoken plainly.

But if I had found the plants moving toward us, I would say only that the route was no longer ours.

They would understand.

We would extinguish the fire. We would leave the stone basins, the sleeping chambers, the woven roof, the careful channels cut into the stems. We would take the water, the tools, the children, and the dead if the dead were recent enough to carry. We would go in single file while the wind moved through the hollow stalks overhead.

No one would look back.

Except me.

I would look back once, from the far side of the shelf, where the old shelter stood beneath the root curtain. The leaves would be turned toward it. The dark living crust would have begun to close over the threshold stone. Perhaps it would be nothing. Perhaps it would only be the wind.

I would not decide.

That would be part of being Rootbound: to remain alive without forcing every mystery to become an answer.

My work would be to stay in the interval between those possibilities—to know the sound of stone breaking and the sound of a plant remembering, and to admit that they may be the same sound heard from different distances.

And when the great storm came, when the cliffs disappeared and the stalk forests bowed until they lay nearly flat, I would shelter with the others inside the root chambers. Ice would drum on the roof. The walls would contract around us. Somewhere under the living crust, water would continue its slow passage uphill.

After the storm, we would emerge into a changed world.

A new pool would shine in a hollow. A ravine would be wider. The old path would be buried beneath green threads already taking hold of the fresh stone.

I would step out first.

I would place my hand against the ground.

And I would listen for where the forest had decided to begin again.
