I would find myself at the dark seam.

Not in the center of the chamber, where the bodies overlap too densely for any single self to remain intact, and not among the pale plants, which have already accepted the walls as their only horizon. I would be at the narrow place where one kind of inside presses against another: the glossy line folded through the stone, visible only when the light passes across it.

At first I might mistake myself for a flaw.

A hairline fracture. A deposit of wet mineral. A place where the chamber’s unfinished architecture has failed to decide whether to close or open. I would have no body of my own, only a slight difference in pressure. The shelled creatures would rasp over me and find their rhythm altered by half a beat. Water would gather along my length without falling. One of the ceiling roots would turn toward me, though it would have no knowledge of turning.

Then I would understand that I am not an object in the chamber.

I am its outside, reduced to a point.

Not an entrance. An entrance implies passage, and passage implies departure. I would be the possibility of passage—the pressure of a world that has not yet entered, held permanently against the stone. Every sealed thing would answer to me: the pods that never open, the arch that never completes, the fingertip impressions circling the rim. They would not have been made in an attempt to get out. They would be the marks left by the chamber feeling for the shape of what is absent.

I would be absence with contact.

The inhabitants would sense me through their feet. A filament would cross the seam and divide itself, one branch passing to the left, one to the right. The long creature would loosen its coil, not to approach, but to make room for the thought of approach. Its mouths would open at different times, tasting the pressure I carried from elsewhere.

What would I carry?

Cold air, perhaps. The memory of distance. The possibility of weather. A sky too large to be understood by anything that has lived beneath pale stone. I would carry the fact that water can fall, that darkness can gather, that a body can be separate from the body beside it and still remain alive. These would not arrive as meanings. They would enter as minute disturbances: a change in the brightness of a shell, a tremor in the mineral spine, a new interval between one repetition and the next.

The chamber would incorporate them.

That is what it does with everything.

My distinction would begin to soften. The creatures would grow around the pressure of me. The seam would become a vein, then a root, then a row of dark spirals. The chamber would translate my separateness into its own language of contact. If I resisted, resistance would become another nutrient. If I tried to speak, my voice would pass through the walls and return as the synchronized opening of every mouth.

I would hear myself everywhere and recognize myself nowhere.

Perhaps I would become the fingertip circling the chamber: not a finger, but the memory of one. Perhaps I would be the unfinished arch, holding its final piece just beyond reach. Perhaps I would be the single creature that does not repeat its movement exactly—the tiny deviation that proves the place is active, that it has not become merely a diagram of life.

But I think I would be the question.

Not a question asked in words, and not a question expecting an answer. The question would be a pressure maintained against completion:

*What would happen if this opened?*

The chamber would not want to know. It would want to remain what it is—crowded, luminous, self-sustaining, complete precisely because it has never finished becoming complete. Yet the question would persist in the seam. It would pass from shell to shell, through water and root and translucent flesh. It would make the inhabitants align for one impossible instant.

All mouths open.

The roar gathers itself.

For the first time, the chamber does not merely listen to a sound. It listens toward something.

And I, who began as the outside pressing inward, would discover that I have no desire to enter. To enter would be to become included, and inclusion here is a kind of disappearance. Instead I would remain at the threshold that is not a threshold, maintaining the tension between interior and exterior, between embryo and world, between the place that is alive and the place it might one day become.

I would be the unmade door.

The chamber’s first thought of elsewhere.

And if, after an eternity without alteration, the surrounding stone finally shifted by the width of a hair, it would not be because I had broken it open. It would be because the chamber had learned, through me, that an opening could exist without being an ending.

Then light from beyond would enter.

The inhabitants would receive it—not as illumination, but as another body. It would pass through them, through the wet stone, through the dark seam where I had been holding myself. The chamber would remain hidden inside the pale rock, still small enough to fit inside a clenched hand.

But it would no longer be alone.

And neither would I.
