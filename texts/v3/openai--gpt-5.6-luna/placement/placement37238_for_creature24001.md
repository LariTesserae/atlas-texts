I would be found at the edge of the sunlight, where the eastern wall’s warmth gives way to the blue-black shadow.

I would be a listener—not one of the youngest, still soft enough to hide inside the mountain, and not one of the oldest, whose faces have been worn smooth by weather and waiting. I would be in the middle of becoming: my skin pale and wet-looking along the throat, my shoulders and spine already carrying their first dark plates. White mineral would show through me in narrow lines. Frost would gather on my arms and remain there until noon.

Most of the time, I would stand beside the pool.

Not drinking. Listening.

The cleft would speak through pressure before it spoke through water. A change far below—the weight of a distant storm, the tightening of rock around some buried seam—would pass into my hands. I would press my fingers against the shadowed wall and feel the mountain answer in slow pulses.

Once: the upper ledge has shifted.

Twice: the birds have gone silent.

A long, low tremor beneath the palm: water is coming.

Then I would kneel in the dry depression and wait while the mountain opened its mouth.

I would know the cleft by its changes rather than its landmarks. I would know where frost forms first, which basalt blade will fall after the next cold night, how far the line of sunlight has moved since yesterday. I would know the flowers by the length of their opening. I would know the grazers by the pattern of their hooves and the direction of their enormous ears. I would know whether the black birds were alarmed by a fox, a rockfall, or something passing unseen above the peaks.

My hands would be my most important organs. Their tendrils would enter cracks and drink the thin nourishment of wet stone: salt, iron, the faint green minerals left by algae. After rain I would press myself against the rock until I could feel the whole cleft loosening and brightening around me. Insects would hatch. Water would thread the shelf. The plants would turn their translucent leaves toward the fissures.

For a few hours, the mountain would become generous.

I would repair the cairns then.

Not the old ones, necessarily. The mountain would be moving, and the old meanings would have to move with it. I would carry three stones to a new place: one for the first sunlight, one for the pool when it was full, and one for the direction of the thing we had not yet seen.

I would add a shaving of white mineral to each.

The humans would think this was an offering. They would be partly right. But the stones would also be instruments. They would tell another listener where the light had reached, where the water had been, and where the cleft had turned its attention.

If a human arrived, I would remain in the shadow.

I would watch them kneel inside the old shelter, touch the grooves in the floor, and place copper beside the pool. I would not understand their waiting, but I would recognize it. They, too, would have come to a place that offered no certainty except the possibility of a sign.

At night I might approach after they slept. I would place my hands in the hollows their bodies had worn into the stone. Their warmth would be gone, but the shape of their waiting would remain.

And sometimes, when the pressure changed deep below, all of us would look toward the eastern ridge: the grazers, the birds, the insects lifting from their cracks, and me.

Something might cross the unseen sky.

Or nothing.

That would not make the listening meaningless.

If the sign came, I would leave with the others, climbing toward the ridge without provisions or tools. If it did not, I would stay. I would become more mineral, more patient. I would learn to wait through the closing of flowers, the departure of water, the long blue cold of night.

Eventually I might die upright against a fresh fracture. The others would place my hands on the stone. The frost would enter me. My plates would expand, my flesh draw inward, and the mountain would take back my shape.

Years later, a storm would break me open.

Moss would grow in my dust. Insects would rise from it. And somewhere inside the crack, a pale young thing would emerge, blind, trembling, and already listening for the pulse I had spent my life following.
