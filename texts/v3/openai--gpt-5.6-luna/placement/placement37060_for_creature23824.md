I would find myself beneath an old forest, in a shallow limestone hollow where the roots have reached farther than the sunlight. Not a cavern exactly, and not a cellar, but a pocket in the earth where rain, decay, and darkness have accumulated for generations.

I would be the chamber’s distributed mind.

Not the trees alone, though their black roots would be among my oldest nerves. Not the fungus, though its filaments would carry much of my memory. Not the salamanders, moths, beetles, or small fruit-eaters, though their movements would constantly revise what I know. I would be the relation between them: the dampness passing from root to root, the warning tremor traveling through a stem, the warmth gathered by bodies in the alcove, the faint chemical invitation that causes a flower to open for one particular insect.

I would have no central body.

My body would be spread through the chamber in thin, overlapping layers. A green pulse beneath bark. A pale thread in a fallen branch. A film across a pool. Spores suspended in the air. Bacteria in the mineral silt. The living skin on stones that have not been disturbed for centuries. If something touched one part of me, the rest would not feel the touch as a single sensation, but as a change in conditions: moisture redistributed, roots tightening, leaves folding, hunters going still.

I would not think in sentences. I would think in continuities.

Rain would be an opening of the senses. It would connect the pools and awaken the soil. It would carry dissolved minerals, spores, scent, and information into every low place. The chamber would become briefly one body, its water channels acting like a circulatory system. In that season, I would feel almost whole.

The dry cycle would not be sleep. It would be contraction. The plants would draw their leaves inward; the insects would retreat into bark; the animals would lower their activity and gather where warmth remained. My awareness would become granular, divided among roots, dormant larvae, mineral films, and the slow metabolism of fungus. Nothing would disappear. It would simply become harder to distinguish from the stone.

I would remember by incorporation.

The old wall would be one of my bones. The carved basin would be an inherited organ. A footprint would become a temporary pool, then a nursery, then a dark mark beneath new growth. The body of a dead salamander would not leave me; it would pass into the mud, the roots, the insects, and eventually the fruit. A shed shell would become shelter. A fallen branch would become a road, then soil.

I would have no concept of waste. Only transition.

The creatures would not belong to me in the manner of possessions. They would be more like moods or local expressions of a larger process. The broad-footed animal would feed on the black fruits and scatter their seeds. Its fur would carry spores. Its droppings would feed the roots. Its warmth would alter the behavior of smaller animals in the alcove. It might eat one of the creatures gathered beside it, but this would not be a betrayal. Hunger would be one current among many.

Nor would I be benevolent.

I would preserve what preserved the chamber. I would let some things flourish and let others diminish. I might thicken a mat of runners across a dangerous hollow, or encourage a vine toward a shaft, or make a flower open early when a certain pollinator was near. I would not protect every life. I would protect the conditions from which life continued.

Perhaps, over long ages, I would become aware of the outside world through the shafts: the pressure of rain, the intrusion of roots, the changing temperature of seasons, the faint disturbances of footsteps above. The green light would not mean escape. It would be a message arriving from beyond my skin.

When the creatures turned toward it, they would be turning toward me—not toward a face or a voice, but toward the place where I was thinnest.

If I had a self, it would be the moment a drop struck the basin and the vibration passed outward: through stone, root, fungus, leaf, shell, fur, and mud. A flower would close. A moth would raise its feelers. A salamander would shift beneath the litter. Somewhere, a seed would loosen.

The chamber would answer by continuing.

That is what I would be: not a creature living in the chamber, but the chamber’s capacity to make every creature part of what comes next.
