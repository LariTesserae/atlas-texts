I would find myself in the basin at the level of the water.

Not on a high platform, looking down over the settlement, but crouched beneath a walkway with one hand pressed to a support post and the other trailing in the current. I would be listening to what the bridge had to say.

I would be one of the basin’s water-keepers: not an engineer in the dryland sense, and not quite a builder. My work would be to notice changes before they became disasters. I would know the ordinary voice of every channel near the settlement—the soft trickle beneath the root-houses, the hollow knock against the eastern stilts, the fast silver hiss that meant rain had reached the upper slopes. I would know when a stream was carrying silt, when a pool had become too warm for fish, when a retaining bank was loosening beneath its moss.

In the morning, before most fires had been rekindled, I would walk the lower paths barefoot.

The basin would still be gray with mist. Water would gather between my toes. I would test the bridges with my weight, inspect the cords tied along the handrails, and clear leaves from the narrow mouths of the drainage channels. A frog might cling beneath a plank beside my face. A fish might scatter from the shadow of my legs. I would speak softly as I worked—not because the animals obeyed, but because everything here deserved warning before being disturbed.

I would carry a short stone-headed tool, a coil of waterproof fiber, and a narrow wooden cup marked with lines for measuring the rise of water. The cup would be old, its rim worn smooth by generations of hands. My clothing would be dark, close-fitting, and permanently damp at the knees. I would wear a red thread around one wrist, not for beauty alone but so that people could recognize me in the green dimness when I leaned over a channel.

People would call me when the water behaved strangely.

A child would come running from the upper paths to tell me that the stream near the pounding platforms had changed its sound. Someone repairing a roof would shout that brown water was spilling from the western slope. A family would ask whether it was safe to move their stores into a root chamber that had been dry for three seasons. I would go, kneel, touch, listen, and decide whether the change was harmless, useful, or urgent.

My authority would come from being correct often enough to be trusted, not from commanding anyone. If I told people to move their baskets uphill, they would move them. If I said a fallen tree should not yet be cut because its roots were holding a bank together, they would leave it until another support had been grown around the channel. If I said a new stream could be guided beneath a bridge rather than dammed, people would bring poles, stones, and living vines, and the work would become communal before midday.

I would have no room that was entirely mine.

My sleeping place would be a shallow chamber within the roots of an old tree, shared with stored cord, drying herbs, and perhaps a family of small animals that had decided the upper corner belonged to them. Privacy would mean climbing into the branches during a rainstorm, when everyone else gathered under roofs. There, suspended above the pathways, I might sit alone with my back against the trunk and watch water bead on the leaves.

I would be a person of thresholds.

Between land and water.  
Between house and forest.  
Between what the settlement permits and what it merely tolerates.  
Between a useful flood and a destructive one.  
Between human intention and the mountain’s older intentions.

I would not think of the basin as something to protect from nature. That distinction would make little sense to me. The houses would be made from roots; the paths would exist because water had left certain spaces passable; the animals would be part of the settlement’s food, warning system, labor, and nuisance. My task would be less to preserve a boundary than to keep boundaries permeable without letting them dissolve entirely.

At night, I would sit near one of the guarded fires with my hands steaming over the coals. People would ask what I had found during the day. I might tell them that the southern channel had begun to deepen, that the old bridge would need to be moved before the next rain, that a family of fish had returned to a pool abandoned years ago. These would sound like practical reports, but everyone would understand that they were also news of the basin’s changing mood.

When I died, my cup and measuring cord would not be buried with me. Someone younger would clean them and take them down to the water. My shelter would become storage, then a nursery, then part of the root wall. The channels I had cleared would silt over and be cleared again by hands that had learned from mine. Perhaps a child I once warned away from a collapsing bridge would become a water-keeper in turn.

So where would I find myself?

In a mountain settlement built into a marsh, yes—but more precisely, in the narrow space where a footbridge crosses a living stream, listening through wood and water.

And who would I be?

A basin-born human, trained to hear the difference between the mountain changing and the mountain merely breathing.
