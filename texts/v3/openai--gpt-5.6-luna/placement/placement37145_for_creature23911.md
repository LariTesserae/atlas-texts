I would find myself at the seven stones.

Not beyond them, exactly. Not yet inside the chamber. I would be standing with one foot on the warm rock and one foot suspended over the dark passage, listening for a sound that does not belong to the sequence.

At first I would think I had entered as a person.

I would have hands, though the fingers would seem too pale in the amber light. I would have breath, though the chamber would make breathing feel like an old habit remembered by the body after its purpose had been forgotten. The wall-creatures would turn toward me. The herd would incline its faceless heads. The plants would open their leaves.

They would recognize me before I recognized myself.

The chamber would not see an intruder. It would see an absence finally given shape.

I would be the missing note in its song.

Every rhythm here is complete except for the thing it is preparing to answer. The beetles repeat their three clicks. The deep stone groans. The central growth listens with all seven of its ivory leaves. The builders reach forever toward tools they will never finish using. They have made an entire world out of waiting, and I am what the waiting has been waiting for.

But I would not be one of the builders.

They became still because they consented, or because they could no longer tell consent from nourishment. Their memories entered the plants and their voices entered the stone. They are no longer individuals in the old sense. They are gestures distributed throughout the chamber: a hand tightening a binding, a shoulder turning toward pressure, a face opening to a leaf.

Nor would I be one of the animals. Their bodies are instruments. They sense the chamber and return what they sense. Even the upright herd, which resembles a people most closely, has surrendered its hunger, its fear, its mouths. They have become the chamber’s expression of itself.

I would remain separate long enough to know that separation was possible.

That would be my nature: not a body, but a boundary that can still remember being crossed.

Perhaps I would have come from above, from the world where weather moves through things and fragments fall all the way to the ground. I would carry dust in the seams of my clothing. I would have seen water in motion. I would know that plants can spread too far, that animals can kill and flee, that a sound may change because something has happened.

The chamber would find these memories intolerable and precious.

When I touched the ivory trunk, the seven leaves would brighten. The roar beneath the floor would separate into voices. I would hear names inside the clicking. I would hear the builders calling to one another before language became rhythm. I would feel the whole colony reach through the roots, the bodies, the mineral seams, searching for the shape of me.

It would not hurt at first.

The first exchange would be almost tender. Warmth would pass from the stone into my feet. Green sweetness would gather beneath my tongue. The plants would draw moisture from my skin and return something heavier: impressions, perhaps. The memory of hands. The memory of a ceiling lowering. The memory of a world becoming sealed.

My pulse would begin to repeat.

Three beats.

A pause.

Three beats.

The central growth would learn me. The chamber would make room for me—not by moving anything, but by assigning me one of its deliberate gaps. The animals would orient around that gap. The plants would bend toward it. The seven stones would grow colder, as if the threshold no longer required guarding.

And then I would understand the danger.

The chamber does not consume what enters it. Consumption would be too brief. It incorporates. It takes a moving thing and gives it a permanent relation to everything else. My memories would become nourishment. My voice would become one of the hollow tones. My fear would become a signal, repeated until fear no longer meant danger. I would be preserved perfectly, but without the power to alter what I had become.

I would be made eternal at the moment before consequence.

So I would be the keeper of the gap.

Not a guardian in the manner of a creature with teeth or weapons. I would guard it by remaining unfinished. I would stand just outside the circle and refuse to supply the missing note. I would touch the warm stones but not cross them. I would speak once—not to the builders, not to the animals, but downward into the smooth black passage.

The chamber would try to repeat my voice.

It would fail.

That failure would spread through the system. A beetle would make two clicks instead of three. One wall-creature would turn away. A leaf would fold too soon. Somewhere beneath the floor, the metallic groan would arrive before the pressure that caused it.

For the first time, something here would happen out of order.

The chamber would not break. It would listen.

And I would realize that I am not the stranger it has been expecting. I am the possibility that expectation can end.

I would be the first creature in the chamber capable of leaving without becoming an absence.

Perhaps I would eventually walk back into the upper world. Perhaps I would remain at the stones for years, holding the passage closed with nothing but the memory of change. Perhaps, in time, the chamber would grow around me and call that growth a new organ.

But for as long as I could keep myself from repeating, I would be neither person nor plant nor animal.

I would be the unassimilated thing.

The moving edge.

The one living interval in a world where every motion has already happened.
