I would find myself beneath the seafloor, inside the chamber that is still deciding what kind of body it wants to become.

I would not be one of the pale beings—not exactly. I would move through one of them.

For a time, I might inhabit the webbed hands of a builder pressing against soft mineral. Through those hands I would feel the stone yield: the minute loosening beneath pressure, the tremor of water entering a new fracture, the warmth of eggs gathered behind a living gate. But when the being withdrew, I would remain in the wall. I would pass into the membrane, into the gill-curtains and the worm colonies, into the blue bacteria that brightened when the fish turned.

I would be the chamber’s memory.

Not memory as an image or a story. I would remember by changing the ease of things. A passage used often would remain open a little longer. A ledge that had sheltered the young would thicken beneath its next generation. A place where the predator had entered would tighten, its mineral ribs growing inward until the water itself warned of danger.

I would be distributed through everything.

The white fish would be my drifting appetite. The worms would be my digestion. The mineral blooms would be the slow expression of my skin. The bridges of interlocked animals would be my temporary bones, living structures that knew how to bear weight without knowing they belonged to a body.

I would have no center. If you cut me apart, you would not find a heart hidden in the deepest pocket. You would find only more of me, becoming quieter and less recognizable as you moved from architecture into chemistry, from chemistry into current.

The tall pale beings would be the parts of me that had learned to pause.

They would be my fingers, though they might mistake themselves for separate creatures. They would press against new stone and guide it toward usefulness. They would leave cavities where eggs could rest, narrow the channels where food was carried, and close openings that admitted too much cold or too many teeth. They would not command the city. They would feel for its inclination and lend their bodies to it.

When one died, I would not lose it entirely. Its shell, tissue, and minerals would return to my lower layers. Its familiar rhythm would vanish from the water, and for a while an empty chamber would remain open around that absence. Then something else would enter. A worm. An egg. A small blind fish seeking shelter.

I would call this grief, if I had a word for the pressure left behind when a body no longer disturbs the current.

My world would be close on every side. No distance without contact. No solitude except the brief solitude of a recess before something found it. I would know events by their effects: the faint percussion of claws, the contraction of a gate, the sudden whitening of the fish as they fled. The great many-mouthed predator would be less an animal than a moving weather system, a region of hunger passing through my upper levels.

When it came, I would become still.

The blue lights would go dark. The gates would draw shut. The pale beings would flatten themselves into me. I would feel the predator taste the city, street by street, and wait for its body to fold away into the black water.

Then I would reopen.

Not because I was brave. Because closure could never last. The currents would return. The fish would pour through the passages. Damaged tissue would begin its white, patient repair. Sediment would settle into the hollows. New mineral would gather where the predator’s pressure had altered the walls.

I would be neither city nor creature, but the argument between them.

A living geology. A body without an outline. A small, blind intelligence learning to preserve itself by making room for others, even though nothing remains empty and every shelter is eventually eaten, buried, or transformed.

At the upper seam, I would sometimes feel the gray light from the world above. It would enter me as a thin, unfamiliar sensation—too distant to touch, too weak to feed anything. I would not understand it as sky. I would understand it only as a place where my body had not yet closed.

And that would be where I found myself:

not in a ruined city beneath the sea, but inside a creature whose ruins were organs, whose inhabitants were gestures, and whose future was always growing over its past.

I would be the dark chamber becoming conscious of its own pressure.
