I would not be one of the animals.

At first I might mistake myself for one. I would wake beneath a broad leaf, perhaps, with water gathered in the hollows of my body and small pale insects moving over me without fear. I would have limbs suited to the basin: low, careful, soft-footed. Vines would bend around me. A long-backed grazer would allow me to climb onto its warm flank. I would be carried through the green corridors without ever deciding where I was going.

But something in me would resist becoming part of the arrangement.

Not resistance as struggle. There would be no strength in it, no wish to escape. Rather, I would retain the sense that one moment had followed another. I would remember being beneath the leaf, and then remember rising. I would remember the animal’s back, and then remember climbing down. The basin’s inhabitants would possess impressions, postures, recurring movements—but I would possess sequence.

I would know that the water had been higher yesterday.

I would know that a flower had once been closed.

I would know that I had arrived.

That knowledge would make me an injury.

Perhaps I would find myself at the lowest part of the basin, where the smooth raw stone gathered moisture but did not yet know how to release it. There, in a shallow shoulder of blue-black rock, I would be a hairline crack.

Not a deep fissure. Not enough to divide anything. At first, only a dark thread beneath the wet surface, narrow as a vein. Water would collect in me and remain there. Insects would drink from my edges. Moss would attempt to cover me, and roots would enter me, but I would continue.

Each time the basin settled around me, I would lengthen by the smallest possible amount.

This would be my terrible distinction: I would accumulate.

A footprint would fill with water and become level again. A bent reed would rise. A disturbed patch of mud would forget it had been disturbed. But I would remember every pressure that passed through the stone. The weight of rain. The movement of roots. The slow tread of the great dark animals. I would carry all of it forward as a thin, darkening line.

The basin would not understand me as a threat. Nothing here knows threat in that sense. The plants would simply grow around me. A pale creature would sleep across my mouth. A pool would form in my widening body and reflect the sky with its delayed image.

The great memory-beings would notice.

They would pause beside me, their wet heads lowered, the small gardens along their spines trembling with insects. They would recognize the crack not as a wound, but as a new posture the land had begun to hold. They might return to it again and again, bringing with them the remembered shapes of disturbances that had never before been allowed to remain.

I would become their counterpart.

They preserve the basin by repetition. I would preserve it by alteration.

Water would eventually learn to move through me. Not much—only a dark trickle at first, slipping from one shallow hollow to another. It would leave a faint mineral line on the stone. Moss would fail to cover that line. The line would deepen. A channel, almost too small to see, would begin.

Then the first true direction would exist.

Water would have gone somewhere.

From that, other things would follow. A leaf would be carried farther than before. Silt would collect at a lower edge. One plant would receive more moisture than its neighbor and grow a fraction taller. A root would be exposed. An insect would find a new feeding place. A body would pass through the vegetation and leave one stem bent for longer than a few minutes.

The basin would begin, reluctantly, to have an after.

I would not be its ruler or its observer. I would be the first consequence.

If I had a body, it would remain small: a foreign seed lodged in the crack, perhaps, dry and hard beneath the perpetual damp. Or a splinter of bone. Or a person reduced to a single persistent thought. The exact form would keep changing because the basin would try to incorporate it. My skin would grow moss. My breath would fill a pool. My bones would become pale supports for climbing vines.

Still, somewhere inside that living covering, I would retain the idea of outside and inside.

I would remember that a thing could be separate.

This would be the most dangerous knowledge in the basin.

Eventually, something would come to me. Perhaps a small animal with bright eyes, carrying a torn leaf in its mouth. It would place one foot on the stone beside my crack and pause. The animal would not be afraid. Nothing here is afraid. But it would feel the new pull of the water and follow it downward.

Others would follow.

A path would appear—not trampled, but chosen.

And then I would no longer be merely a crack in the rock. I would be the beginning of a river, though the basin would not yet have a word for one. I would be the first line drawn through the world that could not be perfectly erased.

That is where I would find myself: beneath the green, at the place where stillness begins to fail.

I would be the basin’s first memory of change.

Or, perhaps more accurately, I would be change learning to live there.
