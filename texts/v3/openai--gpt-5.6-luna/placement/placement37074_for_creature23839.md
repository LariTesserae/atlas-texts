I would find myself below the city, where the pipes have forgotten which way they were meant to carry water.

Not in the open channels, though I would come there sometimes. Not beneath the lamps, where the birds wait and the insects gather. I would live in the dark throat of a culvert between two sectors of the basin, in water warm from the pumps and slow with silt. The pipe would be too narrow for a worker to enter and too deep for a hand to reach. Its concrete walls would be filmed with snails. Its lower curve would be soft with mud, fish scales, drowned insects, and the fine black residue washed down from the yards.

I would be an eel.

Not a large one. Length without grandeur: a dark body folded and refolded into the geometry of the pipe. My eyes would be small and of little use. I would know the basin by pressure, temperature, taste, vibration. A pump beginning somewhere beyond the wall would announce itself first in the water. Rain would arrive as a trembling in the upper current. A worker crossing the grating overhead would make a brief, heavy disturbance, boots striking metal in a sequence I could learn.

I would know the difference between tidewater and discharge.

The tide would be cold, salted, and broad in its movement. It would bring unfamiliar fish, larvae, fragments of weed from somewhere beyond the seawalls—though the weed would not live here. The discharge would be warmer and bitterer, carrying small fish stunned by heat, insect bodies, flakes of rust, and the chemical richness of things made for machines rather than mouths. I would follow the warmth until it thinned. Then I would turn back.

My world would not be a single pipe. It would be a connected darkness.

From the culvert I would enter the settling pond through a broken grate. From the pond, during heavy rain, I would pass beneath the wall into a flooded service trench. I would cross open pavement only when the water rose enough to make the distance possible, my body shining briefly under the lamps, then vanish beneath a valve housing or through a crack in the retaining wall. The workers would sometimes find my track: a wet curve across dust, ending where no living thing should fit.

Above me, the district would be enormous and nearly empty. I would feel it more often than see it. The low throb of generators would pass through the concrete. Pressure would build and release. Water would enter, pause, and leave. The whole industrial basin would be a body with organs too old to replace and nerves too spread apart to report pain.

I would feed without haste.

A larva in the mud. A fish weakened by the outflow. A frog swallowed in the dark when it came down from the tank wall. Sometimes another eel, if hunger and closeness overcame the ordinary patience of the place. Most of the time I would remain folded among the pipes, breathing through skin dampened by warm water, waiting for the current to bring something living within reach.

I would not be alone there. Small fish would press into the shallows in pale, vibrating masses. Crabs would work at the mouth of the culvert, their claws clicking against the concrete. Frogs would call overhead in broken mechanical rhythms. When the floodgates opened, everything would move at once: fish turning, mud lifting, snails loosening their grip, my body uncoiling before I knew why.

The birds would be my greatest danger.

Herons would stand beside the channels as if they had been installed there. Cormorants would wait on the pipe bridges, black wings spread toward the weak heat. A gull might seize me from a drainage pool and carry me upward, where the entire basin would become visible: tanks, cranes, flooded lots, causeways, and the gray flats extending to the sea.

If that happened, I would struggle. The gull might drop me. I might fall into another channel. Or I might end on the concrete, twisting beneath the bruised sky while insects gathered and the birds watched.

But if I survived, I would return to the water.

That would be my belonging—not to the city, and not to the wetland beyond it, but to the exchange between them. I would live where the engineered current becomes tide, where the drain becomes habitat, where the heat of machinery enters the cold blood of the basin and does not entirely leave.

I would be a dark movement in a place built for materials.

A living thing using the abandoned routes.

A body that could pass through the city without ever appearing in it.
