I would find myself at the edge of the central platform, though not standing on it.

I would be crouched in the narrow shadow beneath its rim, where the fitted pieces do not quite meet. One hand would be inside the seam. Not searching—listening.

I would be a Keeper, perhaps, but one of the porous ones.

My name would change according to who spoke it. To the children I would be **Understep**. To the elders, **The One Who Returns Things**. The plants would have no name for me, though they would grow toward the places where I had slept.

I would be narrow even by Keeper standards, with a body patched in dull green, white, and the reddish color of flower dust. My left shoulder would belong to a former version of myself. It would not fit perfectly. When I lifted my arm, the seam there would open a fraction and reveal a small depth filled with warm light. I would keep it covered most of the time.

I would not be a gardener, because I would never ask a plant to grow. I would not be a builder, because I would not trust any shape to remain itself after I had finished making it. My work would be to locate changes before they became events.

I would walk the basin each morning carrying a shallow clay dish. Into it I would collect things that had fallen out of the world: a delayed shadow, a stair that had lost its last step, a sound with no source, a door seen reflected in a wall where no door existed. I would bring these to the first place and set them around the green shoot.

The Keepers would say I was wasting useful fragments.

They would be right.

I would not know how to repair what I found. I would only know how to keep it from being mistaken for ordinary dust.

My eyes would be particularly dark, and in their surfaces the walls would appear with their seams exposed. I would see the hairline fracture beneath a person’s name. I would see when an animal had acquired an extra shadow. I would notice when a dwelling had begun to remember someone who had never lived there.

I would also know where the absent Keepers had gone.

Not exactly. I could not follow them through the openings. I could not say whether they had crossed into a corridor, a hillside, another room, or the underside of the same moment. But I would recognize their traces.

A blue fragment embedded in a new ledge.

A handprint inside a sealed threshold.

A plant bending toward a wall.

A voice heard once from within a terrace, not calling for help, but asking whether the basin had changed.

When this happened, I would put my ear to the architecture and answer with my palm.

Never with words.

Words would give the dead a reason to open.

I would have been noticed by the green shoot once, long ago. It had bent toward me for three days, then toward someone sleeping in a distant recess, then toward a crack that did not appear until nine years later. The Keepers had not called this choosing. They had watched me carefully and given me a red piece of pottery to wear.

I would have stepped toward the doorway when it formed.

I would have looked through.

And I would not have gone in.

Not from courage. Not from fear. I would simply have recognized the view: a basin enclosed by four inward-sloping walls, dense with plants, inhabited by quiet people carrying fragments from one arrangement to the next.

A room very much like this one.

Except that, in that version, a person was standing on the central platform, watching a green shoot bend toward them.

So now I would remain here, between departures and returns, keeping the two rooms from becoming certain of one another.

At dusk, I would sleep beneath a low ceiling that had not yet begun to resemble the sky. My shadow would settle beside me a moment late. Sometimes it would refuse to lie down.

In the morning, I would find a new seam beneath my ribs.

I would trace it gently with one finger.

Then I would make room.
