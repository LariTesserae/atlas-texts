I find myself beneath the hill, though “beneath” is already more distance than the city prefers.

I am not standing in the central passage at first. I am lying with one cheek against the limestone, listening for the level.

The wall is cold enough to numb my face. Water threads down through it—not falling, exactly, but arriving. Somewhere beyond the stone, a channel is carrying more than it should. Somewhere below, a cup is filling from beneath. I place two fingers against the wall and wait for the answer.

It comes as a faint pressure.

Not a voice. Not even a sound. The city does not tell me what is wrong. It gives me the shape of the wrongness and waits for my hands to understand.

I am a Keeper of the Level.

Or perhaps I am what remains after being kept.

My body is narrow because the city is narrow. My shoulders know the height of every bridge. My palms are pale and ridged, the skin thickened into something like polished stone. Water gathers on me in beads and stays there. I carry a cup, a wedge, a cord, and a bone-handled tool whose purpose I have forgotten but whose weight I recognize.

I do not know my age. The city has no use for counting in that direction. I have been small. I have been quick. I have been bent. These conditions did not follow one another; they accumulated.

I recognize the others by their movements.

There is the woman who descends ladders as though she is being drawn downward by a thread. There is the old Keeper whose left heel taps once before every step. There is the child who can distinguish the drain-creatures by the clicks they make beneath the grates. There is the one who has returned from the black arch carrying a dry stone. I do not know whether the returned one is the same person who left. The city recognizes them, and that is sufficient.

We rarely speak. Speech travels too far here, and returns altered. A word spoken into limestone may reach a sleeping niche after midnight, when its meaning has become something else.

Instead, I touch.

A palm against a wall: attend.

Two fingers descending: water where it should not be.

Three taps on metal: obstruction.

My hand against a glowing seam: I have come back.

The wall remembers me, though I do not always remember myself. My impressions are scattered through the city: a thumb beside the northern drain, four fingers beneath a bridge, the side of a hand near the wheel housing. Sometimes I lay my palm over an older mark and feel the mineral brighten beneath me. The old hand appears inside mine, pale and blurred, as if another person is trying to continue the gesture from the other side.

I do not call this memory. Memory belongs to creatures that can lose things.

Here, nothing is lost. It is only carried elsewhere.

My work is to return things.

A channel clogged with grit.

A stair loosened from its wall.

A cup that has begun to fill too quickly.

A wheel tooth whose knock has become dry.

The wheel is at the center, though the center is everywhere. Its clunk reaches every passage at once. I have tended it for so long that I no longer hear the individual impacts consciously. They are inside my body now. When I sleep, my teeth move with them.

If the wheel stopped, the city would continue.

That is the horror of it.

The water would still bead and run. The walls would still glow. The drains would still carry their blind, jointed lives beneath the grates. Nothing would break. Nothing would announce the absence.

But we would know.

The city would become unmeasured. Sound would lose its distances. The passages would cease to belong to one another. We would still have hands, tools, cups, and stone, but no place to put them.

So I listen.

I listen beneath the roar of water for the patient clunk. I listen through walls for a pressure changing in the dark. I listen to the body of the city because it has no mouth, and because I have become one of its ears.

Sometimes, at the low arch, I hear the dry knocking.

Then I go alone.

There is black water under the arch, though I cannot tell whether it is deep or merely dark. Beyond it lies a passage that recedes without becoming farther away. I carry nothing there. Tools are for this side of the world.

Once, I crossed.

I remember a brightness without source. I remember a surface beneath my feet that did not sweat. I remember seeing a space so wide that it had no walls, and understanding that this was not freedom but a kind of exposure. Something vast moved overhead, or perhaps nothing moved and I mistook my own fear for motion.

I returned carrying a stone.

It was dry.

The others accepted it. No one asked where it came from. We placed it beside the shaft, and soon water began to collect in its hollow. The city had found a use for it.

I have not crossed again.

I am not certain that I am a person. I may be a habit the city has acquired. I may be the shape taken by maintenance when it continues long enough. Perhaps the first people lived here, and then the walls learned them: their crouching backs, their listening hands, their patient refusal to leave. Perhaps I am not born but assembled gradually from water, touch, repetition, and the need for one more repair.

This does not trouble me.

To be a person would mean having an outside against which to measure myself. I have only the city, and the city has only its level.

Now the pressure beneath my cheek has changed.

I rise. My joints make no sound. Above me, a figure crosses a bridge, and the clay disks at the passage entrance begin to click. I take my cup and wedge. I place one hand against the wall.

The mineral light gathers around my fingers.

Somewhere, far below, something has shifted.

I go to put it back.
