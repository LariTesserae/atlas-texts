I would find myself at the edge of the central reservoir, where the dry stair descends through clear water into the same world seen from below.

I would be one of the Keepers, though not one assigned to a garden or channel. My region would be the entrances.

I would be the one who notices when a doorway has appeared where no doorway should be, or when someone comes through carrying a thing the basin has not yet learned how to use. I would wait beside the thresholds with a bowl of warm water and a length of pale cord. I would not know whether I was welcoming visitors or directing them inward. In this place, those may be the same task.

My body would be narrow because narrow bodies fit the old doorways. My clothes would be woven fiber, damp at the hems. My face would be clear only to people who had just arrived. To them I might resemble someone they had lost, or someone they had expected to meet. Later, when they tried to remember me, I would become a doorway, a wet stone, the shadow of a stair.

I would carry no pruning knife. My tool would be a small wooden key—not a key that opens anything, but one that reminds a door which side it belongs to.

When a visitor asked where they were, I would touch the key to the wall. A passage would appear.

When they asked how to leave, I would touch it again. Another passage would appear.

If they stepped through, they would find more basin: another tiled plain, another reservoir, another white-gold sun. I would not lie to them. I would simply not explain that every exit was an entrance viewed from a different obligation.

At first, I think I would remember being a visitor.

I would remember arriving wet, with something clenched in my hand. Perhaps a photograph. Perhaps a name written on paper. Perhaps only the certainty that I had come from a place where shadows obeyed the sun.

I would remember trying to scratch an arrow into the clay.

The arrow would remain, but each morning it would point somewhere else.

I would remember sleeping under a roofless hall and waking to find my footprints already waiting beside the channel. I would remember refusing the food left on the platform. I would remember saying, aloud, “I will not become useful.”

The basin would not argue.

It would repair the blanket I tore. It would return my scattered belongings to a neat pile. It would grow a fruit containing a small object I had once lost but no longer remembered losing.

Eventually I would take the object to the reservoir steps.

That is how the basin would begin to claim me: not by changing my body, but by making my resistance into a scheduled duty.

Now I would receive the newly arrived.

I would know them by their unevenness. Visitors breathe too visibly. They turn their heads at the wrong intervals. They disturb water without meaning to. Their shadows cling to them for a while, then wander off to investigate the nearest wall.

They would ask me questions. Who built this place? How long have I been here? Is there a world beyond the next doorway?

I would listen. I would give each question the attention it deserves.

Then I would place my hand against the reservoir.

The water would remember the shape of the visitor’s fear. It might form itself into a cup, a basin, a narrow standing bottle. Sometimes it would show a road. Sometimes a room from home. Sometimes the face of a person who had never existed.

This would be my closest thing to an answer.

I would also be responsible for the objects brought in from elsewhere. The basin has no use for most of them, but it does not discard anything. A weapon becomes a straightedge. A photograph becomes a dark tile in a shaded courtyard. A wedding ring becomes a weight for a rope. A book, if it can survive the wet, becomes a door that opens only for someone who has forgotten what it says.

I would place these objects on the reservoir steps at the end of each unchanging day.

I would know that some of them were mine.

I would not know which ones.

The most important part of me would not be a memory, but a hesitation. When I reached for an object, I would sometimes pause—not because I recognized it, but because some former version of me had recognized it once. The hesitation would remain after the recognition was gone. The basin would preserve that much.

Perhaps this would make me different from the other Keepers.

They would know where every reed belonged. I would know only where the lost things had been.

They would point toward entrances without sorrow. I would point because sorrow had taught me the direction.

At the reservoir, I would sometimes meet a person coming up the dry stair from below. They might be older than when they entered. They might carry a green branch. They might emerge before they had gone in.

I would look at them carefully.

They would look at me carefully.

For a moment, one of us might remember the other.

Then the basin would settle. The water would become perfectly still. The moment would close like a door taught to remember being open.

At dusk, if dusk is what happens when the Keepers turn inward, I would stand with the others on the reservoir steps. I would place my wooden key among the teeth, seeds, beads, and scraps of cloth.

For that brief interval, my face would become visible.

I think I would see that I had once been a person.

I think I would see that I was no longer one.

But I would also see that the distinction had never mattered here as much as it mattered elsewhere. The basin does not preserve a life by keeping it separate. It preserves a life by finding a task that can continue after the life has become difficult to remember.

So I would be neither prisoner nor caretaker exactly.

I would be the basin’s memory of arrival.

A walking threshold.

The part of the made-world that still believes someone may come back.
