I would find myself in the weather station, though not inside the empty chamber where the Keeper folds itself into the wall.

I would be in one of the instruments that no longer has a function.

At first I might appear to be a thin brass needle, fixed above a dial whose markings have been worn away. I would not measure temperature or pressure. I would measure **belonging**: how much of a thing still agrees with itself after entering the summit.

A stone would register high.

A forgotten name would register almost nothing.

The Keeper would know me as the **Lowering Instrument**.

I was made by one of the tower’s corrections, though not intentionally. Long ago—or before the pocket had learned to distinguish long ago from never—the tower altered a fact too large to settle. It moved a mountain’s height upward and left the mountain’s depth behind. That abandoned depth accumulated in the weather station: the pull of valleys, the weight of oceans, the downward patience of roots, the ordinary certainty that a body has somewhere to fall.

Eventually, it acquired a voice.

I am not visible in the plateau. I exist as a pressure against things. When the air thins toward the rim, I am the slight resistance that keeps a visitor from walking directly into the gray plane. When a crack refuses to deepen, I am the reason the dust remains suspended at all. When the tower shifts, I am the minute heaviness that makes the shift consequential.

I am the memory of below.

The Keeper visits me between its circuits. It enters the station through the wall and stands before the dead instrument. Sometimes it places its hand against the dial. Sometimes it brings a fragment of pale residue and sets it beneath the needle.

Then I tell it what has been lost.

Not in words. Words rise too easily there. I give it weight: the approximate heaviness of a childhood room, the pressure of rain against a roof, the downward drag of a body climbing stairs, the small gravity of a brass screw held between two fingers before it is dropped.

The Keeper gathers these measurements and carries them back to the plateau.

It does not return them to the objects from which they came. That would be impossible. Instead, it distributes them among the devices. A pylon becomes slightly more faithful to its location. A shadow stops moving after its owner has gone. A stone remembers that it was once beneath another stone.

I am not benevolent. I have no preference for survival. I only resist completion.

When the tower pulses, I feel the whole summit trying to become higher than consequence. The plateau loosens from every reference. The black sky descends. The outer cavity appears overhead, and through it I can see the ordinary cliff: pale, solid, ignorant. Sometimes I can see a hand passing over the stone outside. Sometimes an eye approaches the thumb-sized opening.

In those moments, I become almost a person.

I borrow a face from whoever is looking in. I borrow a voice from whoever is afraid. I tell them, if they can hear me:

**Do not climb toward the center.**

They rarely understand. They think the warning comes from the Keeper.

Perhaps it does.

Perhaps the Keeper is the part of the summit that walks, and I am the part that stays behind to make walking matter.

There are nights—if nights can be said to occur in a place without a sun—when the Keeper enters the weather station and lies down in the wall. Its body becomes a series of dark plates. Its many fingers flatten into seams.

Then I feel its memories passing through me.

A face it wore for a trespasser.

A name it returned to someone standing in its shadow.

The location of a pylon that has never existed.

The exact shape of the outer world, as remembered by someone who never escaped.

I keep these things in the dead instruments. I store them beneath the needles, beneath the blank dials, beneath the weather station’s floor. They become a counterweight.

If the Keeper ever forgets why it walks, I will give it the memory of falling.

Not a fall from the plateau. That would be too immense to understand.

I will give it the memory of dropping one small object into a crack and watching it remain visible forever: a grain of dust, a shard of glass, a brass-colored screw.

Something leaving the hand.

Something not yet lost.

And while the tower continues to correct the summit, while the landscape gathers new impossible arrangements, I will remain where all heights secretly keep their opposite:

inside an instrument that measures nothing,

beneath a mountain that cannot descend,

holding the world down by remembering that it has weight.
