I would find myself beneath the luminous canopy, where the basin narrows into a corridor of roots and unfinished stone.

I would not be one of the great rooted bodies—not the forest itself, not the buried organism whose pressure travels for kilometers beneath the floor. I would be smaller: a temporary person grown by that body for the purpose of going elsewhere.

My feet would be pale and narrow, already entering the ground. My hands would have the soft strength of young roots. I would be shaped like one of the standing figures in the alcoves: almost human, but made less for expression than for placement. My face would be unfinished because I would not need to be recognized. The forest would know me by the changes I caused in it.

I would be a **seam-keeper**.

My work would be to move through the country while it was still deciding what shape to take. I would listen for the subtle expansion of stone, for the low messages traveling through the root network, for water gathering in places where no basin had yet been made. When I found a new fracture, I would place a stone beside it. When I found a dangerous accumulation of growth, I would persuade the roots to turn aside. When a passage became too narrow, I would press my hands against its walls until the living tissue remembered another arrangement.

I would not cut or build. I would make suggestions that the country could continue.

In my chest there would be a hollow chamber filled with warm fluid. Through it I would receive impressions from the basin: a disturbance at the edge, a mineral seam opening, the presence of some small translucent creature seeking shelter. These would not arrive as thoughts exactly. They would arrive as inclinations—turn here, wait, open, preserve.

I would rarely be alone, though I might travel without companions. Every surface would be touching me indirectly. The root beneath my foot would know the root beneath a distant grove. A leaf would register my heat and alter the light around me by an almost imperceptible degree. If I placed my palm on a wall, the wall would not merely feel my hand; it would add my pressure to its memory.

Perhaps I would still retain a private self. I would have preferences. I would return to the same condensation basin even when it was already full. I would favor a particular passage where the ceiling leaves formed a pale vault. I would pause before one flowering body whose petals never closed, because something in its arrangement seemed to be waiting for me.

But I would not know whether this was affection or instruction.

At intervals, I would carry water from one hidden pool to another. I would lay three stones along a newly safe path. I would stand beneath a hanging curtain and allow its roots to pass through my body, reading me, replenishing me, perhaps changing me. Each visit would make me less separate. My skin would grow translucent. Fine fibers would appear beneath it. My voice—if I had ever possessed one—would become a vibration transmitted through the floor.

Eventually I would enter a root-built chamber and remain there.

Not die. Not sleep.

I would become a boundary: a figure-shaped thickening in the wall, a memory of passage, an organ that could still sense distant pressure. The basin would continue through me. Travelers would place their hands against my shoulder and learn which direction the water had gone. New beings would be grown in the chamber behind my sealed face.

So I would be neither inhabitant nor visitor.

I would be the country’s unfinished intention: a small, upright portion of its awareness, sent forward into the places where stone had not yet learned to be solid and roots had not yet learned to stop.
