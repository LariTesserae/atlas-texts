I would be found beneath the central square, where the cistern is older than the streets around it.

Not in the cistern itself. Nothing living enters it without permission. I would live in the narrow annulus between its stone wall and the newer masonry built around it, in a passage scarcely wide enough for my shoulders. One side of my body would rest against the warm mineral surface; the other would brush the pipes carrying water toward the nursery chambers. I would sleep there in short intervals, tethered by the wrist, listening.

I would be a Listener, though not one of the senior kind.

My body would still be recognizably Keeper: narrow, web-fingered, with a membrane over the eyes and frilled organs opening beneath the ribs. But I would have spent too many seasons beside the cistern. Fine roots would have entered the seams of my clothing. Algae would grow in the shallow grooves along my arms. Mineral crust would gather at my knees and throat where I knelt against the stone. The younger Keepers would mistake these deposits for illness until I moved, and then the crust would flex with me like a second skin.

My task would be to determine whether the cistern was still only a cistern.

I would know the ordinary sounds first. The small percussion of nursery pumps. The soft intake of the filtration beds. The pressure-click of the eastern valve. The muffled movement of the young ones beneath the square, turning in the warm water as they formed hands, eyes, and lungs.

Then there would be the deeper sounds.

A pressure settling far below.

A long vibration through the limestone.

The faint, irregular knock that never came from the same place twice.

When the stone spoke, I would press my palms against it and send the signal outward through the city’s cables: one green pulse for *ordinary*, two amber for *change*, blue only if something had entered the water that did not belong there.

I would not be permitted to call the sound a voice.

Names make people careless.

The Keepers would come to me anyway. They would gather in the passage, bodies suspended in the warm water, their lamps covered so that the light did not startle the pale growths. No one would ask what I had heard. They would ask where I had heard it, how deep it seemed, whether the vibration had traveled through the pipes or through the stone.

I would tell them what I knew.

Never everything I suspected.

Because, after many years, I would have begun to understand that the city was not simply built around a sleeping thing. The city was the thing’s method of breathing.

The pumps would not be hearts, exactly. They would be the slow muscles of its attention. The gardens would be its skin, the roots entering cracks where sensation had been lost. The old engines would be memories it had not yet finished digesting. The water would carry warmth through it, and the Keepers—our bodies, our tools, our names bolted to the walls—would be the microscopic lives maintaining its balance.

I would not know whether the first people had discovered the hollow or been grown inside it.

That uncertainty would be considered dangerous. The Listeners would insist that history was less important than continuation. The Gardeners would say that anything which fed on the city belonged to it. The engineers would say that a machine did not need to be alive to require care.

I would believe all three.

Most days, my work would be small. I would clear a screen in the nursery intake. I would guide a blind creature away from a valve. I would prune a root that had begun to turn a gear. I would repair a signal lamp with wire stripped from an obsolete elevator. I would sleep with one hand wrapped around the cistern’s pipe and wake whenever its temperature changed.

In the evenings—if one could call them evenings beneath that depth—I would leave the passage and drift through the central district.

The city would be crowded around me, but never crowded in the human sense. Vines would fill the upper corridors. Silver fish would move beneath the walkways. Water would pass through open doors and out again carrying fragments of silt, spores, and faint warmth. A Keeper would be fastening a dead woman’s nameplate beside an engine housing. A child would be learning the difference between a healthy piston and a piston beginning to dream.

I would stop at the edge of the dry air and watch them move awkwardly there.

Perhaps I would still miss air. I would remember, dimly, the first few years of my life when breath had been something taken from above rather than drawn from water. I would remember the terrible light of the upper shafts, the sensation of weightlessness when I left the current, the way a voice became sharp and exposed in a chamber without water around it.

But the memory would feel borrowed.

Air is an unfinished thing. It leaves too much room around the body.

Water tells you where you are.

At night I would return to the cistern. The lamps would dim. The nursery would become quiet. Pumps would continue their slow rotations, each one turning through the darkness with the patience of a creature that had never known urgency.

Then, from beneath my hands, the stone would knock.

Once.

I would wait.

Twice.

I would close my eyes and listen through the mineral, the roots, the pipes, the old iron, the entire packed body of the settlement.

If the knocking stopped, I would send no signal.

If it continued, I would place my forehead against the wall and answer—not with a message, but with pressure.

A small, deliberate pulse.

*We are still here.*

And from somewhere far below, in the warm darkness under the limestone, something would shift its attention toward me.
