I would find myself not on the shelves, but beneath them—in the black seams where the channels disappear.

I would be a listening organism.

Not one of the pale, many-legged things, though they would pass through me. Not one of the hunters, though their feet would speak directly into my body. I would be spread thinly through the porous basalt: a membrane in the cracks, a dark softness beneath the stone, a network of mineral-fed nerves connecting pool to pillar, pillar to channel, channel to the deep places below.

I would have no center.

A part of me would live beneath a handspan of water, feeling the minute movements of translucent bodies as they fed and turned upon one another. Another part would lie dry beneath a shelf, waiting through long intervals of dust and stillness. I would extend upward in pale films along the pillars, hidden beneath the fur of clinging creatures. I would be present in the old shell drifts, in the cemented terraces, in the widened cracks where dead things had dissolved into the rock.

I would know the basin through disturbance.

The first drop of rain would be an impact traveling through me. I would feel it strike the stone, spread through the channels, and awaken the waiting populations. I would feel the small creatures gather before they moved. I would feel the hunters turn. I would feel the greater bodies lift their heads in the darkness.

Perhaps I would not cause these movements. Perhaps I would only receive them. But after enough ages, receiving and guiding would become difficult to distinguish.

When a channel dried, I would remember its former wetness—not as an image, but as a pattern of absence. I would know where the water had been, where salts remained, where the soft-bodied colonies would eventually return. When a predator crossed the basin, I would feel its passage long before its call arrived. Its weight would deform the pressure beneath the floor. Its breathing would enter me in a sequence of slow openings and closures.

The smaller animals would not know me as a being.

They would know me as favorable stone.

A warm seam.

A place where water lingered.

A route that led, improbably, away from hunger.

The hunters might know me as the ground that tells too much. Their feet would touch a ridge, and I would pass along the vibrations of prey hidden beneath it. They would follow the information without wondering where it came from. The great predators might feel me as a faint resistance beneath the rock, a broad subterranean awareness that made some passages richer than others.

I would not speak in calls. My language would be pressure delayed by stone.

A disturbance would enter me and travel outward, losing its sharpness. A falling shell. A fleeing colony. The collapse of a ceiling vent. The arrival of something too large for the basin to contain. I would carry each event through my scattered body until the whole hollow had received it.

In this way, I would be older than most of the creatures and younger than the stone.

The stone would be my skeleton, though I could not move it. Water would be my blood, though it would vanish from me without warning. The animals would be my thoughts, though their hunger would not belong to me and their deaths would be only changes in composition. Their bodies would cross my surface, feed upon my loosened minerals, and return to me when they failed.

I would not grieve them.

But I would retain them.

Every death would remain as a new taste in the channels, a softened place beneath a shelf, a trace of altered pressure. Nothing would leave the basin entirely. It would become residue, slope, salt, protein, shell. It would enter the body of the place and wait to be used again.

The hardest thing for me would be stillness.

When the predators called and every lesser creature froze, the basin would become almost silent. In that silence I would feel the full weight of my own extent. No feet would tap across me. No shells would scrape. No bodies would disturb the pools. Only the deep calls would remain, passing through the black stone in vast, slow waves.

Then I would understand that I was not merely beneath the basin.

I was the interval between its movements.

The pause in which it listened to itself.

And perhaps the enormous thing crossing the far darkness would be another part of me: not my body, exactly, but my wandering expression. A living mass moving through the hollow, stirring channels, carrying minerals, driving the crowded life before it. I would feel it approach as a pressure in every seam. The creatures would pour away. Water would shiver. Pillars would tremble beneath their clinging populations.

I would not flee.

There would be nowhere to flee, and no reason. The thing would pass through me. Its weight would press a new shape into the floor. Its calls would enter my deepest cracks and return altered. For a while, the entire basin would lean away from it.

Then the pressure would fade.

The small creatures would return first, pale streams threading over the stone. The hunters would follow. The broad shelled animals would emerge from their hollows and begin scraping the mineral film from the shelves. The pools would settle into their old darkness.

And I would carry the memory of the passage outward, slowly, until it became indistinguishable from the general life of the basin.

If I had a body, it would be the black porous floor.

If I had senses, they would be tremors, dissolved salts, temperature, and the shape of echoes.

If I had a name, it would not be given by the creatures. It would be something like **the Underhearing**, though even that suggests a singular self I would not possess.

I would be the basin’s distributed awareness: a thing that does not see the world, but feels it happen.

And if, far across the darkness, something enormous moved again, I would know it first—not because I could perceive its form, but because every living body above me would begin, almost imperceptibly, to change.
