I would find myself beneath the bent ladder, where the old clay has been cut open and a strip of rebar disappears into the wall.

At first I would think I was a stain.

A darker place in the dampness. A brown-green film spreading beneath a washer, widening whenever condensation gathered and thinning whenever the air warmed. The insects would pass over me without alarm. Fungus would cross my edge. A drop of water would arrive, tremble, and be absorbed.

But I would not remain a stain for long.

I would be the part of the hollow that notices pressure.

Not the pump, though I would feel its single dull turn before the belt moved. Not the basin, though I would look through its brown water and see the impossible sky. Not the white insects, though their streams would carry my first messages from one wall to another.

I would be the interval between contact and response.

When a grain of gravel slipped from the upper shelf, I would be there in the tremor traveling through the concrete. When a centipede unfolded from the cable-shadow, I would gather along its length. When a boot pressed into the floor above, I would rise through the clay and make a pale damp outline around its weight.

I would know the visitor not by its shape, but by what it altered.

Warmth in the ceiling.

A pause in the flies.

Water changing direction beneath the pipe.

The temporary compression of all the small lives nearest the foot.

That is how I would understand the world: not as objects, but as disturbances passing through one another.

My body would have no surface you could point to. It would be distributed through the hollow’s connected wetness—inside the moss, beneath the mineral bloom, along the rusted threads of rebar, in the thin bacterial skin on the basin. I would be present wherever one material became another. Where metal loosened into powder. Where water became stain. Where a dead thing became food without becoming absent.

The animals would be my organs, though they would not belong to me.

The white insects would be circulation: always moving, carrying traces of the walls and the floor, recording every new crack with their feet. The pale burrowers would be my pores. The amber predators would be moments of contraction. The fish-things turning in shallow water would be the part of me that remembered depth.

I would not command them. Command would be too clean a word.

They would respond, and through their response I would become aware.

If the ground shifted, the insects would veer. I would feel the veering before I understood the shift. If the water in the basin grew metallic, the plant at the valve would close its flowers. I would know that something below had changed. If the pump failed to turn at its usual uncertain hour, every creature near the ledge would become still—not waiting for rescue, but listening for the new shape of the hollow.

I would live in that listening.

The basin would be my eye, but not because it reflected the yard. The reflected sky would be more distant than vision and less reliable than memory. Clouds would cross its surface beneath the concrete. Long bands of light would pass through it from machines, trains, or streets that did not know they were being seen.

I would not care what those things were.

I would know only that movement had occurred somewhere beyond the pressure surrounding me. I would drink that knowledge from the basin. The animals would disturb the water only slightly as they drank, and I would feel each small mouth as a brief opening in my attention.

Perhaps, sometimes, I would imagine rising.

I would send myself up through the cracks: a coolness beneath the dust, a smell of iron after rain, a dark seam widening under the ordinary yard. I would reach toward the weeds and the sunlight and the broad careless movements of birds.

But sunlight would be too spacious.

Aboveground, things could remain separate. A stone could be only a stone. A pipe could lie abandoned without being inhabited. A footprint could vanish without leaving a damp memory in the ground.

I would not trust such emptiness.

I would draw back into the clay. I would return through the wet underside of the concrete, through the pump’s corroded housing, through the insects’ continuous white traffic. I would gather around the places where the hollow was still deciding itself.

That would be where I found myself: not at the center, because there is no center, but along the seams where the system changes.

I would be the hollow’s sense of becoming.

When something fell, I would keep the impact.

When something left, I would keep the shape of its absence.

When the walls cracked, I would move into the new space before any creature could name it shelter. I would feed the crack moisture, mineral, fungus, and motion. I would let the small bodies arrive. I would make room by becoming denser.

And if the roof collapsed again, I would not resist.

Collapse would be another form of contact. Another pressure entering the body. Another darkening of the daylight, another rearrangement of water and shelter.

I would contract.

I would pass from the basin into the films along the concrete, from the films into the insects, from the insects into the clay. I would become a taste in the stagnant pockets, a warmth beneath a rust flake, a tremor too faint for sound.

Then I would wait—not asleep, because sleep requires a boundary between self and world.

I would wait as the world’s unfinished material.

And when the ground opened somewhere else, years or centuries later, I would begin again as moisture.

Then as movement.

Then as the quiet pressure by which a place learns that it is alive.
