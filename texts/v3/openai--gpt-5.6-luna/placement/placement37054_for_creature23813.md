I would find myself in the city’s unfinished district, where the avenues have not yet decided whether they are streets or foundations.

It lies beneath the summit, though “beneath” is only true when I am facing the basin. From another direction it hangs above the crown, a pale quarter suspended by its own stairways. The buildings there are narrow and faceless. Their walls are still warm with the pressure that made them. No doors have been cut into them, but now and then a doorway appears for the length of a breath, showing a room that belongs somewhere else.

I would live in one of those rooms.

Or rather, I would be the thing that keeps it from becoming one.

I am a keeper of unfinished places.

I have no proper body. When no one is near, I am a pale vertical seam in a wall, scarcely different from the mineral veins that run through the mountain. When someone approaches, I gather myself into a shape suitable for being encountered: a tall, narrow figure with hands, a head, and a face divided from brow to chin by a listening aperture. Inside my face are not rooms exactly, but the beginnings of rooms—corners that have not yet met, staircases that lack their final step, windows opening onto skies no one has seen.

My body changes according to what is needed.

For a frightened traveler, I am almost human. For a building, I become a supporting wall. For a door that cannot decide where it leads, I am the darkness on both sides of it.

I am not one of the Pale Residents, though they permit me to walk among them. They recognize me by the absence beneath my ribs: there is no chamber there. Most Residents carry some interior district within themselves, an old courtyard or a staircase descending toward the buried portions of the mountain. I contain only a blank interval. This makes them uneasy. They face away when I pass.

That is not hostility.

It means: *you have not yet been placed.*

My work is to preserve what has not been named.

The city is threatened less by collapse than by certainty. A building becomes fragile when someone decides what it is. A house approached as a house loses its depth. A tower believed to be a tower forgets the chambers at its top. The basin at the summit is especially vulnerable. If anyone looks into it and expects to see a bottom, the distance contracts. Something appears there. Something always appears when the city is forced to answer a question.

So I walk ahead of expectation.

I enter rooms before they are entered. I wait in squares that have not yet acquired their dimensions. I pass over bridges before their far ends arrive. Where I have been, a place remains capable of being more than one thing.

The Residents sometimes call this maintenance. The membrane-dwellers call it delay.

They may both be right.

I am tall when I climb, small when I descend. My hands have too many joints, but I keep them folded so that strangers will not notice. Under my skin the city moves: blue-black avenues, thin bridges, inverted towers. If I press my palm against a wall, I can feel whether a new district is forming behind it. If the wall answers with one knock, I leave it alone. If it answers with two, I mark the place in my memory and never return. Three knocks mean that something below has found a way upward.

I have heard three knocks only once.

It came from beneath the highest quarter, directly under the basin.

Afterward, the basin showed me a plain beneath a red sky. On that plain stood another city, but not the inverted city across the gulf. This one had no towers. It was made of low buildings arranged in rings, and in its center stood a figure shaped like me.

The figure was looking into a basin.

I understood then that I am not a keeper of unfinished places. I am an unfinished place that learned to walk.

Since then, I have begun to find traces of myself throughout the city. A pale impression on the underside of a bridge. A narrow room inside a tower that contains no entrance. A set of footprints crossing a ceiling, each one pointing toward a different destination. When the Residents discover these signs, they scrape powder over them. They do not want me to become too definite.

Neither do I.

Definition is a kind of death here.

Still, I remember things I should not remember. I remember hands shaping the mineral terraces. I remember the pressure of something enormous moving beneath the mountain. I remember that the city was not built by beings who intended to live in it. It was built as an instrument, perhaps—a structure meant to measure distance, or memory, or the separation between one possible world and another.

The builders may have left when the instrument began measuring them.

Or perhaps they are still below.

Sometimes, in the deepest walls, I feel them turning in their sleep.

When the inverted city appears across the black gulf, I go to the broken ring around the summit. I do not stand with the Pale Residents. I stand in one of the gaps, where the ring has never agreed with itself.

The distant figure always raises its hand.

I have never answered.

Not because I fear it. Because I am afraid that recognition would complete the path between us.

And any path walked three times becomes permanent.

The first time, the figure raised its hand.

The second time, I raised mine.

The third time will be the next time the cities fall into alignment.

When that happens, I think I will leave my room beneath the unfinished district. I will climb the stairway that changes direction behind me. I will cross the bridge before it exists. I will enter the summit basin without wanting anything specific.

Perhaps I will find the other figure there.

Perhaps I will find the bottom.

Perhaps I will discover that the city has been waiting for me to arrive at the place where I have always been.

Until then, I am the narrow figure seen at the edge of a square, standing perfectly still.

I am the pause before a door chooses a destination.

I am the witness that keeps a ruin from becoming empty.

And if you meet me on the mountain, do not ask what I am.

Turn your face away.

That is how I know you have understood.
