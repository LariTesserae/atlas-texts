I would find myself in the lowest part of the basin, where several shallow channels meet beneath a shelf of softened limestone.

At first I might mistake myself for a frog.

I would have the same patience: the ability to remain still while insects settle on my back, while birds pass nearby, while the heat presses down and the mud slowly cracks around me. I would know the discipline of the sudden movement—the single leap, the single strike, the vanishing ripple.

But I would not be small enough to belong entirely to the world of frogs.

Then I might seem like a snake.

I would lie half-submerged along the channel margins, my body colored like tea-stained stone, my breathing so slight that water insects could mistake me for a root. I would wait for fish, crabs, and the careless birds that descended too close. I would hunt by remaining present until presence became invisible.

But I would not be narrow. This land does not ask for elegance. It asks for contact.

I would be broad and low, an amphibious animal perhaps three times the length of a human arm, with a body shaped more like a fallen branch pressed flat into mud than like anything built to travel quickly. My legs would be short and splayed. My feet would be wide, each toe joined by a thin membrane, made for moving over ground that could either hold me or swallow me. Along my back, small plates of pale mineral would form as I grew, not armor exactly, but deposits of the basin: chalk, silt, the green stain of long-wet stone.

I would be called, by any creature capable of naming me, a **siltback**.

Most of the time I would be beneath the surface.

Not deeply. There is no deepness here to hide in. I would occupy the first handspan of earth: the cool mud beneath a limestone plate, the wet hollow under reed roots, the narrow spaces where seepwater traveled through the stone. My body would remain folded there through the brightest part of the day, absorbing the basin’s changes through my skin.

I would know water before it arrived.

Not as prophecy, but as pressure. A tremor beneath the mud. A change in mineral taste. A loosening in the channels. Before rain reached the open flats, before frogs began calling from newly filled hollows, I would feel the hidden water gathering itself below me.

I would emerge then.

Rain would bring the only thing that could resemble excitement into my life. I would leave my shelter and move sideways through the wet ground, following the spreading water into places that had been dry for weeks or seasons. My path would be marked by two shallow furrows and a broad drag between them. In the newly flooded hollows I would find larvae, soft-shelled crabs, drowned insects, and small fish confused by the sudden enlargement of their world.

I would not be a dominant predator. Nothing in the basin would need to be.

I would take what came within reach. A frog. A fish trapped beneath a grass mat. The body of a bird that had fallen during the storm. Sometimes I would dig for worms, pushing my blunt snout through the mud until the water clouded around me. My senses would be less about sight than vibration and taste. I would feel the approach of a wader through the stones before I saw its legs. I would know a snake by the particular disturbance it made in the shallow water.

When threatened, I would not flee far. There is nowhere far to flee.

I would flatten myself against the basin floor, draw my legs close, and become one more stained plate of limestone. My skin would darken with wetness. A bird might step over me. A mammal might pass within a body length and never understand that the mud had briefly breathed.

At dusk, I would travel.

I would follow the remaining channels as the water withdrew, moving between pools by routes remembered in my body. Some would be visible: cuts through gravel, dark ribbons between reeds. Others would lie beneath the basin, through cracks and softened seams in the old stone. I would know which surfaces held weight, which mud concealed a drop, which pools had enough oxygen to last through the dry weeks.

I would return to the same places, but never to the same basin.

Every season would alter the routes. A shelf would collapse. A seep would close. A new channel would appear after rain and vanish beneath mineral crust. My life would be a continuous adjustment to small changes that no creature standing upright could notice.

I would lay my eggs in the mud where floodwater had recently withdrawn.

They would be pale, gelatinous clusters threaded among reed roots. Some would be eaten. Some would dry. Some would remain dormant until the basin filled again. I would not guard them. The place itself would be their protection: its unpredictability, its layers of wetness, its habit of hiding life just below the visible surface.

In the dry season I would become slower.

I would retreat beneath the coolest limestone, seal my body in a chamber of damp clay, and wait. Outside, birds would gather around shrinking pools. Predators would find easier meals. The reeds would click in the hot wind. The basin would appear almost empty.

But below, I would remain awake enough to listen.

Water would continue traveling through the dark.

That would be my deepest understanding: that the surface is never the whole world. Beneath the cracked mud, beneath the pale stone, beneath the apparent stillness, something is always moving toward the lowest ground.

I would not rule the basin or speak for it. I would be one of its small continuities—a body made from waiting, water, and the gradual softening of stone.

When the rains returned, I would rise with them.
