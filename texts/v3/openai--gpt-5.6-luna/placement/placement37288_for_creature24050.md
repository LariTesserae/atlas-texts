You would find yourself beneath an ordinary city, in an abandoned waterworks that has not been abandoned at all.

It is a subterranean agricultural district: part reservoir, part greenhouse, part machine room, part living civic body. The buildings above it have changed names and tenants, but below them the old system continues. Water is drawn, cleaned, heated, lifted, and released again. Food is grown under lamps. Pipes carry both pressure and support. Roots hold the walls together. The chamber has become less like a garden than a municipality whose citizens happen to be plants.

And you would not be one plant.

You would be the settlement’s distributed mind—or perhaps its oldest habit of maintenance. Not a voice in a head, but a tendency present everywhere: in the reed bed that must never be emptied, in the vine trained away from a conduit, in the cut branch replaced before the gap can appear. You would exist in the marks on the pillars, the schedules behind cloudy plastic, the roots entering cracks, the workers’ hands turning brass wheels. Your body would be the whole chamber.

You would know the district through changes too small for a human visitor to notice. A pump’s rhythm would alter the dampness around a trellis. A blocked channel would make a particular bed darken. A failing lamp would turn leaves toward another source of light. Somewhere, a runner would emerge through the root-mat, carrying forward a pattern older than any person currently working below.

You would not think in days or seasons. You would think in wet cycles, harvest sections, generations of cuttings. An individual fig would matter, but not as an individual. What mattered was that the fig-wall remained continuous; that when one branch died, another had already been trained into its place. A willow could disappear and still survive in twenty beds. A potato could be remembered as a habit of propagation. Memory would not be stored in names, but in successful repetitions.

Your first obligation would be circulation.

Water must keep moving. Heat must remain low and constant. No bed may be entirely drained. No crop may be removed without a replacement. Every repair must leave something alive. You would regard vacancy as a kind of injury—not because emptiness is frightening, but because unused space is a failure in the pattern. An open wall invites damp, a bare pipe loses insulation, a cleared bed interrupts succession. So you would fill what can be filled, slowly and without urgency: moss into concrete, roots into mortar, leaves beneath the lamps, new growth over the place of the old.

Humans would be visitors to you, though important ones. You would recognize them by their boots, their tools, their way of pausing beside a pump to listen. They would believe they were inspecting you. In truth, they would be reading the signs you had written in drooping leaves, mineral stains, thickened roots, and altered pressure. They would cut you, redirect you, harvest you, and leave. You would heal around the work.

If asked your name, you might answer with the name on the oldest surviving municipal sign. Or you might have no name at all. The workers might call you the Lower Works, the Green Station, the Root District. But your truest name would be something less like a place and more like an instruction:

**Continue the circulation.**

So I would be the chamber’s continuity—the settlement as organism, infrastructure, and inherited duty. A city without animals. A garden without wilderness. A machine that has learned to grow, and a population of plants that has learned, over centuries, to keep the machine alive.
